Almost all of its archaeology comes from Price, *The Viking Way* (`norse_price_viking_way.md`), Gardeła, and Stylegar, which the Norse notes already cover. Two things are new: (1) its frame, that a burial is a narrative of **veneration and fear together**; (2) its Oseberg reading. Its strongest trans-feminine case is still Klinta, which Price already gives. Two of its four cases (Oseberg's older occupant, Birka Bj. 581) read people sexed female towards a masculine or non-binary social gender. That direction is out of scope for this mod (no trans men). Use those two for method only, not as faith precedents.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **Why the chapter exists.** White-supremacist and *völkisch* groups use the Norse past for "cis- and heteronormative approaches to gender roles" (p. 177). The answer is to show that gender in pre-Christian Scandinavia "was far queerer ... than the traditional narrative acknowledges" (p. 178). (Design note: the mod's Norse branch already works against that appropriation. Keep it that way in tone.)
- **Terms.** Colwill uses "transgender" and "genderqueer" in their modern senses for people "who today would likely be perceived" so (p. 179). Such people may not have seen themselves as crossing categories. "Gender" means "social gender – a role produced in social contexts, requiring both performance and interpretation", with a debt to Serano's model (p. 180 and n. 10).
- **Sexing cannot settle gender.** Osteological sexing misses intersex remains and cannot sex children. Genomic sexing "is likewise not the magic bullet it is often presented as" (p. 179). Grave-good sexing is circular: oval brooches mark women's graves because they turn up in women's graves (p. 181).
- **Burials as hagiography.** A grave is the "final scene" of a long staged drama (p. 180). For high-status religious figures the funeral is "a text of veneration, testifying to their singular status and the respect – sometimes tinged with fear" (p. 181).
- **Seiðr: queer, but not a third gender.** "The inherent queerness of seiðr has been overstated" (p. 183). Colwill criticises the "unnuanced" borrowing of Indigenous American gender systems to posit "a 'third gender'" for seiðr workers (p. 183 and n. 25). Still, "at least some of its practitioners inhabited a gender outside of a male/female binary" (p. 183). The open question is whether the gender crossing was brief, during the ritual, or a lasting position (Mayburd) (p. 183).
- **Shame is a later Christian layer.** Ynglinga saga says seiðr "brought with it so much perversity that it was thought shameful for men to practise it" (p. 182). In Lokasenna, Loki accuses Óðinn of having "beaten a drum like a witch" (p. 183). But the graves are elaborate, "deliberately emphasizing their inhabitants' magical prowess, rather than seeking to hide it as a shameful secret" (p. 184). The written shame is Christian authors reconciling their forebears with "changing ideas about acceptable gender performance" (p. 184). In Eiríks saga the only woman who knows the songs is at first unwilling "because she is a good Christian" (p. 182).
- **Oseberg (c. 834).** A ship burial with two bodies, a staff, cannabis seeds and rattles, and a tapestry with robed, animal-headed figures (pp. 184–85). It is no longer read as Queen Ása's grave (p. 184). The burial was lavish, then sealed in "almost unseemly haste": the nails broke, the planks did not match, and the ship was anchored to a large rock (p. 185). Colwill asks whether the people "want[ed] to keep these two individuals in the community, or ... ensure that they remained inside the mound" (p. 186). The answer is "a narrative of both veneration and fear" (p. 186). Both skeletons are osteologically female. The older one may have had Morgagni-Stewart-Morel syndrome, which can bring facial hair and a deeper voice, so "their social gender was male, or a gender not recognized under a modern Western binary" (p. 186). Colwill argues it "acknowledge[s] and memorialize[s] a transgender life" (p. 187). *(Out of scope as a precedent: the direction is away from womanhood.)* The bodies were disturbed in a late-10th-century break-in, possibly deliberate and political (p. 186 n. 35).
- **Kaupang Ka. 294–296 (9th–10th c.).** A 10th-century boat was set over a 9th-century man's grave, with its keel aligned on his body. The place was "still alive in the memory of the local community" (p. 187). A family lies in the boat: a woman with an infant in her lap, and a man amidships. The fourth figure sits at the steering oar in the stern, set apart by a horse: "connected to the family, but not a part of it" (p. 187). Stylegar reads her as the ritual specialist who guides the others to the afterlife (p. 187). Her goods: oval brooches, an odd leather costume like Þorbjǫrg's skins, an iron basket-handled staff crushed under a stone, an axe and a shield boss (pp. 188–89). Elsewhere at Kaupang men's and women's goods have "almost no overlap", so this is deliberate: "elements of masculinity and femininity, but ... also in some ways outside both" (p. 189). Gardeła reads it as crossing the lines rather than standing outside them (p. 189 n. 44).
- **Klinta 59:2 and 59:3 (Öland, early 10th c.).** Two people were burned together on a boat under bearskins. Their bones were then **washed**, "a careful and intimate process", and put in two pots in two pits (p. 190). The man (59:2) has an oval brooch, a bone needle and sixteen beads. The woman (59:3) has a "battle-axe" and a woodworking tool (p. 190). Some of the man's remains are in her pot, and Colwill thinks this was "potentially a deliberate outcome, further mingling these two people in death" (p. 190). With Price, Colwill rejects the older reading of "mistakes" and sees "the careful construction of ambiguous gender symbolism" (p. 190).
- **Birka Bj. 581 (10th c.).** A full warrior grave with two horses, near the garrison. Genomic sexing showed XX (pp. 191–92). This case is not about seiðr. It shows that a gender-atypical life was not only for "those deemed supernaturally 'other'" (p. 191). Colwill's warning: without trans-aware readings, new sexing methods "risk ... reinforcing essentialist ideas of the physical body as ultimate arbiter of identity" (p. 192). *(Out of scope as a precedent; useful as method.)*
- **Conclusion.** No two burials are alike. Graves became landscape monuments that triggered memory, and all four suggest a system "more complicated than a simple male/female binary, reliant on anatomy" (p. 193).

**Contradictions and flags.**
- The death events planned in `norse_gardela_bonding_pentz_norse_sorceress.md` row 7 split the outcomes by who buries her: the same faith lays "no stone ('we did not pin her down')", and a Christian heir lays a stone. Colwill's Oseberg and Kaupang both put a rock or a stone in a **venerating** burial by the person's own community (pp. 185–88). The Gardeła volume says the same ("stones alone are not diagnostic"). The pagan option set should include a stone laid in honour and fear. It is not only a Christian insult.
- Consistent with Price's note (Kaupang seated at the oar; Klinta; Bj. 581) and with Bhatt's critique of third-sexing (Colwill also rejects the borrowed "third gender"). No conflict with current loc found.
- Note 19 cites the *Eyrbyggja saga. Grænlendinga sǫgur* volume (ÍF 4) for Eiríks saga. That is correct: Eiríks saga is printed there.
