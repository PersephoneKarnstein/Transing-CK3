## 1. The argument

**Thesis.** "During much of this time, women's property rights were steadily improving ... Then the Mongol invasion ... precipitated a re-Confucianization of the law and a swing back toward patrilineal principles" (p. 1). The Song was not a golden age, but its law worked "to the detriment of the agnatic patriline", benefiting "daughters and other nonagnates" (pp. 97–98). The Yuan reversal came from an "extraordinary confluence" of steppe household-and-service policy and Zhu Xi's Learning of the Way (p. 267).

### 1a. Song: daughters, extinct households (戶絕) and the state

- **How common it was.** Rough genealogy figures for 1050–1400 show 22% of men with no surviving son. "Cut-off" households "may have prevailed in over 20 percent of households in Sung times" (p. 97 and n. 98).
- **The base statute (Tang 737, copied into the Song code).** Agnates sell the movables, pay the funeral, and "give the remaining property to the daughters". With no daughters the property goes to agnates; with no relatives, the government audits and confiscates. A "clear and verifiable" testament overrides all of this (p. 98).
- **The Song widened it.** Cut-off status came to apply to each conjugal unit (*fang*) inside a joint family, so an orphaned girl took her father's share even if her uncles lived on (p. 99). "When she had no brothers, she virtually took her father's place in the line of property transmission" (p. 99).
- **The state grabbed more, then backed off.**
  - 963: married daughters get ⅓, with ⅔ confiscated (p. 100).
  - 1015: estates with no daughters no longer go to agnates; the government takes and sells them (p. 105).
  - 1026: the confiscated portion goes instead to people resident three years or more who helped run the estate, "uxorilocal sons-in-law, foster sons, children of the wife from a previous marriage, and even tenants". Only if there are none does the state confiscate (p. 105).
  - 1132: a posthumous heir with no daughters gets ⅓, the state ⅔, capped at 3,000 strings (pp. 106–107).
  - Testamentary freedom was cut under the New Laws (1064–86) and partly restored in 1162 (pp. 108–109).
- **Judges resisted.** "Some judges were openly critical of aggressive policies of state confiscation." A Jianyang judge said officials there "should follow principle (*li*) and not seek profit" (p. 107). Fan Yingling quoted the caps "to coerce the parties" but "did not confiscate any property in either case" (p. 107).
- **Residence and care beat blood.** "Those residents in the household who helped manage the estate and care for the aged owners were favored to inherit over those who may have had more abstract claims of patrilineal connections" (p. 104).
- **Table 2 (p. 111).** "Sons & adopted daughters": the adopted daughter takes the same half share as a natural daughter. Unmarried daughters alone take everything, up to 5,000 strings. With a posthumous heir, an unmarried daughter takes ¾. With only a posthumous heir, the state takes ⅔.
- **Clergy estates.** By a regulation of 1195–1200, when a monk died his (non-resident) relatives arranged his funeral "and the government awarded them property from the estate for that purpose" (pp. 105–106). The rest is not discussed.
- **Orphan girls.** The state audited and held property for orphan girls, returned "when they 'came of age' as opposed to when they married" (p. 95). "Female households" (*nü-hu* 女戶) often meant such girls and their estates (p. 95).

### 1b. Song: the wife's own property

- Dowry and anything bought in the wife's name stayed hers and left with her: "Once property was attached to a married woman ... it stayed with her permanently" (p. 114).
- **Madam Wang v. her stepson (CMC 10:365–6; pp. 115–120).** Her husband died in 1216. She remarried and took her 23 *chung* of dowry land, 47 *chung* bought in her name, and the household goods. The judge laments widow chastity and then rules for her: "The contract was made in Madam Wang's name. What more can be said?" (p. 117). He can only "request", not order, that she house the destitute stepson (p. 118).
- Women used such capital to run pawnshops and shipping, and "on their own initiative, repair temples or build dams" (p. 140).
- **Lady Shang-kuan's grave cloister (pp. 156–158).** Kinsmen secretly sold the Chi family graves to a monastery. She redeemed them with her whole dowry, bought more land, "and she set up a cloister to protect them". Woodcutters afterwards would not touch the trees, saying "This land was given by Lady Shang-kuan." Birge notes that such grave cloisters were often tied to White Lotus groups (p. 157 n. 59). This is a woman using a religious house to hold land against her own husband's lineage.

### 1c. The Confucian reaction (Song theory)

- The Learning of the Way wanted the "descent-line system": shared agnatic property, with ritual run by the eldest male heir. "Fundamental to these principles was the subordination of juniors to seniors and women to men" (p. 144). Zhu Xi praised wives who gave their dowry to the husband's household; Huang Kan wanted widows chaste and kept in the husband's house (pp. 151–197, skimmed; summarized on p. 281).

### 1d. Yuan: the reversal (the era arc)

- **1260 and 1264, Khubilai.** If a cut-off household has "no one suitable to inherit", everything "will all be confiscated by the government" (p. 220). "Suitable" now included **brothers**, a steppe lateral inheritance never allowed in Chinese law (p. 222). The property of minors went under state management (p. 221).
- **Adoption came under military scrutiny.** "Even adoption of an agnatic nephew, a routine matter in the Sung" was allowed only if the adoptee took over the uncle's military or tax service (p. 223).
- **Daughters lost the estate.**
  - 1273 and the code of 1316/1321: a sole daughter inherits only if the state "invite[s] in a husband to continue the household and assume the tax and corvée obligations" (p. 227).
  - "A daughter without brothers could now enjoy her father's property only through her husband" (p. 227).
  - There is no evidence of the Song half-share rule under the Yuan (p. 217).
- **Chang Ah-Liu's case (1268–71; pp. 223–226).** A Kaifeng woman sued to keep her parents' estate. Four offices ruled four ways: first for two bondservants; then for her husband as uxorilocal son-in-law; then for confiscation; finally ⅓ to the two married daughters and ⅔ confiscated. Her mother's household had been registered as a "woman's household" (*nü-hu*).
- **1303, the dowry law.** Proposed by Dorchi, a Mongol or Uighur official at Huizhou. A remarrying woman's dowry "should all be taken over by the family of her former husband. She is absolutely not permitted to take them away with herself, as was formerly done" (p. 263). The Ministry of Rites added an exception for a wife "expelled without proper cause".
- **1304, chastity awards.** A widow before 30 who vowed chastity and kept it past 50 got door insignia and corvée exemption. This is the origin of the memorial arches (pp. 264–265).
- **1309.** In-laws may arrange a widow's remarriage and keep the betrothal gifts; a chaste widow must stay in her husband's house (pp. 265–266).
- **1313.** Women may not file lawsuits except where no male relative can act for them (pp. 268–269). The complaint behind the rule says litigious widows "would ... visit tea houses and wine taverns and lodge with Buddhist and Taoist monks" (p. 268).
- **1330.** The levirate is banned for Chinese (p. 275).
- **After the Yuan.** "The 1303 law that prevented a widow from keeping her dowry was carried over into all later codes in China before the modern era" (p. 277). Practice varied widely (pp. 278–279).
- **Religious property followed lineage power.** "Buddhist temples in the South, which had been heavily patronized by women, similarly lost large amounts of property to lineage organizations, and many were abandoned in the Ming" (p. 295 n. 7).
- **Excluded from game text.** Birge records widows driven to suicide by forced levirate marriage (pp. 273–274, 295). The mod's rule bars suicide content, so none of this is used.

**Usable events and characters.**
- **Madam Wang** (Song, 1216 onward): a remarried widow walks out with 70 *chung* of land in her own name; the judge must rule for her against his own sermon (pp. 115–120). A Song-era Yutai sister's "the contract is in my name" event.
- **Lady Shang-kuan** (c. 1095–after 1135): the grave cloister (pp. 156–158).
- **Chang Ah-Liu** (Kaifeng, 1268–71): four rulings, ending in confiscation of ⅔; the Yuan-era counterpart of Hsieh Ch'i-ku (pp. 223–226).
- **Chin Wang-erh** (1273): a 13-year-old orphan whose land the state takes until it can find her a husband (pp. 226–227).
- **Sengge Ragi**, Supreme Princess of Lü (d. after 1330): chaste widowhood as estate control (pp. 274–275). A possible on-map Yuan noblewoman for a Yutai court event (verify in vanilla history).
