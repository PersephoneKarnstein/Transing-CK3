## 0. Framing warning

- **The subject is sex and love between men.** Boswell's daughters do not exist in this book, and the mod's daughters are not in it either. They are women. Nothing here is evidence about them, and nothing here may be used to frame them as gay men, as cross-dressers, or their womanhood as a sexual vice. Where Boswell reports a hostile writer or law that lumped gender crossing in with "sodomy", the note records it as **how an accuser or a law miscategorised people**, and the design may use it only in a hostile voice that gets her wrong.
- **Boswell sets gender aside on purpose.** He says the senses of "effeminate" unrelated to sexual object choice are "excluded from consideration here" (p. 24 n. 43). He argues that effeminacy and male love were not linked in tolerant societies (pp. 24-25). His book is therefore thin on exactly the people the mod is about.
- **Where he does touch gender crossing, he folds it into "gay".** He calls the North American *berdache* "a respected gay person with clearly defined roles" (p. 34 n. 63). He supposes that some early Germans held "a role similar to that of the berdache", taking "feminine social roles" (p. 184). The mod must not repeat this move in either direction: a daughter is not a gay man, and a gay man is not a daughter.
- **Reception (general knowledge; not checked in this pass).** The book was famous and influential on publication and has been contested since. The main lines of criticism:
  - his transhistorical category "gay people" (the essentialist side of the constructionist debate; compare Karras's acts-not-identities framing in `hist_karras_medieval_sexuality.md` ch. 5 summary, line 17);
  - his translations, above all of Paul's vocabulary (Appendix 1);
  - his picture of an eleventh- and twelfth-century "gay subculture" and of a church largely indifferent until c. 1150;
  - his reading of the Islamic world as indifferent "if not admiration" (p. 194), which later Islamicists do not share.

  Read his law-by-date material (chs. 7, 8, 10) as a convenient finding aid with full citations. Check any translation against the Latin he prints in the notes before quoting.
- **What the mod may take:** dated hostile law and hostile clerical voices, region by region; the gap between rhetoric and enforcement; a few period descriptions of people read as of "both sexes" or as "effeminate", always as the record's classification.
- **What it may not take:** any claim that a historical person was "really" one of the daughters; "berdache" or any third-category language in the narrator's voice; Boswell's tolerance thesis as a reason to soften hostile law; anything on Jews except as recorded context (persecution is never a mechanic, and Jews are never the wrongdoers); depiction of any execution, torture or mutilation.

## 1. Findings, by date and place

### 1.1 Roman and Byzantine law (Adelphai background)

- **342.** A statute against men who "marry" in a woman's role, which Boswell reads as outlawing gay marriages; he calls it propagandistic, with no penalty for noncompliance (p. 123). Peter the Chanter later cites it as precedent for secular punishment (p. 277).
- **390.** The edict that later writers garble into a burning law (pp. 177 n. 30, 183 n. 49). See §1.3 for Regino.
- **533, 538, 544: Justinian.** The *Institutes* (533) first bring all male same-sex acts under the adultery law; Novels 77 and 141 (538, 544) are "largely hortatory" (p. 171). Malalas (a contemporary) says those found guilty were then castrated, and many died (p. 172). Procopius says the law was used against the Green faction, the rich and the emperor's enemies, and that Theodora used it against a young Green (p. 173).
  - Boswell: "no indication that any church official suggested or supported the emperor's action" (p. 172).
  - For the Adelphai this is a datable instance of **castration as a punishment** in sixth-century Constantinople. It fits `faith_design.md` §12 item 1 ("Byzantium rarely castrated as punishment"): rare, but attested under Justinian.
- **John the Faster's penitential** (attributed to the patriarch of 582-595, but possibly as late as the tenth century; p. 365 n. 28). A Greek penitential exists for an Adelphai confessor scene; Boswell prints only a short extract.
- **Ancyra canon 16 (314).** The canon later read as condemning male same-sex acts was ambiguous in Greek ("those who have abandoned reason"); the Latin West knew it through mistranslations (p. 178 n. 33). Peter Damian's only conciliar authority was this canon (p. 211).

### 1.2 Visigothic Spain (c. 650-711)

- **c. 650.** The first Western civil law against male same-sex acts, with castration (p. 174). The church "failed to cooperate" for forty years until ordered; its canon (Toledo XVI, 693) mitigated the penalty, and the king then imposed both penalties together (p. 176).
- The same code singled out Jews; Boswell records anti-Jewish laws in the same breath (pp. 174-175). **Record only.**
- The castration law survived in the vernacular *Fuero juzgo* (p. 288 n. 56).

### 1.3 Frankish, Lotharingian and Rhineland law (Good Ladies at Prüm, v2 1066)

- **Germanic law codes** (Salic, Ripuarian, Burgundian, Bavarian, Alemannic, Saxon, Thuringian, Lombard): none proscribes same-sex acts (pp. 176-177 and n. 27).
- **Charlemagne** (*Capitulare missorum generale*, sec. 17; 802 by Goodich's dating): shocked that monks were "sodomites", threatens unspecified punishment; no civil law follows (p. 177).
- **789, *Admonitio generalis* c. 49.** Ecclesiastical only; recommends "hard" penance via Ancyra; no penalty named (pp. 177-178 and n. 31).
- **Mid-ninth century: Benedict Levita's forgery.** A spurious "Capitulary of Charlemagne of 779" invokes "Roman law" to justify burning "sodomites". It is "an invention by Benedict", who forged about three-fourths of his collection (p. 177 n. 30). The only surviving Carolingian civil statute on the subject is this forgery (p. 177).
- **829, Council of Paris.** Forbade priests to use penitentials for these acts, probably seeking more severity (p. 182).
- **c. 860, Hincmar of Reims**, in his treatise on **Lothar II of Lotharingia's divorce from Theutberga** (p. 203 n. 128).
  - He widened "sodomy" to every release of seed outside procreative marriage (pp. 203-204).
  - He is one of the few writers to discuss women together. He says they are "reported" to use "instruments of diabolical operation", and that they sin by fornication (p. 204).
- **c. 906, Regino of Prüm** (abbot of Prüm, d. 915).
  - His own scheme is "largely gender blind": the act, not the partners, is the sin. Anal intercourse costs three years whoever the partners are; intercrural acts between men cost one year; boys get less (p. 183).
  - He appends harsher canons by "others" (pp. 254-259 of his book). They include the Ancyra canons and "a garbled version of the edict of 390, mentioning the penalty of burning". Boswell calls these "curiosities" to Regino (p. 183 and n. 49).
  - Goodich reads the same appendix as a real conflict between thirty years' penance and execution (Goodich p. 26); see the companion note.
- **d. 1025, Burchard of Worms**, *Decretum* bk. 19 (the *Corrector*, circulated separately as a confessor's manual).
  - He treats these acts as a kind of fornication. Habitual anal intercourse by a married man costs less than one act of adultery (pp. 205-206 and n. 138).
  - All his penances are prefaced "if you had a wife"; he gives none for the unmarried, though he does elsewhere (p. 206 n. 140).
  - His civil legislation for the diocese of Worms is silent on the subject (p. 206 n. 141).
- **Penances were commutable.** One fast-day for a penny or prayers; a year for thirty shillings or thirty masses (England, tenth century; Regino 2.447-453 has similar provisions) (p. 181 and n. 41).
- **Thirteenth century, German lands.**
  - The *Sachsenspiegel* (c. 1233) and the later *Schwabenspiegel* prescribe no penalty (p. 286).
  - Gregory IX sent the Dominicans to root it out of Germany, Austria above all, but his remedy was preaching and example (pp. 294-295; no date given).
  - One published instance of capital punishment: *Annales Basileenses*, under the year 1277 (p. 293 n. 73). **Date and place only.**

### 1.4 Reform rhetoric and papal restraint, c. 1049-1102

- **c. 1051, Peter Damian, *Liber Gomorrhianus*.** Boswell dates it "around the year 1051" (p. 210) and Leo IX's reply *Nos humanius agentes* to 1051 (p. 365). The region note says 1049 (Payer's date).
  - Leo IX praised the book but refused to degrade clerics who had not sinned "as a long-standing practice or with many men" (pp. 211-212; translation pp. 365-366).
  - The Lateran synod of **1059** took up Damian's reforms on every point but this one (p. 213).
  - **Alexander II** (Anselm of Lucca) carried the book off and locked it away (p. 213).
- **1098, Orléans.** Ivo of Chartres protested the consecration of John, the archbishop of Tours's lover, popularly nicknamed **"Flora"** after a courtesan. Urban II took no action; John ruled almost forty years (pp. 213-214). "Flora" is a **hostile nickname** given to a man; it is not a daughter's name source.
- **1102, Council of London.**
  - The council ordered that "sodomy" be confessed as a sin and degraded clerics.
  - Anselm suppressed publication. He wrote that the sin "has hitherto been so public that hardly anyone is embarrassed by it" (p. 215; text p. 215 n. 26).
  - Boswell holds that the edict was never published (p. 216).
- **Boswell's general claim:** before the thirteenth century the few such laws were civil, and church records usually gave no penalty or a mild one (p. 174).

### 1.5 The hostile turn, 1120-1350

- **1120, Kingdom of Jerusalem** (the Nablus canons, Mansi 21:264). "The earliest and most drastic legislation" of the High Middle Ages: burning for "sodomites" (p. 281 and n. 39). The same council dealt severely with adultery, possibly unenforced (n. 39).
- **1179, Lateran III.** The first general council to rule on these acts: clerics deposed or confined to a monastery, laymen excommunicated (pp. 277-278).
  - **Peter the Chanter** (Paris, d. 1197) argued for secular punishment, but in a text of 1191/92 or later, after the council; that he argued for it *at* Lateran III is unproven (Baldwin 1970, pp. 9, 13–15; `hist_baldwin_peter_chanter.md`). He used the 342 statute as precedent for secular prosecution (p. 277).
  - Lateran III passed into the *Decretales* (p. 278).
- **1215, Lateran IV.** It "seemed to retreat somewhat": no laymen are mentioned, and clerics are told to avoid the sin "especially" (p. 278).
- **1231, Constitutions of Melfi** (Frederick II, Sicily). Silent on these acts. The code protects prostitutes and contains no restrictions on Jews or Muslims (pp. 287-288).
- **Mid-thirteenth-century codes** (Boswell: "between 1250 and 1300", the act went from legal "in most of Europe" to capital "in all but a few" compilations, p. 293):
  - *Fuero real* 4.9.2 (Castile, Alfonso X): castration before the people, then death (p. 288). The first European civil law to speak of "nature" (p. 288).
  - *Siete Partidas* 7.21 (c. 1265; in force only in the fourteenth century): death, with exemptions for the coerced and those under fourteen (p. 289).
  - *Li livres de jostice et de plet* (Orléans legal school): graded penalties ending in death for a third offence; **"unlike almost all other"** thirteenth-century laws, it names women too (p. 290).
  - *Coutume de Touraine-Anjou* and the *Établissements de Saint Louis*: death for "bougrerie", goods to the lord; Boswell argues "bougrerie" was deliberately ambiguous (pp. 290-291).
  - Beaumanoir, *Coutumes de Beauvaisis* (1283): explicit (p. 291).
  - Italian cities: campaigns from 1233; statutes at Siena (1262) and Bologna (1265), linked to heresy, with confiscation; Florence and Perugia by the mid-fourteenth century (p. 291).
  - Norway, the Gulaþing law "compiled around 1250": permanent outlawry. Boswell reasons from Grágás's silence that it was a thirteenth-century addition (pp. 291-292 and n. 67).
  - England: the *Laws of Henry I* and Glanvill are silent. *Fleta* (c. 1290) and *Britton* (early fourteenth century) condemn the act; the same article puts sex with Jews and with animals beside it (p. 292 and nn. 69-71).
- **Enforcement.** Records are "completely inadequate" to say how many died (p. 293). The Crown of Aragon registers record no such proceeding before 1500 (p. 293 n. 74). "Sodomy" was a reserved sin in some dioceses, e.g. Lund in 1227 (p. 293 n. 75). Iceland, late twelfth century: nine or ten years' penance, with commutations (p. 294 n. 77).
- **1307-1312, the Templars.** Arrests in France from October 1307; dissolution at Vienne. Tribunals outside France found the order innocent (pp. 295-297). Boswell's point is that the accusation of sodomy had become an effective weapon (p. 297). **Date and law only;** no scene is to be drawn from his account of the interrogations.
- **1323, Pamiers.** Arnald de Vernhola was tried for heresy, including his belief that these acts were no worse than fornication (p. 285).

### 1.6 People read as "of both sexes", "effeminate" or crossing dress

All of these are a source's classification, not the mod's.

- **The *Liber monstrorum* wonder** (Boswell calls it a "sixth-century treatise", p. 184; modern dating is later, see Cross-checks). Boswell prints the Latin:
  - the person was *utriusque sexus*, looked more male in face and chest, and was taken for a man "by the unknowing";
  - *sed muliebria opera dilexit*, and deceived unknowing men "in the manner of a harlot" (p. 185 and n. 55).

  Boswell renders *muliebria opera* as "the female role [in intercourse]" and decides the person was "almost certainly a homosexual male" (p. 185). That is his reading. The Latin can bear "women's work". The second clause is the "deceiver" shape of `faith_design.md` rule 3.
- **"In puellam hermaphroditam"** (Latin epigram, seventh or eighth century manuscript). It is about a woman who loves women; Boswell notes that *Hermaphroditus* "often signified 'homosexual'" (pp. 185-186 and n. 58).
- **Peter the Chanter** (Paris), *De vitio sodomitico* = *Verbum abbreviatum* ch. 138, PL 205:333–335, the short version (long version 1191/92; short by 1197; Baldwin pp. 13–15). Boswell's heading "d. 1192" (p. 375) is a slip for 1197 (cf. p. 277). Translation pp. 375-376:
  - the church lets a hermaphrodite "use the organ by which (s)he is most aroused or the one to which (s)he is more susceptible": "more active" may wed as a man, "more passive" as a woman;
  - if (s)he "should fail with one organ, the use of the other can never be permitted": perpetual celibacy, to avoid any likeness to the "role inversion" (*alternitatis*, n. 53) of sodomy. The verbs have no stated subject, hence Boswell's "(s)he" (n. 51).
  - The same chapter (p. 377) speaks of men who "change themselves from males to females", meaning men who have sex with men. It is recorded only as the Chanter's classification and is **never reused of a daughter**.

  He treats the hermaphrodite as an inculpable condition (p. 376 n. 50). Dante later has his "sodomites" cry "Nostro peccato fu ermafrodito" (*Purg.* 26.82; p. 375 n. 50).
- **Orderic Vitalis on William Rufus's court** (c. 1087-1100, written later). The charge of "effeminacy" rests on new clothes, shoes, grooming and long hair, and the chroniclers object to beards too (pp. 229-230). Boswell judges Orderic "obsessed" and untrustworthy on this (p. 230).
- **Giraldus Cambrensis on William Longchamp** (1190s): a mother brought him her daughter "dressed as and trained to imitate a young man" (p. 229 n. 69). **A girl presented as a boy; out of scope.** Nothing is built on it.
- **Islamic Spain, girls dressed as boys** (p. 195 and n. 99; Ibn Shuhayd). **Out of scope** (cis women in boys' dress for a man's gaze).
- **c. 1317-1318, William of Adam**, *De modo Sarracenos extirpandi* (Boswell gives no date).
  - Among the Saracens there are "effeminate men in great number" who shave their beards, paint their faces and take women's dress. They wear bracelets on arms and legs and gold necklaces (p. 282; Latin n. 42).
  - It is crusade propaganda, not observation; Boswell sets it beside the forged Alexius letter and Jacques de Vitry (pp. 279-282).
- **Neckam (d. 1217)** writes "Effeminate men who violate the law of nature are thus said to imitate hares", and adds Tiresias (p. 306).

### 1.7 Women together

- Gregory III's penitential (eighth century): 160 days (or four Lents) for acts between women, one year for the *molles* (p. 180 and n. 38).
- Hincmar, c. 860 (p. 204; see §1.3).
- **Tegernsee, Bavaria, twelfth-century manuscript: love letters between religious women** (pp. 220-221). Boswell quotes one ("To G., her singular rose") in full from Dronke.
- Aelred (twelfth century) condemns "femina in feminam" alongside men (p. 223 n. 48).
- *Li livres de jostice et de plet* (Orléans, thirteenth century) is the rare code that names women (p. 290).

### 1.8 Islam and Byzantium in Boswell

- **Islamic world.**
  - Boswell holds that the Qurʾān is "mildly negative" and that most Muslim cultures treated these relations "with indifference, if not admiration" (p. 194).
  - He gives Andalusi poetry and the Taifa courts (pp. 195-197).
  - He gives *khanatha* (male prostitute; also "hermaphrodite", al-Zahrāwī's surgery ch. 70) from as-Saqaṭī's and Ibn ʿAbdūn's *ḥisba* manuals (p. 196 n. 100).
  - Ibn ʿAbdūn's charge that Christian priests are "fāsiq, zānī, lūṭī" (p. 233 n. 86) is a hostile Muslim voice about Christian clergy (Seville, c. 1100).
  - Boswell says the sources call the Valencian co-rulers Mubārak and Muẓaffar only "youths", not eunuchs (p. 196 n. 104). That is his reading against Pérès; do not rely on it.
- **Cairo.** Only Jacob of Verona (c. 1335): the "sultan" kept youths bought from the Baltic, Greece and Italy, sold at Cairo (p. 283 n. 44). Mamluk, fourteenth century, and hostile. **Nothing for 1066.**
- **Baghdad.** Nothing specific beyond the *Thousand and One Nights* debates (pp. 256-258, not read closely).
- **Byzantium.** Justinian and Theodora (§1.1); the forged letter of Alexius I to Robert of Flanders, which uses atrocity stories about Muslims to rouse the West (pp. 279-280; text pp. 367-369).

### 1.9 Animals and "nature" (hostile Latin voices)

- **Peter Damian, letter to Montecassino** (*De bono religiosi status*, PL 145): the hyena's sex changes make it a "dirty animal" to be avoided (p. 305).
- **Bestiaries** (twelfth to fourteenth centuries): plates 9 and 12. A twelfth-century Latin bestiary (Morgan MS 832) and a fourteenth-century German one (Munich clm 6908, fol. 79) call the hyena "sometimes male and sometimes female" and liken it to the children of Israel turning to idols.
  - Boswell's own caption: "Although Jews and gay people were often tacitly linked ... such explicit comparisons were relatively rare" (plate 9).
  - This is an anti-Jewish image. Under `faith_design.md` §13 (line 317) it is barred from Ha-Nehpakhot's text.

### 1.10 Jews (recorded only; never a mechanic)

- Boswell's thesis that the fates of Jews and of gay people in Europe were "almost identical" (p. 15).
- Visigothic anti-Jewish law (pp. 174-176).
- The Rhineland massacres of the first crusade (pp. 272-273).
- William of Norwich (1173 account) and the ritual-murder libel (p. 273).
- Lateran III and IV against Jews, and the badge (pp. 273-275).
- Expulsions (England 1290; France) (pp. 274-275).
- Fleta's pairing of sex with Jews with sodomy (p. 292).
- The leper scare of 1321-1322 (p. 272 and n. 4).
- Hebrew poets of Spain (Moses ibn Ezra, Judah Halevi) writing love verse to youths (pp. 233-234).
