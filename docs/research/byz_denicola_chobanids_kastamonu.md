Quotations are short and verbatim. Translations of Persian, Arabic and Greek sources are De Nicola's (or those he cites).

**Scope and bias.** A history of the Turkmen family that ruled Kastamonu (Byzantine Paphlagonia), c. 1211–1309, mostly through Persian manuscripts written for them. The political chapter rests on Ibn Bibi, Aqsara'i, the Anonymous of Konya, Pachymeres and Gregoras, which often disagree. De Nicola is careful to mark speculation. Kastamonu is about 250 km north-east of Dorylaion (general knowledge); the book never mentions Dorylaion or Eskişehir. Its frontier faces Nicaea across the **Sakarya (Sangarios)** (p. 50), the same frontier Dorylaion lay near. That last point is general knowledge.

**Relation to other notes.** `byz_sophoulis_banditry.md` gives the Balkan band-to-lord paths (Momčilo, Dobromir Chrysos). This book gives an Anatolian Turkish one over a century, and it is slower, more bureaucratic and more dependent on overlords. Read with `byz_peacock_urban_notables.md` for the towns, and `byz_blessing_goshgarian_anatolia.md` for akhīs and convents.

**Relevance:** high for the band-to-dynasty ladder and for how a frontier lord held power between larger states. Moderate for Christians under a Turkish lord and for akhī lodges. Very low for women (De Nicola says there are none in the sources). Nil for eunuchs and gender crossing.

## 1. The frontier land

- **Turks first came in 1073–74.** Danishmandid Turks surprised Alexios Komnenos near Kastamonu (p. 40). Anna Komnene says the region was deserted by Greeks and Turks raided freely (p. 29).
- **Back and forth.** John Komnenos retook the castle twice in 1132–33, but could not hold the land. The Seljuks took it after 1143 and made it an *iqṭā'* for the sultan's family (p. 40).
- **Settlement.** Turkmen came in waves (11th c., then the 1250s–60s) (p. 31). Ibn Sa'id counted 100,000 Turkmen tents around Kastamonu in the mid-13th century. De Nicola calls the numbers unreliable (p. 41). Cumans came from across the Black Sea; Persian townsmen followed from the 1260s (p. 31).
- **Greeks.** Greek people may have remained the town's majority **until the 1250s** (p. 31). "We know almost nothing about the fate of the Christian communities" (p. 32). The region shifted from Christian to majority Muslim by 1308 (p. 32). Across Anatolia by the late 13th century, Christians "could have remained a numerical majority but mainly irrelevant" politically (p. 143).
- **Living between camp and town.** The Turkmen kept winter and summer camps and a love of riding and hunting while settling (p. 32). The first Chobanid mosque was **outside** the town, perhaps by the ruler's camp (pp. 93–94).
- **Trade and slaves.** Black Sea trade in slaves, grain and horses ran through Sinop (p. 31). Slaves captured around Kastamonu were sold at Crete for Venice (p. 31). Kastamonu residents were themselves enslaved and sold to Christian traders in the early 14th century (p. 124). Bandits are reported on the Amasya–Kastamonu road (p. 38 n. 86).

## 2. From band to dynasty: the Chobanid ladder

The best Anatolian case in this reading of a war band becoming a ruling house. Each rung is dated.

1. **A chief with followers.** Husam al-Din Choban's ancestors came as part of the Turkmen military contingent on the Byzantine–Seljuk border (p. 41). A claim that they were of the Kayı tribe comes only from a 15th-century source and may be invented, as Osman's was (p. 41).
2. **Back a claimant (1211).** When Kaykhusraw I died, Husam al-Din and another chief, Amir Qizil, came to Kayqubad's aid at Ankara "with their numerous servants, followers, possessions, men" (Ibn Bibi, pp. 43–44). Ankara fell; Kayqubad was jailed; Husam al-Din went home (p. 44). **He was on the losing side and kept his men.**
3. **Towns granted after a revolt (1214).** Ankara and Çankırı (Gangra) were allocated to the Chobanids after their citizens revolted against Seljuk authority (p. 45). They probably collected revenue and kept order with little say over the towns (p. 45).
4. **The claimant wins (1220).** Kayqubad became sultan and confirmed Husam al-Din as ruler of north-western Anatolia (p. 46).
5. **The great commission (early 1220s).** He commanded the Seljuks' only overseas expedition: from Sinop to Sudak in the Crimea. He beat a Qipchaq army, made the Rus pay yearly tribute, sacked Sudak, took hostages from its "respected" families, left a garrison and declared Islam the city's religion (pp. 46–47). De Nicola, after Peacock, says such declarations did not mean the people converted (p. 47).
6. **Recognised as amir.** He was "the first Turkmen chief to hold autonomous control over the region and be recognised as amir by the sultan" (p. 47). His title was *malik al-umarā'* ("king of amirs"), of unclear duties (p. 43).
7. **A generation of eclipse (c. 1240s–1280).** His son Alp Yürek held at most military command over the Turkmen. A non-Chobanid, the Cuman-born **Shams al-Din Yavtash**, held the post of *beylerbey* until c. 1256 (p. 48). The **revenues** of Kastamonu were given to Mongol-appointed viziers. Taj al-Din Mu'tazz's son held them hereditarily until 1309 (p. 49). **Command and revenue were split between two holders** (p. 49). Kastamonu Turkmen took Tokat (1250–57) and lost it (p. 50). Alp Yürek died c. 1280, perhaps as a "martyr" in battle (p. 52).
8. **Switch overlords (1277–84).** After the Mongol strongman Mu'in al-Din Parvana was executed, the Chobanids defended Sinop against Trebizond beside his soldiers (1277), and were spared when the Mongols crushed other Turkmen (p. 52). Muzaffar al-Din (r. 1280–91) captured one Seljuk claimant, backed the other (Mas'ud), and went with him to the Ilkhan at Tabriz (pp. 55–56). He was confirmed as ruler of Kastamonu in 1284 (p. 56).
9. **Conquest and court culture (1284–91).**
   - He took the two Byzantine castles of **Gideros** (September 1284) with siege engines and naphtha. The defenders fled by sea (p. 57).
   - He commissioned Persian works: an astronomy from Qutb al-Din Shirazi, a mirror for princes, and Khu'i's letter-writing manuals for a chancellery (pp. 56–57, 207–208).
   - A son, Nasir al-Din, lived for a while in Constantinople and became *mustawfī* (treasurer) at the Seljuk court (p. 57).
10. **The fall (1291–1309).**
    - Turkmen rebels backed the imprisoned claimant against him. Muzaffar al-Din died c. 1291 (pp. 58–60). Byzantine support for the rebels "cannot be ruled out" (p. 60).
    - His son 'Ali raided across the Sakarya from 1299. A peace drafted in 1302 was **rejected by the Turkmen factions in his own army**, so 'Ali fought beside **Osman** at Bapheus (1302). He made peace with Constantinople in 1304 (p. 62).
    - In 1309 Sulayman Pasha, son of **one of their own subordinate chiefs** (Yaman Jandar), rebelled at Eflani, besieged the last Chobanid, Mahmud, in the castle and later executed him (pp. 62–63).
- **De Nicola's summary.** They went "from local warlords" to "a proto-beylik on the border between Islam and Christianity" (pp. 208–209). In buildings: "from military protectors on the outskirts of town to central actors" in three generations (p. 98).

## 3. Christians and a Christian prince at the frontier

- **The future emperor in a Turkmen town.** About 1256 Michael Palaiologos fled Nicaea for the Seljuk sultan. He was **robbed by Turkmen nomads from Kastamonu** on the way. The sultan gave him command of the Christian troops serving the sultan; he fought for the Seljuks at Aksaray against the Mongols (1256). Then he went with the *beylerbey* to Kastamonu and may briefly have governed it (pp. 50–51). He fled before the Mongols in 1257 and became emperor in 1261 (p. 51).
- **A church made a mosque, perhaps.** Tradition says Husam al-Din turned a church into the Atabey Gazi mosque on a Friday, expelling the clergy mid-sermon. But an inscription dates the mosque to **1273**, so the story may be later embroidery (p. 94).
- **Turkmen exiles in Byzantium.** Many Turkmen who had backed the deposed sultan Kaykaus II were relocated to the European side of the Byzantine Empire (p. 51).
- **The Byzantine hand.** Constantinople probably encouraged Turkmen rebels against an awkward neighbour (p. 60).
- **A Muslim physician across the frontier.** Sa'd al-Din, a Persian doctor posted to Zalifre (Safranbolu), found it a "desolate place" with no friends and no guild (*asnāf*) (p. 190). The amir of Samsun sent him to practise medicine in Christian territory, the *dār al-kufr* of Canik (p. 192).
- **Converts.** A religious manual for the Chobanids lists as sins sending a Jew or Christian who asks about faith to a scholar instead of answering, and regretting conversion when one's parents die. De Nicola reads these as advice for new Muslims on the Byzantine border (pp. 161–62). There is "no mention of how Christians or Jews should be treated" in its section on punishing unbelief (p. 161).

## 4. Akhīs and lodges in the Chobanid lands

- **First akhī record.** Şeyh **Ahi Şurve** endowed a zāwiya in Kastamonu in October 1303 (pp. 30, 103).
- **His endowment.** Land for the lodge; properties in nearby towns; a farm on the Black Sea coast; two gardens; and a forest. All were given in perpetuity, never to be sold, pledged, granted or inherited. The revenue was split in **thirds**: one for the community, one managed by the founder, one for the poor (p. 103).
- **De Nicola's definition.** Akhīs were leaders of "groups of unmarried Muslim men", "often violent" religious men, but mostly craftsmen, artisans and merchants (p. 30). He reads them as evidence of "an incipient wealthy urban class connected to trade" (p. 30).
- **Ibn Battuta at Kastamonu (1330s).** He stayed 40 days and found it "one of the largest and finest of cities" (p. 30). He slept in a zāwiya at Taşköprü founded by an amir he calls Fakhr al-Din, probably Muzaffar al-Din Choban. The amir had endowed it to feed and lodge poor Muslims from the Holy Cities, Syria, Egypt, Iraq, Khurasan and Rum (p. 97).
- **A young akhī's village.** On the road to Sinop he spent a night in a hospice "on a lofty mountain without any habitations". **One of the "Young akhīs", Nizam al-Din of Kastamonu**, had founded it and endowed it with a village whose revenue fed wayfarers (Ibn Battuta, p. 102).
- **Who paid.** Lodges were founded with the rulers' money but also by wealthy local landowners and traders (p. 102).

## 5. Women, eunuchs, gender crossing: findings and absences

- **Women: absent.** "We do not have any reference to women in the Chobanid court", so we cannot know if the Chobanids married Christian wives as the Seljuks did (p. 32).
- **Women in forms of address only.** Khu'i's ranks for letter-writers include the sultan's wife (*mukhaddarāt*, "a matron"; elsewhere *khātūn*), a sister and a daughter (pp. 183–84 and n. 152).
- **Women seen by a traveller.** Sa'd al-Din praised Sinop for "girls of different origins such as Rus, Alani, Rumi and Qipchaq" in elegant dress (p. 192). The book does not say whether they were free or enslaved. Given the slave trade through Sinop (p. 31), do not use this as event content.
- **Eunuchs: none.** The rank list includes "servants" (*khuddām*) (p. 184) but no eunuch office.
- **Gender crossing: none.** The nearest thing is the Qalandar dervishes, men who shaved "their hair, beard and eyebrows" (p. 151). In one story their founder Savi shaved to be unattractive to a woman trying to seduce him (Ibn Battuta's version, p. 154). That is renunciation, not crossing. Do not claim it.
- ***Ikdishān*** appears as an office in Khu'i's ranks. De Nicola glosses *ikdish* as "of a mixed religious or ethnic origin or a hybrid", here an office (p. 184 and n. 161, p. 202). The word's other Turkish meaning, a gelding, is general knowledge and **not** in the book. Do not build a eunuch mechanic on it.
- **Gender rules in the manual.** Three of Abu Hanifa's fifteen sins regulate men and women. Another is wishing "sodomy and adultery legally accepted" (pp. 161–62). De Nicola reads them as tension between the religious establishment and daily practice (p. 162). Usable only as hostile-world context.

## 8. Risks

- **Slavery.** The Chobanid world traded people (pp. 31, 46, 124). No mechanic sells people. The Sinop "girls" (p. 192) are off-limits.
- **No bandit peoples.** Turkmen robbed Michael Palaiologos and raided Byzantium; Byzantium sent its own expeditions against the Turkmen (p. 51) and probably backed rebels (p. 60). Violence ran both ways across the frontier. No culture gets a raider trait.
- **Conquest piety.** Ibn Bibi's poem on Sudak and the church-conversion legend (pp. 47, 94) are triumphal genre. Don't reproduce them as the narrator's view.
- **Women's absence is a finding.** Any woman ruling a frontier lordship rests on the mod's invention, or on Seljuk and Mongol cases elsewhere (Mahperi Khatun in the Blessing–Goshgarian note), not on this book.
- **Dorylaion is not in this book.** The Sakarya frontier link and all Dorylaion facts are general knowledge and should be checked against a source on Byzantine Phrygia before they go into loc.
