## 1. The provisions

### 1.1 Insult, verse and the right to kill

**Words in prose (K §237, II pp. 195-97).**
- Words "requiring full personal compensation" (words that "cannot be taken in a good sense") carry **lesser outlawry**, whether or not the target heard them. They also carry 48 ounce-units of personal compensation. Ambiguous words carry half compensation, but are actionable only from a household man against his householder or a slave against a free man (cf. K §80, I pp. 131-32).
- "Every word is to be as it is spoken. No word is to be taken according to the language of poetry": everyday usage is the standard.
- **With no witnesses there is no suit.** If the two are alone, "he shall avenge the words of the other with words of his own". St adds that the reply is then free of penalty. If either then **boasts** of it ("backbiting"), that is lesser outlawry.
- Said at the General Assembly, the personal compensation is **doubled**.
- Each of these carries lesser outlawry, prosecuted with a panel of twelve:
  - taunts, or asserting a bodily blemish, "even if he speaks the truth";
  - **giving someone a name he did not have before**, if he takes offence, and spreading it;
  - mockery and **exaggeration** (*ýki*, defined in Add. §422 as saying what cannot be true of a man, in order to mock him);
  - **shaming slander** (*níð*): carving a "wood-shame" or raising a "shame-pole".

**Verse (K §238, II pp. 197-99).**
- "A man has no right to compose defamation or praise of anyone."
- A stanza with no mockery in it is fined three marks; more than one stanza is lesser outlawry.
- **Half a stanza** with defamation or mockery, or praise "put together in order to mock", is **full outlawry**.
- Reciting it, teaching it, or even learning it is a separate suit, with the same full outlawry.
- Joint composition makes every contributor fully outlawed (four men to a half-stanza, eight to a stanza, Add. §424).
- "Wide-ranging" verse aimed at no one, if it goes round the district, is full outlawry, and anyone may claim to be its target.
- Verse mocking a **dead** Christian is full outlawry, prosecuted like a killing case.
- Mocking verse on the kings of Norway, Denmark or Sweden is full outlawry.
- A **love-verse on a woman** is full outlawry. The case is **hers** if she is twenty or older; otherwise, or if she declines, it goes to her legal administrator.
- **Níð at Lögberg** is full outlawry, and the speaker also falls with forfeit immunity at the target's hands until the next General Assembly.

**The killing words.**
- K's own clause sits inside the verse chapter. If someone hears in poetry words "for which a man has the right to kill", the translation's gloss being "that he is womanish or has been buggered", and he kills or wounds for it, he must then bring a suit for the malicious speech (St: "in order to clear himself"). (K §238, II p. 198)
- **St's fuller rule** (Add. §423, St §376, II p. 354) adds the **three expressions**: "womanish", "buggered" and "fucked". These are the translators' renderings of *ragr*, *stroðinn* and *sorðinn*; the Old Norse words are not printed in this translation.
  - All three carry **full outlawry** and are prosecuted like other full-compensation words.
  - "In retaliation for those three words a man also has the right to kill."
  - The time limit is the same as for the women's cases: **up to the next General Assembly**.
  - The speaker forfeits immunity at the hands of everyone who goes with the insulted man.
- **Other rights to kill run to the same limit.** The man injured by a wound or a marking blow may avenge it up to the General Assembly at which he must sue, and so may his company (K §86, I pp. 141-42). The six women a man may kill for are his wife, daughter, mother and sister, the **foster-daughter he brought up** and the **foster-mother who brought him up** (K §90, I p. 154).
- **Castrating a man** and the **shame-stroke across the buttocks** (*klámhögg*) are both assessed as major wounds (K §86).

**Design reading.** The law grades contempt finely and protects **the target**, not the norm. It punishes:
- the person who says or carves *níð* (lesser outlawry);
- the person who makes verse of it (full outlawry);
- the person who names a man as penetrated (full outlawry plus a right to kill him).

The private exchange of insults with no witnesses is **licensed self-help**: words for words. That is the flyting's legal basis. The killing right is real but carries a legal tail: the killer must publish the killing and win a clearing suit before the next Assembly.

### 1.2 Dress

- **K §155** (II pp. 69-70, "What behaviour towards a woman is liable at law"):
  - "If women become so deviant that they wear men's clothing, or whatever male fashion they adopt in order to be different, and likewise if men adopt women's fashion, whatever form it takes," the penalty for either is **lesser outlawry**.
  - It is a summoning case. Five neighbours of the accused are called. **"The case lies with anyone who wants it."**
  - The same chapter separately punishes a man who puts on a woman's headdress (*faldr*) **to beguile a woman**, also with lesser outlawry. St adds "or puts on women's clothes" (n. 94, citing *Þrymskviða* and *Njáls saga* ch. 129).
- **K §254** (II p. 219) repeats the clause with detail. "If in order to be different a woman dresses in men's clothes or cuts her hair short or carries weapons," the penalty is **lesser outlawry**, with five neighbours, and the case lies with anyone. "The same is prescribed for men if they dress in women's clothing."
  - Two sentences earlier the same chapter makes it **lawful to castrate vagrants**, with no penalty "even if they get lasting injury or death from it" (see §1.5).
- **Divorce.** Grágás gives **no** dress ground for separation. The lawful grounds (K §149, §151, and the Vol. II Guide p. 395) are:
  - dependents and destitution;
  - a major wound inflicted by one spouse on the other;
  - forbidden kinship;
  - being taken abroad against one's will;
  - the bishop's leave for incompatibility;
  - for the wife's property, three years without sharing her bed.

  The breeches and low-necked-shirt divorces of *Laxdæla saga* chs. 34-35 are saga, not this law. (That saga reference is outside these two volumes.)
- **The qualifiers.** "In order to be different" and "deviant" frame the offence as **affectation**, not identity. The case is **public**: any rival can bring it. The clause catches **any woman bearing weapons**, cis or not, which is notable for a faith with clergy who fight.

### 1.3 Outlawry: lesser and full

**Lesser outlawry (*fjörbaugsgarðr*).** Sources: K §§51-53, I pp. 92-95; K §67, I pp. 118-19; Vol. I Guide, "Lesser outlaw".
- **The life-ring.** A **confiscation court** is held a fortnight after the sentence, normally outside the outlaw's home. At it he pays a **"life ring"** of one mark to the chieftain who named the court; one ounce of it is the "sustenance pledge". The chieftain may take a cow or an ox instead. The rest of his property is adjudged as a full outlaw's would be.
  - If the life-ring is not paid, he becomes a full outlaw.
  - **If no confiscation court is held lawfully, the sentence lapses** (K §53).
- **The three homes.** "He is to have three homes", no more than one day's travel apart.
  - He is immune at each home and within bowshot of it.
  - He is immune on the road between them, if he uses it no more than once a month.
  - If men approach, he must step off the road beyond spear's reach.
  - If no homes are reported at the confiscation court, he has no immunity.
- **Passage abroad (K §53).**
  - He must ask for passage at up to three ships each summer, for up to three summers.
  - Shipmen who refuse are fined three marks. In the third summer a refusal is lesser outlawry, unless the ship already carries three outlaws.
  - He is immune on the road to the ship and within bowshot of its huts or gangway.
  - If he skips a summer, or is still in Iceland at the onset of the third winter, he becomes a full outlaw.
- **Exile and return.**
  - Abroad he has normal immunity.
  - If he comes back before **three winters**, he is a full outlaw, unless a panel finds the sea drove him back.
  - After three winters he is "as free from penalty as if that outlawry had never befallen him". He takes up any inheritances that fell to him, his dependents and his unpaid debts.
- **A second offence.** A lesser outlaw who commits another lesser-outlawry offence becomes a full outlaw (K §52).

**Full outlawry (*skóggangr*).** Sources: Vol. I Guide, "Full outlaw"; K §55, I pp. 96-98; K §70, I p. 120; K §102, I p. 164.
- He forfeits all property and all civil, family and church rights. He may be killed with impunity, and **this holds abroad as well**: he forfeits immunity "in relation to our countrymen in foreign countries just as here" (K §55).
- **Prices on heads** (K §102): three marks for a man outlawed for killing at the General Assembly, a house-burner, a slave who kills his master or mistress, or a murderer. Eight ounce-units for everyone else.
- Bringing back a man outlawed that same summer is full outlawry. For one outlawed the summer before, the shipmen must kill him or hand him to his prosecutor within half a month (K §70).
- **Mitigated outlawry.** The Law Council may permit terms of mitigation (passage abroad, local or temporary banishment). It needs the consent of **all** Law Council men and no veto from outside (K §55, cf. K §117).
- **Confiscated property** goes first to the wife, the creditors, and the chieftain's due. **Half the rest goes to the prosecutor and half to the men of the assembly** (or of the Quarter). None goes to a king or a bishop.

**Assisting an outlaw.** Sources: Vol. I Guide, "Assistance"; K §55; Ia 126.
- **"Assistance"** covers sheltering, sustaining, ferrying, advising, or simply sharing food and quarters.
- For an outlaw outlawed by private settlement, the first assister is prosecuted to **lesser outlawry**. Once that case has come before the Quarter Court, anyone who helps him afterwards is a **full outlaw** and is tried in the Fifth Court.
- Ignorance is a defence: boarding an outlaw unknowingly for up to three nights carries no penalty.
- In a shared household, a householder who does not consent can protect himself. He names witnesses that he objects and tells five neighbours. He is then liable only for sharing quarters, not for other help. The same applies to household people, and the declaration holds for twelve months.

**The three homes.** There is no church sanctuary in K. The "sanctuaries" are the lesser outlaw's **three homes**, the road between them, and the zone round his ship. The other immunity is the **assembly peace**: an assembly, once formally inaugurated, has immunity within its bounds while it lasts (Vol. II Guide, "Immunity").

**Lifting outlawry.**
1. **Time.** Lesser outlawry ends after three winters abroad.
2. **Mitigation** granted by the Law Council.
3. **Lapse**, if no confiscation court is held.
4. **Reprieve by killing outlaws** (K §110, I pp. 171-72), for outlaws in cases that could have been settled, except theft:
   - one outlaw killed makes the man "an outlaw who may be given passage";
   - two make him a lesser outlaw;
   - three give "complete" reprieve.

   The outlaw may do the killing himself, or a free man may kill on his behalf. The killer must name the reprieve he claims to five neighbours when he publishes the killing. It binds everyone only once it has been announced at **Lögberg**.
   - A note in a later source (translators' n. 147) credits Eyjólfr Valgerðarson with this law, in a famine winter c. 975, when "outlaws then killed each other".

**Women outlaws.** Women could be outlawed. "A man has no right to kill a woman with a live child in her womb ... even if ... she is outlawed" (K §95, Ia 170-71).

### 1.4 Adoption, fostering, inheritance and dependants

**There is no *ættleiðing* in Codex Regius as translated.** Neither volume, nor either Guide to Technical Vocabulary, has an adoption or legitimation ceremony. The Vol. II Guide says plainly: "A child conceived before marriage was not legitimised by marriage" ("Illegitimate", p. 380). *Ættleiðing* (leading a son into the kin) is a **Norwegian** institution (Gulaþing and Frostaþing law). Those texts are outside this source and must be verified before use. In Grágás the functional equivalents are these four:

1. **The inheritance-trade (*arfsal*)** (K §127, II pp. 26-27; n. 139). A person, with the consent of his heirs, exchanges all he will leave for **lifelong maintenance** by another.
   - The deal binds only if five neighbours swear that it is a fair agreement.
   - It is void if the taker has put out "neither money nor effort" on the other's behalf within twelve months.
   - An heir may revoke it before the next General Assembly. It is also revoked if neighbours' valuation differs from the deal by five ounce-units or more.
   - No one may trade his inheritance in order to desert his own dependents.
   - Those taken on by inheritance-trade rank among a man's maintenance duties (K §128, II p. 29).
   - **This is the closest thing in Grágás to adoption:** a contract making a stranger your dependent and your heir-in-effect, policed by neighbours.
2. **Fostering** (K §141, II p. 46; Guide p. 378).
   - **The contract.** A paid fosterer must bring the child up **until sixteen**. If "defects" appear, part of the payment is returned. If either party ends the fostering early, the resources are divided by neighbours' assessment. On a foster-parent's death the surviving foster-parent, or that parent's heirs, may keep the child.
   - **The bond.** The Guide notes that the bond had "particular recognition if fostering began before the age of eight and continued to sixteen".
   - **Fostering also creates kin-like rights.** The foster-daughter and foster-mother are two of the six women a man may kill for (K §90). Whoever "has maintained a woman in her early years" may take payment if someone has intercourse with her (K §142, II p. 47).
3. **"Led into the law."** A freed slave swore oaths when "led into the law" (Guide, "Oath", and the phrase *lögleiddr*). This is the law's only rite of **entering** the legal community.
   - Its edges are harsh. A child born of a woman who freed her own slave in order to marry him is a "**nookling**" (*hornungr*) and not a lawful heir (K §118, II p. 7).
   - Freed people inherit and are inherited, and childless freedmen's property returns to the freedom-giver (K §119).
4. **Gifts to illegitimate children** are capped at twelve ounce-units without the heirs' consent (K §127, II p. 26).

**Inheritance order** (K §118, II pp. 3-9, with Table 1 on p. 2).
- **Order.** Legitimate son, then **legitimate daughter**, then father, brother (same father), mother, sister (same father), then half-siblings through the mother. Illegitimate kin come next in the same pattern, then grandparents and grandchildren, uncles and aunts, cousins.
- **Shares.** One heir in a prior class excludes everyone after. Heirs in the same class share equally.
- **Men before women.** "If a man and a woman are equally close, then in that case the man is always to inherit." Women of equal degree divide among themselves.
- **Who is not a lawful heir:**
  - a child whose mother was not bought with a bride-price of a mark or more, or not wed, or not betrothed;
  - the child of a slave father;
  - the "scrubling", conceived before the mother was freed;
  - the "nookling";
  - the children of full outlaws ("cribling", "outcast's brat");
  - children of a bigamous or bishop-unlicensed second marriage;
  - children of beggars;
  - a man who cannot tell which way a trough-saddle faces.
- **Ages.** A male inherits at **sixteen**. A **married** woman takes an inheritance at any age. An unmarried girl takes it at sixteen but has care of property only at **twenty**.
- **A concealed child.** A woman who hides an illegitimate child from her kin loses her right to inherit (K §127, II p. 28). The Guide notes this rule was later rescinded.

**The ring-lady (*baugrýgr*)** (K §113, Ia 200-01; Laws I pp. 175-83).
- The wergild list is all male except "one woman who is both to pay and to take a wergild ring, given that she is an only child". That woman is the ring-lady.
- **As receiver**, she is the dead man's daughter. She takes the **three-mark main ring "like a son"**, but only if no proper male receiver exists and only "until she is married, but thereafter kinsmen take it".
- **As payer**, she is the killer's daughter. She pays the main ring like a son "until she enters a husband's bed and thereby tosses the outlay into her kinsmen's lap".
- The surrogate-son status is limited: one ring, only an only child, only in a breach of the male line, and only until marriage.

**Dependants (*ómagar*)** (K §128, II pp. 29-31; K §§141-143, pp. 46-52).
- **Order of duty.** Everyone must maintain his own dependents: first his **mother**, then his father, children, siblings, those he would inherit from, those taken on by inheritance-trade, and his freedmen.
- **Debt-bondage.** If he lacks the means, he must **go into debt-bondage** for his mother, then his father.
- **Children.** The father pays two-thirds of a child's keep and the mother one-third. If both parents are destitute, the father's family carries two-thirds and the mother's family one-third.
- **Allocation.** Five neighbours allocate destitute dependents among kin by oath on a book, a year at a time.
- **Remote kin.** Duty reaches second cousins with four seasons' surplus, and third and fourth cousins only with six to eight seasons' surplus or a cash payment (p. 50).
- **Spouses.** A husband or wife must maintain the other.
- **Fines.** Letting an allocated dependent become a vagrant, or deserting dependents, is lesser outlawry.

**Commune boarding** (K §235, II pp. 191-92).
- Vagrants entitled to no board must not be given houseroom. Gifts to them are limited to material for shoes and clothing.
- Boarding any lawful itinerant **worse or better than prescribed** is **lesser outlawry**. If the commune does not prosecute, the bishop may appoint someone to.

**"Perverse" vagrancy** (II p. 52) is going house to house "because of indolence or such other failings as make good men unwilling to have them". Children of such vagrants are not their kin's charge.
- **It is lawful to castrate vagrants**, with no penalty "even if they get lasting injury or death from it" (K §254, p. 219; St places it right after the perversity definition, n. 148).
- A man need not take on more than two illegitimate third cousins by the same father "**unless the father of the children is castrated**" (p. 50). The translators read both clauses as "preventive rather than retributive" (n. 132).

### 1.5 Sorcery (K §7, Christian Laws Section, Ia 22-23)

All of this is in the **Christian** section, so it is post-conversion law. The translation uses "spells", "witchcraft", "magic" and "black sorcery". It does not print the Old Norse terms, so this note does not assign *galdr*, *fjölkynngi* or *seiðr* to particular clauses. Check Finsen's text before glossing. **Seiðr is not named in the translation.**

- **Worshipping "heathen beings"** (assigning property to anyone but God and His saints) is **lesser outlawry**.
- **Ordinary magic is lesser outlawry**, summoned locally and tried by a **panel of twelve**. The clause covers anyone who "uses spells or witchcraft or magic", and magic includes uttering, teaching, or **getting someone else to utter words of magic over himself or his property**.
- **Black sorcery is full outlawry.** It is defined as bringing about "the sickness or death of livestock or people" through words or magic.
- Charging stones with power to tie on people or livestock, trusting in such stones, keeping "unborn" livestock unmarked, and "superstition of any sort" are all **lesser outlawry**.
- **A berserk frenzy** is **lesser outlawry**. The same penalty falls on everyone present **unless they restrain him**.
- The Vol. II Guide ("Panel of twelve", p. 390) names sorcery as a typical "public interest" case for the chieftain's panel.

**Reading.** The law punishes the *client* as well as the worker ("over himself"). It sets the boundary of the worst crime at harm: magic that makes people or animals sick or die. Ordinary magic gets the same grade as cross-dressing and *níð*.

### 1.6 The thing: Lögberg, the Lawspeaker and the Law Council

- **Lögberg (the Law Rock).** This is where the Lawspeaker sits and allots places. Publishings, summonses, announcements and the law recital happen there; an audience of at least twenty was normal (Vol. I Guide). People who sit there without his leave are fined three marks. Keeping the Lawspeaker from his seat is lesser outlawry, as assembly-balking (K §117).
- **The Lawspeaker** (K §116, I pp. 187-89) is elected by the Law Council for **three summers**. He must recite the assembly procedure every year and the whole law over three years, consulting five legal experts if his memory fails. He must tell anyone "what the article of the law is", but give no further advice. He announces mitigations, the calendar, and new laws at Lögberg. He receives 240 ells a year and half the General Assembly fines.
- **The Law Council** (K §117, I pp. 190-93) has three benches of 48: the chieftains, each with two advisers, plus the bishops. Its procedure:
  - Licences pass only if **no seated member refuses and no veto comes from outside**.
  - Disputed law goes first to the books, and the bishops' books prevail. Failing the books, the chieftains vote. A minority of twelve or more must swear oaths, and the majority swears one more oath than the minority.
  - The new article is then **announced at Lögberg**.
  - Absent or obstructive chieftains face lesser outlawry and the loss of their chieftaincy.
- **Assembly peace.** An inaugurated assembly has its own immunity (Vol. II Guide, "Immunity"). Insult at the General Assembly doubles personal compensation (K §237). *Níð* recited at Lögberg is full outlawry plus forfeit immunity until the next Assembly (K §238).
- **The truce speech** (K §114) names a truce-breaker "truce-ravener". The peace speech (K §115) curses the oath-breaker to be driven off "as far and wide as ever men drive outcasts off", and to shun "every home save hell".

### 1.7 Women's legal capacity, separation and the household

Sources: the Vol. II Guide, "Woman, wife" (pp. 402-03), and the clauses it cites.

- **Rights.**
  - Women inherit after males of the same degree.
  - A woman may own land, but may sell only limited amounts without her legal administrator or husband.
  - She **may own a chieftaincy but not act in it**; only one man may act in a chieftaincy at a time (Vol. I Guide, "Chieftain").
  - A woman may be a **householder**, but a man must represent the household at the assembly (Vol. I Guide, "Householder").
  - At twenty an unmarried woman may have care of property, choose her residence, and be principal in lawsuits. She must **transfer the conduct** of a case to a man, and **killing cases never fall to women**.
- **Liabilities.** Women pay tithes, may undergo ordeal, and may be prosecuted and outlawed. They are liable for men's clothes and short hair (§1.2).
- **Betrothal.** Her legal administrator gives her in betrothal. She can refuse only to become a nun or anchoress (from c. 1100), or after unreasonable rejections. A widow's kinsman may reject two proposals, but her own choice prevails at the third.
- **The household and money (K §152, II pp. 66-67).** The husband has charge of their property and of buying and selling. If the wife owns a share in the household, "she is to run the indoor household if she wishes and the dairying". On her own authority she may spend **half an ounce-unit a year**.
  - **There is no clause about keys in Grágás.** The keys-at-the-girdle image comes from poetry and saga (and later Norwegian law), not this code.
- **Separation (K §§149-151, II pp. 63-66; Guide p. 395).**
  - No separation is allowed without the bishop's leave, except for dependents or a major wound.
  - A wife taken abroad against her will may declare herself separated "wherever they are".
  - A wife who separates because of kinship may be harboured "even if forbidden by veto".
  - The bishop decides on remarriage.

## 4. Corrections to the existing notes

Checked against the translation. Notes are cited by file and row.

1. **`norse_gade_homosexuality_law.md`, §1.**
   - **Where the three words are.** Gade's "Staðarhólsbók, vígslóði ch. 376" is, in Dennis-Foote-Perkins, **St §376**, printed as **Add. §423 (II p. 354)** among the *Miscellaneous Articles* on verbal injury, not in the Treatment of Homicide (*vígslóði*).
   - **K's own wording.** Codex Regius's version sits in the **poetry** chapter (K §238, II p. 198) and glosses only two terms ("womanish or has been buggered").
   - **The dead.** "Slandering the dead in these terms" overstates. K §238 makes **any** mocking verse on a dead Christian full outlawry; it is not tied to the three words.
2. **Gade row 3** ("no murder crime and no kin claim"). The killer **must bring a suit** to clear himself (K §238 and St). The right lasts only **until the next General Assembly** (Add. §423). See design row 4.
3. **Gade row 4** ("an event chain ending in a fine or outlawry"). The three words carry **full outlawry**, with no fine option (Add. §423).
4. **`norse_clover_regardless_of_sex.md`, row 5.** "The ring has gone to her husband's lap" inverts K §113. On marriage the paying ring-lady "tosses the outlay into her **kinsmen's** lap". Clover's §1 summary (duty passes to kinsmen) is correct; only the design loc is wrong. Note also the narrow conditions: only child, no proper male payer or receiver, the main ring only.
5. **Clover §1** ("women ... brought suit until 992"). This is not Grágás. In the code a woman of twenty may be **principal** in a suit but must **transfer its conduct to a man**, and **killing cases never fall to women** (Vol. II Guide, "Woman"). The 992 claim rests on non-legal evidence; label it so if used.
6. **`norse_meulengracht_sorensen_unmanly_man.md`, §1 Grágás bullet** ("the only other cases are killing and sex with a close kinswoman"). K §86 gives the **same** right to avenge, up to the General Assembly, for **wounds and marking blows**. The six women of K §90 include the **foster-daughter and foster-mother**, not only blood kin.
7. **MS, dress bullet** ("Staðarhólsbók chs. 155, 254 ... penalty not given; other notes give lesser outlawry; verify"). Verified: **lesser outlawry**, five neighbours called, and "the case lies with anyone who wants it". The chapter numbers are **Konungsbók K §155 and K §254** (II pp. 69-70, 219); the St parallel to K §155 is St §144. K §254 adds cut hair and carrying weapons for women.
8. **MS row 10** ("the law of the keys"). Grágás has **no keys clause**. Its nearest rule is K §152: a wife who owns a share of the household may run "the indoor household ... and the dairying", with a spending cap of half an ounce-unit a year. Name the flavour after Auðr if wanted, but do not attribute the keys to this law.
9. **`norse_reading_list.md` #43** ("the law on *ættleiðing* ... check the index"). There is **no *ættleiðing*** in the Codex Regius translation, its Additions, or either Guide. Grágás's analogues are the **inheritance-trade** (K §127), **fostering** (K §141) and **"led into the law"** for freed slaves. *Ættleiðing* belongs to Norwegian law.
10. **`README.md` l. 154 / `tm_baeddel_desc`** ("A man who went the other way was *ergi*, outlawed by law and killed by custom"). Gade and MS already flag this, and the README still carries it. The code outlaws **the speaker** of the three words (full outlawry) and **cross-dressing** in either direction (**lesser** outlawry: three years abroad, then full restoration). It never outlaws being *ragr*.
11. **`beliefs.md` (l), Norse cell** ("full outlawry and licence to kill for the three words; lesser outlawry for cross-dressing in either direction"). **Confirmed** (Add. §423; K §155, §254).
12. **Any saga-derived divorce-for-dress mechanic.** It is not law (§1.2). Attribute it to *Laxdæla saga*.
