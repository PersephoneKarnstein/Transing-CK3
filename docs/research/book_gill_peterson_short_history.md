## 2. Key concepts, in her terms

### 2.1 Trans misogyny
Her definition opens the book: "“Trans misogyny” refers to the targeted devaluation of both trans femininity and people perceived to be trans feminine, regardless of how they understand themselves." (GP:95). It works "through the logic of the preemptive strike" (GP:95). "It trans-feminizes its targets without their assent, usually by sexualizing their presumptive femininity as if it were an expression of male aggression." (GP:95). Whoever strikes is cast as the victim and also the judge (GP:95). It borrows from homophobia and ordinary misogyny and usually comes in "local idioms of racism and class antagonism" (GP:97). In the Conclusion she sums it up: "Feminizing people, regardless of how they see themselves, is the pretext for dehumanizing them." (GP:716).

### 2.2 Trans-feminization; trans femininity vs trans womanhood
*Trans femininity* is a classification made by outside observers. *Trans womanhood* names people who chose to live as women (GP:170). *Trans-feminized* names people subjected to trans misogyny who did not, or do not, want to be known as trans women. Her main colonial case is "the trans-feminization of populations to dispossess them of Indigenous ways of life, kinship structures, languages, social roles, and political values" (GP:154). For her, "labeling many kinds of people “trans women” is continuous with that colonial project" (GP:164).

### 2.3 The global trans panic: the state first, then the man
Her central historical claim: "Trans misogyny formed first as a mode of colonial statecraft that modeled for individuals how to sexualize, dehumanize, and aggress trans-feminized people through panic" (GP:162). Police were the first enforcers (GP:162, 248). The pattern then passed from the colonial state to men in the street: "Psychology followed the example of the state." (GP:242). "The blending of state violence with interpersonal violence is a signature outcome of the global trans panic" (GP:302). She dates the start of a pattern that targets people *for being trans feminine* to the later nineteenth century (GP:160), while granting that trans women of various kinds existed earlier. Because it "had to be invented", it "can one day be overcome" (GP:340). Its targets were wider than trans women: "Trans misogyny, as a pattern of violence, exceeds trans womanhood in its scope." (GP:346).

### 2.4 Deception and killability
Following Serano and Bettcher, the charge of deception is the usual excuse for violence. "The trope of deception is a blanket projection of a culture that sheds accountability onto the women targeted by violence." (GP:140). The moment of "discovery" is often invented after the fact (GP:140), and trans women who pass well are more endangered, not less (GP:140). In court the trans-panic defence turns her femininity into the provocation (Latisha King, GP:132; Jennifer Laude, GP:306–308, 334). Gender-critical politics does the same thing on a larger scale: trans women are converted "from the empirically disproportionate recipients of sexual violence into its all-powerful, ontological perpetrators" (GP:696).

### 2.5 Public space, policing and "public women"
In each of her cases trans femininity becomes a crime by being seen in public: hijras dancing and in women's clothes (GP:232), the Crow agent's haircuts (GP:244), New York's "masquerade" laws (GP:276), Nancy Kelly on the streetcar (GP:294–296), street queens who "do live in the police system" (Newton, GP:544). Separate-spheres ideology put respectable women at home, so a feminine person in the street broke that rule just by being there (GP:238, 422). To police and reformers, "“public women” were all guilty of prostitution, regardless of what they did for money" (GP:358). The gentrifier in *Sex and the City* wants a block "policed and emptied of Black trans women" for the sake of her rent (GP:120).

### 2.6 Sex work and downward mobility
She argues against both the stereotype and its moralising rebuttal. Taking up womanhood meant losing status, and the two contracts open to women, marriage and unskilled labour, needed passing (GP:358). Sex work was a way of life inside the new service economy, not a fall from some other life: "it’s a job that embraces contradiction, instead of moralizing work and value" (GP:494). Rescue feminism makes trans women respectable by removing the money: "Trans womanhood is, accordingly, made respectable when it’s stripped of labor and money." (GP:352). The colonial charge that hijras were prostitutes became their condition: "what began as an accusation and a British fiction became the condition of many hijras" (GP:240).

### 2.7 Race at the root, not added on
She says psychological accounts leave racism "demoted to an add-on, rather than acknowledged as at its root" (GP:150). Mary Jones was mocked as a free Black woman more than as a trans woman: "The phrase “practical amalgamation,” not the putative mismatch between her womanhood and her body, was the scandal." (GP:394). Her clothes were the evidence against her: "The sheer pretension of a free Black woman strolling the city like a white woman signaled her real duplicity, inviting mockery." (GP:396). "Everything about free Black womanhood was sexualized and ridiculed by the antebellum white public." (GP:400). Through Spillers she argues that Atlantic slavery "ungendered" captives, so that "we lose at least gender difference in the outcome" (Spillers, quoted at GP:450). Through Snorton, Black gender was "not fixed but fungible, which is to say, revisable within blackness" (GP:454). The same suspicion that let Mary Ann Waters live as a woman could be turned on her as proof she was a fugitive in disguise (GP:456).

### 2.8 Against romance, "always existed" and the martyr
She will not write a history of trans women: "I do not think a coherent history of trans women is possible, or worth attempting." (GP:164). Northern writers have "romanticized non-Western, Indigenous, and ancient societies into a self-serving and ethnocentric definition of trans femininity that mirrors their own" (GP:164). In many cultures a trans-feminine life "has not necessarily required leaving kinship structures behind or adopting an identity positioned against social norms" (GP:166). She is just as hard on the present-day martyr cult. "Black trans women are often claimed uncomfortably like possessions by those looking backward for guidance, though they do not share in their struggle." (GP:506). The trans woman of colour becomes "a figure for other people" (GP:652), "a preface to someone else’s work, used up in memoriam" (GP:656). Waiting for redemption lets the culprits off; the street queen's demand was "to transform what is already here, instead of hoping that one day the world will be redeemed" (GP:666).

### 2.9 Desire for realness is not naïve
Butler and hooks read Venus Xtravaganza's killing as the price of wanting a conventional life (GP:638–644). Gill-Peterson replies that Xtravaganza explained her own life and dangers on camera, and that her wish for security names a double standard applied to poor trans women (GP:646–650).

### 2.10 Interdependence, respectability, mujerísima
Gay men, straight men and non-trans women define themselves against trans femininity, and trans misogyny is "a fear of interdependence and a refusal of solidarity" (GP:206). Trading away femininity for acceptance never works: "The respectability strategy sacrifices femininity to curry favor with dominant, misogynist ideas of power." (GP:720). Trans-inclusive feminism that pleads sameness is a scarcity politics: "Championing the inclusion of trans women by saying they are indistinguishable from non-trans women is the product of a scarcity mindset." (GP:712). Her alternative is *mujerísima* (GP:724) and travesti refusal of the state's licence to gender (GP:738–742), including Wayar's "We travestis are not men or women" (GP:740).

## 3. Colonial India in the book (for the Mālai faith)

What she says, all from Hinchy (GP:850–876 for the notes):

- **Before the panic.** Hijras performed in public, claimed *badhai*, gifts "to which they were spiritually entitled" at births and marriages, and were initiated into discipleship in childhood. Not all had surgery: "It was their asceticism that distinguished them" (GP:222).
- **Her definitional claim.** "hijras were not then—and are not today—transgender." (GP:224). Hijras are "arguably much older than the Western concept of gender" (GP:224), and present-day hijras are caught between Hindu nationalists and postcolonial pluralists and do not always line up with Indian trans activism (Saria, GP:224). "the violence of a trans panic made hijras trans feminine in the eyes of the British." (GP:224).
- **The panic.** Judge Unwin's 1852 sermon: hijras as "eunuchs dressed as women" with "vested rights" and "a King" (GP:228). Hinchy: "What the British didn’t know drove the hijra panic." (GP:230). In 1865 the province set out to "reduce" hijras and "gradually lead to their extinction" (GP:232).
- **The Criminal Tribes Act, 1871.** It set up a police register and an inventory of property, outlawed inheritance in hijra households, restricted travel, and criminalised dancing in public and women's dress (GP:232). Hijras were given their own section because "hijras were so feminine they were regarded as ungovernable" (GP:234), a "doomed race" (GP:234).
- **Dress as proof.** Sodomy was hard to prove, so "cross-dressing was used as its practical proof" (GP:236), as in the Boulton and Park case of 1870.
- **Its failure, and its damage.** Most hijras "were able to outwit the police", some petitioned and were deregistered, and the law withered (GP:238). But police violence rose: one officer cut hijras' hair and stripped their clothes and jewellery (GP:238). The loss of badhai income lasted, and impoverishment under colonial rule did more harm than the Act itself (GP:238–240).
- **The "shadow dynasty".** The British imagined hijras as a rival sovereignty with its own king (GP:588).

## 4. Gill-Peterson and Bhatt: where they disagree, and where they agree

`book_bhatt_third_sex.md` records that Bhatt rejects Gill-Peterson's sentence on hijras. Both sides are set out below.

**Weighting (project decision).** On hijras, on South Asia and on what trans women there want and call themselves, the project gives Bhatt's view more weight than Gill-Peterson's. Bhatt is a trans woman from India writing about her own country and people like her; Gill-Peterson writes as a historian from the US. Revathi, a hijra writing in Tamil about her own life, is weighted the same way, and her self-description agrees with Bhatt. Gill-Peterson stays a source for the colonial state's documented actions (the 1871 Act, the registers, policing), where her archival work is the evidence, but not for whether hijras are women. Where the two conflict on India, the Mālai design follows Bhatt.

## 7. What does not transfer

- **Her history is modern and colonial.** It runs from 1810 (Solvyns) to 2023. It is evidence about British India, the United States, the Philippines and Latin America after 1800. It is not evidence about any practice in 867–1453, in Mali, Tamil country or anywhere else.
- **Her account of the hijra is of the 1850s–1900s** and of present-day Odisha (Saria). Nothing in it is evidence for medieval Tamil temple life. The Mālai page already records that no source puts anyone assigned male in a temple woman's role (Orr 2000; Ben-Herut 2018).
- **West Africa appears only as the Atlantic slave trade's point of capture** (GP:450–452). She says nothing about Ghana, Mali, the Mande, jelis, smiths or the Sahel. Spillers's "ungendering" and Snorton's "fungible" gender describe Atlantic chattel slavery and its aftermath. They do not describe medieval Sahelian slavery and must not be read back into it (G12).
- **The trans-panic defence, hate-crime law, NGOs, Pride, Stonewall, the transsexual clinic and self-ID law** are modern institutions with no medieval counterpart. They are not models for any event.
- **Her sacral language for street queens** (transubstantiation, the queen's "two bodies", GP:596–602) is her reading of a 1963 novel and of mid-century queens' own self-image. It is not a precedent for a sacred social role, and rule 7 stands. Kantorowicz's "two bodies" and the fourteenth-century rule for deciding the sex of "hermaphrodites" by the sex that predominates (GP:602) are the one medieval thread in the book. Read here at second hand through Kantorowicz, they need a primary source before any use.
- **Mujerísima and travesti politics** are modern Latin American, living, and racialised and classed in their own way. The faiths may share the spirit of "femininity is the reward, here and now" (GP:760), which fits rule 15, but they take no word, rite or claim from travestis.
- **Her refusal of a "coherent history of trans women"** is a method, not a ban: the mod's faiths are marked inventions (rule 14) and do not claim to be that history.
