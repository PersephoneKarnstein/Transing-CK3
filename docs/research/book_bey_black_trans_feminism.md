# Bey, *Black Trans Feminism* (2022): guardrails for Badenya

**Citation.** Marquis Bey, *Black Trans Feminism*. Black Outdoors: Innovations in the Poetics of Study (series eds. J. Kameron Carter and Sarah Jane Cervenak). Durham and London: Duke University Press, 2022. ISBN 978-1-4780-1517-8 (hardcover), 978-1-4780-1781-3 (paperback), 978-1-4780-2242-8 (ebook). LCCN 2021015695.

**Sources read.** The whole book, in full: front matter and acknowledgments, introduction, six chapters, conclusion and all endnotes (`scratchpad/books/bey_btf.txt`, 12,001 lines). The bibliography (l.10749–11581) and index (l.11582–12001) were checked for citations only. Cited below as **BTF l.N**, by line of that text file.

**Quotes.** Every quote is verbatim, 25 words or fewer, and was checked by script against the cited lines (soft hyphens and line-break hyphens removed, running heads skipped). Everything else is paraphrase.

**Pronouns.** Bey speaks of "my they/them pronouns" (BTF l.9512–9513) in the endnote on their own position, and in the companion book says they are pronoun indifferent (see `book_bey_cistem_failure.md`). This note uses "Bey" and they/them. In that endnote Bey says "I am black" and adds: "I am not trans per se but enact subjectivity in ways that seek a trans and transed engendering of sociality" (BTF l.9550–9553). Bey cites Judith Butler and jayy dodd with the pronouns each used at the time (BTF l.9630–9652, l.10343–10366); this note does the same.

**Purpose and limits.** The book is about Black trans people, Black feminism and trans feminism in the United States, with some Brazilian and British material. It is read here as the project read scholarship on antisemitism for Ha-Nehpakhot (`docs/silk_and_silver.md`, the stereotype-to-rule table): to find the racialised tropes about Black women, Black trans people and Africa that a game could reproduce, and to set a rule against each. **It is not evidence about West Africa before 1453, and nothing in it is used as such.** Bey writes that the book "takes many of its sources and discourses from Americanist texts and histories" (BTF l.1652–1653). They hope it is "portable outside of the specific U.S. context" (l.1655–1656), but they mean portable as a politics. That does not make it a history of Mali. The categories Bey works with are made in the Atlantic world after 1500: "Black" as a race, the Middle Passage, the plantation, Jim Crow, "cis" and "trans". None of them is to be carried back onto the 13th-century Sahel. See §5.

**Left out on purpose.** Private people. The book quotes tweets by private users (e.g. BTF l.7197–7199, l.10053–10057) and names members of Bey's family. None of their words or names appear here. The book also prints a list of murdered Black trans women (l.7632–7641). Their names are not reproduced, and no in-game use of them is allowed. Suicide and self-harm material is marked in §6 and is off-limits.

**Relation to existing notes.** Bey's chapter "Faceless" in Heaney (ed.), *Feminism against Cisness*, is already read in `book_heaney_feminism_against_cisness.md` (§2.7, row 4). There the mod sides with Bhatt against Bey's personal refusal of the category, under belief A3 (daughters are women). This note and its companion `book_bey_cistem_failure.md` divide the work. This note covers Black women, Black trans women, Africa and the abolitionist, fugitive frame. The companion covers cisgender, masculinity, language and kinship. Where they overlap, a row here points there. Rule numbers refer to `docs/faith_design.md` §16.

## 2. Key concepts, in Bey's terms

- **Abolition and gender radicality.** Abolition is wider than prison abolition: "Abolition, as articulated here, is broader than just prison abolition" (BTF l.1210–1211). It aims to make every captivity impossible, taxonomy included. It is set against revolution understood as "a Marxist seizure of the means of production" (l.1235–1237), because "Abolition, on the other hand, is a doing away with the state" (l.1248–1249). It is also everyday and small: Bey borrows "a pragmatic coalition—or a micropolitics—of a thousand tiny causes" (l.8623–8625). "Radical" means uprooting, not returning: "to be radical is not to want to go back to a prelapsarian image of perfection" (l.1360–1361).
- **Unfixation and paraontology.** Blackness, transness and feminism are forces, not possessed identities, distinct from "people deemed black". In Bey's words: "Blackness, thus, will outlast 'race'; transness will outlast 'gender'; feminism will outlast 'women.'" (l.889–890).
- **Gender self-determination,** in Bey's version, is not "any person, any gender". It is "the ethical requisite to say 'No'—or better, to decline to state—with regard to the imposition of gender" (l.1287–1288). Bey also calls it social: "The gender self-determination affixed to black trans feminism is a social dance" (l.1279–1281).
- **Opacity** (after Glissant): "opacity denotes a departure from the Western imperative of transparency, inherent to which is a reduction" (l.693–695). It is a right not to be made legible to medical and regulatory knowledge.
- **Traniflesh** (ch. 2): flesh, in Spillers' sense of what comes before the captive body, joined to a trans fugitivity. It is "on the run from gender" (l.3543–3545, quoting Che Gossett).
- **Becoming-black-woman** (ch. 2): a posture of refusal open to anyone, explicitly "not really about 'black women'" (l.3316–3317).
- **Against violence as the sole lens.** "This to me troublingly only allows (black) trans femme subjectivity to emerge through violence" (l.559–560). Bey rejects a Blackness in which "Blackness becomes, simply, that thing that can get you killed" (l.1922–1923). Of the conclusion they write: "Death, though pervasive in academic, material, and social discourses, will not enter here" (l.1605–1606).
- **Against representation:** "Representation troublesomely relies on a fundamental tokenization" (l.3992–3994).
- **Fugitive hope** (conclusion): hope without the promise that things will get better. It is grounded in ordinary life and joy: "I believe in life" (l.8931–8932, quoting Assata Shakur).
- **No home.** "If traniflesh might be a place to live it cannot be our home" (l.3649–3650). "It is in the spirit of radical openness and coalition that we spurn home" (l.3657–3658).

## 4. Where Bey bears on the mod's own framing

These are disagreements of framework, not tropes. They are set out fairly, with what each side would say. The companion note covers cisgender, "woman" as a category, and inheritance (§4 there).

**4.1 Abolition of categories vs. faiths with Houses, gates and names.** Bey's book is a call to abolish the categories people love, even when the people who hold them object: "We cannot half-ass abolition" (BTF l.473). "We still, even when Grandma doesn't (think she) want(s) it, work to abolish the world" (l.489–490). The mod builds the opposite kind of thing. Its faiths are institutions with Houses, a three-step gate, a jeli who names the daughter, a tenet on inheritance, and holy sites.
- *Bey's side.* A gate that turns someone into a "Daughter of the House" is a sorting machine. A House that buries "its own" draws a line round who belongs.
- *The mod's side.* Bey's abolition is also "everyday", built from "a thousand tiny causes" (l.8623–8625), coalition and care: "We need, in other words, to be held" (l.514–515). A House that feeds, roofs, claims and buries is that kind of holding. And the mod is set in 867–1453, where a woman without kin or a House had no standing at all. Bhatt answers the abolitionist directly: refusing the category does not stop others forcing you into it (Heaney note §2.7, Bhatt Concl. l.1000).
- *Verdict.* No change to the design. Keep rule 8 (sisters before the knife) and the open membership of Houses, so that the gate admits rather than polices.

**4.2 Self-determination as "declining to state" vs. "no daughter names herself".** Bey's self-determination is a right to "decline to state" (l.1287–1288). Badenya's gate ends with a jeli speaking her name before the lord, and badenya.md draws the rule that "no daughter names herself".
- *Partial agreement.* Bey also calls self-determination "a social dance" (l.1279–1281), not a private act. Badenya makes womanhood social in the same way: claimed by a woman, spoken by a jeli. Bey's opacity (l.693–759) supports the mod's existing rule that no one asks *since when* (Heaney note row 4).
- *Tension.* In Badenya, a public naming before the lord is the price of standing.
- *Guardrail.* The jeli's words are praise and never interrogation. No event in the gate asks her to prove, show or explain anything (rule 5, rule 22). Stepping back stays honoured (rule 23).

**4.3 "Not about black trans women" vs. a faith of trans women.** Bey's axiom, after Nat Raha, is that "a radical feminism must center the needs, experiences, and material concerns of trans women, trans femmes, and nonbinary femmes" (l.765–767). Yet the book "is not about black trans women" as an identity (l.1619). The mod keeps the first half and refuses the second: its faiths are for women, and daughters are women (A3). This repeats the disagreement already recorded in the Heaney note.

**4.4 Materialism.** Bey distrusts "matter" taken as unmediated truth (l.615–646), but says "This is not to elide materiality" (l.2818–2819). They reject the Marxist model of revolution as seizing the state (l.1235–1249).
- *Against the mod.* The mod's materialist transfeminism (Bhatt; `book_transgender_marxism.md`) grounds womanhood in labour, reproduction and sex-class.
- *With the mod.* Bey's sharpest claim in the companion book, "The oppression created the class of women" (`book_bey_cistem_failure.md` §2), is the materialist claim.
- The disagreement is about the remedy: abolish the category, or make room for trans women within it.

**4.5 Home.** Bey spurns home (l.3596–3664). Gumbs' women walk out of the house, and Bey reads that as freedom (l.5105–5137). The mod's central unit is the House, and Badenya renews it with *The New Roof*.
- *Bey's side.* A home that shelters also confines, and Bey names the violence of family homes (l.3640–3648).
- *The mod's side.* The House is not the family home but a chosen one. It is a refuge from the family home, close to what Bey praises in Selenite: names women choose, and kin who are made rather than born (l.7471–7489).
- *Verdict.* No change. Rule: a daughter may leave a House without penalty or shame.

**4.6 History and the past.** Bey refuses origins and golden pasts ("I have little interest in anchoring this project", l.1656–1657; l.1360–1361; l.3590–3592) and reads history for its might-have-beens (l.4380–4415). This agrees with rules 2 and 19. Bey's use of Oyěwùmí in ch. 2 does not fit their own refusal of origins, and the mod follows the refusal (row 3).

## 5. What does not transfer

- **All the evidence.** The archives are US and modern: the 1735 South Carolina "negro cloth" act (l.1073–1086), the 1848 Columbus and 1868 cross-dressing ordinances (l.1100–1124), the Fugitive Slave Acts (l.920–998), the 1859 arrest of Caroline Wilson (l.1171–1198), Black Lives Matter and #SayHerName (l.7601–7650), Brazilian *quilombo* (l.2686–2687), and US poetry and blogs from 2013–2020. None of it is evidence about Mali, Ghana or the Sahel before 1453.
- **"Black" and race.** Bey's subject is a category made by the Atlantic slave trade and the US. In the game's Sahel, identity runs by lineage, caste (*nyamakala*: smiths, bards), town, language, free or slave status, and faith (Muslim or not). Mapping Bey's Black/white axis onto Mande society would erase all of these (row 11).
- **Atlantic slavery, the Middle Passage and fugitivity** as frames (row 10). The trans-Saharan trade and court slavery existed, but they are different institutions with different sources.
- **"Cis", "trans", "nonbinary", "femme", "gender".** These are modern words, never used in-world (rule in the Heaney note §7). Bey's broad "trans" (anyone who misaligns) also conflicts with rule 18. See the companion note.
- **The colonial-origin thesis.** Bey's reliance on Oyěwùmí (l.3319–3325) and, in the companion book, Lugones assumes that binary gender arrived with Western colonisation. Mali before 1453 was not colonised by Europe. Its own sources record a public patrilineal ideology next to the hidden *badenya* (Koné 2004), and the sister's line next to Maliki law. Rule 2 and the Oyěwùmí note already reject a colonial-origin story; this book does not change that.
- **Christian Transfiguration** (ch. 3, l.3891–3913) and Deuteronomy 22:5 (l.1051–1058). These are Christian frames, irrelevant to a Mande faith at Muslim courts.
- **Deleuze and Guattari's "becoming-woman"** and "traniflesh" as a theory of the subject. Out of scope for a game whose daughters are women, not postures.
- **What may be used, as inspiration only and never cited as evidence:** Selenite's insistence on chosen names in death (supports *The House Buries Its Own*), and Gumbs' Tubman, who "renamed herself after her mother" (l.4936–4938), which echoes the Mande mother's-name-first custom. That custom is already sourced in Mande material (Conrad and Condé 2004; Niane 1965; Jansen 2018). The Tubman line adds nothing to the evidence and must not be cited for it.
