# Oyěwùmí, *The Invention of Women* (1997): design reading

**Citation.** Oyèrónkẹ́ Oyěwùmí, *The Invention of Women: Making an African Sense of Western Gender Discourses*. Minneapolis and London: University of Minnesota Press, 1997. ISBN 0-8166-2440-2 (hc), 0-8166-2441-0 (pb). The copy read is the third printing, 2001 (copyright page, l.18-38). Our reading list (`south_reading_list.md` #17) gives the 13-digit form of the paperback ISBN, 978-0-8166-2441-6, which matches.

**What was read.** The whole plain-text file (`scratchpad/books/tr/07.txt`, 10,403 lines): preface, acknowledgments and note on orthography (l.94-574); ch. 1 (l.576-1873); ch. 2 (l.1874-3970); ch. 3 (l.3971-5711); ch. 4 (l.5712-7272); ch. 5 (l.7273-8231). The notes (l.8233-c.9500) were read where the text pointed to them (ch. 1 n. 36, 39; ch. 2 n. 1, 36, 77); the bibliography and index were searched, not read.

**How cited.** Printed page (from the running heads) and line of the text file, e.g. "p. 45, l.2505". The OCR drops most Yoruba diacritics and garbles some words ("obmrin" for *obìnrin*); this note uses the book's own plain spellings (obinrin, okunrin, oko, aya, iya).

**Quotes.** Verbatim, 25 words or fewer, each checked by script against the text file. Everything else is paraphrase. Living people the book names from fieldwork (the author's family, two village heads, a woman hunter, Esu devotees) are left out of every proposal on purpose.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **Thesis.** In Old Oyo before the 19th century, "gender was not an organizing principle in Yoruba society prior to colonization by the West" (p. 31, l.1901-1902). The category "woman" "simply did not exist in Yorubaland prior to its sustained contact with the West" (p. ix, l.130-132). "There were no women in Yoruba society until recently" (p. 78, l.3928); there were *obinrin*, whom she renames "anafemales".
- **What ranked people instead.** "The ranking of individuals depended first and foremost on seniority, which was usually defined by relative age" (p. xiii, l.264-265). Seniority is relational: who is senior depends on who is in the room (p. 42, l.2353-2357). It carries duty as well as privilege; the worst insult is "senior for nothing" (*agbaya*, p. 41-42, l.2336-2347).
- **The method.** Four lines of argument.
  1. *Language* (ch. 2, ch. 5). Yoruba pronouns, names and kin terms do not mark sex; they mark age (pp. 40-43). *Obinrin* and *okunrin* are anatomical terms for reproduction only, with no social rank attached ("a distinction without social difference", p. 36, l.2109-2110).
  2. *Institutions* (ch. 2). In the lineage compound, everyone born in is *oko* ("owner/insider") to every in-marrying *aya*, so a daughter of the house is "husband" to her brothers' wives (pp. 44-46). Crafts, priesthoods and hunting pass by lineage, not by sex (pp. 68-71).
  3. *Historiography* (ch. 3). Johnson (written 1897, published 1921) and later historians turned gender-free *oba* and *alaafin* into "kings" and female rulers into "regents" (pp. 85-97).
  4. *Colonialism* (ch. 4). Indirect rule recognised only male chiefs; mission schools trained boys for office and girls for wifehood; land became private and male; "customary law" was made in courts without women (pp. 123-152). "The creation of 'women' as a category was one of the very first accomplishments of the colonial state" (p. 124, l.5861-5862).
- **What she says she is not doing.** The claim "should not be read as antimaterialist hermeneutics" (p. x, l.156-157). She limits it to Oyo and "I hesitate to apply them broadly", even to the rest of Africa (p. xiv, l.311-312). She does not deny that gender exists elsewhere now (p. 15-16, l.1223-1229).
- **On "third gender".** She rejects the label for African "woman marriage", the "berdache" and the hijra: "even the appropriateness of naming them 'third gender' is questionable" (p. 11-12, l.1043-1054). Her reason is the opposite of Bhatt's (she thinks gender itself is the Western import), but the conclusion is the same.
- **On genderlessness.** "Yoruba genderlessness is not to be read as androgyny or ambiguity of gender" (p. 174, l.8017-8018). It means attributes were not sex-coded at all.

**Dating, plainly.** Nothing in the book is datable to 867-1453 except the Esie figures, and those are a museum-catalogue date she takes from Stevens (1978) without discussion. Her "pre-nineteenth-century Oyo" is reconstructed backwards from Johnson, 19th-century travellers and her own 1990s fieldwork. She is not consistent on the fall of Old Oyo: 1829 at p. 44 (l.2440), 1836 at p. 108 (l.5197); Law's title has c. 1836. Matory (`waf_matory_oyo.md` §E) makes the same point: her earliest document is 1829.

**Where Oyěwùmí and Bhatt agree, unexpectedly:** both reject "third gender" as a label pinned on non-Western people by Western scholars (Oyěwùmí p. 11-12; Bhatt's "The Third Sex"). Both reject the scholar's "male as norm" when female rulers are turned into regents (Oyěwùmí pp. 86-95). The mod can use the second point without taking her thesis.

**The Bakare-Yusuf date (open question in `book_bhatt_brown_trans_les.md` §7).** The 1997 book cannot settle it, since the review came later. Bhatt prints "(2000)" in a heading and says "in 2000 she published" (Bhatt l.462-463) and gives no journal. Crossref for DOI 10.1080/1472584032000127914 (checked 2026-10-21) gives *African Identities* 1.1, pp. 119-140, print date April 2003, typed "Book Review", with no author in the metadata. **Use 2003, *African Identities* 1.1: 119-140.** An earlier circulating version in 2000 is possible but unattested in anything read; one guess, unverified, is that "2000" was misread from the "32000" inside the DOI string. Do not cite a 2000 version.

## 5. Usable material

Almost nothing here is period-true for 867-1453, and nothing is Mande. What survives:

1. **A pattern, not a fact: chroniclers turn ruling women into regents or "manly" exceptions.** Johnson's Iyayun "ruled the kingdom as a man until her son was of age"; his Omosun was "masculine in character" (Johnson as quoted, p. 94, l.4585-4607). Whatever one thinks of Oyěwùmí's thesis, the move she documents (pp. 86, 94-96) is a general habit of court historians and needs no Yoruba setting.
2. **Seniority by order of entry, with a built-in loser.** Her lineage model ranks newcomers by when they arrived, so a late arrival of any age starts at the bottom (pp. 45-46). The shape fits a House; the shadow (Bakare-Yusuf) comes with it.
3. **"Who is speaking" over "what is seen".** Her thesis that Yoruba knowing runs through hearing, not sight (pp. 2-3, 14-15), is contested as anthropology, but as a faith's *self-description* it suits Badenya's third step, the jeli's word that cools.
4. **The denial pattern as hostile voice.** "That is a stranger's custom" is the move she makes on same-sex love (p. 63, p. 117) and that Bhatt names as the regime's (ch. 4, l.384). Usable as an attributed hostile line in any court.
5. **Esie (12th-14th c. by Stevens's dating).** Armed female figures in the Igbomina country. **Do not use yet:** the date is one catalogue's, the figures are still propitiated (p. 113, l.5407-5409: since the late 18th century), and the site is Yoruba, not Mande.

## 8. Cautions

1. **This book is the founding text of the genderless-past idea the mod forbids** (§16 rule 2; Bhatt row 2). Nothing from it goes into any faith's main voice as history, and the narrator never says "seniority, not gender" of any society, Yoruba or Mande. The one place its thesis may appear in-world is as someone's mistaken belief.
2. **Never adopt "anamale" or "anafemale".** The terms define people by anatomy (p. xii). The mod's rules run the other way: recognition is separate from the body, and the Mark has no cause.
3. **Yoruba is not Mande.** Oyěwùmí herself refuses to generalise beyond Oyo (p. xiv). Badenya must not borrow *oko*, *aya*, *iyawo*, *ilemosu* or any Yoruba term.
4. **Living religion and living people.** Ṣàngó, Oya and Esu worship and the Egungun are practised today; Esie's figures are propitiated. The author's family, the two *baale*, the woman hunter and the Esu devotees are real, named, living or recently living people. None appears in any proposal.
5. **The same-sex denial.** "Homosexuality does not seem to have been an option" (p. 63) is unevidenced. Do not quote it as fact about any people; it is usable only as the pattern behind §7.1's "stranger's custom" line.
6. **Orompoto.** Matory's Igboho tellers make her a daughter who "turned male" (FtM, out of scope); Oyěwùmí, after Smith's informants, makes her a woman ruler (p. 95). Both are 20th-century tellings about c. 1560: out of period either way.
7. **Bhatt's FGM and lesbianism quotes are from the later anthology**, not this book. Cite them to that work, not to *The Invention of Women*.
8. **No suicide content.** The book has the *arokin*'s story of a royal daughter burned to death on her wedding journey (pp. 101-102) and the crown prince's death with the king (p. 92). Neither is used.
