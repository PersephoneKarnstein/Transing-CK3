## 1. The dhimma: what protection was and what it cost

- **Protection as a writ.** The Pact of 'Umar "was a writ of protection (aman, or dhimma)" granted by the Muslim community to its protégés (p. 25). In return for safety of life and property and freedom of worship, dhimmis paid the jizya and kharaj and behaved "befitting a subject population" (pp. 25–26). The pact was probably redacted under 'Umar II (717–20), "when there was a definite hardening" (p. 25).
- **The marks.** Dhimmis "had to wear clothing that distinguished them from the Arabs". Once a common Islamic fashion spread, this meant "badges or specially dyed outer garments" (p. 26). Other clauses: no new houses of worship, no loud prayer or processions, no arms or horses, no houses higher than Muslims', no Arabic names or titles, and exclusion from government service, "at least in theory" (p. 26).
- **The text the Maghreb knew.** The version Stillman translates is from al-Turtushi's *Siraj al-Muluk* (pp. 157–158). Al-Turtushi was an Andalusi Maliki of the early 12th c. (general knowledge, not the book), so this is the form of the Pact current in the Islamic West. It includes "We shall not attempt to resemble the Muslims in any way with regard to their dress" (p. 157), "We shall always adorn ourselves in our traditional fashion" (p. 158), "We shall not display lights in any of the Muslim thoroughfares" (p. 158), and the forfeiture clause: whoever breaks it has "no covenant of protection" (p. 158).
- **Women in the dress code.** In al-Mutawakkil's decree of 850 (Abbasid, not Maghrebi), dhimmis wore honey-coloured hoods and belts. "If any of their women went out veiled, they had to be enveloped in a honey-colored izar" (p. 167). This is the earliest women's dress rule in the volume.
- **Who paid.** Abu Yusuf (late 8th c.): the jizya falls on "all adult males, but not upon women and children" (p. 159). Collectors were not to beat dhimmis or leave them standing in the sun, and a saying attributed to the Prophet threatens "Whoever oppresses a protégé" (p. 161). Dhimmi merchants paid 5% customs against the Muslims' 2.5% (p. 162). *Guardrail: this is tax and money. Note it, don't build on it.*
- **Enforcement varied with time and place.** In the High Middle Ages the Muslim majority "was sufficiently prosperous not to be overly concerned with enforcing the humility of its dhimmi neighbors"; "there were no ghettos at this time" (p. 62). The laws of *ghiyar*, "differentiation", were enforced "with greater consistency" only in the later Middle Ages (p. 65). Under the Ayyubids most Jews wore a mark on turban and cloak, but upper-class dhimmis could often evade it and "pay for their exemption" (p. 68).

## 2. Ifriqiya: Fatimids, Zirids and Qayrawan

- **Fatimid tolerance (909–969 in Ifriqiya).** The Fatimids "showed relatively more tolerance toward their dhimmi subjects than had most Islamic rulers". They did not impose the discriminatory tariffs and "had even fewer qualms than most Islamic rulers in employing nonbelievers in the civil service" (pp. 43–44). Stillman's reasons: most of their subjects were Sunni and hostile to them, and an infallible Imam was not bound by the jurists (p. 43).
- **Qayrawan as a centre.** For most of the 10th and 11th centuries Qayrawan was "the outstanding major spiritual and intellectual center of Jewry outside of Iraq" (p. 44). Isaac Israeli (d. 950), physician and philosopher, and his pupil Dunash b. Tamim (p. 44). There were two yeshivot: that of Jacob b. Nissim Ibn Shahin (d. 1006/7), later headed by his son Nissim (d. 1062), author of a Talmud commentary and *The Book of Comfort*; and that of Hushiel and his son Hananel (d. 1057) (p. 45).
- **A copied hierarchy.** The Jews of Qayrawan "possessed a strong, hierarchal communal organization that they patterned after that of Baghdad". Nathan ha-Bavli wrote his account of Baghdadi Jewry for them (p. 45).
- **Who led.** "The recognized head of the Jewish community before the Muslim authorities was usually some distinguished citizen who also served at the court" (p. 46). Before c. 1015 this was the *rash ha-qehillot*, head of the congregations. In 1015 Ibrahim (Abraham) b. 'Ata', **court physician to the Zirid governors Badis and al-Mu'izz** and son of a *rash ha-qehillot*, received the title *Negid ha-Gola* from Hay Gaon. Successive heads of Tunisian Jewry held the title "throughout most of the eleventh century" (p. 46).
- **The decline.** Many Maghrebi merchants moved to Egypt after 969, and more after "the devastating Bedouin invasions that overwhelmed Ifriqiya between 1050 and 1057" (p. 44; chronological table p. xxiv: "1050–57 Banu Hilal Bedouin ravage Ifriqiya").

## 3. The nagid in the letters: title, service and protection

- **The letter of 1015** (Bodl. MS Heb. d 65 f. 9, pp. 183–185). It is written in Qayrawan to Joseph Ibn 'Awkal in Fustat. The sultan Badis has returned "from the Maghreb safe and victorious, as well as ... my Master Abu Ishaq the Nagid, along with him" (p. 183): the physician went on campaign with his lord. Hay Gaon's letters dignify him "with the title 'Nagid of the Diaspora'". He then "summoned the people before his blessed seat" and took up a collection for the yeshivot (p. 184). His written authorisation is "validated in court" (p. 185).
- **The letter on his death** (Antonin 904, pp. 186–188), from the same writers: "he was the protector of the community and its support" (p. 186). Now "the enemy and the opponent have become arrogant toward us", and in the provinces "the governors and officials are crushing them while they can find no one from whom to seek aid" (p. 187). Stillman glosses "the enemy and the opponent" as code for Jew-baiters (p. 187 n. 7). The same letter reports famine, Qayrawan sealed off, and the Further Maghreb and Sijilmasa "in chaos" (p. 188).
- **Design point.** In these letters the nagid's protection is **personal and ends with his death**. The title from the gaon only recognises a standing he already had through service at court (cf. Rustow, `jud_maghreb_rustow_heresy.md` §3).

## 4. The state over the community: licences, writs and the ban

- **Leaders need a writ.** Geonim and exilarchs "had to be approved by the government", and "A writ of approval continued to be absolutely necessary for all principal leaders of Jewish communities" (nagid, *shaykh al-Yahud*) down to modern times (p. 38). The writ also confirmed "the restrictions of his own and his communities' dhimmi status" (p. 38).
- **The state could block and bribe.** Muslim authorities "could and did block the appointment of religious leaders" (p. 38). Disputes were "open invitations to government involvement", often settled "in favor of the faction paying the highest bribes" (p. 39). *Guardrail: bribery is finance carrying content. Don't build on it.*
- **Avoiding the ruler.** It was "a generally accepted principle within the Jewish community to avoid involving the Muslim authorities in internal Jewish affairs", though often broken (p. 39). Autonomy existed "precisely because the state did not care what they did so long as they paid their taxes, kept the peace, and remained in their place" (p. 39).
- **A ban by the notables (al-Andalus, c. 1110–15).** Maimonides tells how at Cordova "the notables and wisemen of our community ... gathered in the synagogue" and had the would-be messiah Ibn Arieh "publicly flogged", fined and "placed ... under a ban of excommunication", with all his followers (p. 245; history p. 60). No ruler's licence is mentioned. The flogging implies some coercive power, which fits Rustow's point that Iberian communities had unusual state-backed powers (Rustow, Epilogue). This is Andalusi, not Maghrebi.
- **The Karaites (Fatimid Palestine).** A Fatimid decree orders "that neither of the two Jewish communities, namely the Rabbanites and the Karaites, are to be allowed to interfere with each other" (p. 198). Stillman prints it without a date. Rustow dates and explains it (1030; see her note §4).
- **Muslim courts protected property.** In the first half of the 11th c., the head of the Jewish community of Cairo ("Rabbanite, Karaite, and Samaritan") defended a synagogue before the Shafi'i chief qadi with Muslim witnesses and won (pp. 189–190).

## 5. Courtiers: how they rose, served and fell

- **Ibn Killis.** A Jewish convert to Islam who "was brought to the attention of Caliph al-Mu'izz by some court Jews" in Ifriqiya and planned the conquest of Egypt (p. 43).
- **The turban without the mark (Qayrawan, before 1062).** The nagid Ibrahim b. 'Ata', physician to the Zirid amir al-Mu'izz b. Badis, was carrying his master's message to the Maliki jurist Abu 'Imran al-Fasi. The jurist, taking him for a Muslim "because he was not wearing a Jewish badge", "stained the Jew's turban on the spot and then sent him packing". The amir refused redress: it was a good lesson "showing the power of Islam and the veneration inspired by the Muslim scholars" (p. 63). This is **the only Maghrebi ghiyar incident in the volume before the Almohads**, and it shows the mark being enforced against a courtier by a jurist and not by the ruler.
- **The fall of a courtier hurts his people.** After the assassination of Abu Sa'd al-Tustari in Cairo (1047), a merchant in Qayrawan wrote: "The couriers have brought news that has left us stunned" (p. 62). "The fall of a Jewish courtier was a cause of deep anxiety for his brethren" (p. 62).
- **Merinid Fez (after 1250, outside the brief's window but inside the game).** Sultan Yusuf b. Ya'qub (1286–1307) had Jewish courtiers of the Waqqasa family; Khalifa the Elder was his majordomo (p. 78). Stillman's explanation: rulers employed Jews "because of the latter's extreme vulnerability and, hence, according to Islamic political psychology, dependability", as they used "Turkish guards, black slaves, and eunuchs" (p. 79). Jews "had no power base" and "could expect no sympathy among the Muslim masses" (p. 79). And: "The few Jewish courtiers who are known to us from the Arabic sources were, in fact, all put to death by the rulers they served" (p. 81).
- **The ruler as shield.** In Fez on 10 March 1276 a massacre "was halted only by the appearance on the scene of Sultan Ya'qub b. Yusuf" (p. 80). In 1438 the Jews of Fez were moved to the *mellah* beside the palace "for their own protection", though they felt it "a sudden and a bitter exile" (pp. 79–80). In 1465 the Fez mellah was almost wiped out after the sultan made Aaron b. Batash vizier (p. 81). *Guardrail: massacres and forced relocation are not modelled. Note them as background only.*
- **The physician of Tlemcen (15th c.).** A Muslim pupil of the Jewish physician Ibn Ashqar of Tlemcen wrote that he held "the chief medical post" and was in the ruler's entourage, but "does not get involved in anything that has to do with government" (p. 288). He gave his pupil the *ijaza*, the licence to teach and practise (p. 288 n. 3). This is a good model for service without politics.

## 6. The Almohads and after

- **Conversion, not expulsion.** "Most of the urban Jewish population from Tunisia to Morocco had outwardly professed Islam during the height of the Almohad terror. Those …" (p. 76). The converts were suspect, so the Almohads treated them "much like dhimmis, forcing them to wear distinguishing clothing" (p. 76). Ya'qub al-Mansur (r. 1184–99): "If I were sure of the sincerity of their Islam, I would let them mix with the Muslims", but "I am uncertain about their case" (p. 76).
- **An exception.** Stillman calls the Almohad period "a definite aberration": forced conversion of Jews "was extremely rare", no more than half a dozen cases in thirteen centuries, all under "heterodox fanatics" (p. 76).
- **What followed.** Maghrebi Jewry "emerged from the Almohad period spiritually and numerically impoverished". Leaders fled, "like the Maimonides family", or were martyred, "like R. Judah b. Shushan" (p. 77). No native Christians survived, so the Jews became "the dhimmis par excellence" (p. 77). Under the Hafsids "the jizya was collected and the laws of differentiation observed, but without the special rigor of the Mamluk Empire" (p. 77). "As Almohad rule disintegrated, Moroccan Jews began openly to reestablish their communities" (p. 78).
- **Messianic hope, Fez 1130.** Moses al-Dar'i, from the Dra valley, a pupil of Joseph ibn Megash, came to Fez and announced the Messiah. Maimonides' father "tried to dissuade people and even forbade them from following him" (pp. 243–244). Maimonides calls the Almohad founder "a rebel in the Maghreb who instituted the forced conversion" (p. 240). *Guardrail: the al-Dar'i story turns on selling property and taking "loans from the Muslims" (p. 244). It is a finance story, so don't use it.*

## 7. Gender and the body: what the volume has and lacks

- **Has:** the dhimmi woman's honey-coloured *izar* (850, p. 167). Women exempt from the jizya (p. 159). Under the Mamluks (1354, Egypt) dhimmi women had to wear coloured wraps and were barred from Muslim women's baths, and dhimmi men had to wear a neck ring in the bath "so that even undressed they could not be mistaken for Muslims" (p. 70). Eunuchs appear only as Stillman's comparison for the dependable outsider at court (p. 79). The 1276 Fez riot began with a rumour that a Jew "had acted improperly toward a Muslim woman", and Stillman reads it as a stereotype (p. 80). *Do not use: it is a sexual slander.*
- **Lacks:** no khuntha (intersex) law, no mukhannathun, no cross-dressing, no Purim customs, no women's prayer and no women's quarters for the Maghreb. I searched; nothing is there. The only Maghrebi women in the parts I read are unnamed.
