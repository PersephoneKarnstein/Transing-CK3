**Relation to existing notes.** This is the sequel to Halm's *Die Kalifen von Kairo* (2003), which covers 973–1074 and is still unavailable. **It does not narrate the 1060s.** The civil war, the *shidda* and Rasad's fall are assumed known from the earlier book. - a compact **description of Fatimid palace staff**: eunuch offices, names, concubines and princesses;

It has **no *mukhannath* material**, nothing on entertainers before the 1120s, and almost nothing on Byzantine–Fatimid relations after 1071.

## 1. The argument in brief

### The thesis
Halm's frame is that from 1074 Egypt has a **sultanate in all but name**. An Armenian ex-slave general, Badr al-Jamālī, holds the army, the vizierate, the judges and the Ismaʿili mission. The caliph keeps only "the nimbus of religious head" *(trans.)* (p. 37). Badr's son al-Afḍal is named his heir in September/October 1084, which makes the office hereditary (pp. 37, 46). Halm calls Badr, not Saladin, the "first sultan of Egypt" (p. 37). The court described in the book is the court of a **caged caliph**.

### The recap: what Badr found (pp. 17–21)
- **The country.** It was "disorganised", in "chaos and anarchy". The troops had plundered the caliph's treasuries (p. 18).
  - Turkish *mamlūks* under Ildengiz and Yaldakūsh held Cairo.
  - The Sudanese (Black) contingents had withdrawn to Upper Egypt (p. 18).
  - Berber Luwāta held the western Delta and hoarded grain. Bedouin held Middle Egypt, and a hereditary emir held Aswān (p. 21).
- **The invitation, 1073.** The caliph called Badr in. Badr's condition: he would bring an army and leave no Cairo officer or vizier in post (p. 18; *MI* II 311, *secondhand*).
- **Arrival.**
  - He landed at Damietta in **December 1073**, out of the sailing season, with a hundred ships from Acre. He levied a contribution on the merchants of Tinnīs (pp. 17–18).
  - He reached Cairo **on the evening of 27 January 1074**, alone. His men followed singly on foot until he had 900 (p. 18).
- **The banquet.** Badr feasted the Turkish officers. As each went drunk to the latrine he was killed. Each killer was rewarded with "the house and goods of his victim" *(trans.)* (p. 18). Only then did the caliph show his hand. He made Badr vizier and gave him the chin-banded turban and the chief judge's *ṭaylasān* (p. 18).
- **Vizier turnover.** In the previous sixteen years the vizierate had changed hands **49 times**. The last vizier, Ibn Abī Kudayna, held it eleven times (pp. 19, 21).
- **The provinces, 1075–76.** The Delta was retaken in 1075. Middle Egypt and Aswān followed in summer 1076 (p. 21).
- **Recovery.**
  - From 1072 to 1078 the Nile never failed: "seven fat years in a row" *(trans.)* (pp. 43–44).
  - Badr forced hoarders to sell and remitted the harvest tax for three years (p. 44).
  - The northern quarters of Fusṭāṭ had been destroyed "during the troubles". Badr let their materials be reused and cleared the ruins, which became a rubbish ground (pp. 78, 81).

### Badr and the palace
- **The charter.** His appointment handed him "everything that happens behind [the caliph's] throne" *(trans.)* (p. 19).
- **Cairo opened.** Badr ended Cairo's exclusivity as a palace city and opened it for general residence. He built his own palace, the *Dār al-Muẓaffar*, and the first *sūq* inside the walls (p. 84; undated in Halm).
- **The palace gate.** The caliph stayed in the Great Palace but now faced **crowds at the palace gate** on the *Bayn al-Qaṣrayn* (p. 84).
  - During the Turkmen invasion of the Delta (Dec 1076–Feb 1077), a crowd begged the caliph for help.
  - **He answered through a eunuch** that he too was only a man. They should fast, pray and give up wine (p. 26).
  - When ships were readied, the crowd shouted that he and Badr meant to flee by water (p. 26).
  - After the victory of late February 1077, a wave of piety followed, and **six drunks were executed** (p. 27).
- **The regiments.**
  - Badr's 1074 army was "mostly Armenians", and **mostly Christians**, unlike Badr himself (p. 53; *Patr.gesch.* II 219).
  - The Black slave troops (*sūdān*, *ʿabīd*) were brought back under control. They formed **the centre** of Badr's line in February 1077 (pp. 26–27).
  - So Rasad's old Black regiments did not vanish: they became Badr's.
- **The women's quarters.** Halm says nothing of Badr touching them, beyond the charter's phrase.

### The palace and court, 1074–1101
- **Rasad seen in 1078** (p. 47; *Patr.gesch.* II 207–211, Engl. 321–326, *secondhand*). The new Coptic patriarch Cyril II was consecrated on 18 March 1078.
  - **The procession.** He came to Cairo in procession. He entered the palace by the River Gate (*bāb al-baḥr*).
  - **The door.** At the throne-room door "the eunuch ʿAnbar ('Ambergris') receives him and leads him in alone" *(trans.)*.
  - **The audience.** Inside, al-Mustanṣir waited "together with his mother and his sister, wrapped in a cloud of perfume" *(trans.)*. He asked: "Bless us and our palace" *(trans.)*.
  - **Then Badr.** Only afterwards did the patriarch go to Badr's palace.
  - This is the one scene in the book where Rasad is physically present.
- **Rasad's letters.**
  - **August/September 1078.** Halm dates the start of the Yemeni queen's regency by "the two letters addressed to her from the caliph and his mother Sayyida Malika" *(trans.)*, dated Ṣafar 471 (p. 363 n. 142). "Sayyida Malika" matches the heading of Sijillāt letter 51 (`isl_sijillat_mustansiriyya.md`).
  - **June/July 1087.** In a flood of Cairo letters to Yemen, "even the caliph's mother turns to her in consolation" *(trans.)* (pp. 60–61). The note gives this as Sijillāt "Nr. 52 from the caliph's mother", and says all the letters cited are dated Rabīʿ I 480 / June–July 1087 (p. 363 nn. 151, 153).
  - **A misprint.** The main text on p. 60 prints the year as **1078**. The note's 480 AH, and the context (al-Mukarram I died in 1084), show that **1087** is meant. See §4.
- **Palace staff (pp. 149–152).** Halm builds this mostly from the 1122 robe list, so it describes the *later* court, but he presents it as Fatimid practice generally.
  - **Eunuchs.**
    - They were expensive "and therefore not very numerous" *(trans.)*, citing Lev (p. 150 n. 18).
    - Slavs early on, then more and more Blacks.
    - The older term was *khādim*, the later usually *ustādh* (p. 151).
  - **Names.** Eunuchs were named after precious things and scents: Jawhar, Yāqūt, Miʿḍād ("armlet"), Kāfūr, ʿAnbar, Rayḥān. Some had odder names, such as Fanak ("fennec") or Jundub ("locust") (p. 151).
  - **The *muḥannakūn*, from 1025.** The chin-banded eunuchs first appear "in 1025 under al-Ẓāhir": three leading eunuchs were given cotton turbans and chin-bands of white *sharb* (p. 151; Musabbiḥī I 71 f.).
  - **Trust.** Eunuchs entrusted with one of the caliph's concubines enjoyed "special trust" (p. 151).
  - **Offices.** "All the senior officials inside the palace were eunuchs" *(trans.)* (p. 151). Halm lists:
    - the intendant of the palaces (*zimām al-quṣūr*), the treasurer, the head of the table, the cellarer, the keeper of the wardrobe and the keeper of the mausoleum;
    - the head of the audience hall and the curtain-drawer (*mutawallī al-sitr*);
    - the head of the stirrup and the parasol-bearer (pp. 151–152).
  - **The police.** The chief of police of Miṣr and Cairo, usually combined with the *muḥtasib*, was "traditionally reserved for a eunuch" *(trans.)* (p. 151; no note).
- **Concubines and princesses (pp. 149–150).**
  - **No wives.** "There seem to have been no wives of the caliphs" *(trans.)*. Nearly all caliphs were sons of slave concubines.
  - **The *umm walad*.** A concubine who bore a son could not be sold and was freed at her owner's death. As a caliph's mother she often worked "behind the scenes". Such women often founded small mosques in the Qarāfa (p. 150).
  - **Mothers' origins.** Al-Ḥākim's mother was a Greek Christian whose brothers rose in the church. "The mother of al-Mustanṣir was a Sudanese" *(trans.)* (p. 150; no source note).
  - **Daughters.** The caliphs' daughters "apparently were never married". Other princesses married only within the family. Princes received rich allowances but never a province or an army (p. 149).
  - **Named by their eunuch.** In the 1122 robe list the concubines are not named. Each is designated **by the eunuch in charge of her** (p. 150).
- **Festivals.**
  - **Under al-Afḍal (1094–1121).** Public pomp was "strongly restricted". The young caliphs were kept out of sight, and the "four birthdays" (*mawālid*) were suppressed (p. 164).
  - **Revived in 1122** by al-Baṭāʾiḥī. Halm lists:
    - Fast-Breaking, Sacrifice, ʿĀshūrāʾ and Ghadīr Khumm (18 Dhū l-Ḥijja);
    - the Prophet's, Fāṭima's, ʿAlī's and the reigning imam's birthdays;
    - the four "burning nights" of Rajab and Shaʿbān;
    - New Year, the Nilometer and the Canal rides;
    - Coptic feasts kept officially: Christmas, Epiphany, Maundy Thursday and Nawrūz (pp. 164–165).
  - **Halm misdates Nawrūz** to the spring equinox (pp. 149, 164); see §4.
  - **The palace band.** Feasts had the caliph's band (*rahajiyya*), banquets and gifts (p. 165).
  - **Al-Āmir's pleasures.** In the 1120s al-Āmir sat above a platform at the Qarāfa "while the people of the [Sufi] order danced before him" (p. 173). He "loved spectacles and amusements" (p. 175).
- **No *mukhannathūn*.** No *mukhannath*, cross-dressing performer, mask or shadow play appears anywhere in the parts read, or in the index.

### Succession, 1094–96 (pp. 87–91)
- **The deaths.**
  - Badr's death date is uncertain (March–June 1094, or as late as early 1095). He was nearly eighty and had lost his speech, and al-Afḍal governed for him (p. 87).
  - Al-Mustanṣir died at the end of December 1094, aged 65 (p. 88).
- **The candidates.**
  - The four eldest sons were Nizār, ʿAbdallāh, Ismāʿīl and Aḥmad. Nizār was born **26 September 1045** (p. 366 n. 9; Ibn Muyassar 62).
  - Aḥmad was born in 1074 or 1075 (n. 6). Halm says the son announced in Sigill 6 of March 1060 was an earlier Aḥmad who died young.
- **The coup.** Al-Afḍal enthroned the youngest, Aḥmad, as al-Mustaʿlī.
  - Nizār: "Even if they cut me to pieces, I would pay homage to no one younger than me!" *(trans.)* (p. 88).
  - Nizār went out to fetch his father's written designation, and slipped away to Alexandria.
- **The homage.** On **Thursday 28 December 1094 (18 Dhū l-Ḥijja 487)** al-Mustaʿlī was set on the throne. That date is the **Feast of Ghadīr Khumm** (p. 89). Halm does not remark on the coincidence; we do.
- **Resistance.** The chief *dāʿī* Barakāt proclaimed ʿAbdallāh and was executed with the chief qāḍī (p. 90).
- **Nizār's revolt.** At Alexandria he ruled as al-Muṣṭafā "under the parasol" until November 1095. He surrendered under safe-conduct. He was handed to al-Mustaʿlī's people "in the palace, and they walled him in, so that he died" *(trans.)* (p. 90).
- **The women's role.** It is limited to the chancery. On **6 February 1096** al-Mustaʿlī **and his mother** each wrote to the queen of Yemen with the official version (pp. 90–91; Sijillāt 43, 35).
- **Absences.**
  - **Halm never names Nizār's mother**, and has no *rūmiyya*.
  - He does not mention al-Mustanṣir's sister's deathbed testimony, which C&C p. 38 report.
  - No eunuch has any role in the 1094 events.

### Christians, Armenians and Rūm
- **Badr and the Christians.**
  - Badr was a Muslim, but friendly to Christians (pp. 46–47).
  - He once ordered black belts for Christians and yellow marks for Jews. The order "apparently was not enforced" *(trans.)* (p. 47).
  - He moved the Coptic patriarch to Cairo for good (p. 48).
  - He held a synod of 47 bishops in his gardens on **29 August 1086** (p. 48).
  - He threatened the Negus in 1089 (pp. 52–53).
- **Christian Cairo.**
  - In 1078 the patriarch preached in the Church of the Virgin "in the quarter of the Greeks (*ḥārat al-Rūm*)" in Cairo itself (p. 48).
  - In **June/July 1087** a ship from Constantinople brought the young Armenian prelate Gregory, a nephew of the catholicos Gregory II (p. 54).
  - Gregory was given **"a church of the Virgin belonging to the Melkites (Greek Orthodox)" at the canal bridge** *(trans.)*.
  - The Armenian troops received an empty church in their own suburb north of Cairo (p. 54).
- **Byzantium recedes.**
  - Manzikert, 19 August 1071 (p. 24).
  - The Seljuqs took Antarados (1081), Tarsus (1082) and Antioch (December 1084), "the last Byzantine base on the Syrian coast" (p. 39).
  - No Byzantine embassy, captive or palace woman from Rūm appears for 1074–1101.

### Later palace scenes (beyond the first campaign, for date-gates)
- **The palace exile, c. 1080.** Ḥasan-i Ṣabbāḥ reached Cairo on **29 August 1078** and stayed seventeen months. By his own account, the courtiers were "filled with envy". Badr meant to send him "to the Island (Tinnīs?), to be held there for ever" *(trans.)* (pp. 68–69).
- **A hundred-year-old eunuch.** Lāmiʿ, a eunuch "over a hundred years old", brought down the Christian tax-farmer "the Monk" c. 1127 (pp. 167–168). He must have served at court in Rasad's day, though Halm does not say so (our inference).
- **The Pearl in 1130.** Al-Āmir was stabbed in October 1130 and carried "to al-Luʾluʾa [a riverside palace in Miṣr]" during the Nile flood, where he died (p. 176).
- **The parrot.** Al-Ẓāfir (1149–54) kept a white parrot that "called many eunuchs by name and title" *(trans.)* (p. 223).
- **Gossip that killed, 1154.** The caliph's love for Naṣr, the vizier's son, became "an open secret". Naṣr was talked into killing him, which he did on 15 April 1154. Two Black eunuchs had come with the caliph; one was killed and one escaped (pp. 235–236).
- **The princesses' hair, 1154.** The palace princesses, led by al-Ẓāfir's sister, sent Ṭalāʾiʿ ibn Ruzzīk a letter "with their hair, cut off in mourning" *(trans.)* (p. 238).
- **The 1171 count.**
  - The palace was said to hold 12,000 people, of whom only the caliph and his kin were male. Halm adds: "the eunuchs were, of course, not counted among the men (*faḥl*)" *(trans.)* (pp. 292–293; *MHB* I 497).
  - Saladin's father was lodged in "the little palace al-Luʾluʾa ('the Pearl') on the canal" (p. 293).

## 4. Answers to open questions / corrections to other notes

Do not edit those notes from here; these are the changes to make.

1. **`later_starts/mukhannathun_fatimid_cairo.md` §6 Q7 (when did Rasad die?).** **Not answered: Halm gives no death date.**
   - **Certain.** She is alive in 1078. She receives the patriarch beside the caliph after 18 March 1078 (p. 47). She writes to the Yemeni queen in Ṣafar 471 / Aug–Sep 1078 as "Sayyida Malika" (p. 363 n. 142, = Sijillāt letter 51).
   - **Possible.** Halm also credits Sijillāt **Nr. 52** to "the caliph's mother" and dates it Rabīʿ I 480 / June–July 1087 (p. 363 nn. 151, 153; p. 61). The Sijillāt note reads the date as Rabīʿ II 480 / July 1087. It also shows the letter is headed "daughter of al-Ẓāhir", and only Mājid's conjecture makes it Rasad's.
   - **Probable origin of "1087".** El-Azhari's "1087" most likely comes from this letter: a last *possible* sighting, not a death date.
   - **Rule.** Alive to 1078 for certain. A death after mid-1087 is defensible only on Mājid's and Halm's attribution, so mark it unverified.
2. **Same note, Q6 (Pearl Pavilion).** **Not answered.**
   - Halm says nothing of al-Ḥākim's destruction or of any rebuilding. He names al-ʿAzīz as founder (p. 265) and attests use in 1130 (p. 176), so Sanders's 1123 remains the earliest sighting after 1012.
   - His source note reads *MHB* I **367** (p. 386 n. 112, checked on the image), against Sanders's *Khiṭaṭ* I:467–468. One is a misprint; check the Būlāq volume.
   - New: al-Āmir's canal belvedere of 1112–13 for watching the Khalīj (p. 173; *secondhand*, from al-Baṭāʾiḥī via al-Maqrīzī). Do not conflate it with the Pearl.
3. **Same note, Q5 (Abū l-Sarī).** Not in Halm. Neither are Ibn Buṭlān or Ibn Riḍwān.
4. **Same note §3.2, "1073–74: Badr arrives".** Make it precise: landed at Damietta in December 1073; entered Cairo on the evening of **27 January 1074** (pp. 17–18). This agrees with Lev and with the Sijillāt note's Jumādā I 466.
5. **Same note §3.2 / §3.1, Nawrūz.** Keep 1 Tūt. **Halm puts Coptic Nawrūz at the spring equinox** (pp. 149, 164). That is wrong for Egypt; do not copy it.
6. **Same note §5, request 1 (Halm 2003).** Still wanted; this sequel does not replace it. Halm's own cross-references give the pages to ask for:
   - *Die Kalifen von Kairo* pp. 395–402 (the 1060s–70s; nn. 33, 38, 84, 132);
   - pp. 54–68 (festivals; p. 373 n. 58);

7. **`isl_cortese_calderini_fatimid_women.md`, "54 viziers and 42 qāḍīs" between al-Yāzūrī and Badr (C&C p. 113).** Halm: "in the preceding sixteen years the vizierate had changed **forty-nine** times" (p. 19). The counts differ; cite both.
8. **Same note, Nizār's mother as *rūmiyya* (p. 55).** Halm is silent on her. He does supply Nizār's exact birth: 26 September 1045 (p. 366 n. 9; Ibn Muyassar 62). The *rūmiyya* claim stays secondhand, from one Ismaʿili source.
9. **Same note, al-Mustanṣir's sister claiming the deathbed designation in 1094 (p. 38).** Not in Halm. He reports a deathbed designation only from Ibn al-Ṣayrafī 59 (p. 366 n. 11). He concludes that no designation of Nizār "apparently" took place (p. 90).
10. **`isl_tolino_eunuchs.md` row 4 ("*muḥannak*" an anachronism for 1066).** Too strong. Halm dates the first chin-banded eunuchs to **1025** under al-Ẓāhir (p. 151; Musabbiḥī I 71 f.). The *office list* is 12th-century; the rank is not. The Musabbiḥī passage is in the repo's own edition (Cairo note §Sources), so verify it there before relying on it.
11. **Same note row 6 ("the standard date for Nizār's birth, 1045, is not from these sources").** Now sourced: Halm p. 366 n. 9.
12. **`isl_lev_state_society_fatimid.md` row 9 (eunuchs few and dear).** Halm agrees (p. 150), but he cites Lev pp. 74–78 for it (n. 18). This is not independent confirmation.
13. **`isl_el_azhari_queens_eunuchs.md`, eunuchs ran the police and *ḥisba* (pp. 269–270).** Halm says the same office was "traditionally" a eunuch's (p. 151, no note). Against it stands the 1024 Kutāma chief of the lower police (Cairo note §1.2). Treat it as common, not as a rule.
14. **Same note, "Rasad (Cairo, 1036–87)".** The 1087 end rests on Mājid's attribution of letter 52, which Halm repeats (item 1). Keep it marked unverified.
15. **`isl_sijillat_mustansiriyya.md`, row 10 (al-Mustaʿlī's birth).** Halm supports the note: Sigill 6 (March 1060) announces an earlier Aḥmad who died young. Al-Mustaʿlī was born in Aug–Sep 1074 or 2/4 Sep 1075 (p. 366 n. 6). Do not create al-Mustaʿlī in a 1066 history file.
