4. Gareth Lloyd Evans, "Female Masculinity and the Sagas of Icelanders" (59–75). Covers Auðr's breeches, Ólof as "Óttarr", and Freydís and the sword. **Read in full.** Gender-crossing by women; method only under the scope rule.

6. Philip Lavender, "Vulnerable Masculinities and the Vicissitudes of Power in *Göngu-Hrólfs saga*" (97–111). Disability, promises, and the *ragr* insult for an oath-breaker. Read for magic (n. 33) and *ragr* (p. 110).
7. Ásdís Egilsdóttir, "Masculinity, Christianity, and (Non)Violence" (113–26). Churchmen's non-violent masculinity; female saints "acting in a manly way". Skimmed.
8. Thomas Morcom, "Inclusive Masculinity in *Morkinskinna* and the Defusal of Kingly Aggression" (127–45). Covers the Eysteinn–Sigurðr *mannjafnaðr* and the small men who stand under the king's sword. **Read in full.**
9. Brynja Þorgeirsdóttir, "Emotions of a Vulnerable Viking: Negotiations of Masculinity in *Egils saga*" (147–63). Melancholy and lovesickness in Egill. Skimmed; low relevance.
10. Alison Finlay, "'Þat þótti illr fundr': Phallic Aggression in *Bjarnar saga Hítdœlakappa*" (167–82). Covers the carved *níð*, the shame it brings on the one who raises it, *níð* against kings, and the bishop's answer. **Read in full.**
11. David Ashurst, "Male Bedpartners and the 'Intimacies of a Wife': *rekkjufélagar* and *vífs rúnar*" (183–202). Bed-sharing as a recognised, emotional, legal bond between men. **Read in full.**
12. Carl Phelpstead, "Companions, Conflicts, and Concubines: Clerical Masculinities in *Lárentíus saga biskups*" (203–16). Clerical friendship networks. Skimmed; low relevance.
13. Jessica Clare Hancock, "'That which a hand gives a hand or a foot gives a foot': Male Kinship Obligations in the Heroic Poetic Edda and *Völsunga saga*" (217–36). Fathers, sons and brothers; Óðinn stands by his outlawed son. Read pp. 217–21.
14. **Afterword**, Evans & Hancock (237–40). The ethics of the field against far-right use of the einherjar. **Read in full.**

## 1. The argument in brief

**What updates Meulengracht Sørensen and Clover (Introduction; Evans).**
- Meulengracht Sørensen remains foundational. He showed that saga society rests "to a very large extent [on] an aggressive masculine ethic" (*Unmanly Man* p. 21, quoted p. 7), and that manliness can always be challenged by insult.
- Clover's one-sex model is "far from universally accepted" (p. 6). It is criticised for "selective and oversimplified readings", for "its erasure of the importance of physical sex", and for leaving no room for multiple masculinities (p. 6; Bandlien, Bagerius, Friðriksdóttir, Evans).
- Evans is blunt: Clover's assertion that women can perform masculinity "is clearly correct, but her larger model cannot be seen as an accurate reflection of saga literature" (p. 61). "It does matter in the sagas whether a subject is a manly man or a manly woman" (p. 61).
- Finlay supports him: in the sagas "physical sex is thought of as fixed – hence the seriousness of being likened to a woman or female beast" (p. 181).
- Clunies Ross (cited p. 8) holds that "the practice of seiðr is antithetical to masculine status".
- Lavender notes the claim and its limit: "traditionally scholars have claimed that magic (normally referring to seiðr ...) is harmful to masculinity". Yet romance heroes use magic objects "without any necessary negative consequences" (p. 106 n. 33).
- Vohra (cited p. 7): blood-brotherhood extends a man's reach, but "women are not able to participate in these relationships".

**Women crossing (Evans; Friðriksdóttir). Method only under the scope rule.**
- *Laxdœla saga* ch. 35: Auðr is divorced for wearing breeches "like *karlkonur*" (masculine women). The word survives only in the Möðruvallabók manuscript, and dictionaries emend it away (p. 63 n. 16). The breeches matter because of their *setgeiri*, the gores sewn in for male genitals (p. 66).
- "Grágás prescribes harsh punishments for both men and women who wear clothing deemed at odds with their sex" (p. 59 n. 3).
- Ermenga's turn as a knight (*Mágus saga*) draws no blame because it is "a temporary means to an end" (pp. 87–88).
- Friðriksdóttir reads Þorbergr in *Hrólfs saga Gautrekssonar* as identifying as male, like a transgender man today (p. 91). **That is a trans-man reading and out of scope.**

**Níð (Finlay).**
- *Níð* "attacks the target's masculinity usually through ... a slur on his sexual performance", and likening him "to a woman or a female animal confirms the gendered focus" (p. 167 n. 1).
- *ragr* is "more general in its application, encompassing cowardice and fear as well as the more specifically sexual charge" (p. 171).
- In Norwegian law *berendi* is a full-penalty word. It means a being that can give birth, a female (animal) (p. 170).
- **The pole shames the one who raises it too.** In *Bjarnar saga* the onlookers said "the situation of neither of those standing there was good" (p. 168).
- Meulengracht Sørensen came round to Ström's reading. The aggression is condemned "not only because it is thought to be shameful, but also because it is uncivilised, savage" (*Unmanly Man* p. 57, quoted p. 169).
- The carved *níð* drew a **lower** penalty than the verse that came before it (p. 168).
- Other poles. In *Vatnsdœla saga* the pole has a carved head and runes, is driven into a mare and is turned toward the target's farm (p. 176). In *Gísla saga* a craftsman is ordered to carve two figures so that the *níð* will "remain forever" (p. 174).
- *Níð* against kings is a category of its own in law. Heimskringla claims Icelandic law ordered a *níðvísa* against King Haraldr Gormsson "for each nose" in the land (pp. 176–78).
- **The bishop's answer.** Heathens claim Bishop Friðrekr "has borne nine children" by Þorvaldr. Þorvaldr kills the poets. The bishop instead turns *bera* (bear a child, or carry one in the arms): he would gladly carry any child Þorvaldr fathered (p. 170).
- The creator of *níð* may work through a proxy, a poet or craftsman, or speak for a whole people (p. 181).

**Flyting between equals (Morcom).**
- In *Morkinskinna*'s version of the brothers' *mannjafnaðr*, Eysteinn closes: "King Óláfr and I gave you away like you were our sister". In this version he is the one who uses the feminising barb (pp. 136–37). In the *Heimskringla* version Sigurðr strikes first with "as your father's daughter" (n. 35).
- Larrington flags the "particular risk" of effeminising insults (n. 35).
- Outcome: neither king wins outright, and each holds the other "within the limits of sanctioned kingly masculinity" (p. 138).
- **Standing under the sword.** After Eysteinn's death Sigurðr grows unmoderated. Three men curb him by standing still and accepting the expected death-blow:
  - Óttarr birtingr, a candle-boy who "stood upright and did not yield", is made a *lendr maðr* (pp. 140–41).
  - Áslákr hani, "a small man", is rewarded with three farms (p. 142).
  - Bishop Magni "stretched out his neck" (p. 143).
- Morcom calls this "the passive and static variety of courage" (p. 142).

**Licensed speech (Friðriksdóttir).**
- Disguised as an old vagrant, Mágus can say in verse "*ragr ertu, Ubbi*" in the hall. The king laughs it off (pp. 82–84).
- One of the disguises is Víðförull, "Wide-Traveller", an Óðinn name. In it he slanders the legendary heroes from a conjured "glass ceiling", like Óðinn as Bǫlverkr (p. 81).
- His magic and Óðinn-likeness associate him "with the perversion of seiðr" (p. 84), yet the saga plainly endorses him.

**Bonds (Ashurst; Falk; Roby).**
- Bed-partners (*rekkjufélagar*, *húðfatsfélagar*) formed "a supremely important social, rather than erotic, bond" (p. 190).
- Seafaring partners "made legal agreements and incurred legal responsibilities" (p. 200).
- Skúli jarl orders paired *húðfatsfélagar* "to cast lots between them as to which was to go" to war (p. 196).
- Jógrímr dives back into the sea for his *félagi* and drowns (p. 195).
- A slander comparing Þormóðr to "a mare among stallions" is avenged by killing, and King Óláfr approves: "It was allowable that their talk displeased you" (pp. 189–90).
- Fostering: Eiðr chooses his own foster-father (p. 31). The curriculum is "proficiencies and lineage-wisdom and martial arts" (p. 31). Fosterage contracts usually ended at about sixteen (Hansen, p. 40 n. 12).

**Óðinn and the Afterword.**
- When Sigi is outlawed, Óðinn does not reject him but "flees with him ... and did not leave him before he had taken him to warships" (*Völsunga saga* ch. 1; pp. 220–21).
- The Afterword: the Christchurch killer's "see you in Valhalla" casts the einherjar as "a violent, white, intensely homosocial masculinity" (p. 239).
- Óðinn "acts in ways that, were he human, would undoubtedly cause him to be coded as queer" (p. 239).
