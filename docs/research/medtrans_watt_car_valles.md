Read in full. Quotes are verbatim, and line numbers refer to Thorpe's edition as cited by Watt. Primary source: Heldris de Cornüalle, *Le Roman de Silence* (13th c., Nottingham MS WLC/LM/6), with *Yde et Olive*, *Tristan de Nanteuil*, Pietro d'Abano and Marie de France's *Lanval* as comparanda. Framing is in `medtrans_kim_bychowski_visions.md`.

**Scope note: low relevance, and mostly out of scope.** Watt reads Silence as a **trans man** ("a trans man, rather than a cross-dressing woman", as the issue introduction puts it). Trans men are out of scope for this mod, and nothing here should become a trans-masculine mechanic. What carries over is structural: inheritance law as the engine of assigned gender, the vertical ladder of sex, forced regendering at the end, and a few period texts about men becoming women.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **Silence's sex is assigned by inheritance law.** King Ebain bans female inheritance, so Cador and Eufemie raise their child as a son, keeping open the option to *desvaleter* ("dis-boy") him later (pp. 138–139). "As a girl, Silence can reproduce but not inherit; as a boy, he can inherit but not reproduce" (p. 139).
- **"Disguise" is the critic's assumption.** Watt quotes Cromwell: "Is it deceit or disguise ... when an individual wears clothing considered appropriate for their identity?" (p. 136). Silence declares "vallés sui et nient mescine" (line 2650: "I'm a young man and not a girl", p. 145) and repeatedly chooses to live as a man.
- **The medieval sex ladder runs one way.** Jerome: a woman who serves Christ "will cease to be a woman and will be called man". That was praise, and "the reverse was often not true" (pp. 141–142). Pietro d'Abano (13th–14th c.) held that a womanly man treated wrongly "might change into a woman, and thus would be changed into something worse" (p. 143). In *Yde et Olive* and *Tristan de Nanteuil*, angels change women into men as "a divine recompense" (p. 144 n. 24).
- **Puberty is a crisis.** Nature, Nurture and Reason debate the twelve-year-old Silence (pp. 139, 150–151). Watt links this to modern trans adolescence and to the medieval markers of manhood: "a beard, prominent body hair, a muscular chest" (p. 150).
- **Secrecy and shame drive the ambivalence.** Cador's instruction to his "son" is to *cover yourself* ("Si vos covrés viers tolte gent", line 2457, p. 147). Silence fears that becoming a woman is a fall: "it'll be a wicked hour / When I step down, when I'm on top" (pp. 153–154).
- **The narrator keeps pointing at the missing part.** "He is, beneath the clothes, a girl" (line 2480, p. 155). Watt argues that sex attribution actually runs on comportment and recognition, not genitals (Salamon, p. 159).
- **Silence is read as a vulnerable young knight**, like Lanval: a Potiphar's-wife queen, a stingy lord, and the crisis of the *juventus* under primogeniture (pp. 160–169).
- **The ending regenders Silence by force.** Merlin exposes Silence. Ebain marries Silence without verbal assent (p. 169). Nature spends three days "removing / Everything masculine that was on it" (line 6673, p. 170). It is "a transformation, not a revelation", and "his silence at the end makes his attitude toward his new body ... obscure" (p. 171). The law is reformed so that women can inherit again.
- **Close.** Sandy Stone: transsexuals "not as a class or problematic 'third gender,' but rather as a genre" (pp. 172–173).

### Event / historical-character flags
- **No historical persons.** Silence, Yde and Blanchandine are fictional and assigned female at birth, so they are out of scope as characters.
- **Pietro d'Abano** (Padua, c. 1257–1316) is a real physician whose *Expositio problematum* says a man may change into a woman (p. 143). He is usable as a **hostile physician's citation** in Latin-West events after c. 1300, which fits the era-decay timing.
- **Easter egg (in-group, event text only, per §9):** Silence's declaration inverted, "Car mescine sui et nient vallés" ("for I am a girl and not a youth"), as a line spoken by an Anglo-Norman or French daughter at her declaration.

### Methodological cautions
- **Fiction, not people.** Watt applies Cromwell's criteria for historical people (own statements, efforts to change the body) to a fictional character (p. 145) and acknowledges the counter-view that the resemblance may be "purely coincidental" (p. 141 n. 16). The mod should cite romances as period imagination, never as evidence of lives.
- **Trans-masculine readings are out of scope.** Do not borrow Silence as a sister or recast the plot. Use the article only for its structures and period texts.
- **Pronoun practice.** Watt argues for "he" as an analytic choice and surveys mixed practice (pp. 137–138). For the mod's own (trans-feminine) historical citations, follow the person's attested self-naming where any exists (as with Rykener's "Eleanor").
