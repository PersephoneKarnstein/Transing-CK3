## 1. The argument in brief

- **Halakhically binary, discursively plural.** The tannaim discuss the *androginos* and *tumtum* at length. But "Rabbinic gender is ... halakhically binary" (p. 2), and visibility does not mean a gentler life (pp. 1–2). The rabbis treat intersex people "dispassionately", not as portents (p. 6; p. 255).
- **Who counts.** Only the *androginos* and *tumtum* challenge the rabbinic binary (pp. 31–34).
  - The *petzu'a daka*, *kerut shofkhah* and *seris adam* (men with injured or removed genitals) are barred from marrying a Jewish woman. Otherwise they "are treated as men for all intents and purposes" (p. 32).
  - The *seris ḥammah* (born infertile male) is "an infertile (but unequivocal) male" (p. 32).
  - The *aylonit* is an infertile woman, "treated halakhically as an unequivocal woman" (p. 33).
  - Lists of "four to eight genders in the Talmud" online are, in her word, "overstated" (p. 31).
- **Six models** (Introduction, pp. 17–20; Epilogue, pp. 252–254). Most rabbinic rulings use the first: they apply the rules of whichever sex is stricter in the case at hand.
  - uncertainty (*safek*) with the precautionary principle;
  - Rabbi Yose's *beriah le'atzmo*, "a creation sui generis";
  - the "non-" model, which excludes intersex people from every gendered verse;
  - the maleness model;
  - Rabbi Elazar's part/part model;
  - the paradox of T. Nazir 3:19.
- **Ch. 8, T. Nazir 3:19.**
  - The case: a group sees an *androginos* in the street. They bet nazirite vows on whether the person is a man, not a man, a woman, not a woman, both, or neither. The ruling is that "all of them [must] become nazirites" (p. 216).
  - Elsewhere the rabbis release hyperbolic or uncertain vows. The parallel case right before it, a passer-by who may or may not be "Ploni", releases almost everyone (pp. 234–237). Only the *androginos* (and the half-wild *koy*, M. Nazir 5:7) get unanimous stringency (pp. 241–242).
  - Lev rejects three explanations: the gravity of vows, censure of starers, and the stakes of sex as such (pp. 240–242).
  - She argues the text answers a "radical unknowability" by making every contradictory statement true at once (pp. 242–250). It "points to a person who can 'paradoxically' and yet truly embody a nonbinary sex/gender" (p. 250).
  - The halakhah reads like a joke, but "the butt of this joke is not the androginos ... but binary sex/gender itself" (p. 251).
- **Epilogue.** Even this binary system makes room for intersex people, "never a comfortable space. It is sometimes an acrid space" (p. 258). Reparative reading means taking what sustains and leaving the rest (pp. 256, 259).

## 2. The brief's four questions

**1. Does any text allow a change of legal sex?** No. Nothing Lev reads lets anyone pass from one sex to the other.
- **The *tumtum*.** The only movement is the *tumtum* "who is torn and found to be a male" (M. Yevamot 8:6; p. 33, p. 276 n. 22). That is discovery of a sex already there, not a change. "Every tumtum is an 'actual' male or female" (p. 33).
- **The *androginos*** "resides permanently" between the sexes (p. 34). In Rabbi Elazar's model ze is male and female in turns, never moving from one to the other (ch. 7 close).
- **Roman texts**, by contrast, describe people "female at birth" who "changed into" or were "discovered to be" male (p. 35; p. 60). That is FtM and out of scope.
- **This confirms the README:** "No rabbinic text lets anyone pass from male to female." Strassfeld's note adds the Bavli's later "found to be female" emendation. That too is discovery, not change.

**2. The *tumtum*'s surgery.** Lev does not know what the Mishnah means by "torn": surgery to expose a hidden sex, or testes descending at puberty. "We have no evidence one way or another" (p. 33). Genital surgeries were known in the Greco-Roman world (p. 276 n. 23, citing Brooten).
- The early rabbis "do not 'solve the problem' of intersex by 'excision'" (p. 87).
- Lev reads the majority's binary as the precursor of modern "corrective" surgery. She reads Rabbi Yose's sui generis as a refusal of it (p. 107).
- The surgery question is intersex medicine, not the sect's knife. **It gives no support either way to the mod's "no knife" rule and should not be cited for it.**

**3. The *aylonit*.** She is not intersex in the rabbis' eyes.
- Her description in T. Yevamot 10:7 (no breasts, thin hair, deep voice) may be "the simple inverse" of the *saris*'s description rather than an account of real bodies (pp. 32–33). Lev's earlier article "How the 'Aylonit Got Her Sex" (AJS Review 31, 2007) argues this at length.
- The only rabbinic hint of anything else is B. Ketubbot 11a calling her "ram-like" (דוכרנית) "since she does not give birth" (p. 276 n. 17).
- Post-rabbinic authorities sometimes treat her as a separate sex (same note, citing Irshai). This answers part of the open item in `faith_design.md` §13 ("*Trans Talmud* for the *aylonit* chapter"): **the *aylonit* is no precedent for a turned woman.** She is a born woman whose womanhood the law never doubts.

**4. Lev's stance on trans readings.**
- **Her subject is intersex, and she resists using it to think about other things.** She cites the intersex activist Morgan Holmes against making intersex people "hermaphrodite caryatids bearing the burdens of social order for everyone else" (p. 8).
- Her stated aim is to ask what the texts teach "about early rabbinic attitudes toward intersex itself", not about gender or gender identity in general (p. 9).
- She notes without judgement that the same texts have been used to think about trans people:
  - by Strassfeld (p. 7);
  - by Rabbi Eliezer Waldenberg in two 1967 rulings on transgender cases (pp. 7–8);
  - by Hillel Gray (p. 8).
- "Gender identity" is absent from rabbinic thought: "the notion of identity itself, in its modern sense, is absent" (p. 87; also ch. 7 n. 95).
- Her one opening is a parenthesis at the end of ch. 8. The halakhah might push us to make room for "intersex persons (and others?)" who choose to remain "neither, both, or a creation sui generis" (p. 251).
- So Lev neither endorses nor forbids trans readings. **She asks that intersex categories not be borrowed as stand-ins.**

## 3. What matters for the design

- **The sect's ground is right, and Lev is a second reason for it.**
  - The sect claims Dinah's turning (Berakhot 60a) and Kalonymus's prayer. It does not claim the *androginos*.
  - It refuses "a created being of its own" as its self-description (`faith_design.md` §13, hard limits).
  - Lev shows that the phrase (*beriah le'atzmo*, Rabbi Yose, T. Bikkurim 2:7) is the intersex text par excellence. Its modern reclamation by Elliot Kukla, as "A Created Being of Its Own" (HUC thesis, 2006; Intro n. 84), is a reclamation for intersex and gender-variant people. Borrowing it for a trans women's sect would make intersex people the "caryatids" Holmes warns against (p. 8).
  - **Record this as a second reason for the refusal**, beside "the faith refuses it as a compromise".
- **"A body made by human hands is still a man's"** (`tm_hipukh.0040.desc`; the README on daughters of the knife) is **confirmed**. The *seris adam* is a man "for all intents and purposes", limited only in marriage (p. 32).
- **"What came forth male is male"** is the rabbis' stance in the mod's own words. Lev's evidence fits it: genitals at birth are "the baseline" for which halakhot one follows (p. 24). Keep it as a reaction line. It is not a quotation, and nothing should present it as one.
- **Dress.** In Tosefta Bikkurim's table the *androginos* "does not wrap himself [like a woman does], and he cuts his hair, like men" (p. 75). Nowhere may ze dress as both (p. 79). This confirms `jud_rabbinic_primary_texts.md` §1 ("The Mishnah dresses him as a man"). Even the law's in-between person is dressed as a man. That makes the dress kept on after Purim (`tm_hipukh.0003`) the real offence the worthies would name.
- **The Megillah.** In T. Megillah 2:7, which is "virtually identical" to the blessing text Lev quotes (pp. 91–92, nn. 19, 22), people of doubtful sex read the Megillah only for "their own kind", and women do not read it on behalf of "the majority" (adult men).
  - `tm_hipukh.0010` has an elder reading the scroll **in the women's gallery**, for women. The tannaitic text does not forbid that.
  - Whether medieval Provençal authorities let a woman read the Megillah for women is *unverified*; it needs a medieval code before any loc line asserts it.
- **The crowd at the revel.** T. Nazir 3:19 is a street crowd staring at someone and betting on hir sex (pp. 215–219). Lev reads the ruling as turning the joke onto the binary (p. 251). This parallels Rosen's point about Kalonymus's jokes, that women "do not occupy the place from which the joke is funny" (`jud_rosen_circumcised_cinderella.md` §1). Rule for `tm_hipukh.0011` (The Revel) and any later crowd scene:
  - the young men may laugh at the dress as a costume;
  - **the event text never invites the player to laugh at her, or to guess at her body**;
  - do not port the nazirite-vow wager onto a daughter. It is an intersex text, and Lev's caution applies.
- **"Never comfortable ... sometimes acrid"** (p. 258) is a good register for how Jewish courts treat daughters in the mod. The −10 `tm_jewish_daughter_opinion` is tolerance without comfort. So is `tm_hipukh.0040.b` ("One does not cite miracles. But she keeps her place"), which matches "space is made". No change is needed.

## 4. Corrections to current docs

1. **README, "No rabbinic text lets anyone pass from male to female."** Confirmed for the early layer (Lev pp. 32–35). No change.
2. **`faith_design.md` §13, Open: "*Trans Talmud* (2022), for the *aylonit* chapter."** Lev answers the design question even before Strassfeld's chapter is read. The *aylonit* is an infertile born woman, never of doubtful sex in the early sources (pp. 32–33), so **do not build an *aylonit* argument into the sect.**
   - A tempting in-voice line would be "the aylonit has no breasts and a deep voice, yet the law calls her a woman". **Do not use it.**
     - It re-centres the argument on bodies, against the tenet that "a woman is made by what she keeps".
     - The rabbis' answer, that she came forth female, is decisive in their own terms.
   - Keep the open item only for Strassfeld's reading of time and eunuchs.
3. **"Six/eight genders" talk.** The hard limits already say the narrator never claims the Talmud recognised six genders. Lev gives the scholarly basis: the popular lists are "overstated" (p. 31). Cite her if the README explains the rule.
4. **Do not cite intersex surgery debates** (Waldenberg, the *tumtum* torn open, Lev's Epilogue on "a knife") for the sect's no-knife tenet. That tenet stands on "hands of heaven" against "human hands" (Yevamot 8, Maimonides Issurei Bi'ah 16). It belongs to a different argument.

## 5. Design implications (concrete)

- **Doc change (`faith_design.md` §13 hard limits):** after "No 'a created being of its own' as the sect's self-description", add: "It is the intersex text (R. Yose, T. Bikkurim 2:7), and its modern reclamation belongs to intersex people (Lev pp. 8, 31–34)."
- **Loc:** no change needed from Lev. Optionally, a Rabbinic opponent's line in `tm_hipukh.0003` or `0040` can draw on the dress halakhah: "Even the androginos wraps himself as a man and cuts his hair as a man." It is accurate (p. 75), and it lets the law be strict without the narrator making intersex people the sect's foil. Use only in a rabbi's mouth, never as the sect's argument.
- **No new mechanics.** Lev's book is about intersex law, which the sect is not. Its value is in confirming three boundaries the build already keeps: no change of legal sex in the sources; a man made by human hands stays a man; the intersex categories are not the trans women's precedent.
- **Flag, not for the Jewish sect.** In a note, Lev quotes Christian Laes calling "the sophist Favorinus of Arles" (2nd century, called *androthêlus*, beardless and high-voiced) "the most famous 'transsexual' from antiquity" (ch. 1 n. 49, p. 281). It is an Arles coincidence. Favorinus was a pagan sophist, and the label is Laes's. **Do not attach him to Ha-Nehpakhot.** Whether any other faith could claim him in its own voice is a question for the user.
