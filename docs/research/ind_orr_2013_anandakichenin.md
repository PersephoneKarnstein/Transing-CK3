## 1. Orr 2013, "Words for Worship"

### 1.1 Argument in brief

- **The sources.** Tamil temple inscriptions (8th–20th c.) use a mixed Tamil-Sanskrit "inscriptional maṇipravāla" (¶4).
  - Their Sanskrit words are mostly *not* the Āgamas' words, and their meanings have shifted (¶24).
  - The vocabulary was shaped by temple and village **accountants** (*kaṇakku*), not by priests (¶27).
  - Terms seem to have "migrated from temples to texts rather than vice versa" (¶26).
- **The same across sects.** Āgamic vocabulary is "quite consistent across the sectarian boundaries between Śaiva, Pāñcarātra, and Vaikhānasa" (¶2). The inscriptions of Vaiṣṇava and Jain temples are "very similar to the Śaiva ones in terms of their form, content, and vocabulary" (¶3). Words for worship are shared between Jain and Hindu inscriptions (¶26).
- **The donor's view.** Inscriptions rarely name an "image" at all. The deity "her or himself" is the subject who "graciously appears" (*eḻuntaruḷi*) (¶19). The language reflects "the devotee and donor ... for whom the image is god" (¶27).

### 1.2 What matters for the design

- **Kanchipuram, 758 (Text A; SII 4.827).**
  - A Pallava queen, Dharmamahādevī, endows the Muktīśvara temple. Its name bears hers, so she is probably its founder (Text A note).
  - The temple's establishment includes "fifteen women (whose names are given)", beside twelve ascetics and five mantra-teachers. Line 3 of the inscription is "a string of female names" (Text A).
  - This is the earliest of the six texts. It is a Śaiva temple at Kanchipuram founded by a woman with named women on its staff, a century before the 867 start.
- **Kuttalam, 992 (Text B; SII 13.170).** Queen Cempiyaṉ Mahādevī's temple lists "flywhisk women" among its personnel, alongside priests, hymn-singers, Veda reciters and twelve drummers (¶22). It also pays three garland-makers (¶20).
- **A goddess married with a bride's property (Text C; ARE 1918 no. 192; 1160 CE).**
  - A brahman village assembly gives land as ***strīdhana***, a woman's own property brought to marriage, to the "bedchamber goddess" Āḷuṭaiya-nācciyār. The land pays for her consecration and **marriage** (*tirukkalyāṇam*).
  - A donor named Iruṅkoḷar had set up her image.
  - The bride is the goddess, and the property is a bride's property given in her name.
- **Kanchipuram is also Viṣṇu's.** *Viṇṇakaram*, "Viṣṇu's house", is the word Tirumaṅkai Āḻvār uses for the Vaikuntha Perumal temple at Kanchipuram. It is in inscriptions from the early 9th c. (¶11).
- **Words for the line.**
  - **Before c. 1400.** Rights were *kāṇi* (rights in property), *paṅku* (shares), *nāḷ* (days, i.e. turns), and *koṟṟu* (daily rice allotment).
  - **From the 15th c.** They give way to *kāṇiyāṭci*, *mariyātai* (Skt. *maryādā*, honours), *carvamāṉiyam* and *cutantiram* (Skt. *svatantra*, independence) (¶23).
  - Endowments are *kaṭṭaḷai* or *ubhaya* from the 13th c. (¶23).
  - An endowment is *dharma* / *tarumam* from the 8th to the 20th c. (¶23).
- **"Caused to graciously appear"** (*eḻuntaruḷuvitta*). This is the donors' own verb for setting up an image or a temple, far commoner than Sanskrit *pratiṣṭhā* (¶¶17–18). "Caused [it] to graciously rise up" (*ukantaruḷuvitta*) is 12th–13th c. (¶17, n. 23).
- ***Tirumeṉi*, "sacred body".** This is used of the god's image from the 9th c., and also of the king's person (¶19, n. 25).

## 2. Anandakichenin 2020, the lady from Tirukkōḷūr

### 2.1 Argument in brief

- **The text.** The *Tirukkōḷūr peṇpiḷḷai rahasyam* ("the secret of the woman from Tirukkōḷūr") is 81 rhetorical questions. A woman leaving the town of Tirukkōḷūr asks them of Rāmānuja, who asked why she was leaving a place he longed to reach (pp. 96–97, 102).
  - Each takes the form "Did I do X, like so-and-so did?" (p. 102).
  - Fourteen of the figures named are women, one fifth of the references (pp. 103–104).
- **The date is unsure and late.**
  - The text is embedded in the *Paṉṉīrāyirappaṭi guruparamparāprabhāvam* (GPP12k), a hagiography usually given to Piḷḷai Lokam Jīyar, **c. 16th c.**, and "abundant" in interpolations (pp. 96–98 and nn. 5–6).
  - The Ācāryas named stop at Parāśara Bhaṭṭar, which suggests a 12th-c. origin. But vārttai 11 seems to quote Maṇavāḷa Māmuṉi (15th c.), and several events postdate the alleged meeting (pp. 99–100; p. 108 n. 28).
  - **The kernel is 13th c.** Nampiḷḷai's *Īṭu* commentary on *Tiruvāymoḻi* 6.7.1 tells the meeting in two lines. There the woman is unnamed and "does not say much either" (p. 98 n. 7).
- **The verdict.** Women's speech was praised because it "does not threaten the established practices". It insists on its own worthlessness and avoids the Vedas (p. 127). It received no commentary, and it "did not disturb or erase the boundaries of the male dominion over serious scholarship" (pp. 127–128).
  - The Śrīvaiṣṇava community, about half women, claims "equality with a difference": liberation and non-Vedic learning, but little encouragement to write (p. 95).

### 2.2 What matters for the design

- **The town is where the girl goes to the Lord.**
  - Rāmānuja greets the woman with Nammāḻvār's verse *Tiruvāymoḻi* 6.7.1, on "Tirukkōḷūr, the town which my young deer[-like daughter] enters for sure" (p. 129). In it the poet speaks as a **mother** whose daughter (the poet's own girl-persona) has gone to the Lord's town.
  - The joke is that a woman is *leaving* the place the girl of the poem runs *to*.
  - Tirukkōḷūr is in the far south, near Āḻvārtirunagari. The location is not given in the source; it is my addition.
- **The two garlands.** In vārttai 36 (p. 115), Toṇṭaraṭippoṭi Āḻvār offered "sacred garlands", or the poem *Tirumālai*. A variant reads "two garlands", which a modern editor explains as the **flower garland (*pūmālai*) and the song garland (*pāmālai*)** (n. 40). The double garland of flowers and of song fits the faith's name. The gloss is modern, so use it in the faith's voice.
- **Āṇṭāḷ "ripened while still unripe"** (vārttai 11, p. 108): devoted to God as a child. The phrase seems to be from Maṇavāḷa Māmuṉi (15th c.; n. 28).
- **A woman who asked twice.** In vārttai 40 (p. 116), Sumatī, the lady from Koṅku, forgot Rāmānuja's teaching. She "boldly" asked to be taught again and received his sandals (from GPP12k). This is a model for a second-try vow ("next Mārkaḻi").
- **The first woman author.** Tirukkōṉēri Dāsyai's *Tiruvāymoḻi* commentary is "13th c.?" and is "the only Śrīvaiṣṇava work attributed to a premodern woman so far" (p. 97 n. 4). She was "presumably a disciple of Nampiḷḷai (13th c.)" (p. 128). This dates the Narayanan note's "Garland of Words", tentatively, to the 13th c.
- **Rāmānuja at her house.** At the end, Rāmānuja goes to the woman's house and gives her his leftover rice and foot-water. She had promised that the town's festivals would be kept if he came (p. 130). That ending is the 16th-c. GPP12k's.
- **Lines to avoid** (brief: no suicide content). Vārttai 3 (the ascetic's wife who "gave up the body"), 38 (Tiruppāṇ "disappeared" into the god at Srirangam), 73 (a priest and family who threw themselves on the burning deity) and 76 (a woman who leapt into a flood) (pp. 106, 115, 124–125).

## 3. The Ben-Herut review: what it says, and what it does not

- **What the review says.** It summarises Ben-Herut's argument: Harihara's 13th-c. Kannada *Ragaḷegaḷu* differs from later Vīraśaiva self-understanding on "temple worship, gender parity, rejection of Brahmanical customs", partly because of Vijayanagara-period politics (p. 252).
  - Ben-Herut "cautions against seeing in such principles modern notions of egalitarianism or feminism" (p. 253).
  - His ch. 5 treats the Vaiṣṇava as the "opponent other", fought out "mostly in the locus of the palace" for royal patronage (p. 253).
  - Ch. 6 treats the Jain as the "wholly Other", whose treatment "can take terrifying proportions" (pp. 253–254).
- **What it lacks.** **It says nothing about the devotee as Śiva's bride.** The point the brief hoped to source there must stay sourced to Ramanujan's vacanas (`ind_ramanujan_bhakti.md`: Basavaṇṇa 703, Mahādēvi 68) until Ben-Herut's book itself is read.
- **For the design.**
  - Kannada Śaiva–Vaiṣṇava rivalry is a court contest in the 13th-c. witness (p. 253). Hostile reactions to a sect that crosses both should come from courts and their patrons.
  - Ben-Herut's caution on "feminism" applies to the Garland's own claims: the narrator does not call the śaraṇas proto-feminist.

## 4. Corrections to the candidate

1. **Kanchipuram has a woman-founded Śaiva temple with named women on staff by 758** (Orr 2013, Text A), and a Viṣṇu *viṇṇakaram* by the 9th c. (¶11). This strengthens Kanchipuram as the seat. See the conclusion in `ind_govindacharya_azhvars.md` §5.
2. **The temple world was shared across sects.** Vocabulary, form and content are the same in Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava and Jain inscriptions (¶¶2–3, 26). A gift-in-stone faith that crosses Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava temples is true to the inscriptional record. Its *theology* is the reconstruction.
3. **Brides in the inscriptions are goddesses.** The only "marriage" in Orr's texts is the 1160 wedding of a goddess image, paid for with *strīdhana* by a brahman assembly (Text C). The Garland may borrow the *form*: a bride's property given in the god's temple. It does not show a human bride.
4. **The Tirukkōḷūr woman is 13th c. at the earliest, and only as a two-line anecdote.** The full *Rahasyam* is c. 16th c., of doubtful authorship (pp. 97–100). Do not use it as an in-period woman's voice. Use only the *Īṭu* kernel (13th c.).
5. **Tirukkōṉēri Dāsyai: "13th c.?"** (pp. 97 n. 4, 128). The Narayanan note can drop "no date" and say "probably 13th c., tentative".
6. **Ben-Herut cannot yet be cited** for the bride of Śiva.

## 5. Design implications

- **Decision name: "Cause the Lady to Appear"** (from *eḻuntaruḷuvitta*, ¶¶17–18). This renames the Orr note's "Set Up the Lady". A Garland daughter endows a goddess image; the inscription says she "caused her to graciously appear".
- **Event, "The Lady's Wedding Land"** (Text C, 1160). A village assembly asks the Garland daughter to fund the goddess's marriage festival with land given as the goddess's own *strīdhana*.
  - Option A: fund it, for piety and a share.
  - Option B: ask that the land be written as *hers*, the daughter's, held for the goddess. This gives more legacy but angers the assembly.
- **Era modifier text.** "Shares and turns" (*paṅku*, *nāḷ*) before c. 1400. "Honours and independence" (*mariyātai*, *cutantiram*) after, matching Orr 2000's "rank without gifts" (¶23).
- **Court position flavour.** "Flywhisk woman" (Text B, 992), held at a queen's temple. It sits beside the "Reciter Before the Lord" of the Narayanan note.
- **Loc device: the Tirukkōḷūr question.** For a failed vow or a humble refusal, in the faith's voice: "Did I keep the dawns, like Kōtai did? Did I wake my friend, like the girls did?" The form is attested; the source is late (16th-c. frame).
- **Holy-site text, c_madurai (narrator voice).** "South of the river is Tirukkōḷūr, the town that the poet's girl ran to." (TVM 6.7.1, quoted p. 129)
- **Reaction lines.**
  - Śrīvaiṣṇava teacher (from p. 127): "Speak if you must, daughter, but say first that you are nothing."
  - Kannada Śaiva poet at court (Ben-Herut via p. 253): "At this court the Vaiṣṇava is answered, not embraced. Whose bride are you?"
  - Garland dissenter (from p. 128): "They let her ask eighty-one questions and wrote no commentary on one."

**Do not.**
- Present Śrīvaiṣṇava women's circles, the *Mummaṇi rahasyam* tradition or Tirukkōṉēri Dāsyai as the faith.
- Use the "gave up the body" and "leapt into water" vārttais.
