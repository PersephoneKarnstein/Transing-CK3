# Revathi, *Our Lives, Our Words: Telling Aravani Lifestories* (2011): reading note

Source read in full:

- A. Revathi, *Our Lives, Our Words: Telling Aravani Lifestories*, translated from Tamil by A. Mangai (New Delhi: Yoda Press, Sexualities series, ed. Gautam Bhan). ISBN 978-93-80403-06-9. Plain text `scratchpad/books/revathi_lives.txt`, 1,971 lines, kept outside the repo. Cited below as **R:line**.
- The brief gives the date as 2010. The imprint page says "First Published in 2011" and the copyright is 2011 (R:47, R:51). The Hindi edition came out in 2010 (R:119-121). The Tamil original, *Unarvum Uruvamum*, came out in 2005 (R:154, R:157).

**How the text file is laid out.** The extraction prints two book pages side by side, so most lines hold a left column and a right column from different pages. Line numbers below are for the column the text sits in. A range such as R:1845-1847 means "these lines, that column only". Quotes are verbatim, 25 words or fewer, and keep the translation's spelling (Revathi's narrators write *nirvanam*; the chapter title is "Niravanam").

**What kind of book this is.** It is not one narrator per chapter. Revathi interviewed many aravanis in Tamil Nadu and a few in Bengaluru (R:290-292). She then cut their accounts into short first-person pieces and arranged them under nine themes. The same narrators come back across chapters. The book does not say outright that a recurring name is always the same person, but the details agree (Aruna, Rajam, Santhi Amma). The three Kokila pieces are less certain. The narrators are published under these names, and this note uses them only to cite the text.

**Words.** The narrators call themselves *aravani* (the Tamil community word, R:158), *hijra* (most often in this translation), and sometimes *kothi*. Several are clear about how they want to be described, and they do not all say the same thing. See §3.1. This note uses she/her for every narrator. The book does too, except where a narrator quotes her family or a stranger misgendering her.

## 2. Why the stories were collected (the editors' own words)

- **Revathi.** The book began as a way out of personal collapse. Friends urged her to work for the community, and she undertook the study (R:275-282). Hearing others' stories changed how she saw her own. She found almost nothing in Tamil about hijra lives (R:283-286). She deliberately did not write academic research: "I have presented it in the form of sharing among ourselves of the joys and sorrows we encounter in our lives." (R:293-294). Some people asked who was paying her. She answers that she was paid, and that the point is to change stigma: "This book is to make society understand that it was no fault of ours but the mistake of society." (R:268-269, right). The community said a hijra would understand better, could hear what could not be told to men or women, and that the study would prove hijras' achievement (R:287-298). Writing also "resurrected" her (R:299-300).
- **Bhan.** The collection breaks with English writing in which hijras "have remained objects of discovery and description" (R:141-142). Because the community made it themselves, "these stories escape the trap of simply being testimonies to tales of violence, exclusion and eternal victimhood." (R:133-135). They are also about rights, citizenship and ethics: "The stories in this collection are stories of this izzat." (R:118, right).
- **Mangai.** Revathi "does not lay claim to a single authentic narrative, but asserts, instead, multiple strands that converge at various points while remaining distinct and disparate." (R:187-189). Mangai treats claiming the old myths as one of two strategies, not as history: "taking over the annual Koothandavar ritual as a sanctioning of the transgender presence was one mode." (R:180-181).

For the mod, the editors give one rule: **this is the community talking among themselves to change public attitudes.** It is not ethnography to be mined for exotic rites. Bhan's list of outsiders' stereotypes (R:109-117) is a list of what this note must not reproduce.

## 3. Themes across the stories

### 3.1 How the narrators describe themselves

- The narrators do not share one self-description, and the note should not invent one for them.
  - Revathi: "Recognize me as a woman and give me all the rights given to a woman." (R:250-251).
  - Rajam: "I married him as a woman. I am determined to be a woman." (R:1204-1205, left).
  - Roja to hospital staff: "'I am not a woman but a hijra.'" (R:809). Rati says the same to her lover (R:1310-1312).
  - The Ayah, of her own generation: "I live in the shape given to me. Whether you castrate or not, if you are a hijra, you are one!" (R:1481-1483).
  - Kokila on new names: "When we became hijras, we gave ourselves a new name." (R:1857-1858).
  - Vanitha calls herself a kothi and has chosen no surgery (R:809-815, right).
- The surgery is a turning point for some and not for others. Radha experienced it as becoming a woman, and her elders told her "it was not easy to become a woman." (R:1700). Kokila called it "a rebirth" (R:1715) and later regretted it. Sudha wants it but delays it for her family's sake. The Ayah and Vanitha reject it as the test of belonging. Sudha names the ranking of operated over unoperated as the community's own internal discrimination (R:1804-1806, right).
- Nobody in the book calls herself a "third gender" or a "third sex". The phrase does not appear. Revathi's framing is rights and recognition (R:241-251). This supports Bhatt's use of Revathi against the "venerated third sex" literature (`book_bhatt_third_sex.md`). Note: the Koovagam speech by Revathi that Bhatt quotes is **not** in this book.

### 3.2 Childhood, birth family and the double household

- **Childhood.** Every childhood account has girls' games, housework, plaits and flowers, and teasing, followed by the end of schooling. Aruna's "alloy" teacher (R:417-426) and Ranjitha leaving after the 6th standard (R:389-390) are typical. Several narrators were good students pushed out by ridicule, not by failure.
- **The family's violence is often about honour and caste.** Ranjitha's brother invokes their "respectable caste" (R:398-399). Sudha's father accuses her of spoiling "family honour and heritage" (R:557-558).
- **Inheritance is the key material fight.** Devi stays in male clothes for thirteen years to keep her share of the land. She says: "Though I am a hijra, I want a piece of land to stay in. I want it to be my own." (R:712-713, right). Sudha takes her father before a *panchayat* (R:583-611). Santhi Amma's uncle sold the family's land (R:864-866).
- **Money without love.** Vanitha: "One is not a son or a daughter because one is born as male or female." (R:838-839, right). And: "We give money and love. Once you receive the money, how come your affections vanish?" (R:841-842, right).
- **Two families.** Several narrators keep both their birth family and their community family. Santhi Amma did so from the day she chose her Guru (R:376-385). Aruna gives the reason: "In the family, we will be supported only as long as the parents are alive. But in the hijra community we would always be welcome." (R:1845-1847, left).
- **Acceptance can be won.** Kokila's place in her village (R:726-742) and Devi's harvest crew (R:714-730) show acceptance won through labour, levies and stubbornness, not granted.

### 3.3 Finding the community; Gurus and households

- People are found by looking: peanut sellers who notice a girl watching them (Santhi Amma, R:363-370), a kothi who explains that the community comes out at night (Aruna, R:477-485), a woman on a bridge (R:487-496).
- **The Guru as mother.** "Guru is the mother to all of us who leave behind our parents and family." (R:1500-1502, right). Adoption is public, with betel, flowers and a feast (R:372-377, R:1511-1519). Kin terms run through the household: Guru, chela, *gurubayi* (sister), *nani* (grandmother), daughter-in-law.
- **Self-governed law.** "These are not written down in any law book. But we live according to these rules." (R:1545-1546, right). The *jamaat* settles debts between Gurus and chelas (R:1524-1547). Santhi Amma: "It is not the government that gives approval. It is our own system." (R:870-871, right).
- **The household's hard edges.** Elders worked young chelas as servants (Ranjitha, R:1925-1942). Debts could double (R:1541-1543, right). The *jamaat* admits only those "attracted to men" (R:1541-1542, left). Sudha: "This kind of ordering-about hinders our struggle for human rights." (R:1775-1776, right). Ranjitha: "In those days, hijras were treated as bonded labour. Today, we are free individuals." (R:1925-1926, right).
- **The Ayah's temple lineage** (R:1430-1445). She says her household's adoption practice "came down to us from the devadasi practice." (R:1430, right). This is a 20th-century family memory, and the Ayah dates the break to the state's abolition of temple dancing (R:1485-1493). It is **not** evidence about Chola temple women; see §5.

### 3.4 The operation (*nirvanam*)

- The book covers both a traditional and a clinical operation in detail, plus aftercare, cost, secrecy and illegality (R:1783-1786, left; R:1754-1756, right).
- Radha's account is a religious rite under the goddess Mata, with forty days of seclusion and a closing procession to the sea (R:1566-1748).
- Kokila's is a hospital operation followed by botched aftercare and solitude (R:1713-1782).
- **For this note: content only, no detail is carried into the repo** (§6).

### 3.5 Work

- **Work is what the narrators talk about most.** Beedi and soap advertising, the *karagam* dance, film-song troupes, flower and vegetable selling, construction, a factory, a computer centre, harvest contracting, bathhouse work, domestic service in Mumbai households, *badhai* in the north, begging, sex work.
- **Pay and equal pay.** Santhi Amma: "We do demand equal pay." (R:935, right). Her ethic of the art: "Our focus should be on the art." (R:931, left).
- **The market sexualises performance.** Costumes shrank from ankle-length to bare thighs (R:933-940, left; R:908-913, right), and organisers ask for fair-skinned dancers (R:915-918, right). Rajini describes the same pressure (R:966-978).
- **Cost of living.** Higher rent, electricity and tailoring charges push people into sex work (Aruna, R:1829-1838, left). The state asks beggars for bank accounts (Santhi Amma, R:1873-1880).
- **Sex work** appears as survival, as family support, as something some narrators would quit, and as a dividing line inside the community. Ranjitha and Kokila want to leave it partly to gain acceptance (R:1943-1952, R:1850-1856). Aruna came to accept it as work (R:1053-1059). The book does not settle the question, and neither should the mod.

### 3.6 Violence

- From family: burning, beatings, poisoning, forced haircuts.
- From strangers and institutions: teachers, employers, supervisors, police, rowdies, hospital staff.
- From the state and property owners: a burned housing site (R:770-780), eviction from a village refuge (R:557-562).
- Sexual violence is recurrent and often begins in childhood. The book does not hide this. Bhan insists the stories "refuse to be limited by" it (R:135-136). See §6 for what the mod must not use.

### 3.7 Love and marriage

- **Love inside the heir economy.** A marriage is often tied with a *thali*, at a temple or in secret. Lovers' families want heirs, and men are married off. Gopal Amma arranges her partner's marriage herself and ends up raising his children: "For the children I am everything!" (R:1135-1136, left).
- **Domestic life is possible.** Rajam lives as a daughter-in-law and "Anni" in a joint family, and with her husband raises her brother's daughter (R:1199-1253, R:1365-1436).
- **Sudha's counter-view.** Marriage to a hijra rarely lasts, and pride in a *thali* covers that (R:1800-1806, left).
- Aruna's poem uses the image of jasmine flowers: "We blossomed as jasmine flowers—now / We lay strewn around on streets!" (R:1335-1336).

### 3.8 Religion and festivals

- **Many faiths, one shared rite.** The narrators practise mainstream devotion: Roja's Ayyappa vows (R:781-798), Sundari's temple dance debut (R:459-461), temple weddings, Rajam's vow of her *thali* to her husband's family deity (R:1231-1239), a *dargah* cure (R:1368-1369). On top of that sits the community's own rite. Sudha: "Pothraja Mata riding on a cock is the only Goddess of all hijras." (R:1784-1785, right). Hijras of Hindu, Muslim and Christian birth go to each other's shrines (R:1778-1785).
- **Koovagam** appears once, as one of the few places Sudha could wear a saree (R:546-549). The Koothandavar festival appears in Mangai's note as a strategy of claiming the myth (R:180-181). The book has no account of the festival itself.
- **Myth used for rights.** Revathi cites Ardhanareeswara ("In India, Ardhanareeswara, who is half male and half female, is worshipped.", R:238) and Shikhandi (R:240). She uses them to ask why such a country denies hijras rights. They are not sources of identity for her.
- **Golden-past claims.** Sudha says "In ancient times, hijras had been appointed as friends of queens." (R:1813-1814, left). Santhi Amma gives a *jamaat* origin under the Nawab of Arcot (R:1462-1484, right). These are the narrators' own oral histories. Bhatt warns against building on golden pasts. The mod must not adopt them as history.

### 3.9 Activism and change

- **Organising.** Theni's first protest and press work (R:557-573), the southern-districts rights centre and its self-help groups (R:1790-1797), associations, a charter of demands, Vellore's welfare gains (R:1965-1969).
- **What they ask for is material.** Votes, ration cards, houses, loans, jobs that match their qualifications, property, marriage rights. Revathi's opening poem: "Property we ought to get—sirs / Pleasures we must have / House we need—sirs / And jobs we need." (R:203-206).
- **A community that rejects some divisions but keeps others.** Sudha says there are no caste or communal riots among hijras, but the operated are ranked above the unoperated (R:1797-1807, right).
- **Change is generational.** The young push against the old customs (R:1769-1777). Vanitha ends the book with: "This is just the beginning. We have miles to go." (R:1968-1969).

## 4. Relation to Bhatt and to the mod's existing reading

- **The book supports Bhatt's critique from inside the community.** None of the narrators claims "veneration". What they describe is begging, sex work, rent premiums, bonded household labour and violence. What they ask for is land, houses, jobs and recognition, and Revathi asks for recognition as a woman. Ranjitha's "bonded labour" (R:1925) and Santhi Amma's account of migrating north for *badhai* because there was no other living at home (R:873-888, R:1504-1520) make the same point as Bhatt: what outsiders record as "reverence" is marginalisation.
- **The book also complicates any single line.** Some narrators say they are hijras and not women, and the Ayah's generation saw no need for surgery. Bhatt's argument is against outsiders (Nanda and those who followed) who romanticise the category. It is not a licence for the mod to settle the narrators' self-descriptions for them. The mod simply does not depict this community.
- **The book supports the restrictions already in `south_reading_list.md`.** "Do not connect the faith to hijras, Bahuchara or Koovagam", and "Do not make the temple women into trans women". The Ayah's temple lineage is exactly the kind of tempting bridge that rule exists to block.

## 5. For the mod

**Ground rule.** Mālai (`tm_garland`) never presents the aravani/hijra community, its households (*jamaat*, the seven houses, Guru-chela lineages), its rites (*madikattuthal*, *nirvanam* and its forty days, the milk-pouring, *kappu*, *haldi mehndi*, *paal ootruthal*), its festivals (Koovagam, the Koothandavar festival) or its deities (Mata, Pothraja Mata, Padavettu Amman) as one of the mod's faiths. It never moves any of them into a medieval faith as if they were historical. This book informs Mālai at the level of **themes only**. Mālai's forms stay where they come from: Āṇṭāḷ and the Āḻvārs, the Mārkaḻi vow, and the Chola temple women as read in Orr (`ind_orr_temple_women.md`).

### 5.1 What it can inform (themes only)

1. **The material stakes.** Mālai's line already settles each daughter on a share: land in her own name, inherited down a line of taken daughters. The narrators' fights for a piece of land, a roof and a share of the inheritance (Devi, Sudha, Revathi's poem) confirm that this is the right thing to make central. Write the share as what protects a daughter, and not her devotion or any "honour" granted to her. Event flavour can come from the general pattern: a birth family that takes a daughter's labour and money and then gives the land to a brother. No narrator's story should be retold.
2. **Two households.** A daughter of Mālai can keep ties to her birth kin and her taken kin, and the two pull on her. The birth family's support ends when the parents die, and the taken line lasts (R:1845-1849). This fits "Take as Daughter" and could be used for opinion modifiers or an event about a dying parent.
3. **The taken household's hard edges.** Mālai's elders should not be idealised. The book's tension between generations (elders' authority and debts against the young's demand for rights) can shape a dissent voice or event chain inside the faith: a young daughter questions an elder's claim on her labour. Keep it generic and set in the temple-street world, without Guru, chela or jamaat terms.
4. **An internal hierarchy to write against.** Sudha names the ranking of the operated over the unoperated (R:1804-1806). Mālai has a bodily gate (the dream wedding). Its dissenters, or hostile voices, should be able to say that the faith ranks the re-embodied above those still keeping the vow, and the faith should have to answer. This sits alongside the "one Lord and all the rest brides" risk already written into the design.
5. **Performance as work.** Santhi Amma and Rajini show sacred and festival dance becoming labour, and then being pushed into sexualised display. Mālai's *araiyar* and festival performance should be written as skilled, paid work with a craft ethic ("Our focus should be on the art"), not as eroticised spectacle. This matches the existing rule "do not sexualise the temple street".
6. **Marriage and the heir economy.** Lovers married off for heirs, and a woman raising her partner's children, are generic medieval pressures. They support Mālai's rule that a daughter does not depend on a mortal husband for security. Any romance event between a daughter and a mortal man should carry the risk that his family takes him back for an heir.
7. **Neighbours across faiths.** People of several faiths sharing shrines, and cures sought at a *dargah*, support a tolerant, low-zeal stance toward neighbouring faiths at the level of individual characters. Flavour only.
8. **Against veneration.** Use the book, together with Bhatt, to keep Mālai's daughters as donors and owners, never alms-takers or bearers of blessings and curses. The stereotypes in Bhan's list (R:109-117) are the faith's opponents' slanders at most. Even then, see "Must not".

### 5.2 Must not

- **No aravani/hijra faith, and no transplanting.** None of the community, its households, rites, festivals or deities becomes a mod faith, doctrine, holy site, tenet, decision or event. Nothing from them is given to Mālai or to a medieval character as if it were historical. Koovagam and the Koothandavar festival are modern as trans festivals (`south_reading_list.md`, section 1, finding 4) and stay out.
- **No narrator becomes a character.** No game character, event, name-list entry or loc line is built from an identifiable narrator or from her story. That includes Revathi herself, the Ayah, and the named Gurus and elders (Jayamma, Anippamma, Krishnaveni Ayah and others). Do not add narrators' names to name lists because of this book.
- **No "third gender".** No third-sex framing, no "venerated" or "sacred third" language. No blessing-and-curse power, no clap, no child-stealing, no exposure-as-shaming, even as hostile reaction text, since these are stereotypes still used against a living community. Follow the narrators' own words, and where they differ, do not settle the question for them.
- **No surgery content from this book.** The *nirvanam* accounts (R:1566-1782) are not sources for any mechanic, event, rite or description. Mālai's gate stays the Mārkaḻi vow and the dream wedding.
- **No suicide or self-harm.** This is a mod rule. The material listed in §6 is off-limits, and so are paraphrases of it.
- **No sexual violence or child abuse from this book**, in any form.
- **No sexualising.** Not sex work, not dance, not the temple street. Do not use the word "devadasi" in loc (see `ind_merkle_tying_knot.md`). Do not use the Ayah's temple lineage to suggest that Chola temple women had trans daughters or were trans.
- **No slurs.** The insults the narrators report (*ompathu*/"number nine", *pottai*, *chakka*, *katta vandi*, *kochavadu*, "alloy") do not go into loc, including hostile reaction text.
- **No golden pasts.** Do not treat "friends of queens" or the Arcot *jamaat* origin as history.
- **No copied text.** Nothing from the book goes into the repo beyond the short quotes in this note. The poems (R:193-206, R:1335-1343, the riddle at R:366-377) are not adapted into loc.
