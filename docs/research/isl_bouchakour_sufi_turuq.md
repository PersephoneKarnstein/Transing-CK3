## 1. Claims, as the article states them (unverified)

- **Sources of Maghrebi Sufism.** Three streams (the Mashriq, al-Andalus and the Far Maghreb) met local cults of saints, called in Amazigh *ighurramen* (p. 626).
- **Organised orders** (*ṭuruq*) took shape by the 7th/13th century, with communal life "within ribāṭs (Sufi lodges) and zāwiyas" (p. 627).
- **Abū Madyan** set up a school at Béjaïa under the Almohads. The caliph's jurists warned that he "bears a resemblance to Imam al-Mahdi". He was summoned to Marrakesh and died near Tlemcen before arriving (p. 627). A thousand disciples are said to have become masters (p. 627). (His death in 594/1198 is general knowledge.)
- **Shādhiliyya and Qādiriyya** were the two main orders of the central Maghreb before the Ottomans (p. 628). Ottoman patronage spread the Qādiriyya (p. 628).
- **Shaykh, disciple, covenant.** The shaykh was called *walī Allāh*, "master of the age" or "lord of the hour", and credited with bringing rain and warding off calamity (p. 629). The disciple (*murīd*) owed obedience "like a corpse in the hands of its washer" (p. 630). The covenant (*ʿahd*) was modelled on the oath of allegiance (*bayʿa*) (p. 630). Disciples could be mobilised for jihad and frontier defence (p. 629).
- **Ribāṭ.** The root means "to tie" (Q 8:60). Jurists read ribāṭ as frontier guard, Sufis as a place of worship (pp. 630–631). It lists coastal ribāṭs: Monastir, Sousse, Sfax, Bizerte, Bône, Cherchell, Marsā al-Maghīla, Arzew, Nekor and Ribāṭ al-Fatḥ. It says they peaked under the Aghlabids (800–909) (p. 631). Fatimid–Mālikī conflict turned *murābaṭa* towards Sufism. Frankish threats revived the armed role under the Almoravids and Almohads (p. 631).
- **Rulers gave land.** "Authorities … allocat[ed] high-yield agricultural land to the murābiṭūn" (p. 631).
- **Zāwiya.** After the Almohads fell, zāwiyas replaced ribāṭs in the central Maghreb (p. 632). The first was Abū Zakariyyāʾ al-Zawāwī's at Béjaïa, after *ʿUnwān al-Dirāya* (written 1291–1305) (p. 632). Three types: simple (one founder; teaching), saint-based (round a tomb; grew into towns), and order-based ("often built by ruling authorities") (p. 632).
- **Official zāwiyas.** From the 14th century the Ḥafṣids, Zayyānids and Marīnids sponsored zāwiyas to gain popular legitimacy (p. 632). The zāwiya of Ibrāhīm al-Tāzī at Oran (866/1462) is its example (p. 633). In the 15th century, without central authority, "the murabitun mobilized donations and called for jihad" (p. 632).
