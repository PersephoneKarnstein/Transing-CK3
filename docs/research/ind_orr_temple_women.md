## 1. The argument in brief

- **Who counts.** Orr reads every Tamil inscription of 850–1300 that she could find. Of 820 in her dataset, 304 refer to temple women (p. 34). A temple woman bears one of four terms, has a function in or support from a temple, or is "of the temple" (p. 37).
- **What they did.** Their main recorded role is *donor*, not dancer. 133 of the 304 inscriptions record their gifts (p. 66). Temple men almost never give: only 3 of 219 named temple men in her sample areas are donors, against 42% of named temple women (pp. 66–67, 95).
- **The trend.** Other women fade from the record after c. 1050: queens and palace women vanish, and women's property is increasingly handled by male agents. Temple women go the other way. They become more numerous, more often named, and richer in the 12th–13th centuries (pp. 46, 67–73, 161–163).
- **Why.** Most women got property through marriage. Temple women did not marry, so their wealth stayed under their control (pp. 72–73).
- **Not dancers, not wives of God.** Temple service was optional, vague and mostly honorary. No task was exclusively female, and priest and drummer were closed to women (pp. 93–94, 161–162). "Marriage to God" is unknown before a 17th-century Suchindram manuscript (pp. 73, 156). The "wives of God" image probably comes from Nāyaka-era court culture and "the male sexual imagination" (pp. 176–178).
- **Where.** They thrive at medium-sized temples, in the northern and southern regions, under non-Chola kings, and where royal power is weak. Rājarāja's 400 at Thanjavur were "exceptional, extravagant—and ultimately unsuccessful" (pp. 80–82, 161, 163–164).
- **After 1300.** Under the Pāṇḍyas and Vijayanagara, temple women stop giving. Kings pour wealth into temples and crowd out local patrons. Temple women become passive receivers of rank, house, land and "turns" (pp. 174–177).

## 2. What matters for the design

### 2.1 The terms: "daughter of God" checked (pp. 37, 47–62, 145–148, 161–162)

- **The four terms** are *tevaratiyal* / *tevaratiyar* ("devotee of God"), *tevanar makal* ("daughter of God"), and *patiyilar* / *taliyilar* ("those of the temple", "temple woman") (p. 37). Synonyms of the first include *emperumanatiyal* and *alvanatiyal*, both "devotee of the Lord" (p. 51). A Vaiṣṇava temple woman at Kanchipuram is *avvalvanatiyal*, "devotee of that Lord" (SII 4.134, 1118; p. 138).
- **"Daughter of God" is real, but early and Śaiva.**
  - *Tevanar makal* appears in 24 inscriptions. Seventeen of them fall in 850–985, five in 985–1070, and only one each in 1070–1178 and 1178–1300 (table 2.5, p. 52).
  - It is used most in Thanjavur, Tiruchirappalli and North Arcot districts (p. 57), and clusters at Tiruvidaimarudur, Tiruchatturai and Takkolam (p. 58).
  - "All the *tevanar makals* and *makans* ... appear to be associated with temples dedicated to Siva" (p. 59). Orr links the term to the Śaiva poets, who address God as father more than as lover (pp. 58–59).
  - In SII 2.66 itself the women are *talicceri pentukal*, "women of the temple quarter" (Orr's translation, p. 33). This confirms the Tanjavur note.
- **There was a "son of God" too.** The masculine *tevanar makan* is attested in the early Chola period, though less often (p. 57). At Tiruvidaimarudur, all the temple servants, men and one *tevanar makal*, bore the name Nakkan (SII 19.92A; p. 146).
- **"Devotee" became a woman's word.** *Tevaratiyar* began as a mixed, plural "devotees" that often hid gender (p. 51). By 1178–1300, 91% of its uses name individual women (p. 55). Men with status were renamed *mahesvara*, *sivabrahmana* or *srivaisnava*, groups that excluded women (pp. 55–56).
- **The term is honorific, not a matter of parentage.** Orr rejects the claim that temple women were "daughters of God" because they had no known father (p. 58). The term is a "marker of status", tied to gifts (pp. 57, 62).
- **Titles.**
  - *Talaikkoli*, "she of the illustrious staff", was borne only by temple women and often joined to a god's name (pp. 60–61).
  - *Manikkam* ("ruby") titles were common among temple women after c. 1070 (pp. 147–148).
  - *Nakkan*, a name of Śiva, was probably conferred by some temples and carried the "daughter of God" status. Eight of the nineteen named *tevanar makals* bear it (pp. 145–147).
  - There is no evidence that names were given in a rite (p. 148). Formal name-giving appears only from the 15th century (p. 148).

### 2.2 Gifts, land and "deals" (pp. 17, 56, 65–87, 151)

- **Gifts.** Temple women's gifts were the same kinds as other donors': lamps, food offerings, land, gold, cattle and images (pp. 65–66).
  - Pillaiyar Cirritai Arivai bought five plots from four people, including a Brahman woman, to make a flower garden for the goddess at Chidambaram (SII 12.151, c. 1250; pp. 69–70).
- **Deals.** 21 inscriptions, all from 1070–1300, record a temple woman's gift exchanged for honors (pp. 74–75): a place near the god in procession, the right to wave the flywhisk, consecrated food, or the right to sing a set verse of a hymn. Ten of these grants are hereditary, "to her descendants" (*santanapravesam*, *varkattar*) (pp. 75, 151). Men made only six deals, and they mostly sought paid positions (pp. 75–76).
- **Houses and shares.**
  - A woman received "the first house in the temple quarter" and precedence in festivals, for her and her descendants, in return for gold (ARE 29 of 1940–41; p. 151).
  - Nakkan Centalvi built and endowed a goddess shrine and in return received shares (*panku*) of temple property from the priests (ARE 468 of 1916; p. 60).
- **Goddess shrines.** Temple women often set up a goddess, *Nacciyar*, beside the god:
  - the "Queen of the Bedchamber" (SITI 118, 1119; p. 17);
  - a *Nacciyar* (NK 134, 1213; p. 17);
  - a *Nacciyar* at Cholapuram (TAS 6.15, 1252; p. 74).

  Consort-goddess shrines spread especially in the 13th century (p. 24).
- **Limits.** Temple women could not buy or sell temple service rights as men did (pp. 76–77). Rights did not pass from aunt to niece (p. 77).

### 2.3 Inheritance, daughters, adoption (pp. 72, 82, 148–158; ch. 3 n. 28, p. 228)

- **Maternal inheritance is hedged.** Orr writes that temple women "thus *may* have acquired property through inheritance from their mothers" (p. 72). The reading list's wording, via Ali, is firmer than Orr's.
- **Status was rarely inherited.** Only three cases show a mother and daughter with the same title. One is Kumarati-nankai at Takkolam, daughter of a *tevanar makal*, who is called *tevanar makal* herself thirty years later (pp. 149–150). Orr concludes that inheritance of status was not required (p. 150).
- **Female-led families.** Thirty inscriptions name women as "daughter of" a temple woman, and eight name sons or grandsons. In 83% of the cases where a temple woman appears as someone's relative, the relationship is through the mother, and no father is ever named (p. 156). This pattern grows in the 13th century, mainly in the north (pp. 153, 158).
  - When temple women passed on the merit of a gift, it went to daughters (twice) or a mother (once), never to men (p. 82).
  - Their co-donors are mostly other temple women or their sisters (p. 83).
- **Adoption is not attested before 1300.** Orr speculates that in the 13th century temple women "on occasion adopted daughters as their successors when they had no natural daughters" (ch. 3 n. 28, p. 228). This is her guess. Attested adoption comes from 19th–20th-century ethnography: girls adopted into devadāsī families were usually close kin (p. 8; ch. 1 n. 23, p. 197; p. 133).
- **Some were married.** Two *tevanar makals* were queens, one of a Chera prince and one of Rājarāja I (SII 13.153–154; ARE 385 of 1924; pp. 41, 154). Other temple women are called wives (pp. 154–155). One was the companion (*anukki*) of a chief (SII 22.87; p. 155). A firm "unmarried" definition forms only in the later Chola period (p. 155).

### 2.4 Ritual roles and the Mārkaḻi hymn (pp. 17–18, 56, 104–115)

- **Temple women sang the Mārkaḻi vow-song, and it was the Śaiva one.**
  - At Tirupampuram in 1213, a temple woman who set up images received an early-morning turn, a place at the hunting festival, and the singing of "the seventh verse of *Tiruvempavai* at the *Tiruvatirai* festival in the month of Markali", for her and her descendants (NK 134; pp. 17–18).
  - At Nallur (12th century), *tevaratiyar* paid gold for the right to sing set portions of *Tiruvempāvai* and to dance at festivals (ARE 143, 144, 149, 160, 161, 176 of 1940–41; pp. 56, 105, 107–108).
  - At Tiruvorriyur (11th century), sixteen *tevaratiyar* were charged with its performance (ARE 128 of 1912; p. 108).
  - *Tiruvempāvai* is "the only specific hymn mentioned by name as part of the temple liturgy to be performed by female singers", perhaps because it is "cast in the feminine voice" (p. 108).
- **The Vaiṣṇava hymns were men's.** "*Tiruvaymoli* ... seem[s] to have been considered as suitable only for male performers" (p. 108). In twelve inscriptions on singing it, four name men, eight are unclear, and none names a woman (ch. 4 n. 23, pp. 236–237). Hymn singing narrowed toward Brahmans and new professional groups in the 13th century (p. 109).
- **Dance.** Men danced at festivals in the early period. Women displaced them after 1070, often by buying the right (pp. 104–106).
- **Garlands.**
  - Temple women wove garlands in only five inscriptions, mostly from the 11th century. One is at a Viṣṇu temple: *tevaratiyar* "who carry sacred lamps and weave garlands" at Rajendracola-vinnakar, Mannarkoyil, in 1025 (SII 14.132; pp. 114–115).
  - Garland making later passed to high-status men, including *srivaisnavas* (p. 115).
  - Orr notes that the hagiographies making Periyāḻvār a garland maker "may" reflect late-Chola developments (ch. 4 n. 35, pp. 239–240).
  - In the 15th century, a temple woman's honors included land, a house, the first "turn", and the right to receive one of the garlands offered to the god (IPS 710; p. 175).

### 2.5 Men in comparable roles (pp. 31, 34, 51, 57, 66, 93, 111–113, 137–138)

- Orr builds a "temple men" comparison group (p. 34). It is a mixed group with no shared term (p. 66).
- **Men in roles women also held:**
  - Men also waved lamps (SII 3.149; p. 113).
  - At Srirangam, a man was appointed to flywhisk service in the late 11th century (SII 24.66; p. 111).
  - Men did most garland work (p. 114).
- **Sons of temple women.** In 13th-century northern temples, temple women's sons appear in the *kaikkolar* community (pp. 157, 163).
- **No crossing.** No man appears under a woman's term, and no man appears living as a temple woman. The nearest overlaps are:
  - the gender-blind plural *tevaratiyar* of the early period (p. 51);
  - the parallel title "son of God" (p. 57).

### 2.6 Srirangam and the "centers" (pp. 81, 139–143, 161)

- **Srirangam is not a temple-woman site.**
  - In Orr's sample, Srirangam (a "type II" large temple) received no gifts from temple women (p. 81).
  - "Women are conspicuous by their absence" from the long lists of Srirangam's functionaries (pp. 142–143).
  - The only link is a woman "of Tiruvarankam" who gave a lamp at Tiruverumbur in 875 (SII 13.88; pp. 65, 142).
  - Chidambaram is the same (p. 142).
- **The real "temple-woman centers"** are:
  - Kanchipuram, the only one attested throughout the period. It includes the Vaiṣṇava Vaikuntha Perumal temple (pp. 139–140).
  - Tiruvarur and Tiruvidaimarudur (pp. 140–141).
  - Takkolam, Tiruvannamalai, and Tiruvalisvaram in Tirunelveli (p. 142).
- **What Thanjavur did.** Rājarāja's transfer may have emptied nearby temples of temple women for a century (pp. 141–142).

### 2.7 The woman who marries God (ch. 6 n. 11, pp. 258–259)

- Āṇṭāḷ's 9th-century verses and the late-Chola hagiographies do speak of a human woman marrying the Lord.
- But in South Indian myth such a woman either is a consort-goddess incarnate, or "she dies or disappears by merging into the image of the god."
- "We do not find a model here for human women being transformed through marriage into goddesses while continuing to dwell in the world."

## 3. Corrections to the candidate

1. **"Daughter of God" is a 9th–10th-century Śaiva honorific.** It is not a Vaiṣṇava or Srirangam title, and it is almost gone by Rājarāja's time (table 2.5, pp. 52, 57–59).
   - The faith may adopt it in its own voice.
   - The narrator should say it came from Śiva's temples of the 10th century.
   - For a Vaiṣṇava house, *āḻvāṉ-aṭiyāḷ*, "devotee of the Lord", is the attested Vaiṣṇava-context form (*avvalvanatiyal*, p. 138). *Tēvaraṭiyāḷ* is the commonest form in every period after 1070.
2. **Keep the temple women and the bride apart.** Temple women were not brides of God, and "marriage to God" is 17th-century (pp. 73, 156, 176–178). The Garland's bridal identity must come from Āṇṭāḷ's poems, not from the temple street. The line borrows the temple women's *property and gifts*, not a bridal status.
3. **"She does not marry mortals" is a later-Chola tendency, not a rule.** Two early "daughters of God" were queens (pp. 41, 154–155). If the line forbids marriage, say it is the Garland's own rule.
4. **The mother-to-daughter line is weak; adoption is speculation.** Orr's wording is "may have" (p. 72). Titles were rarely inherited (pp. 149–150). Adoption is Orr's guess (p. 228). Grade "Take as Daughter" as *reconstruction*, built on the female-led families of 13th-century northern Tamil country (pp. 153–158).
5. **Shares came through gifts.** The attested 13th-century way to a hereditary share, house or honor is a gift to the temple, granted "to her and her descendants" (pp. 75, 151). Rājarāja's royal settlement is the exception, and it failed (pp. 161, 163).
6. **Srirangam is a weak temple-street seat** (pp. 81, 142–143).
   - Keep it as the Garland's seat for Āṇṭāḷ's sake (see the Venkatesan note).
   - The temple-street line belongs to Kanchipuram first, then Tiruvarur and Tiruvidaimarudur.
   - The c_kanchipuram holy-site text should carry the temple women.
7. **The Mārkaḻi vow-song temple women sang was *Tiruvempāvai*, not the *Tiruppāvai*** (pp. 17–18, 108). The *Tiruvāymoḻi* was men's (p. 108, n. 23).
8. **Era arc.** The line peaks at 1070–1300 under Pāṇḍya and other non-Chola kings, not at the Chola zenith of 985–1070. That middle subperiod is the low point (pp. 42–46, 163–164). The decline begins after 1300, when temple women stop giving (pp. 174–177). This fits the candidate's "decline under Vijayanagara", but it starts earlier and has a different cause: royal patronage crowding out local patronage.
9. **No man in a temple woman's role.** The only ambiguity is the early plural "devotees" (p. 51). A seeker joining the street is pure reconstruction.

## 4. Design implications

**Line ("the temple street").**
- **"Give to the Home Temple" (decision).** Gold or land to a medium temple in her home county, with the gift engraved. If she is already "of the temple", this can buy an honor:
  - a verse of the vow-song;
  - the flywhisk in procession;
  - a place near the god;
  - "first house in the temple quarter".

  Honors are hereditary "to her descendants" (pp. 75, 151). Royal temples and the largest shrines give less, because temple women's gifts went to medium temples (pp. 80–82).
- **"Set Up the Lady."** Endow a consort-goddess (*nācciyār*) shrine. The temple grants a share and a title (*talaikkoli*-style, "she of the illustrious staff of the Lord of X") (pp. 17, 60–61, 74).
- **Succession.** Shares and honors pass to her daughter or chosen daughter. Faith text: "to her and her descendants" (Orr's *santanapravesam*). Narrator note: adoption by temple women is a later practice, and the Garland claims it early.
- **Royal transfer action.** Keep it, but tag it as a one-off royal gesture whose temple women "did not stay" (pp. 141, 161). It works better as a prestige spike for the king than as a lasting House.
- **Era modifiers.**
  - Before 1070: few women's records, and royal women dominate giving.
  - 1070–1300: the street at its strongest, in the north and far south and under non-Chola rulers.
  - After 1300: "rank without gifts", with a growing tax on the line's autonomy.

**Gate tie-in.** One step can be "buy a verse": pay the temple for the right to sing a verse of the vow-song in Mārkaḻi. That is NK 134 and the Nallur deals turned into a mechanic (pp. 17–18, 107–108).

**Holy-site text (narrator voice, true to Orr).**
- c_kanchipuram: "The women who called themselves devotees of the Lord gave lamps and gardens here in every century of the Cholas."
- c_cholamandalam: keep the Tanjavur note's line, and add: "the settlement did not last."

**Reaction lines.**
- A Śaiva priest: "Daughters of God were Śiva's daughters before your Lord ever had brides."
- A dissenter in the faith: "The women of the street never called themselves his wives. Why do we?" The second follows Orr, pp. 58, 156.

**Do not.**
- Make the temple women trans.
- Call them dancing girls or brides of God.
- Use the slave-sale records as the line's origin.
- Present devadāsī-descended communities as the faith.
