# Khan, "Khwaja Sira Activism" (TSQ 2016): design reading

**Citation.** Faris A. Khan, "Khwaja Sira Activism: The Politics of Gender Ambiguity in Pakistan," *TSQ: Transgender Studies Quarterly* 3, nos. 1–2 (May 2016): 158–164. DOI 10.1215/23289252-3334331.

**What was read.** The whole essay, notes and references included. Page numbers are the journal's. The people and the organisation in it are pseudonyms (n. 1, p. 164); this note quotes none of them and paraphrases what they said.

**Hard rule.** Khwaja siras are a living community. Nothing here becomes a faith, a rite or an in-game group.

---

## 1. Relation to existing notes

**It disagrees with Bhatt.** Khan reads the activists' refusal to be categorised as "genderqueer feminist" resistance. He argues that "the act of sustaining ambiguity may be interpreted as a radical politics of unbecoming" (p. 163). He also says that calling khwaja siras "trans" may be "an imperialist imposition" (n. 2, p. 164). That is the Gill-Peterson position Bhatt attacks ("hijras were not then ... transgender", `book_bhatt_third_sex.md` §1). Celebrating ambiguity as the goal is close to the "pure gender disruption" that Bhatt says Nanda pedestals over transition.

**Bhatt's argument covers its legal subject.** Between 2009 and 2012, Pakistan's Supreme Court recognised khwaja sira as a sex/gender beside male and female, with ID-card options (p. 159). That is the Pakistani counterpart of the NALSA "third gender" ruling Bhatt calls anthropology turned into law. Khan reports it without criticism.

**One point where the facts fit Bhatt.** Khan notes that the public widely believes khwaja siras are intersex, alongside a suspicion that they are emasculated men (p. 159). That is the "dumping-ground" reading of the third sex in Bhatt's chapter.

**History.** Khan says *khwaja sira* was the title of the chief eunuch at the Mughal court, revived in the 2000s as a respectable label to replace the slur *hijra* (pp. 158–159). Post-1453 and outside the map's interest. Nothing for the Adelphai's Byzantine eunuch material.

## 2. Argument in brief

- On a 2011 Karachi talk show, a host asked activists whom they desire and what their bodies are. They answered in circles, about "love" for parents, friends, everyone, and about the "soul" (pp. 160–161).
- Afterwards one activist gave two reasons: one does not discuss sex on television in an Islamic country, and minorities should not have to disclose what ordinary men and women never do (p. 162, paraphrased).
- Khan reads this as a "culturally informed" tactic of resistance to surveillance and categorisation (p. 162), and then, in his own "supplementary" reading, as a model for a genderqueer politics that abjures categories altogether (pp. 162–163).

## 3. What is usable

One small thing, as modern-theory framing only.

- **Refusing the question.** The activist's second reason is a general principle: a stigmatised person need not answer questions about her body that no one asks of anyone else. Bhatt's ch. 3 makes the same point from the other side: "Have you had the surgery?" is the regime's question (`book_bhatt_brown_trans_les.md` §2.3). A refusal option in hostile-questioning events fits both. It borrows nothing of khwaja sira identity or practice.

Not usable: ambiguity as a goal. The mod's daughters are women and the faiths say so.

## 5. Cautions

- The activists' words are tactics for a live television audience in a specific legal moment. Do not turn them into in-world lore or a faith's doctrine.
- Khan's genderqueer reading is his, and he says it is supplementary to what the activists themselves said (p. 163). If cited, keep the two apart.
- Do not use *khwaja sira*, *khunsa*, *zennana* or *hijra* as names, titles or traits anywhere in the mod.
