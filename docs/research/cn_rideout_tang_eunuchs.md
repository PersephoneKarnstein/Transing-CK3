**Coverage warning, read first.** Despite its title, Part II is the unfinished second instalment. It covers only **697–730**: the end of Wu Zetian's reign, Zhongzong, the 710 and 713 coups, and Kao Li-shih's rise to 730. Rideout never reached the Shence (Divine Strategy) Armies, the eunuch Palace Commissioners, the Sweet Dew Incident (835), the eunuch kingmakers of 859–888, or the 903 massacre. **Nothing in either article covers the CK3 window (867–907).** For that, see So Wai-man 2000 (separate note) and Hoeckelmann (`east_tang_ming_goryeo.md`, source A). Section 1.6 below gives a short background so the design table makes sense. It is not from Rideout and is marked for checking.

## 1. The argument

### 1.1 Thesis
- Rideout asks how "this relatively small body, drawn mainly from the aboriginal inhabitants of Southern China and equipped with little or no education" came to dominate the court (I p. 53). His answer is social and institutional, not moral. The Confucian historians attribute the eunuchs' rise to the moral failings of later emperors. Rideout instead blames the breakdown of the equal-field land system, factional ministers, the informer regime of Empress Wu, and Zhongzong's sale of offices. These, he argues, "presented to eunuchs the opportunities" (I p. 54).
- He names the historians' bias: they were divided from eunuchs "not merely by political rivalry, but also by differences of education, religion, and place of birth" (I p. 54). In the early Tang, eunuchs appear in the record mostly when an official prosecuted them.
- The historians' image of eunuchs: "the rats beneath the floor" (I p. 53). Chang Yüeh later calls his own rivals "those rats" (II p. 55).

### 1.2 Sources of supply (the register question)
- **Penal castration came first.** After a treason conviction, males over fourteen were executed and those under it "castrated and sent to serve as eunuchs ... wherein the female members were also enclosed as concubines or slaves" (I p. 54). Western Wei abolished the punishment in 547, and Sui Wendi abolished it generally around 582. Rideout's note quotes Kong Yingda's commentary: castration was abolished, "but females were still enclosed in the harem" (I p. 65 n. 2). The punishment was *gongxing* 宮刑, "palace punishment". **Its male form was the knife and its female form was enclosure in the palace.** This is the strongest hook in the article for Rectified Names (see §2).
- **After abolition came raiding.** Once castration "was now considered an indignity to which no male of Chinese birth should be submitted", the Sui raided the southern and south-western "aboriginal tribes" for boys. Prince Xiu of Shu did so in 602 (I pp. 54–55).
- **Tribute.** Under the Tang, "provincial officials ... send to the capital a quota of castrated boys as part of their annual tribute" (I p. 55). Kao Li-shih [Gao Lishi], born a Feng of Panzhou in Lingnan, was presented in 698 by the Lingnan commissioner Li Qianli (I p. 55).
- **The slave market and sale by parents.** Officials found "an easier source of supply in the slave markets" of Guangdong and Fujian (I p. 55). "The great majority of eunuchs had originally been sold voluntarily by their parents to the officials" (I p. 55). A 749 edict ordered eunuchs in private households to be handed to the Neishisheng within five days. Edicts from 779 onward tried to stop the southern slave trade. Liu Zongyuan, Han Yu and others redeemed slaves at their own cost (I p. 55; n. 6 gives 200+ in 806–13, 1,000+ in 815, 700+ in 820).
- **By 846–859 every circuit (tao 道) paid an annual tribute of castrated boys** "known as Szu Pai" (*sibai* 私白), mostly from Lingnan and Min (Fujian), "the latter being known as the eunuch nursery" (I p. 55). Rideout's source is the *Xin Tang shu* biography of Tutu Chengcui (I p. 66 n. 7). The standard text's phrase is 閩為中官區藪, "Min is the breeding-ground of palace officers". That phrase comes from the received text and is not in the OCR, so check it before quoting.
- *Sibai* in Rideout's reading: 私置白身, "privately kept [eunuchs] without grade". An 826 edict forbade officials and governors to keep them (I p. 66 n. 7). *Baishen* 白身 is the register's term for an ungraded eunuch. In 820 the Neishisheng register counted 4,618 eunuchs as *gaopin* 高品, *pinguan* 品官 or *baishen* (I p. 66 n. 7).
- **A primary text usable in game: Gu Kuang's poem** 囝 (*jian*, c. 757 jinshi), translated in I p. 66 n. 7: "The son was born in Min: an officer of Min having got him deprived him of his manhood." The father at parting says, "I regret that I ever begat you". The son: "until I come to the Yellow Spring, I shall be unable to stand before you as my father." In the received Chinese the cut is 絕其陽, "cut off his yang". This is from the standard text, not the OCR; verify. Note that the poem frames the cut as the loss of the male principle and of the father–son bond. It does not make him a woman.
- Sulayman the merchant (c. 851) reports both foreign captives and Chinese boys "que les parents eux-mêmes ont mutilés pour les offrir au souverain" (I p. 55).
- Adult castrations were rare. Two examples: a pipa master, Lo Heihei, whose "name shows him to be of non-Chinese origin", castrated under Taizong and sent to teach the harem ladies, and An Lushan castrating his Khitan servant Li Zhu'er "with his own knife" (I pp. 67–68 n. 8).

### 1.3 Upbringing, adoption, marriage
- **Adoption was built into intake.** Boys arriving at court "were put under the charge of senior eunuchs, by whom they were formally adopted, and whose family names they assumed". This was "a necessary exception to the law, which allowed foster parents to adopt only children of the same family name" (I p. 55).
- **Eunuchs as householders.** By Wu's reign (684–705) eunuchs "had begun to take to themselves wives". Under Xuanzong they left the palace "for large mansions and landed estates" and adopted "ordinary children of both sexes to whom they could bequeath titles and possessions" (I p. 56).
- **The adoption chains are concrete.** Kao Li-shih was adopted by "the eunuch Kao Yen-Fu and his wife", a eunuch married couple adopting (I p. 68 n. 9; Chang Yüeh's 725 epitaph for Kao Yen-fu). Kao Li-shih in turn adopted several people, **including a daughter, "who in 781 falsely claimed to be the Empress Dowager"**. She was exposed by her younger brother and by Kao Li-shih's adopted grandson Fan Jingchao (I p. 68 n. 9). Yu Chao'en adopted a Xianbei soldier around 760, who took the name Yu Zhide.
- **The law caught up later.** In 791 an edict let eunuchs of the fifth grade or above adopt one male child under nine (I p. 68 n. 9). The *Tang huiyao* editor links this to their control of the guards after 785: "they began to adopt foster-children, to whom they bequeathed their titles, the first signs that foreshadowed their absolute authority" (I p. 68 n. 9).

### 1.4 Religion: eunuchs controlled the Daoist clergy
- "All eunuchs were brought up in the Buddhist faith ... and to Taoism they were also favourably inclined" (I p. 56).
- **"From the reign of Hsüan Tsung they exercised an almost complete official control over the activities of both [orders]."** As Commissioners of Merit and Works (*gongde shi* 功德使) they took commissions on enforced monastery repairs and on buying live animals for release (I p. 56; II pp. 46–47: such missions paid "never less than one thousand string of cash"). Rideout's dating to Xuanzong is loose. The formal *gongde shi* office is usually dated to the 780s, so check this against So Wai-man.
- Kao Li-shih founded "a Buddhist and a Taoist monastery, each of unexampled magnificence" in the capital (I p. 68 n. 13).
- Eunuchs taught the harem ladies "a variety of subjects ranging from Confucian classics to the game of draughts" (I p. 56). There were fifty eunuch students of the Inner Palace, and the Neiwenxueguan was staffed by eunuchs by 741 (I pp. 56, 68 n. 11).

### 1.5 Offices, numbers, conflict with officials (618–730)
- Taizong limited eunuchs to menial duties, no state administration, and no grade above the fourth (I p. 57). There were fewer than 100 posts at first, more than 3,000 eunuchs by 706, and 4,618 graded or grade-due eunuchs by 820, 1,696 of them of the third grade or above (I p. 57).
- **The Palace Envoy (*zhongshi* 中使) is where eunuchs first clashed with officials.** In 640 a frontier-pass dispute led Wei Zheng to say: "It has always been impossible to treat eunuchs on terms of intimacy. They make up stories on very little foundation" (I pp. 58–59). In 675 an official beat a eunuch caught in the Luoyang gardens, and in 682 the prefect Su Liangsi arrested an insolent bamboo-collecting envoy. Both were upheld (I p. 70 n. 25).
- **The Censorate lost army supervision to eunuchs "from the year 705"** (I p. 63; checked against the page image). This is the origin of the eunuch Army Supervisor (*jianjun* 監軍) who matters in the late Tang.
- **Censors against eunuchs, 707.** The censor Wei Chuangong accused a eunuch favourite of the Anle Princess: "If I could get him put to death one day, I should not in the least mind undergoing execution the next" (II p. 47 n. 3). He was blocked by the Chief Censor Dou Congyi, who, faced with "any beardless man", "would pervert justice in order to meet him" (II pp. 47–48 n. 3).
- 710: Ruizong publicly rewarded the sub-prefect Li Chaoyin for throwing out a petitioning eunuch, with an edict about how "the race of eunuchs often acquire influence and in kind and indulgent eras inevitably usurp power" (II p. 49 n. 2).
- **Eunuchs as coup troops.** Yang Sixu cut down the leading rebel in the 707 palace revolt (II p. 45). Kao Li-shih and the Korean slave Wang Maozhong carried out Li Longji's coups of 710 and 713 (I p. 64; II p. 50).
- **The Flying Dragon Stable (*feilong jiu* 飛龍廄), 699, was under a eunuch commissioner.** It later mounted a troop "commanded and possibly also manned by eunuchs" (I p. 65). This is a Tang precedent for Grass's Ming Directorate of Imperial Horses.
- **Kao Li-shih's method.** He built "a eunuch secretariat, through whose agency all transactions between him and his ministers should be conducted" (II p. 51). He used the financier Yuwen Rong "as a counter-weight to the Chief Ministers" but rescued Chang Yüeh at the critical moment: "playing off one faction against another" (II p. 56). After the purge of Wang Maozhong (730) the emperor said: "With Li-Shih in control, I can sleep in peace" (II p. 58). Rideout's note 21 adds that from c. 730 "the chief ministers could only approach the Emperor through a eunuch" (I p. 69).
- A cameo usable for Rectified Names: when the monk Xue Huaiyi was ordained so that he could enter the Inner Palace, "one official rudely proposed that as Huai-I had now all the privileges of a eunuch he had better be castrated" (I p. 70 n. 27). **To the court, "eunuch" was a rule about who may pass the inner door, enforced on the body.**

### 1.6 Background for 835–907 (NOT from Rideout; verify against So Wai-man and Hoeckelmann before use)
- Eunuch Palace Commissioners (*hujun zhongwei* 護軍中尉) commanded the Shence Armies from 796, and the two Commissioners of Privy Affairs (*shumi shi* 樞密使) controlled documents. Together they formed the "four nobles" (四貴).
- In 835, at the Sweet Dew Incident (甘露之變), Wenzong and the ministers Li Xun and Zheng Zhu tried to lure the eunuchs to a "sweet dew" omen and massacre them. Qiu Shiliang escaped with the emperor, and Shence troops killed the chief ministers and many officials. After this the emperor was effectively a eunuch captive.
- Eunuch commissioners enthroned Yizong (859), Xizong (873) and Zhaozong (888). Yang Fugong ran an adoptive lineage of adopted sons placed as generals and governors. In 903 Zhu Quanzhong (Zhu Wen) and Cui Yin massacred the palace eunuchs, and the Tang ended in 907.
- **The point for design: the 867 start date falls inside the eunuch-kingmaker era.** Rideout's earlier mechanisms (adoption chains, envoys, guard command, control of the clergy) are the roots of that system.

### 1.7 Were eunuchs called "not men", a third kind, or women? (Rectified Names check)
In both parts I found **no Tang usage calling eunuchs women, a third sex, or "not men"**. The words Rideout translates and quotes are:
- "beardless man" (II pp. 47–48). **The category is still man.**
- "the race of eunuchs" (II p. 49 n. 2), and "these slaves" (Kao Li-shih on Wang Maozhong's clan, II p. 58).
- Register terms of rank and service: *neijishi* 內給使, *baishen* 白身, *pinguan* 品官, *gaopin* 高品, *sibai* 私白.
- "Deprived him of his manhood" (Gu Kuang, I p. 66 n. 7). The cut removes *yang* and fatherhood; it does not confer womanhood.

The institutional record points the same way. Eunuchs married wives, adopted sons and daughters, bequeathed titles, held generals' rank, and wore officials' robe colours (I pp. 56–57, 66–67 nn. 7–8). The palace treated them as **men of servile rank with no line of descent**, a male sub-register. It did not treat them as a gender between.

**Verdict on the tenet "the palace calls us eunuchs so it need not call us women":** it is fair **as the faith's polemic**, and Rideout makes it sharper, not weaker. The palace kept exactly two registers for those it enclosed: women (the harem, the female form of *gongxing*) and eunuchs (the Neishisheng, the male form). Filing a woman of the Way under the Neishisheng is the palace choosing the male register for her: "eunuch" is how it names a man it has cut. Narrator text must not say the Tang saw eunuchs as quasi-women. The faith may say the register makes her a cut man so that it never has to make her a palace woman. Bhatt's warning still applies: the Way's quarrel is with the register, not with the boys from Min who were filed in it.

## 3. In-world loc lines (attribute in-world; our phrasings)
- "The palace has two doors for what it keeps: the women's gate and the knife. It sent me to the knife, because the women's gate would have named me."
- Tribute commissioner: "Every circuit sends its white bodies. Min's quota is short this year."
- "The father said, I regret I ever begat you. The Way says: then let the mother begin." (After Gu Kuang; use only with the poem credited in-world as "the Min song".)
- Censor ally: "Kill him one day and I will go to the block the next. Bring me your petition."
- Chancellor (hostile): "Rats beneath the floor. Burn the floor and the house goes with them." (Proverb quoted by the Tang historians, I p. 53.)
- Eunuch patron: "I was adopted across a name. I will not tell a temple it may not do the same."
