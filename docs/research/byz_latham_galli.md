# Latham, "'Fabulous Clap-Trap'" (2012): design reading

**Citation (verified from the PDF).** Jacob Latham, "'Fabulous Clap-Trap': Roman Masculinity, the Cult of Magna Mater, and Literary Constructions of the *galli* at Rome from the Late Republic to Late Antiquity," *The Journal of Religion* 92.1 (January 2012): 84–122. University of Chicago Press. DOI 10.1086/662205. **Read:** the whole article, including the notes (JSTOR copy).

**Scope.** The article covers only pagan Latin and Greek authors *writing at Rome*. Christian polemic (Firmicus, Augustine, Prudentius) is explicitly left out (n. 126), and so is Anatolia. Its value to the design is the method. It is the clearest evidence for "the record is a mirror of Roman manhood, not a portrait of the galli".

## 1. The argument

- **The thesis.** The literary gallus is "a history of aristocratic male self-fashioning on the back(sides) of the galli" (p. 86). In summary: "During the late republic the galli were not Roman; in the empire, they were not men" (p. 86). In late antiquity the invective "largely vanished as the cult became more accepted and as devotion to the goddess could be expressed in ways other than castration" (p. 86).
- **Four phases** (pp. 93–120):
  1. *Late republic* (Varro, Lucretius, Catullus). The galli are exotic curiosities, "alluring and repellent at the same time" (p. 101). Varro's satirist praises the "wisdom and chastity" of young galli who look "like Naiads" (p. 96). The high priest is "dressed like a woman, but in purple, 'wearing a crown of gold gleaming with gems'" (p. 96).
  2. *Augustan*. The good Roman goddess is split from her bad foreign devotees. Livy rewrites Polybius' respectful galli as "fanatici Galli" (p. 102). Virgil and Ovid make them "semimares" (half-males).
  3. *High empire* (Seneca, Martial, Juvenal). The galli become "sexually aberrant fanatics" (p. 109). Note that this backlash follows the galli's *own* monuments, which show them "dressed in feminine clothing, wearing earrings, with long hair" (p. 109).
  4. *Late 2nd to 4th century*. The invective fades. Dio, Herodian and Plotinus treat the galli as "colorful scenery hardly worthy of special comment" (p. 116). Roman aristocrats take the taurobolium. Claudian's attack on the eunuch Eutropius (pp. 119–20) is the main late survival.
- **Gallae is attested, in Catullus.** In Latham's paraphrase of Catullus 63, "Attis gathered a group of *gallae* with whom s/he set off deeper into the woods" (p. 99). Attis says "*ego mulier*" (I now am a woman) but is also "*notha mulier*" (no true woman) (p. 99). So *gallae* is a Latin poetic feminine for the galli as a band. It is not a self-designation found on any inscription.
- **Dies sanguinis.** According to the standard reconstruction (Carcopino, Lambrechts, Fishwick), Claudius (41–54) introduced the March funerary cycle. It ran from the *arbor intrat* on 22 March, when the pine of Attis was carried to the Palatine for an all-night vigil, to "the *dies sanguinis* (March 24), during which frenzied devotees lacerated themselves in order to offer their own blood to the goddess (or, improbably, castrated themselves)" (pp. 107–8). The *canna intrat* (15 March) and the *hilaria*, "a masked carnival", came later (p. 108). Note 39: the Day of Blood is "a festival only certainly attested in the imperial era".
- **How the cut was made.** Note 107 summarises the modern consensus. Alvar argues that castration "(of the testicles only) would have taken place in more controlled circumstances than Roman literature allowed". Lancellotti suggests that "it typically happened as an adult under controlled circumstances during the festive rituals". Pliny notes "the use of Samian pottery, which the galli seemingly used to castrate themselves safely" (n. 114). Catullus has a "sharp flint" (p. 99). Juvenal and Martial have a "Samian shard" (pp. 111–12).
- **The archigallus.** This office was created in the late 1st or 2nd century and "filled from the first by 'civilized' and serious Roman citizens", which has led to the assumption that it did not require castration (p. 108, n. 86). In 319 a *sacerdos phryx maximus*, the Roman citizen Flavius Antonius Eustochius, performed a taurobolium for **Serapias**, a priestess or initiate (p. 91). This shows that "Phrygian" alterity was "manufactured".
- **Julian at Pessinus.** Julian wrote his *Hymn to the Mother of the Gods* "during his pilgrimage to Pessinus, the reputed home of the goddess" (n. 120).
- **Survival.** Symmachus still expected his brother home for the Mother's festival in the late 4th century (p. 118). The taurobolium inscription of Aedesius reads "*in aeternum renatus*", reborn into eternity (n. 121).
- **The bridge to Byzantium.** Claudian, *In Eutropium* 1.277–81, tells the eastern court eunuch Eutropius to "with a Phrygian knife destroy what is left of your virility. Leave arms to men" (p. 120). This is the moment when the galli invective is turned on a *court* eunuch of Constantinople.

## 3. In-world loc lines

- *Elder Sister:* "A Roman poet called us *gallae* and then begged the Mother to keep her madness far from his house. We kept the word. He may keep his house."
- *Day of Blood (24 March):* "Every sister gives a little blood today. Only those who have asked give the rest, and only with the old clay."
- *Two memories event:* "One poet saw Naiads in white. Another saw a drunk on her back by the drums. Both are in the books, and the books are all we have."
- *Court reaction:* "The courtier called the parakoimomenos a gallus. The Sisters' envoy said: 'He is not. No one asked him.'"
- *Hilaria:* "Three days ago we mourned him. Today we wear masks, and nobody remembers the wound."
