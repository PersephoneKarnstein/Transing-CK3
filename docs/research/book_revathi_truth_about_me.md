## 2. Themes, in Revathi's own terms

### 2.1 A woman from childhood

- **Girlhood felt natural.** She sweeps, draws the kolam, helps in the kitchen and plays girls' games (R:160–168, 220–224). "I did know that I behaved like a girl, it felt natural for me to do so." (R:249). She compares it to eating: she could not stop because others told her to (R:250–252).
- **Which clothes are the disguise.** After a festival dance in women's clothes she thinks: "I had not worn a disguise I said to myself; I had given form to my real feelings." (R:461–462). Back in men's clothes, "I felt that I was in disguise, and that I had left my real self behind." (R:466–467). She repeats the same inversion to her family years later: "What I’m wearing now is real, true." (R:2766).
- **Her own words at fifteen.** "A woman trapped in a man’s body was how I thought of myself." (R:421–422). In the same passage she asks why God could not have made her "wholly male or wholly female" (R:426–427). This is a lament about being made to suffer, not a claim to be a third thing (see §2.8).
- **She is honest about the gap.** Before the operation she still felt she was a man in women's clothes (R:1672–1674). Back home, wanting marriage and children: "Though I thought of myself as a woman, I felt that I was a man who had become a woman." (R:2958–2959). The gap is social: who will marry her, whether she can bear a child. It is not a doubt about what she is.
- **Being taken as a woman is what she values most.** "My heart melted only when someone thought of me as a woman and spoke to me as they would to other women." (R:4757–4759).

### 2.2 Family violence and the natal family

- **The violence is constant and it is family violence.** Her brothers thrash her and set her to cleaning dung (R:314–323). An elder brother uses a cricket bat (R:795–798). Her father sneers that she will learn only by "drinking their piss" (R:860–861). Her middle brother cracks her skull while her mother urges him to break her bones (R:1392–1403).
- **Her hair is shaved.** Her family shaves her head at the Samayapuram temple to cure her (R:1430–1435, 1448–1451). For her this hurts more than the beating. Hair is the marker everyone attacks. Her nani warns: "For a pottai, nothing is as shameful as having one’s hair cut." (R:1636–1637).
- **How her family explains her.** Each explanation lets them avoid naming her.
  - Her mother speaks of a "seducing female demon" (R:1435).
  - Later her mother tells people someone "poisoned" her mind (R:3020–3021).
  - Her sister calls it fate or someone's sin (R:2863–2866).
- **Her mother's grief is about being a woman.** "Is it not enough that all of us born as women suffer?" (R:2780–2781). She also calls Revathi "a barren tree" (R:2743).
- **Her father accepts her as fate.** "It seems this is his destiny." (R:2826–2827). He ends: "Let him be and eat of our food." (R:2833–2834).
- **Who accepts her follows class and status.**
  - Her poorer maternal kin insist she come to festivals and will say she is a woman.
  - Her wealthier paternal kin speak kindly but never invite her, she thinks to protect their standing (R:2894–2925).
  - Her sister takes her side against their mother so she can carry the lamp with the women. Her sister-in-law teaches the baby to say aunty (R:3937–3971).
  - "She had accepted me, wearing a sari and being the way I am, and she called me her sister!" (R:3955–3956).
- **Property.**
  - Her brothers threaten to tell the court that their brother Doraisamy is not this person (R:4091–4103).
  - The lawyer asks: "Why can’t you be a man for a day?" (R:4119–4120). She signs as Doraisamy and is bought out with a cash share (R:4139–4159).
  - Later her remittances build her parents' house (R:5109–5111, 5202–5212). Her father then denies she gave him anything. After a crisis he makes the house over to her with a life interest for her parents: "Though Doraisamy is my son, he will sign as “Revathi”." (R:6220–6221).
- **Leaving.** "If society scorns us, then we turn to our families, if we have a family. But if family scorns us, who do we turn to?" (R:4523–4524).

### 2.3 The chosen household: jamaat, guru and chela

Revathi presents this as the institution of a living community, in its own words. The note summarises it so that the mod knows what **not** to transplant (§4.2).

- **The first adoption** is at a Dindigul goddess festival.
  - "A guru was like a mother, she would allow her chelas to share her home, and would clothe and feed them." (R:614–615).
  - The *jamaat*, a meeting of elders, records the adoption with betel, a small fee (*thandu*) and her new name. She is to call her guru Amma (R:620–683).
- **The household is modelled on kinship and marriage.** A guru's house is "like the marital home", the mother's house like "the natal home" (R:1500–1501).
  - Revathi names generations up to the great-grandmother's guru and down to the great-granddaughter's daughter, with elder and younger sisters (R:1597–1609).
  - A Mumbai *parivar* counts "over five hundred" (R:1510–1514).
  - There are seven houses, each with an elected *naik* (R:1553–1560).
- **Her account of what the household is for.**
  - Shut out by mainstream society, "hijras turn to their community and its culture for comfort and for nurture" (R:1548–1550).
  - The community "had evolved a culture of their own to enable them to exercise order and restraint and also to exercise their rights" (R:1561–1563).
  - She says it has no caste (R:1550–1552), though *parivars* compete (R:3549–3550).
- **Rules and service.**
  - Juniors greet elders (*paampaduthi*), never let their clothes brush an elder, wash spittoons and clothes, massage feet, keep their hair long, pluck rather than shave, and hand over their earnings (R:1183–1192, 1212–1220, 1610–1623).
  - Taking a husband is forbidden by several gurus (R:1627–1630, 2198–2199, 3155–3159).
- **Care is real.**
  - Her kaalaguru nursed her after the operation: "she even washed my blood-stained clothes." (R:2098–2099).
  - Strangers from the community carry her home: "We are, in a sense, like a flock of crows. We stick together." (R:2041–2042).
  - Her *parivar* arrives "in five taxis" when she is wronged (R:3528–3533).
- **Extraction is real too.**
  - Her own later guru keeps the change from her errands and rations her water (R:3672–3691).
  - The women in her guru's Byculla house are held for three years (R:3723–3728).
  - Her shrewd observation: "even among pottais, only if you had your own chela and naathis were you accorded respect." (R:4776–4777).
  - At the end: "In aravani society, you’re respected if you have twenty chelas of your own and run two hamams." (R:7333–7335).
- **Moving between houses.**
  - Moving is allowed, but debts and gifts are settled between houses before the elders (R:2505–2532, 4837–4861).
  - A daughter can outrank her former mother in a new house (R:4764–4771).
- **Her own practice as a guru** is the ethical centre of the book's account of kinship.
  - She takes in three young women who had the operation on their own and were turned away. Her gurubais ask what they will earn for her, and she answers: "I’ll do for them what a mother would do for her daughters." (R:5688–5689).
  - "I did not want my chelas to earn for me, nor did I wish to use my authority to extract money from them." (R:5724–5725).
  - With Famila: "we did not want our relationship to be one of bonded slavery." (R:7079–7080).
- **A first daughter.** When her first chela Mya flees, Revathi tells her: "Wherever you go, wherever you are, even if you become another’s chela, you’ll continue to be my daughter." (R:3709–3711).
- **Mourning.** When a guru dies, "the chelas become widowed" (R:7190). They observe forty days in white until the guru's sister formally becomes their mother (R:7192–7250). Revathi holds to these customs even while doubting other rules: "I could not give up practices that generations had observed." (R:7229–7230).

### 2.4 The nirvaan operation and its aftermath

- **The term.** The translator's gloss: "To undergo nirvaanam is to submit to castration and turn into a full woman." (R:602–603).
  - There are two kinds: by a doctor, or by a *thayamma*, an elder of the community. The second carries more status and more danger (R:1652–1667).
- **Who decides.**
  - The elder chooses the time, and it is tied to service. Some daughters wait years (R:2230–2236).
  - Her kaalaguru says the goddess must descend. Revathi adds a plainer reason: "there is also the fact that I had been a good chela." (R:2240).
  - Shakuntala's guru put her off "the next day" for four years until she saved up and went alone (R:2337–2344).
  - Her gurubais later object that the three Bangalore chelas paid for their own operations without earning first (R:5641–5649, 5679–5685).
- **What she expected.** "Back then I thought that in order to turn feminine, all I needed to do was get rid of this male object" (R:1663–1664).
  - She went in thinking "finally the female in me would be freed from her male body" (R:1675–1676).
  - On the eve: "whatever happens, whether I live or die, I would do so as a woman." (R:1739–1740).
- **The operation itself** (R:1688–1924).
  - It costs 2,500 rupees each, plus medicines, an ayah and tips. They go without an escort.
  - She watches her gurubai-chela come round and then scream with burning pain. She herself has a spinal block and is awake.
  - A nurse silences her: "Do you want those around you to know what sort of operation you’ve had? Just shut up and put up with the pain." (R:1858–1859). The nurse tells the ward it was a stomach operation (R:1863–1865).
  - A stranger's mother caring for an old man next door tends her, and Revathi calls her a goddess (R:1877–1881).
- **The journey back.** They are mocked at the station, and blood soaks through the dressing. A woman police officer asks why she cannot be "normal" (R:1972–1976).
  - Four hijras find them, feed them and talk the ticket collector round: "For pottais, an operation is a veritable rebirth." (R:2025).
  - "But we—we are not considered human." (R:2054).
- **The forty days** (R:2080–2203).
  - No milk or fruit. Legs kept apart. No mirror, no looking at men, no combing her hair. Ritual baths on the 12th and 20th days. Gifts of wheat, sugar and ghee.
  - At the end, a night rite before the goddess: the green *jok* sari, garlands, a milk pot poured into a well, and a mirror after forty days.
  - She is asked to say: "‘Take my form away and bless me with yours,’" (R:2163–2164).
  - "It seemed to me that my earlier male form had disappeared and in its place was a woman." (R:2169–2171).
- **Afterwards.**
  - "Deep within myself I recognized that I was now a real woman." (R:2254–2255).
  - Her fear in public toilets and on trains goes, and she can stand beside women in the ladies' coach (R:2251–2256, 2272–2274).
  - What does **not** end: her voice gives her away, papers carry her old name, and her family and village argue about her. The operation changed her body. It did not settle who would recognise her.

### 2.5 Labour

Revathi treats every job she does as labour pushed on her by exclusion, and she says so directly.

- **Child and family labour.** Kolam and kitchen work as a girl (R:160–168). Then dung, cattle and cleaning the lorry as a punishment-job when her family decides she will not study (R:317–323, 813–858).
- **Domestic service to elders.** Water, laundry, spittoons, cooking and foot massage. It is presented as what all juniors once did (R:738–744, 1183–1192, 1621–1623).
- **Begging at shops (*kadai*).**
  - Her Delhi nani frames it as reverence. "In this Delhi, for centuries, they’ve treated us like gods." (R:1131). Shopkeepers give money for blessings, and hijras must not desire men so as to stay worthy (R:1157–1167).
  - Revathi's own reading: "we are like beggars, asking money from the shops. We can’t possibly get what we want, nor do we have the right to desire them." (R:1231–1232).
  - In Mumbai she keeps a weekly round of markets (R:1522–1526) and begs vegetables by the sack at Mulund (R:2280–2299).
- **Festival dancing.** Village elders inspect the dancers' bodies before booking them, and the dancers are abused in a school classroom (R:5132–5186).
  - "One’s self-respect had to be hawked thus to feed one’s stomach." (R:5143–5144).
  - "You imagine that you can make a living by dancing, but here, too, you have to sell your body." (R:5194–5195).
- **Sex work (*danda*).**
  - She enters it wanting sexual happiness and finds it is work: "I wanted to experience sexual happiness, but I found myself having to treat sexual experiences as work." (R:2563–2565).
  - Earnings are split half and half with the mistress (R:3263–3271). There are fights over clients (R:3277–3311) and a 100-rupee daily due to her guru (R:4980–4987).
  - She refuses to procure: "I could countenance selling my body to earn a living, but not the bodies of others." (R:3579–3580).
- **The hamam.** Hot water for 5 rupees and back massages for 10, out of which she keeps tips (R:4589–4594, 4795–4799).
- **Her analysis of choice.**
  - "Did I take to this profession, because I wanted to? I did not choose this profession on my own." (R:5362–5364).
  - She uses the analogy of a teacher who wanted to be a doctor (R:5368–5374).
  - It is "society and law" that brought her there, "where many of us are today" (R:5380–5382).
  - "I’m called a hooker. What should the police be called then—they who use us and snatch money from us?" (R:5386–5387).
- **Remittances.** Her sex-work earnings rebuild her parents' house while she tells them she dances in films (R:5202–5212). Later: "Every brick of this house bears witness to how I struggled and slaved to earn that money" (R:6107–6108).
- **NGO work.** She joins Sangama as office assistant at 2,500 rupees a month (R:5828–5830).
  - What she wants: "I wanted to be like other women—go to work, do something for society." (R:5835–5836).
  - Her guru scoffs that danda pays more. Revathi answers: "For generations all we have been able to do is begging or sex work. How much longer should we have to do this?" (R:5845–5847).
  - At the end, out of a job and with her mother on dialysis, she goes back to sex work (R:7301–7307), and then back to Sangama (R:7348).
- **Reproductive labour in marriage.** She does all the housework while her partner preaches equality (R:6614–6629, 6906–6917, 7030–7034).
- **Money as standing.** "In this world, it is enough that one has money. Respect and regard follow." (R:3457–3458).

### 2.6 Police and state violence

- **The police make the community the suspect.**
  - Her family has local hijras detained on the accusation that they made her beg (R:1319–1327).
  - After her guru's murder the police suspect the community and want to beat witnesses (R:7205–7214).
- **Street policing.** A plainclothes man robs her and threatens her with sex (R:4930–4948).
  - At Cubbon Park station she is beaten, made to sweep and stripped before a prisoner, and a lathi is used on her body. She is coached to plead guilty, then a bribe replaces the fine (R:4992–5074). The note does not reproduce the details.
  - Police hold her as bait to arrest fifteen men, then beat her (R:5261–5322).
  - "from our perspective there was no difference between a police and a rowdy." (R:5124–5125).
- **Documents.**
  - The scooter registration book must follow the ration card, Doraisamy (R:5421–5435).
  - The licence inspector throws her papers back because he has "never had such a case" (R:5460–5471). After a letter from the village officer and a bribe, the licence names her "‘Revathi who is Doraisamy’" (R:5494).
  - Her bank deposit could only be made "in the name of Doraisamy" (R:4181–4182).
- **Rowdies.** Rowdies are the state's shadow. They extort her guru's household (R:2616–2621, 7166–7171), throw stones at the hamam at night (R:4666–4679), and in the end kill her guru for refusing them money (R:7136–7147).

### 2.7 Kinship with women, and the women's movement

- **Women's rites and women's company.**
  - Carrying the rice-flour lamp in the women's line at Mariamman is a high point. She is admitted at her sister's insistence, and the women praise her (R:3937–4027).
  - At the village tap the women chat with her about cooking and say she carries her pot "just like us women" (R:4220–4227).
  - A flower seller calls her *akka* and asks her to be the day's first buyer (R:4745–4751).
  - Her sister's mother-in-law poses as her mother-in-law to get her a lease (R:6533–6549).
- **The women of Byculla.** She sees the women sex workers as kin she cannot help: "I too have a mother and sister." (R:3579). They call her *pinjamma*, "little mother" (R:3602–3604).
- **Shared danger.** On a night bus a driver gropes her. Afterwards: "There was no safety for women who travelled alone." (R:3825–3826). Shouting down auto drivers who bet on her sex, she realises "I was merely going through all that a woman does, all the time." (R:5570–5571).
- **The women's movement.** The book does not narrate the women's movement as such. What it shows:
  - Sangama's work alongside "dalit women, child workers" and against dowry and communal violence (R:5913–5915).
  - Her wish "to fight against sexual violence as such" (R:5911–5912).
  - Phone calls to "the women’s groups in Bangalore" about her wedding, where some refuse to believe the man has married her (R:6805–6810).
  - Her testimony at the World Social Forum, which includes her marriage (R:6988–6994).
  - Her charge that her partner preached women's equality in public meetings and then walked out on a woman (R:7030–7036).
  - The translator, V. Geetha, has been in the Indian women's movement since 1988 (R:33–35). That is front matter, not Revathi's narrative.

### 2.8 The desire for recognition as a woman

- **What she asks for** is to be named, housed, employed and married as a woman. She does not ask to be revered.
  - Her name: "My name is Revathi and that’s what you should call me from now on." (R:2751–2752).
  - Work: "I want to go to work as many women do." (R:5355–5356).
  - Marriage: "marriage would confirm that I was living a womanly life" (R:6600). She wears a thali because neighbours check her neck (R:6601–6611).
- **The Koovagam speech** (R:6307–6342) is the source Bhatt quotes. Checked here:
  - "Listen, I am not diseased. I consider myself a woman. But I possessed the form of a man." (R:6318–6320).
  - She asks the state for counselling, hormones and surgery as other countries provide (R:6321–6332).
  - "We were not born to beg or do sex work." (R:6334–6335).
  - She also asks for ration cards, housing, jobs and medical care (R:6338–6340).
- **Her list of rights** includes ration card, property, passport, work, marriage and raising a child, and "the right to change our sex" (R:5975–5980).
- **The licence** that left her sex blank was a compromise she accepted, not a goal (R:5492–5494). Her threat to go to the papers was that the government "only issues licences to men or women, not to people like me" (R:5482–5483). Read in context, this is a protest at being refused, not a claim to a third category.
- **Where the book does contain the "neither" frame.** There are two places, and neither is Revathi's account of herself.
  1. Her Delhi nani's origin tale, in which the people at the forest edge tell Rāma, "We belong neither to mankind, nor to womankind." (R:1151–1152). The same tale grounds the begging economy: a hijra's word comes true, so shopkeepers pay for blessings (R:1155–1161).
  2. Revathi's own laments to God (R:426–427, 4525–4526), which ask why she was not made wholly female.
- **Outsiders who sacralise her also exclude her.**
  - Her paternal relatives say "he is close to God." (R:2915) and do not invite her.
  - A priest lets her into a virgin goddess's sanctum because "You and that goddess are the same." (R:4293). A devotee calls her "unblemished, unlike us" (R:4296–4297).
  - She is moved, and she still records it as something done to her by onlookers.
- **This bears out Bhatt's reading.** "Veneration" here is the outward face of being kept out: of the family's functions, of work, of marriage.

### 2.9 How outsiders name her

Recorded here so that the mod can write hostile *reactions* without importing modern slurs (§4.2).

- **Schoolboys and strangers.** "Number 9", "girl-boy", "female thing", *Ali*, "Post box!" (R:175–176, 230–233), and "Disguise! Disguise! Female disguise!" (R:459–460).
- **Crowds at the festival dances.** Men or women? Number 9s? *Devadasis*? (R:777–778). Then "Original or duplicate?" with grabbing (R:779–780).
- **Police and clients in Karnataka** use *khoja* (R:4938, 4995, 5232).
- **Brothers.** "pottai", "mother-fucking sissy", and obscene fantasies about her body (R:317, 1395, 4470–4476).
- **The landlady** keeps saying Doraisamy and cannot "get my tongue to pronounce the name" (R:4233–4234). Educated people, even when mocking, say Revathi (R:4239–4241).
- **Hijras use these terms for themselves too.** In the community "pottai" is ordinary speech, and it is also the word her brothers spit.

## 3. Content warning: suicide, self-harm and sexual violence

These passages are listed so that no game content draws on them. They are given factually and briefly, without detail.

- **Suicidal thoughts.** At about fifteen (R:427). Praying to fall from the hill and die if she cannot reach Delhi (R:919–920). During her brother's beating (R:1405–1406). In police custody (R:5057–5058). In her marriage (R:6942–6944). Her colleagues stay with her afterwards for fear she will harm herself (R:7013–7014). Out of work at the end (R:7330).
- **Self-harm.**
  - She cuts her forehead with a shard of glass when her mistress beats her (R:3359–3363).
  - She hits her head against a wall after her brothers' abuse (R:4527).
  - She beats her head in grief at her guru's death (R:7148–7149).
- **A suicide attempt.** She douses herself in kerosene on the street after her father denies her money; there is no match and she stops (R:6125–6143).
- **Suicide.** Her chela Famila dies by suicide; Revathi performs her rites (R:7065–7094). Three young women threaten to die if she will not take them in (R:5654). Sangama's founder mentions suicides among gay men (R:5794–5795).
- **Sexual violence.**
  - Her friend is raped by rowdies on the hill fort (R:509–522).
  - Revathi is raped at knifepoint (R:2616–2632).
  - She is sexually tortured in police custody (R:5018–5047).
  - Dancers are abused by village elders (R:5182–5186).
  - She is groped on a bus (R:3768–3785).
- **Other violence.** Children are beaten (passim). Her skull is fractured (R:1400). A client attacks her with a knife (R:5238–5243). Her guru is murdered (R:7136–7147).

**Rule for the mod.** None of this is game content. The mod's rule against suicide and self-harm content covers all of it. Sexual violence, custodial torture and the details of the surgery are also off-limits (§4.2).

## 4. For the mod

The Mālai (`tm_garland`) is a reconstruction set in Tamil country between 867 and 1453. Revathi's memoir is a modern life, 1970s to 2000s, written by a living woman about a living community. It is **not evidence** for anything medieval, and nothing in it may be presented as historical. What it can do is keep the faith's writing honest about lived experience: what being taken as a daughter feels like, what a gate costs the person who goes through it, what work a daughter is pushed into when she owns nothing, and how others refuse to name her. Every use below is a *theme* written fresh in the faith's own medieval terms. None borrows an institution, a rite, a term or a line.

### 4.1 What it can legitimately inform

1. **The texture of a chosen household and mother/daughter kinship** (`Take as Daughter`, the Temple Street tenet, *A Share on the Temple Street*).
   - **Care is shown as work.** In the book the mother cooks, nurses, washes the bloody cloths and stands between her daughter and the men at the door (R:2075–2099, 2572–2575, 7166–7171).
     - Write the Garland's elder doing these things in events: tending a daughter after the vow fails or succeeds, and facing a creditor on her behalf.
   - **The bond outlasts distance and changes of house.** Mya stays "my daughter" whatever house she joins (R:3709–3711).
     - A daughter of the Garland who moves to another court, or another mother, should keep her first mother's opinion and a flavour line. She should not be cut off.
   - **The faith's ethic should be Revathi's as a guru, not that of her worst gurus.** The mother does not take the daughter's earnings, and does not hold the gate over her as leverage (R:5686–5689, 5724–5725, 7080).
     - This fits the existing design, where daughters are donors and owners, not alms-takers (`book_bhatt_third_sex.md` row 4).
     - A *Take as Daughter* event could give the elder an explicit refusal: "your share is your own".
     - This contrast is the faith's *own* ethic. It must never be written as a judgement on any real community (§4.2).
   - **Mourning a mother is kinship work.** When an elder dies, her taken daughters grieve as daughters, and the eldest takes on the youngest.
     - Revathi's widowhood for her guru (R:7188–7250) shows how heavy this is.
     - The Garland's version must use its own forms: a Tamil temple-street death, a share passing on, the women of the street choosing. It must not use *roundap*, white saris or bangle-breaking as a hijra rite.
   - **There is no caste on the street, but there is rivalry between lines.** Revathi says hijras do not observe caste, yet *parivars* side against each other (R:1550–1552, 3549–3550). This is a fair model for friction between two daughters' lines over a share.

2. **What a gate rite means to the person who goes through it** (*Keep the Pāvai Vow*).
   - **Waiting and timing.** Revathi waits on an elder's choice; others wait for years (R:2230–2236, 2337–2344). The vow's "Next Mārkaḻi" failure text already has this. Add a line acknowledging that years of waiting are themselves a cost: stress that grows with each failed year.
   - **Companions carry her.**
     - The four strangers on the platform (R:2002–2042) and the gurubai who goes first and then smiles at her (R:1704–1712, 1771–1773).
     - The hill-fort *thozhis* who made a place where they "could be women only on the hill" (R:498–501).
     - This independently supports the Garland's social gate, kept with *tōḻi*. The Tamil word is the same, and Revathi's friends are the people without whom she would never have left.
     - Use the *theme*: companions are the precondition. Do not cite the memoir as a source for medieval *tōḻi*.
   - **The elder who walks with her is a witness, not an owner.** The vow's elder daughter should not be able to withhold or sell the rite. If an event lets an elder delay it, the faith's own text should judge the delay as a wrong.
   - **Joy, fear and the mirror.** The fear of not surviving it, the vow to die "as a woman" (R:1739–1740), and the first look at herself afterwards (R:2165–2171). A success event can carry the feeling of being seen for the first time in her own form, written in the faith's own idiom (the dream, the staff, the name). It must not use a mirror rite, a forty-day seclusion or a goddess's descent.
   - **The gate does not end the contest.** After success her voice, papers, family and village still argue about her (§2.4, §2.9). Post-gate events, such as *A Pandit's Objection* and the family reactions, should keep coming. Success is not the end of being misnamed.

3. **The labour a daughter is pushed into when she has no share.**
   - The memoir shows a ladder of work forced by exclusion:
     - service to elders;
     - begging under the name of blessing (R:1131, 1231–1232);
     - dancing for patrons who inspect her (R:5141–5145);
     - sex work (§2.5);
     - money sent home to kin who then deny it (R:6095–6108).
   - **Design use.** The Temple Street share exists *against* this ladder. Flavour text for the share and the gifts can say so plainly: the share is what lets a daughter give rather than ask. The existing landless paths (the market lamp, the dwindled quarter, the caravan) can be framed the same way.
   - **Remittance and inheritance** are the safest strong theme. A daughter's earnings flow back to her natal house, and her brothers contest her share there (R:4088–4159, 5994–6221).
     - **Event candidate: *A Brother's Claim*.** A Garland daughter's natal brothers dispute her portion when her father's land is divided. She can sign it away for coin, insist and lose kin opinion, or have her father settle it on her with a life interest for her parents.
     - The last option rests on Revathi's gift deed (R:6208–6221) as a *theme*. It needs a medieval legal source before any pandit cites it. Check the *putrikā* and *strīdhana* readings in `ind_pechilis_karaikkal.md` and the Manu row of the faith page.

4. **How outsiders misname her** (hostile reaction text, family events).
   - **The family's ways of not naming her.** These can be written as medieval Hindu-court reactions, paraphrased and in-world:
     - possession by a demoness (R:1435);
     - someone poisoned her mind (R:3020–3021);
     - fate or a past sin (R:2864–2865, 2826–2827);
     - a "disguise" or "costume" (R:2748, 2756, 2765);
     - "a barren tree" (R:2743), which matches Bhatt's barren-woman reading;
     - an elder who keeps the old name because her tongue is used to it (R:4233–4238).
   - **Reverence as exclusion.** "he is close to God", but not invited (R:2915–2918). The priest who says she is the goddess's equal (R:4293). This is the exact shape of Bhatt's "veneration" and suits reaction text at other Hindu courts: praise that keeps her out of the wedding hall.
   - **The name on the document.** Signing under the old name, or a record that holds both names (R:4139–4145, 5494, 6220–6221). The faith's *A Name in Stone* already lets a daughter keep or change her name. A variant could have the temple clerk cut the old name beside the new, giving her a choice to pay to have it struck or let it stand.

5. **Women's kinship as recognition.**
   - The sister who insists she carry the lamp with the women over their mother's objection (R:3937–3951).
   - The sister-in-law who teaches the baby "aunty" (R:3966–3971).
   - The women at the tap (R:4220–4227).
   - **Design use.** The Pāvai vow already depends on the women of the court. A **sister** of the seeker (born kin) could be a high-value companion with her own line, and a mother who holds back could be a minus. This is a theme, not the Mariamman rite.

6. **Corroboration for the faith's stance against the third thing.**
   - Revathi's own claim is woman, in her words (R:421–422, 2254–2255, 6318–6320). The "neither" frame appears only in a community origin tale and in laments to God (§2.8).
   - This supports Bhatt's caution, which the faith page already follows. Use it in research notes only, never in game text.

### 4.2 Must not

- **Must not present the hijra/aravani community as one of the mod's faiths.** The same goes for its households, its rites and its goddess worship. The Mālai has no link to it. No *jamaat*, guru–chela house, seven houses, *naik*, *reeth*, *nirvaanam* or *thayamma*, forty-day rule, *jok*, milk-pot rite, Pothiraja Mata or Santhoshi Mata, Koovagam, Aravan, *thali* to Aravan, *roundap*, *badaai* or *hamam* may appear as a Garland institution, rite, mechanic, trait, decision or holy site.
- **Must not transplant any of the community's practices into a medieval faith as if they were historical.** No event may imply that hijra or aravani institutions existed in Tamil country in 867–1453, or that the Garland is their ancestor. The nani's Rāmāyaṇa origin tale (R:1140–1161) is not scripture for anyone in the mod.
- **Must not use the community's own words in localisation or keys.** That includes pottai, aravani, hijra, chela, guru, nani, gurubai, kaalaguru, parivar, jamaat, nirvaanam, danda, paampaduthi and jiyo. The faith's own vocabulary (Daughter of God, *tōḻi*, the share, the staff) is enough.
- **Must not use modern slurs** (Number 9, *Ali*, *khoja*, "girl-boy", "original or duplicate") as medieval reaction text. Write period-plausible hostility from period sources instead (Zwilling and Sweet, Doniger, Bhatt's barren-woman reading).
- **Must not frame anyone as a "third gender"**, and must not cite Revathi's laments or the origin tale as a self-description. Follow her: she considers herself a woman (R:6319).
- **Must not turn reverence into a mechanic.** No "daughters bring luck", first-buyer blessing, flower choice, or "a daughter's word comes true". This is the veneration-as-marginalisation that Bhatt and Revathi both describe (R:1131, 1231–1232, 4293).
- **Must not use any suicide or self-harm content** (§3), including the kerosene scene, the glass cut, Famila's death and the moments of ideation. No event, flavour text or trait may draw on them.
- **Must not depict** sexual violence, custodial torture, bodily inspection ("are they real?"), the castration surgery or its aftermath. Misnaming in game is spoken and social. It is never a body check.
- **Must not sexualise the temple street**, and must not tie sex work to the Garland or to the temple women. This matches the existing devadāsī caution. If the faith's events ever refer to the labour of the shareless, they do so from the daughter's side and without scenes.
- **Must not put Revathi, Famila, Mya, Shakuntala, her guru or any named person in the game**, nor any recognisable episode of their lives.
- **Must not quote the memoir in localisation**, or put its lines, paraphrased closely enough to recognise, in a character's mouth. Unlike Bhatt's note, this note offers **no** lines for in-world adaptation.
- **Must not cite the memoir as evidence for medieval practice.** It can be cited in design notes as a modern testimony that shaped tone and ethics. The faith page's source list should not add it under "Primary sources".
- **Must not copy book text into the repository** beyond the short quotations in this note.
