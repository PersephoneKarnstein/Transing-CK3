## 2. The argument in brief

- **A state agency.** From the 1270s the Angevin counts taxed the Jews of Provence as a body. The *tallia judeorum* was 2,000 pounds a year in two instalments, which assumes c. 4,000 households, or some 20,000 Jews (p. 70). The Jews collected it themselves through a *Universitas Judeorum Provincie*. A 1334 assembly at Barjols split Jewish Provence into three "lands" (*Aquensis*, *Riparia* along the Rhône, *Montanea*), each sending two syndics, three "majors" and three "minors". It had a permanent office with six staff in Aix (pp. 18, 70–71, 79). After the plague Queen Joanna halved the tax (pp. 71, 83). This "super-community" survived to the expulsion c. 1500 (p. 18).
- **Weak local autonomy.** Provence has no communal register and few responsa. The counts and lords "were zealous in keeping jurisdiction over Jews" (p. 68). Jews themselves used Latin notaries, civil courts and even church courts (pp. 10, 68).
- **The ban needed the lord's licence.** Jewish excommunication (*ḥerem*, *niddui*) could be used only with the lord's leave (pp. 81, 84–90). Abba Mari wrote from Montpellier, c. 1305: "we have no right to excommunicate except with the consent of our lord" (p. 86). The licence was granted for a set term and for set purposes (§3.3).
- **Tolerance and its limits.** In 1320 Robert of Naples said that nowhere in the world were Jews so well treated (pp. 11, 15). Yet Provence was "not a medieval paradise" (p. 11). The Inquisition pursued baptised Jews who had returned. There were ritual-murder rumours, the 1348 massacres, and the 1424–30 riots at Manosque and Aix (p. 11).
- **Oligarchy.** A few rich families ran the communities. Kalonymus's portrait of the plutocrat (*Even Boḥan* pp. 36–37) is quoted in full (pp. 74–75, 82).

## 3. What matters for the design

### 3.1 Community structure

- **Arles, 1215.** The oldest privilege lets the Jews of Arles elect **three** leaders "in the manner of the Christian consuls", with considerable judicial powers (pp. 77, 152, 154). Their virtues are listed as *prudentes, idonei, boni, honestes* (p. 154).
- **Arles, 1405: the *bayloni*.** The archbishop's vicar, acting as official, licenses the community's leaders, "called *bayloni*", to pronounce the ban (p. 87; §3.3).
- **Reillanne, 1313: two *berurim*.** The only surviving Provençal communal ordinance (Paris, BnF héb. 651) names two officers, "R. Isaac the *barur*" and "R. Samuel the *barur*". They need the community's consent to spend money. The alms chest is kept in one's house and the other holds the key, and "it shall not be opened save with the leave of both" (p. 60). The ordinance also sets rules for hosting "guests" (refugees from England, Anjou and France), **charity "for the marriage of a daughter or sister"**, and the king's tax (pp. 60, 82). Disputes over its wording go to Rabbi Isaac ha-Cohen of Manosque (p. 63).
- **Syndics and consuls** elsewhere (Aix, Manosque, Marseille). At Manosque three *sindici* led the community (p. 317) and appointed the cantor for the High Holy Days (p. 320). Aix in 1336 had syndics Vitalis Astrugui, Jacob de Stella and Bonsenhor de Rocamartina (p. 159). They fixed meat prices in the Jewish butchery, and half of each fine went to the community's alms, *elemosina* (pp. 160–161).
- **The court of three, ad hoc.** Arles had no standing Jewish court in 1387. "For each case one elected three arbitrators" (p. 296). They sat in the house of "a physician of great renown", Master Abraham Avigdor (p. 301). At Manosque the rabbi and two colleagues sat as *arbitratores compromissores*, "friends of the parties", and the families then registered the outcome with a Christian notary (p. 192).
- **The rabbi is "Magister".** In Latin documents the rabbi is *Magister*, or "Herbinus" and "Erbi" as the notaries heard "rabbi" (p. 191). Isaac ha-Cohen kept a yeshivah of 22 students at Manosque (pp. 191–192 n. 23).
- **Burial.** At Manosque, Leonetus was "in charge of the funerary needs of the community" (p. 316). That is the nearest thing to a confraternity office in the Manosque documents.
- **Confraternity and school, Arles, 15th century.** Hildenfinger's Arles documents (summarised p. 152) record "the foundation of a charitable confraternity" and of a public school for poor children, paid for by a rich physician's bequest (pp. 152–153). Shatzmiller does not say whether women belonged to it.
- **Marseille: two communities.** Benjamin of Tudela (c. 1166) found two *qehillot* of about 300 souls each, by the sea and up by the tower: the bishop's upper town and the count's lower town (p. 138). Each had its own syndics in 1278, under a third, city-wide *universitas* for the tax (pp. 141–142). The two merged only around 1348 (p. 141).
- **Size.** Aix in 1341 had 203 households and 1,205 persons (5.9 per hearth). Clans of 20–30 lived under one roof (p. 156). The same year Aix restricted where Jews might live, "a sort of ghetto" with its bounds written down (p. 156). In 1446 Arles paid 570 of 2,475 florins (20.6%), second only to Aix (p. 94). Arles left the tax union in 1382 and later rejoined (p. 100). Many settlements were tiny: Gordes had four families (p. 182), Reillanne seven taxpayers, Viens three (p. 182).

### 3.2 The synagogue, the street, and women's roles

- **The synagogue was "the schools".** The Latin records say *scolae* (Provençal *escolas*), *scole seu sinagoga* (pp. 311 n. 1, 325, 353). At Manosque the synagogue was a converted house (p. 311). There were two, "one for the well-to-do and another for the poor", "perhaps two floors of one and the same building" (p. 321).
- **Women in the synagogue.** Shatzmiller finds no explicit women's section or gallery. Women attended on the Sabbath, sometimes with infants (pp. 345, 353). In 1292 Blanca silenced Comprada "in the presence of many": "you should not talk in a place where respectable ladies are, nor where I am" (p. 312 n. 2, his translation). The phrase implies a recognised place of the "good ladies" (*bone domine*), *unverified* as a separate gallery. In 1338 a quarrel between two women, Petita and Provinciala, began inside the schools. It spilled into the street, where their kin joined in; the court records that it "totally disturbed" the Sabbath service (pp. 345, 353–357).
- **The *benda*.** Witnesses say Petita came out *desbendada*, her *benda* (head-band or veil) torn off, and that Provinciala "was binding on her *benda*" afterwards (pp. 355–357). A woman's head-band, *unverified* as a specifically married woman's covering.
- **The Jewish street.** The records say *carreria*, and the Jewish quarter *juiverie*. At Manosque Petita was dragged "from the synagogue door through the street" (p. 353).
- **A widow speaks in the assembly.** At a Manosque assembly of the Jews in 1296, every man present agreed with the two leaders, Joseph d'Aleste and Bonusnometus. The minute records one exception: "Rosa ... speaking for herself and for her family" (p. 217, Latin n. 33), who refused to litigate and sided with the town's syndics. Shatzmiller calls her "mistress of her faculties", ready for "a non-conformist attitude" (p. 218).
- **Women in credit and court.** Hundreds of Jewish and Christian women appear in the Manosque registers: they "buy and sell, testify in court and contract debts, exchange insults and even come to blows" (p. 209). Rosa ran lending in grain and coin through factors and lawyers (pp. 213–214). She tested a clipped coin in the open street and refused it (p. 215). Aster lent in coin and wheat in 1313 (p. 63). Jewish women swore "on the holy law of Moses" as witnesses and as accused (pp. 353–357).
- **Marriage law in use.** There were proxy marriage by a messenger carrying the *shetar shiduḥin* and the *shetar sheliḥut qiddushin* (Arles–Valencia, p. 299); marriage brokers and notarised dowry contracts "in Hebrew or in Latin or in both" (p. 302); and girls betrothed by fathers and guardians. Bonahora was allowed to choose between two brothers (p. 193). Christians were sometimes invited to Jewish weddings, "to the great indignation of the Church" (p. 302).

### 3.3 Lords, licences and protection

This section grounds the sect's "landless, under another lord" play.

- **The ban was licensed by the lord, for a term.**
  - *Arles, 1405.* The archbishop's vicar licensed the *bayloni* to use the ban for two purposes only: honest tax declarations, and punishing "those who break the Law of Moses or the rules of the community". Every sentence had to be reported to him, and the licence ran **for one year** (p. 87).
  - *Marseille, late 14th century.* The official authorises the elected Jews to pronounce "the Jewish curse called in Hebrew *herem*", *in scolis et sinagogis* (p. 88).
  - *Manosque, 1310.* The Hospitallers' court indicted Rabbi Isaac de Talardo for banning a congregant "without a mandate obtained from the court". The court called this "pretending to have temporal jurisdiction" (p. 89). A side effect: the community's circumciser refused the excommunicate's son, a second man botched the circumcision, and the child died.
  - *Montpellier, 1305.* Both factions asked the lord. He allowed only one of three proposed bans (pp. 86–87).
- **A ten-year privilege.** At Gordes, 14 September 1312, Guiraud de Simiane, lord of Apt and Gordes, granted four Jewish householders the right to live and lend in his town "for ten consecutive and complete years", renewable. They paid four gold florins a year on Christmas Eve and registered their loans with his notaries (p. 182). Lords of Trets (1350) granted similar privileges (p. 73).
- **"They are ours."** In the 1262 treaty with Marseille the count claimed the Jews as his: "they are ours" and "belong to our treasury". This is the genesis of their status as *servi camere* (p. 16). In 1276 Charles I ordered the inquisitor of Provence to stop extorting money from the Jews "which belongs to us", to release Jews he had jailed, and to impose no new badges (p. 20).
- **The *conservator judeorum*.** By the late 14th century a royal official guarded Jewish privileges and judged their cases. In 1446 his office took 500 of 2,475 florins (p. 94 and n. 13).
- **Courts and fines.** Christian courts heard synagogue brawls on their own authority, as breaches of the peace and as disturbance of "divine office". The fines were income for the lord (pp. 321–324). The court's language shows respect for *officium divinum* in the synagogue, not contempt (p. 323).
- **Christian courts as mediators.** In 1336 the archbishop of Aix himself mediated a peace between two feuding Jewish factions (pp. 73, 165). The archbishop of Aix, Robert de Mauvoisin, kept a Jewish astrologer, Mossé de Trets, in 1317 (p. 11).
- **Dues to the archbishop of Arles.** In the 15th century the Arles Jews paid "pepper and wax" to the archbishop and the crown (p. 152).

### 3.4 Physicians and translators

This section grounds `tm_hipukh.0030` and a possible physician variant.

- **Angevin translators.** All are men.
  - *Farag (Faraj) of Agrigento*, interpreter to Charles I, translated al-Rāzī's medical encyclopaedia *al-Hawi* (*Continens*) from Arabic into Latin between 6 Feb. 1278 and 12 Feb. 1279. He was paid to oversee the copyists. Paris BnF lat. 6912 holds the book and portraits of him, which make him "the earliest Jewish man of letters whose face is known" (pp. 7–8, 22–23). In June 1278 Charles demanded a tract on "sickness of the ears" from him: "Send us the book immediately" (p. 22).
  - *Moses of Salerno* translated a treatise on healing horses (p. 23).
  - *Kalonymus ben Kalonymus of Arles*, an Arabist who first translated science into Hebrew for Hebraists, was invited to work for Latinists (p. 13). Robert met him during his stay in Avignon (1319–24) and asked him to translate from Arabic into Latin, "although Kalonymos had not previously done such work" (p. 23).
  - *Judah Romano*, a Latinist in Rome, joined Kalonymus at Robert's court. The two may have worked together on Averroes' *Destructio destructionis* (Vat. lat. 2434) (pp. 23–24 and n. 30).
- **A translator who won privileges, possibly.** Immanuel of Rome wrote to Kalonymus's family that he was instrumental in obtaining royal privileges for the Jews. Shatzmiller can link this to Robert's boast only with "possibly" (p. 24).
- **Physicians.** Jewish physicians "by dozens or even hundreds treated a Christian clientele", against hostile legislation, and often worked with Christian colleagues (p. 11). Named physicians:
  - Master Leo, physician at Manosque, Mandina's father (pp. 211–212);
  - Abraham Avigdor, at Arles (p. 301);
  - Bonafos Barbavayre of Avignon, who lent the tax syndics 1,500 florins in 1336 (p. 100);
  - Bonsenhor Dieulogart, at Aix (p. 158);
  - Abraham Salamonis, *medicus* of Saint-Maximin, exempted from the tax (p. 93 n. 11).
  - Provençal statutes of 1294–1310 regulated, among other things, "the practice of medicine" by Jews (p. 183).
- **No women physicians or translators** appear in this volume.

### 3.5 Expulsions, conversions, refugees

- **Anjou 1289**: Charles II expelled the Jews of Anjou and let them live in Provence (pp. 5, 15). **England 1290**: English refugees settled at Manosque (*Anglicus*, the "de Chartres" family; pp. 277–278). **France 1306**: the Narbonne *nasi* family was expelled with all the others (p. 130). Reillanne's 1313 ordinance and Manosque's measures of 1311 were written to feed and house these "guests" (p. 82). Kalonymus refused to study at Manosque because its Jews were "all French", whose ways he did not like (pp. 64, 276).
- **Southern Italy, c. 1290–94**: forced mass conversion of some 1,300 Jews (pp. 7, 20).
- **1348**: Provence was where the plague riots began (p. 11). Hundreds, perhaps thousands, were killed (p. 71).
- **Provence, c. 1500**: the tax body existed "until the expulsion of the Jews in 1500" (p. 18). After the expulsion, *néophytes* who converted rather than leave were taxed as a group under Louis XII (pp. 108–109). The volume does not treat **1394** (the French royal expulsion).
- **Conversion and return.**
  - *Bella*, a beguine at Lausanne for five years, returned to Judaism to marry. Shatzmiller calls this the first known case of apostasy under the bull *Turbato corde* (p. 218). The Inquisition hounded her mother Rosa for twenty years (pp. 218–221).
  - *Bonisac Dulcini*, Aix's leading syndic, converted between 1336 and 1345 and took the name *Elsacius* (p. 166).
  - A *de Ratel* brother testified in 1339 "on the Holy Gospels", having converted (p. 344).
  - The Angevin state treated a convert as a lost taxpayer and seized his goods (p. 19).

### 3.6 Narbonne and Kalonymus

- **The *nasi* of Narbonne.** The Kalonymides of Narbonne held the title *nasi*, rendered *rex judeus* in Latin. They claimed Davidic descent, and Benjamin of Tudela says the ruler of the city had given them lands "of which no man can forcibly dispossess him" (p. 130). The 1217 tax exempted the "honour of the Jewish king" (p. 131). Their seal bore a Davidic lion. They lasted until 1306 (pp. 130–131).
- **Kalonymus.** Shatzmiller dates Kalonymus "1286–after 1328" (p. 74). The *Even Boḥan* was finished in Tebet 5083, i.e. between 10 Dec. 1322 and 7 Jan. 1323 (p. 317 n. 13). He returned to Arles on 18 April 1328, and in 1329 the treasurer of Provence paid "master Calonus" six gold ounces for translating (p. 24). This is "the last we hear of him".
- **Kalonymus's Day of Atonement.** Kalonymus describes a quarrel between families that wrecked every service of a Yom Kippur, "presumably in his own town, Arles" (pp. 317–318). Shatzmiller treats it as resting on real events.

3. **Who licenses, and who shuns** (`tm_hipukh.0003`, README "Before the elders"). Historically the Jewish worthies could not shun (*niddui*) on their own authority in Provence. The Christian lord or archbishop licensed the ban, for a term (Arles 1405: one year) and for listed purposes (pp. 86–89). "The worthies grant the county a licence" inverts that. The worthies' *ruling* is fine. The *licence to live so* belongs to the lord, and so does the licence to shun. See §5.
4. **"The archbishop confined the Jews of Arles to a single street"** (Chotzner, in `jud_kalonymus_even_bohan.md`). Shatzmiller neither confirms nor denies this for Arles. His evidence for a bounded quarter is Aix in 1341 (p. 156). Keep it attributed to Chotzner.
5. **The women's gallery** (`tm_hipukh.0010`). Not attested for Provence in this volume. Nothing contradicts it, and Blanca's "place where respectable ladies are" (p. 312) suggests a women's place. Mark it *unverified*, or have the loc say "among the women" instead of "in the women's gallery".
6. **`tm_hipukh.0030` "A Book in Arabic"**. The real pattern is Arabic → **Latin** for Christian princes (al-Hawi, the horse treatise, Averroes), paid in gold ounces, at Naples, Rome or Arles (pp. 22–24). "A language my scholars can read" is right in substance. The documented translators are all men, so a woman translator is the faith's own claim, which is fine.

## 5. Design implications

- **Names.** Apply §1's weighted list. Optionally split `tm_hipukh.0002` into "a name from the scroll" (Dinah, Esther, Sara) and "a name of the street" (Rosa, Mairona, Bella, Regina, Blanca and the rest).
- **Two licences, both from the lord.**
  - `tm_worthies_licence` (10 years) stays, re-voiced as **the lord's licence**, modelled on the Gordes privilege (ten years, renewable, a yearly payment at Christmas, p. 182). Suggested loc: "The lord of the county has confirmed the privilege for ten years. The *bayloni* have no more to say."
  - Add a **herem licence** on the county, a variable lasting 1 year (Arles 1405). *Niddui* in `0003` (and in `0040`'s "let the worthies decide") should apply only if the county holds a herem licence or its holder is Jewish. Otherwise the shunning cannot be pronounced: "The *bayloni* have no licence from the archbishop to pronounce the ban."
  - A lord who took her people under protection in `0030.a` can **refuse** the kahal's request for a herem licence. That gives the landless play its teeth. Historically the archbishop could, and the Manosque court prosecuted an unlicensed ban (p. 89).
- **"Before the elders" as a court of three.** Arles named three arbiters for each case and sat them in a physician's house (pp. 296, 301). The elders could be "the three named for the case", and the hearing could be in the house of a physician of the street. The Arles 1215 virtues (*prudentes, boni, honestes*, p. 154) can stand in for "the worthies".
- **New small events (proposals).**
  - **"Where the Good Ladies Sit."** In the schools on the Sabbath, a woman tells her: "you should not talk in a place where respectable ladies are, nor where I am" (adapting p. 312). Options: answer, appeal to the *bayloni*, or go home. If it turns into a brawl, the lord's court fines it (pp. 322–323), which ties to the county licence.
  - **"The Widow Speaks for Her House."** A Rosa de Grassa-type widow of the sect, at the community assembly, speaks "for herself and for her family" against the leaders (p. 217). It reinforces `0003.c` ("I am the lady of the house"). The Inquisition subplot (Bella the beguine) is usable for a Christian-hostility branch, but only as doctrine and procedure, never as a Jewish villain.
  - **"The Benda."** When the women of the ḥevrah name her on Shushan Purim (`0002`), they tie a *benda* on her head (pp. 355–357). This is a period-true visible sign, with no claim beyond "a woman's head-band".
  - **Dowry charity.** Reillanne kept a fund "for the marriage of a daughter or sister" (p. 60). A ḥevrah decision could pay a sister's marriage portion: a small gold cost and opinion with the ḥevrah.
- **Physician variant of `0030`.** "A Physician of the Street": a daughter of the Turning treats the lord. Options: retain her as court physician (Jewish physicians served Christians "by dozens or even hundreds", p. 11), or bar her (the statutes of 1294–1310 regulated Jewish medicine, p. 183). Model the commissioned translation on Farag's *al-Hawi*, a year's work with the scribes paid on top (p. 22), and on the six gold ounces paid to Kalonymus in 1329 (p. 24).
- **Holy sites.** Narbonne is supported as the seat of the Davidic *nasi* who held unalienable land (pp. 130–131). In loc it should be a place of memory after 1306. Montpellier is supported by Abba Mari and the 1305 ban controversy (pp. 85–87). Manosque is an option for flavour (Isaac ha-Cohen's yeshivah, the richest women's records), not a holy site.
- **Antisemitic content to avoid.** The volume records the 1296 ritual-murder rumour at Manosque (p. 219), coin-clipping charges against English Jews (pp. 215, 278), Jewish moneylending, and Jewish hired violence (pp. 299–304). None of this should become event text. The moneylending context may appear only as plain economy (grain loans, notaries), never as a trait or a motive.
