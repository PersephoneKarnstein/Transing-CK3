**Scope and bias.** An art-history volume. It is strong on buildings, endowments and inscriptions, and weak on events. Akhīs get two chapters, both cautious about what the sources prove. Dorylaion is never named, but **Eskişehir (Dorylaion) is in Goshgarian's list of towns with akhīs** (p. 121), and **Kütahya**, about 80 km away, is one of Selçuk's five centres of akhī endowments (pp. 97–98). Distances and the identity Eskişehir = Dorylaion are general knowledge.

**Relation to other notes.** Read with `byz_peacock_urban_notables.md`, which argues that the akhīs were tied to sultans, and `byz_denicola_chobanids_kastamonu.md`, the frontier dynasty. For bands generally, see `byz_sophoulis_banditry.md`.

**Relevance:** high for akhī hospitality, initiation and craft rules, and for how Christians and Christian buildings lasted under Seljuk lords. Moderate for women as patrons (one rich case, Mahperi Khatun). Low but real for women in brotherhoods (one late source and a scatter of titles). Nil for eunuchs and gender crossing.

## 1. Futuwwa and the akhīs: origins and who joined

- **Caliphal futuwwa.** The Abbasid caliph al-Nasir (r. 1180–1225) brought futuwwa into court life and invited the Seljuk sultan Izz al-Din Kay Kawus (r. 1211–20) into it with an emissary and gifts (Selçuk, p. 95). He sent Suhrawardi (d. 1234) to spread his reformed version in Anatolia (Goshgarian, p. 119).
- **The first written code** was al-Qushayri's (d. 1074). It stressed learning the code from a master by emulation (Goshgarian, p. 118). So the code is post-1055 in written form, and the Anatolian brotherhoods flourish in the 13th–14th centuries (p. 119).
- **The word.** *Futuwwa* means "male youthfulness", yet entry required physical maturity (*bulūgh*) (Goshgarian, p. 119). *Akhī* is glossed as Arabic "my brother", or a version of Turkish *akı*, "brave man", the title of "master" members (p. 130 n. 24).
- **Members.** Ibn Battuta: the akhī is a man whom "artisans and other unmarried, single young men" make their leader (quoted in Peacock 2021, p. 23; Goshgarian, p. 121).
- **Faith.** Every Islamic Anatolian futuwwa treatise names non-Muslims as the first group to exclude (Goshgarian, p. 121). Yet Aflaki mentions **Armenian akhīs in Konya and Sivas**, and an inscription near Konya suggests **a Greek akhī** (p. 121). Goshgarian supposes that "not all akhis in late medieval Anatolia were actually Muslim" (p. 121).
- **Christian brotherhoods of the same kind.** Two Armenian futuwwa treatises (*manktut'iwn*, "the qualities of being a male youth") were written at Erzincan by the priest Yovhannēs (d. 1293) (pp. 123, 130 n. 33). The Armenian title *manktawag*, "leader of the youths", is attested from the 11th to the 15th centuries (p. 130 n. 33). Selçuk notes parallel Christian organisations "around futuwwa-like ethics" (p. 101).
- **Conversion.** Scholars have often said akhīs and dervishes converted Christians. Armenian sources do not blame them for it; they blame the Mongols for forced mass conversions (Goshgarian, p. 121).

## 2. Initiation, oaths, dress and rules

- **The girdle (*şedd*).** Entry and promotion were marked by tying a girdle on the pupil. It stood for chastity and for the craft. Each craft had its own way of girdling (Selçuk, p. 105).
- **Craft tools at initiation.** In the *Fütüvvetnâme-i Kebîr*, the barbers' rite uses a stone and a razor and names Salman al-Farisi. The baker's apprentice gets a work shirt. The bathhouse apprentice gets a vest, turban, short breeches and a shawl girdle. The butcher's gets a large knife and a sharpener. The carpenter's gets a saw and a hammer (Selçuk, p. 105). Tanners invoke **Ahi Evran**, the patron saint of Anatolian tanners (p. 105).
- **Three ranks and an oath on the scales.** There were three ceremonies, up to "the one who is bound with a girdle" (*sahib-i fütüvvet*). The master lifts the merchant's scales and tells the apprentice to trade with equity, never give short weight, and remember the scales of the hereafter. The apprentice kisses the masters' hands (Selçuk, p. 107).
- **Patron saints of crafts**, named when opening a shop, in prayer and at girdlings (p. 105). The girdling's own legend runs from Adam, girdled by Gabriel, who saved flour and honey for Eve because she was absent. Hence *helva* is sent to those absent from the ceremony (p. 112 n. 52).
- **The six traits:** open the face (friendliness), the door (hospitality) and the tablecloth (generosity); close the eyes, the tongue and "the belt of his trousers" (p. 111 n. 44).
- **Work is a duty.** Nasiri (1290): "Always have an occupation" (Selçuk, p. 104). Al-Burgazi: "Akhis must have a craft to keep them occupied" (p. 104). This is the difference from Sufis, who disliked trade (pp. 102–104).
- **Market law.** Futuwwa manuals parallel the *hisba* rules of the market inspector: a just price, full weight, destroy false coins (pp. 106–107).
- **The Armenian girdle** is "the belt of the travellers", drawn along the loins "to bridle desire" (Yovhannēs, book's tr., Goshgarian, p. 128).
- **Daily life prescribed** down to how to walk, eat, drink and laugh (Goshgarian, p. 119). Every treatise worries about "pretenders" (p. 122).

## 3. Hospitality: the lodge and the road

- **The daily round.** The akhī builds a lodge (*zāwiya*) with furnishings and lamps. Members work by day and bring him their earnings in the afternoon. Guests are put up and fed. If none come, they eat, sing and dance (Ibn Battuta, cited by Selçuk, pp. 104–105).
- **For the guest:** a meal, the bath, sweetmeats and fruit, the Qur'an, then singing and dancing (p. 105).
- **The Armenian rule for guests.** If a *vardapet* (teacher) comes from another country, the head brothers visit and honour him, and send him off "with love" and gifts. They do the same for bishops, monks and hermits (Yovhannēs, Goshgarian, p. 127).
- **Leader as refuge.** The leader of the youths becomes "a place of meeting for those lacking a leader and for those who are foreign" (Yovhannēs, p. 127).
- **Where the lodges were.** Endowment registers (1220–1600) put akhī hospices around **Ankara (22 endowments), Kütahya (11), Kastamonu (11), Tokat (10)**, with few at Bursa and İznik (4) (Selçuk, p. 98, Table 4.1). They sat on trade routes. Selçuk tentatively says the route "corresponds to the Byzantine frontier" on the Nicaean and Pontic sides (p. 98).
- **The lodges linked town and road.** They may have helped merchants travel safely (Selçuk, pp. 97, 102).
- **Sufi and akhī buildings cannot be told apart** (pp. 100–101). Pancaroğlu: caravanserais, Sufi and akhī lodges all gave food and lodging to passers-by (p. 100). Caravanserais clustered on the Konya–Aksaray–Kayseri road (p. 101).
- **The caravanserai network.** About 300 road caravanserais were built under the Seljuks, mostly 1180s–1280s, on the routes from Konya and Kayseri to the Mediterranean and the Black Sea (Tavernari, p. 59). They served "merchants, pilgrims or wanderers" and projected the state into the landscape (p. 87).
- **Aflaki's map of who sleeps where.** A madrasa is for imams, a khanaqah for shaykhs, a caravanserai for merchants, and "lodges (zaviya) for the rogues (runud)" (Baha al-Din Walad via Aflaki, Goshgarian, p. 115). The same text shows each group in buildings "not their own" (p. 115).

## 4. The akhīs' political role (this book's view)

- **Ottoman land grants.** In the Bursa cadastral surveys (1487 survey, listing grants from Osman, r. 1290–1324, onwards), akhīs got far fewer grants than sheikhs and prayer leaders. Osman gave 2 to akhīs, 4 to sheikhs (Selçuk, p. 99, Table 4.2). Selçuk suggests akhīs lost influence early where the Ottomans centralised (pp. 97, 108).
- **Ankara.** The "akhī republic" was dismissed long ago, but akhīs were real local leaders and patrons (Blessing, p. 207). Two brothers paid to restore the Aslanhane Mosque (inscription 1298). Akhi Sharaf al-Din's mausoleum is dated 1330; he died in 1351 (p. 207).
- **Ibn Battuta's akhīs** are useful but "need to be taken with a pinch of salt" (Blessing, p. 207).
- **Selçuk doubts any direct tie between akhīs and Ottoman guilds.** The title was used loosely in Ottoman times (p. 101 and n. 30).

## 5. Women in the brotherhoods, sexuality, eunuchs and gender crossing

- **The *bacıyan-ı rum*.** The 15th-century Ottoman chronicler Aşıkpaşazade names four groups of medieval Anatolia: frontier warlords (*gaziyan-ı rum*), dervishes (*abdalan-ı rum*), akhīs (*ahiyan-ı rum*) and "the women's branch of akhi associations" (*bacıyan-ı rum*) (Selçuk, p. 96).
- **Women with the akhī title** do appear in sources of the period. How they got it is unclear: perhaps as daughters or wives of prominent akhīs. Land grants to such women could be inherited (Selçuk, pp. 96–97). Bayram is "quite certain" the women's organisation existed (p. 109 n. 5). Selçuk is not.
- **Goshgarian:** futuwwa "was occasionally practised by women" (p. 120), citing the titles and Aşıkpaşazade.
- **The prescriptive rule excludes women.** Most Sufi and akhī texts specifically exclude women (Goshgarian, p. 120). Futuwwa "explicitly prefers masculinity" (p. 121).
- **The Armenian exception.** The first Erzincan treatise sets prayer for both sexes: each male makes 12 genuflexions, "every female, 20". Goshgarian reads women as "tangential or associate members" (p. 126).
- **Celibacy and marriage.** Members were to stay virgin until the marriage crown and avoid "foreign" women after it; one who does not is "called a dog and not a man" (Yovhannēs, pp. 126–27).
- **Homosocial intimacy.** Goshgarian reads master–novice bonds, guest hospitality and Kostandin of Erzincan's wine poems (d. 1314) as ambiguously homoerotic (pp. 122–28). This is **her interpretation**, which she calls tentative (p. 129). A hostile Cairo critic, al-Turkumani (early 14th c.), attacked older members gazing at "the naked bodies" of youths when investing them with "the pants of futuwwa" (p. 130 n. 30).
- **Eunuchs: none** in any chapter read or searched.
- **Gender crossing: none.** The only disguise is religious: a hagiography of Hacı Bektaş has a man who looks like a Christian monk and is really a Muslim dervish, and the *Battalname* has "a Sufi under the guise of a monk" (Öztürk, p. 149 and n. 48).
- **Design consequence.** A women's branch of the akhīs is attested only in one late chronicle and some titles. That is enough for **the faith's own voice** to claim a sisterhood of the road. The narrator must say the evidence is thin and late (15th c.). Nothing here supports trans women in any brotherhood.

## 6. Christians, churches and convents under Turkish lords

- **Cappadocia did not end in 1071.** Churches were carved and painted again in the 13th century (Öztürk, p. 138).
- **Two rulers named.** Inscriptions name the Nicaean emperor Theodore I Laskaris (1204–22), the earliest at Karşı Kilise, dated 1212. St George at Belisırma names Sultan Masud II (1282–1304) alongside Andronikos II (1282–1328) (Öztürk, p. 138 and n. 11). Öztürk reads these as the "dual identity" of Christians under Turkish rule.
- **Convent and lodge are easily swapped.** After Yürekli, Öztürk says a Christian monastery converts easily to a Sufi convent. The prayer niche (*mihrab*) is "the primary difference" (p. 148).
- **Shared saints.** Christians came to Hacı Bektaş's shrine until the 19th century, believing a Christian saint, **Charalambos**, lay there (Öztürk, p. 150). St George and Khidr were venerated by both faiths, and military saints were popular (Yalman, pp. 243–44). St Amphilochius at Konya became "Plato" (Aflatun) and was visited by Christians and Muslims for centuries before it became a masjid (Yalman, p. 240).
- **Seyyid Battal Gazi at Seyitgazi**, "a site on the western borders of the Seljuk realm", was "discovered" by a Seljuk royal woman, the "sultan's mother" (Yalman, p. 243). That Seyitgazi (ancient Nakoleia) lies about 40 km south of Eskişehir is general knowledge, not from the book. **This is the nearest shared shrine to Dorylaion in any of the three sources.**
- **Christians in the population.** The west of Anatolia was overwhelmingly Greek in the early 13th century; Sinop and Antalya were mainly Greek Christian. An Egyptian source of 1207 says Greeks enjoyed fair treatment (McClary, p. 58 n. 94, citing others). Vryonis held that Christians remained the majority and the main tax source in the later 13th century (same note; McClary calls him "dated and at times questionable").
- **Christian craftsmen** probably worked under a Muslim amir or architect. Whether their names are missing from buildings because of their *dhimmī* status is unknown (McClary, p. 32).
- **Churches kept for Christian queens.** Churches remained in Konya and Alanya for Christian royal wives free to practise their faith (Yalman, p. 236). Chapels survive in Seljuk palaces (p. 236).
- **A woman builds a church.** Gurji Khatun, Kaykhusraw II's Georgian wife and Rumi's patron, may have commissioned Kırkdamaltı Kilise in the Ihlara valley, and may be the woman shown giving a donation to St George (Yalman, p. 246).
- **Converts both ways.** The amir Hasan Gabras apostatised from Islam about 1179; Gurji Khatun's father also became Christian (Yalman, p. 243).
- **Mixed marriage words.** The children of mixed marriages were *mixovarvaroi* in Byzantine sources and *iğdiş* in Seljuk ones (Yalman, p. 225). See the caution in §9.

## 7. Women as patrons: Mahperi Khatun and the Seljuk queens

- **Who she was.** Daughter of Kyr Vard, lord of Kalonoros (Alanya). When Sultan Kayqubad (r. 1220–37) took the city in 1221 after a long siege, the terms included her hand in exchange for a land grant, the city of Akşehir (Yalman, p. 231). Kyr Vard was a Christian, Greek or Armenian, present at Levon I's coronation in 1198 (pp. 231–32).
- **She built only as queen mother**, under her son Kaykhusraw II (r. 1237–46) (p. 232). Queen mothers built more than women of any other rank (Bates, cited p. 233).
- **What she built:**
  - the Kayseri complex: mosque (1237–38), madrasa, double bath and her own tomb tower (pp. 226–29);
  - two to six caravanserais on the roads north to the Black Sea, two of them attested by inscription (p. 232);
  - the zāwiya of Shaykh Turasan on a remote mountaintop, dated 1242–43, a shrine with hospice rooms and a kitchen (pp. 241–42).
- **Her titles.** "The great queen", "the wise", "the ascetic". Her cenotaph calls her "the Mary of her age" and "the Khadija of her time" (pp. 230–31). The cenotaph lid is a **reused late antique sarcophagus** (p. 237).
- **Did she convert?** Her son wrote to the Latin emperor Baldwin II stressing his mother's religious freedom, which indicates she had not converted (p. 236). Yalman reads her as holding both identities, following Shukurov's "dual identity" (pp. 226, 246).
- **The court historian erased her name.** Ibn Bibi never names her (p. 233), perhaps a damnatio memoriae after Kayqubad's suspicious death (pp. 235–36).
- **Her end.** After Köse Dağ (1243) she fled to Cilicia with her daughter and daughter-in-law. King Het'um seems to have handed the refugees to the Mongols (p. 239). She was last mentioned in 1254 (p. 244).
- **Other royal women as patrons:**
  - Kayqubad's cousin Ismat al-Dunya wa'l-Din built a mosque at Uluborlu (1231–32) "from her personal property", and two caravanserais north of Antalya (p. 234).
  - Malika Adiliyya, the Ayyubid wife, was killed with her sons in the succession struggle. Her daughters built her tomb in 1247–48 (p. 233).
  - Gawhar Nasiba, daughter of Qilij Arslan, had a hospital built for her by her brother in 1205 (p. 235).
- **Rules for women's titles.** Women born into the dynasty were "virtue of the world and religion" (*'ismat*). Women who married in and bore children were "purity …" (*safwat*). Most princesses' inscriptions give the title without a name (p. 235).
- **Endowment logic.** Elite women founded religious institutions more often than secular ones. Hans and bazaars were built as **income for the pious foundations** (Bates, cited p. 233; p. 245).

## 9. Risks and cautions

- **The *bacıyan-ı rum* are not proven.** One 15th-century chronicler, plus some women with the akhī title who may have inherited it (pp. 96–97). Use it only in the faith's voice.
- **Goshgarian's homoerotic reading** (pp. 122–28) is a tentative interpretation of ambiguous texts. Do not cite it as evidence of same-sex practice, and do not attach any faith to it.
- ***İğdiş*.** The books gloss it as the child of a mixed marriage (Yalman, p. 225) or a townsmen's office (Peacock 2021, pp. 26–27). The Turkish word also means a gelded animal. That is general knowledge, not in these books. **Do not build a eunuch mechanic on *iğdiş*.**
- **Ibn Battuta overstates** (Blessing, p. 207; Peacock 2021, pp. 23–26).
- **The "colonising dervish"** (Barkan) is a contested topos (Yalman, p. 243). Do not write Islamisation as a dervish conquest of Christians.
- **Nationalist historiography.** Selçuk (pp. 96, 108–109) and the editors (pp. 7–10) warn against reading akhīs as a purely Turkish institution or the period as Turkification. Keep loc neutral.
- **Do not write Turkmen or any people as raiders by nature.**
