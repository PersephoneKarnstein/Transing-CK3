# Nora Noralla on trans women, Islamic law and the Egyptian state: guardrails and answers for the Cairo storyline

Written 2026-10-26 for the Adelphai Dorleyaiou v2 storyline at the Fatimid court in Cairo, 1066 (`later_starts/mukhannathun_fatimid_cairo.md`). Nora Noralla is an Egyptian trans woman, a human-rights lawyer and researcher, and executive director of the Cairo 52 Legal Research Institute (Handbook, list of contributors). She writes from inside the community she studies (FA p. 89). On her own community, the project ranks her above outside scholars. **All three works are modern.** They are not evidence for 1066. This note uses them for three things: guardrails (§2), the strategies her community uses against religious-legal hostility, rewritten in a medieval idiom (§3), and a check of her few claims about the pre-modern past (§5).

## 1. The arguments in brief

### 1.1 YIMEL 2023: Al-Azhar's fatwas, health policy and the courts

**Thesis.** In modern Egypt, trans lives are governed "by Islamic Sharia more than anything else" (p. 1). Three Al-Azhar fatwas, from 1981, 1988 and 1997, set the rule: surgery is permitted to "reveal" a hidden sex, which in practice means for intersex people, and forbidden "at the mere wish to change sex" (pp. 9–16). Over the 2000s the rule passed into medical policy and, through a more religious judiciary, into the courts (pp. 5, 17–28).

**The fatwas (pp. 9–17).**
- **Gad al-Haq, 27 June 1981.** It answered a Malaysian enquirer (p. 9). Every later Sunni fatwa builds on it (p. 15).
- **Tantawi, 8 June 1988.** It was written in reply to a Medical Syndicate letter about an Al-Azhar medical student (pp. 11–13). Noralla reprints Skovgaard-Petersen's 1995 translation.
- **ʿAṭiyya Ṣaqr, May 1997.** It restates Gad al-Haq (pp. 13–15).

**The same medieval hadith run through all three.** Each fatwa rests on four texts:
- "God sent no disease without a cure";
- the physician who cut a vein for Ubayy ibn Kaʿb;
- the ʿArfaja hadith of the nose of gold, where the forbidden becomes lawful "when necessary for medical treatment" (p. 10);
- the Umm Salama hadith of the *mukhannath*, with the commentators' distinction between one who acts so "deliberately" and one who is so "by natural disposition" (p. 12).

Each fatwa then closes on the Anas hadith: the Prophet "cursed the hermaphrodites among the men and the over-masculine women, saying 'expel them from their houses'" (Tantawi, p. 13; also pp. 11, 15). **This is the same hadith the Cairo storyline uses as its hostile instrument** (*Daʿāʾim* §1597; Cairo note §2.1).

**Noralla's reading (pp. 15–17).**
- The fatwas "do permit SRS, but only for intersex people" (p. 16).
- Being trans is not a sin as such. It becomes one if the person "insist[s]" and does not seek the permitted treatment, which is therapy to accept the birth sex (p. 16).
- Behind this lies the scholars' "fixation on making everyone outside the binary, in their opinion, fit in the binary" (p. 16).
- Two further arguments came into the later debates: the body as God's gift, to be accounted for at the Resurrection, and the ban on "unwarranted change in Allah's creation", citing Q 95:4 (p. 17).

**From fatwa to policy (pp. 17–19).**
- **The vocabulary.** Fatwa discourse split *taṣḥīḥ al-jins* ("sex correction", permitted) from *taghyīr al-jins* ("sex change", sinful) (p. 17).
- **The 2003 ban.** That year the Medical Syndicate's code banned "sex change". It allowed "correction" only after a committee review, with two years of psychiatric evaluation and a chromosome map, and with an Al-Azhar representative on the committee (pp. 17–18).
- **The veto.** The committee stopped work in 2014. When it resumed in 2017, Al-Azhar's representative held a veto (p. 18). Between 2014 and 2017 no case was approved for gender identity disorder (p. 19).
- **The consequences.** Care was pushed into expensive underground clinics. A trans man bled to death in 2021 after a premature discharge, and there was no paper trail through which anyone could be held to account (p. 19).

**The courts (pp. 20–28).**
- **Before the shift.** Prosecutors and judges of the 1980s, "the product of the Nasserist secular era" (p. 23), deferred to forensic doctors. They cleared the surgeons in 1988–91 (pp. 23–25). They ordered the student readmitted in 1991, 1999 and finally 2006 (pp. 25–26).
  - The 1999 court reasoned that "society and authorities alike are now treating her as a woman" (p. 26).
  - Al-Azhar University has ignored the final judgment "to this day" (p. 26).
- **The shift.** By 2016 a court refused a trans man's change of documents because his transition broke Sharia (pp. 27–28). It cited Article 2 of the Constitution and the fatwas, and held that personal freedom is limited by "accepted social standards" (p. 28). A 2013 judgment had refused a trans woman on the same ground (p. 28).
- **The wider climate.** A state council deputy chair said in 2017, "Here in Egypt, public order is Islam" (p. 22).

**Context Noralla insists on.**
- In the 1980s, trans identity was cast as "a new Western phenomenon that would destroy the Islamic morals of Egyptian society" (p. 8). The charge fed the conspiracy talk of the *Infitāḥ* years (pp. 8–9).
- The fatwas leaned on the conversion-therapy wing of 1980s medicine, at a time when other doctors already backed surgery (pp. 7–8).
- The surgeon's being a Christian sharpened the 1988 scandal into a charge of an attack on a Muslim young man (p. 24).

**Openings (pp. 29–31).**
- Al-Azhar has reversed itself before, on child marriage, female genital cutting and organ donation (p. 29).
- In the Grand Imam's 2021 statement, "the word 'unwarranted' is the key to any possible change in that discourse" (p. 31).

### 1.2 FA 2025: trans Egyptians on identity and violence

**Method.** Noralla interviewed 15 trans Egyptians, 13 of them assigned male at birth (pp. 90, 93). She did so as an insider, and from exile, which she calls a privilege forced on her (pp. 89–90).

**Three understandings of "transgender" (pp. 91–95).**
1. **The majority view.** Being trans is a condition one is "born with", a medical "journey" from one binary to the other (pp. 91–92).
2. **The minority view**, among activists and the upper class. Being trans is free self-identification, but it cannot be lived openly in Egypt (p. 92).
3. **Two sex workers** who reject the label "trans woman" and use their clients' terms for themselves (pp. 92–93). The respectable majority blamed them for police attention. Noralla reads their reply as a reproach to those who "seek socio-religious validation" by distancing themselves from the "most deviant" (p. 94).

**One point of agreement.** All three groups held that pathologisation is "the only viable approach for survival" (p. 94). Society accepts only the medical, binary account (p. 94).

**The cycle of violence (pp. 95–102).**
- **Three phases of danger.** Coming out to the family, the "in-between phase" when one does not yet pass, and the wait for new documents (p. 95). Those who do not pass are "the most vulnerable" (pp. 95–96).
- **The police.** Arrests rest on the 1961 anti-prostitution law, public-morality clauses and the 2018 cybercrime law. Wigs and dresses are "often presented as evidence against them" (p. 96). Detention brings abuse amounting to torture (p. 96).
- **Medicine.** Doctors offer "cures" and tell parents the child is curable (p. 97). An underground market prices care by how rich the patient looks (p. 98).
- **Documents.** Getting them changed is arbitrary, and in court the fatwas decide (pp. 98–99).
- **Housing and work.** Landlords refuse. Neighbours police a woman living alone, and one called the police on such a woman (pp. 99–100). Participants answer by renting through friends or finding "supportive" flats (pp. 99–100). For many, sex work is the only way to pay for transition (p. 101).
- **Exile.** None of the participants wanted to stay in Egypt (p. 102). Life there was manageable only for those who pass and have changed documents (p. 103).

**Conclusion (pp. 103–105).** Pathologisation is "both a medical framework and a survival strategy" (p. 104). Yet the experiences "ultimately reflect resilience, survival, and resistance" (p. 104). "The act of existing is in itself a significant form of resistance in the Egyptian context" (pp. 104–105).

### 1.3 Handbook chapter 2025: Egypt and Iran compared

**Thesis.** The Sunni fatwas (Gad al-Haq 1981, Tantawi 1988) and the Shiʿi one (Khomeini, 1980s) share one logic: being trans is an illness, and the cure must restore the binary. They differ only on which cure, surgery in Iran and psychiatry in Egypt (pp. 204, 208–209). "Both sides imported the pathologizing of Transgenderism concepts which were becoming popular in the West" (p. 209). In their own decade, these approaches "were not necessarily conservative" (p. 204).

**Stance.**
- The chapter "avoids the tropes about Islam being an inherently transphobic religion" (p. 205).
- It treats trans people "not as victims, but as people whose mere existence is an act of defiance" (p. 205).
- Outsiders should "refrain from enacting overly Western strategies" and should ask local trans people what advocacy they want (p. 213).

**Iran (pp. 207–211).**
- **How the fatwa came.** A trans woman's personal petition to Khomeini, Qur'an in hand, produced it. Noralla calls the story "debatable" in its details (pp. 207–208).
- **The permit and its test.** The state built a permit system (p. 210). The examiners test for conformity, treating gender stereotypes as proof of sex (p. 210).
- **What the permit does and does not do.** It shields from arrest but not from daily harassment, and it gives no recognition of trans people as such (pp. 210–211).
- **Survival.** Many survive by passing and moving away from their old lives (p. 211).

**Egypt (pp. 212–213).** This section restates YIMEL.

**Final remarks (pp. 213–214).**
- Sharia is "dynamic and adaptable" (p. 214).
- The fatwas are 35 to 39 years old and need revising (p. 214).
- "As transgender people were responsible for the issuing of outdated Fatwas before, they will undoubtedly be responsible for any new or reformed Fatwas" (p. 214).

## 2. Guardrails for the Cairo storyline

Each rule names the modern harm, with its source, and then the rule for writers of loc and events.

1. **The expulsion hadith is a live weapon, not a museum piece.**
   - **The harm.** The Anas hadith ("expel them from their houses") and the "deliberate versus born" gloss close all three modern fatwas (YIMEL pp. 11, 13, 15). Through those fatwas they underpin the 2003 ban and the 2016 refusal (pp. 17–18, 27–28).
   - **Do:** voice it only through a hostile character, show what it costs, and give the Sister an answer in the same event (§3).
   - **Don't:** let the narrator endorse it. Don't "resolve" it by having a Sister prove she belongs to the blameless kind (the "born so" kind). That is exactly the split the fatwas use to exclude trans women (YIMEL pp. 16, 31).

2. **No "sex correction" plot.**
   - **The harm.** Modern Egyptian law and fatwas legitimise only the person whose "hidden" sex a physician can "reveal": the intersex person (YIMEL pp. 10, 13, 16–18). That frame denies trans women, and it also coerces intersex people into surgery (FA p. 94).
   - **Don't:** let an examination, a midwife or a physician "discover" that a Sister was really a woman all along. Don't treat a *khunthā* finding as her vindication. Don't use the *khunthā* as a stand-in for the daughters (Cairo note §3.3; `isl_mukhannath_recent_studies.md` row 6).
   - **Do:** keep the *khunthā* rules in the court's register as the court's own category. A Sister may name it as a trap.

3. **No bodily examination as a plot device.**
   - **The harm.** The 1988 forensic team gave the student an anal examination (YIMEL p. 24). Forced anal examinations are routine in detention (FA p. 84). The courts send trans people for chromosome maps (YIMEL pp. 20, 27).
   - **Do:** where an inspection is threatened, the event is the Sister's refusal, or a patron stopping it. Any inspection happens off-screen and is never described.
   - **Don't:** make "what the examiner found" the reveal.

4. **Al-Azhar in 1066 is not modern Al-Azhar.**
   - **The facts.** Noralla dates its founding to 972 and calls it "one of the oldest Islamic schools of Fiqh" (YIMEL p. 4 n. 22; ch. p. 214 n. 1). In 1066 it was the Fatimid state's mosque and a site of the Ismaʿili *daʿwa*, where lectures were given to women too (`isl_sanders_ritual_fatimid_cairo.md`; `isl_cortese_calderini_fatimid_women.md`).
   - **Don't:** stage "the shaykhs of al-Azhar issue a fatwa" against the Sisters. In any form it reads as the 1988 fatwa in costume.
   - **Don't:** name or paraphrase modern figures such as Gad al-Haq, Tantawi, ʿAṭiyya Ṣaqr or Aḥmad al-Ṭayyib. Don't use their phrasing in-world: "unwarranted sex change" and "mere wish" are 20th-century formulas (YIMEL pp. 13, 30).
   - **Do:** route the law through the Fatimid *qāḍī*, the *dāʿī* and the *muḥtasib*, as the Cairo note does.

5. **Islam is not uniquely transphobic, and the Sisters' Christianity is not the enlightened alternative.**
   - **The facts.** Noralla rejects that trope (ch. p. 205). She traces the modern rulings to 1980s Western medicine (ch. pp. 204, 209; YIMEL pp. 7–8). She shows jurists reversing themselves (YIMEL p. 29) and Sunni and Shiʿi jurists reaching opposite rulings from one logic (ch. pp. 208–209).
   - **Do:** show the jurists disagreeing. The Fatimid code is harsh on the act and silent on dress, and a Mālikī can be milder still (Cairo note §§2.1, 2.5). Show that Byzantium has its own knife market (`isl_el_azhari_queens_eunuchs.md` §2 row 1).
   - **Don't:** let any event contrast "harsh Islamic Cairo" with a tolerant Rūm.

6. **No Rūmī saviours.**
   - **The facts.** Noralla warns outsiders off imposing their strategies and tells them to ask the people concerned (ch. p. 213). She also explains why insiders alone can reach the community (FA pp. 87–89).
   - **Don't:** have Byzantine-born Sisters "rescue", convert or speak for Cairo's *mukhannathūn* or singers.
   - **Do:** give the locals their own networks, trades and judgement, as the Fusṭāṭ broker's household does (Cairo note §1.1). Let the Sisters ask and learn. Any alliance with them is between equals.

7. **Don't make the narrator echo "a foreign corruption."**
   - **The harm.** Modern Egypt cast trans people as a Western import and a conspiracy (YIMEL pp. 8–9).
   - **The risk.** The Adelphai *are* a Rūmī faith, so a hostile *qāḍī* calling them a Rūmī contagion is in period. The narrative must not let that charge stand alone.
   - **Do:** always show that gender-variant Egyptians were there first: the soft ones paraded at Fusṭāṭ's old mosque in 913, and the Fusṭāṭ households of the 1020s (Cairo note §§1.1, 3.1). Noralla makes the same move for modern Egypt (YIMEL pp. 2–3; FA p. 83).

8. **Don't map modern Shiʿi permissiveness onto the Fatimids.**
   - **The facts.** The "Shiʿi permits, Sunni forbids" split dates from the 1980s, from Khomeini against Al-Azhar (ch. pp. 207–209). Even in Iran the permit came with conformity tests and no recognition (ch. pp. 210–211).
   - **Don't:** write the Ismaʿili caliphate as kinder "because it is Shiʿi". The Fatimid code stones the receptive partner in the act (Cairo note §2.1).

9. **Police and morality law: no torture on screen, and clothing is never evidence in the narrator's voice.**
   - **The harm.** Detention brings beating, sexual violence and solitary confinement (FA p. 96). Police turn wigs and dresses into "evidence" of prostitution (FA p. 96; p. 106 n. 12).
   - **Do:** the attested medieval instruments (the parade on a camel, the *ṭurṭūr*, a nearby exile) may appear. Keep them brief, public, non-sexual and non-graphic. Let a Sister or a *qāḍī* name the evidentiary gap (§3).
   - **Don't:** show violence in custody. Don't let any event treat a Sister's veil, henna or gown as proof of a crime.

10. **No respectability politics.**
    - **The harm.** Trans Egyptians who seek validation distance themselves from sex workers, and the sex workers name that as the betrayal it is (FA pp. 93–94). For many, sex work pays for transition (FA p. 101).
    - **Don't:** let the Sisters buy safety by disowning the singers' houses, the *mukhannathūn* of Fusṭāṭ or women who do not pass.
    - **Do:** give the faith a stake in those households (a shared table, a shared quarter, a shared debt).
    - This also follows the project rule on sex work (memory: sex work in faiths).

11. **"Born so" and "sick" are tactics, never the narrator's truth.**
    - **The facts.** Most of Noralla's participants use the medical account to survive, and all three groups call it the only viable way (FA pp. 91–92, 94, 104). She herself calls for depathologising (ch. p. 214; YIMEL p. 31).
    - **Do:** a Sister may plead nature or need before a *qāḍī* or physician. The event should show that it is a plea, and what it costs: some other Sister is left outside it.
    - **Don't:** let the narrator call any Sister sick or her nature a defect. Don't portray a physician's "cure" as kindly (FA p. 97; `isl_mukhannath_recent_studies.md` row 4).

12. **The in-between is the dangerous time, and passing is not the moral.**
    - **The facts.** The novice, not yet passing, faces the greatest danger (FA pp. 95–96, 104). Yet "the act of existing" is itself resistance (FA pp. 104–105).
    - **Do:** weight risk mechanics toward newly arrived or newly taken daughters.
    - **Don't:** reward passing as the goal, or punish its absence in the narrator's voice.

13. **No botched secret rite as spectacle.**
    - **The harm.** Modern Egyptian trans people die from unregulated surgery in clinics the law created (YIMEL p. 19; FA p. 98; ch. pp. 212–213).
    - **Don't:** model any event on the 2021 death. Don't make "an unlicensed cutter in Cairo kills a novice" a warning beat: it tells players that transition kills.
    - **Do:** if the Rite of the Knife fails in the Cairo set, keep it off-screen and grieved. Place the blame on the barber's ban and the *muḥtasib*, the gatekeepers, never on the daughter's choice.

14. **No living people.** No character, name or plot is drawn from:
    - the Egyptian or Iranian public cases in these works;
    - Noralla herself;
    - any FA interviewee;
    - any modern official.
    
    The fatwa-writers stay out of in-world text.

15. **A living community is never a faith or a history.**
    - **The facts.** Egypt's trans community is not monolithic (FA p. 91).
    - **Don't:** write any line implying that modern Egyptian trans women descend from the *mukhannathūn* or the Adelphai, or that the Adelphai prefigure them.
    - **Don't:** use *khawal*, Ḥasanjiyya or ʿArānib in 1066. They are 19th- and 20th-century terms (FA p. 83; YIMEL p. 2), and the Cairo note §1.6 already bans *khawal*.

16. **Leaving Egypt is a choice, not the moral.**
    - **The facts.** Most of Noralla's participants hope to emigrate (FA p. 102), and she writes from exile herself (FA p. 89).
    - **Do:** a Sister may choose to leave, at a real cost.
    - **Don't:** make "flee to Constantinople" the good ending. It recasts a Rūmī faith as the West offering asylum, and Rūm is no refuge (rule 5).

17. **A patron's shield is a permit, with conditions.**
    - **The facts.** Iran's permit shields from arrest but demands a performance of normative gender, judged on stereotypes (ch. pp. 210–211). Egypt's 1999 court recognised a woman because society "treating her as a woman" made her one (YIMEL p. 26).
    - **Do:** when Rasad's favour protects a Sister, attach conditions: the veil, the women's quarters, spinning and household work. Make visible what the conditions cost the Sisters who cannot meet them.

18. **Families are divided, not uniformly violent.**
    - **The facts.** Families beat children or send them to "cures" (FA pp. 95, 97). Some public figures were praised for accepting a trans child (YIMEL p. 30; not to be named).
    - **Do:** give families mixed responses, with violence kept off-screen.

19. **Vocabulary.**
    - **Don't** use any modern term in loc: transgender, transsexual, gender identity disorder, surgery, hormones, documents or papers, intersex.
    - **Do** use the sources' own words (*mukhannath*, *khunthā*, *muʾannath*) only in the mouths of the people who used them. The Sisters' own words for themselves are the faith's.
    - "Third gender" never appears in the narrator's voice (Cairo note §4.5).

## 5. Historical claims to check

Noralla says almost nothing about the medieval period. She does not discuss the Fatimids or the Mamluks at all. Her pre-modern claims are few and mostly secondhand.

1. **The *mukhannathūn* "after the Islamic conquests of Egypt"** (YIMEL p. 2). She cites only Almarri 2018 (n. 3), which concerns Medina and the Gulf, not Egypt. **Secondhand and misdirected.** Egyptian evidence does exist (al-Musabbiḥī, al-Kindī via Shoshan; Cairo note §§1.1–1.2, 3.1), but she does not use it.
2. **The *ghulāmiyyāt* as the "counterpart" of the *mukhannathūn*: people assigned female at birth with a masculine expression** (YIMEL p. 2). The source is a blog post on love poetry (n. 4).
   - **Problems.** The *ghulāmiyyāt* were slave-girls dressed as boys at the ʿAbbasid court in Baghdad, not an Egyptian or self-chosen identity (Rowson 2003 p. 48, via `isl_el_azhari_queens_eunuchs.md` §1).
   - **Verdict. Do not use** for Cairo 1066.
3. **"Gender ambiguity … since ancient times"** in Egypt (YIMEL p. 2). The source is a *Washington Post* book review (n. 2). Popular, secondhand, and pharaonic, so outside the window.
4. **Gender variance persisting "during and after the British colonization"** (YIMEL p. 2). The source is a school news site (n. 5). Weak, but modern and irrelevant to 1066.
5. ***Khawal*, *Ḥasanjiyya*, *ʿArānib*** (YIMEL p. 2; FA p. 83). Noralla dates them to the 19th and early 20th centuries, which agrees with the Cairo note §1.6 and with Fortier. **Confirms the rule: not for 1066.**
6. **Fiqh "has always emphasized a cis binary", and any identity outside it was "naturally labeled sinful, even if tolerated"** (YIMEL pp. 3–4, citing an online article, n. 19). This agrees broadly with Sanders on the *khunthā* (`hist_sanders_khuntha.md`). But "always" flattens real nuance: the Mālikī exemption from slander for softness "by nature" (Cairo note §2.5) and the Fatimid code's silence on dress (§2.1). **Secondhand and too general.**
7. **Al-Azhar "founded in 972 … one of the oldest Islamic schools of Fiqh"** (YIMEL p. 4 n. 22; ch. p. 214 n. 1).
   - **The problem.** Al-Azhar began as the Fatimid congregational mosque. In 1066 it was Ismaʿili, a site of the *daʿwa*, not a Sunni school (`isl_sanders_ritual_fatimid_cairo.md`; `isl_cortese_calderini_fatimid_women.md`). Its Sunni identity is post-Fatimid. That is general reference knowledge, not checked in this pass; confirm it against Halm or Sanders before stating it in docs.
   - **Verdict. Anachronism risk** (rule 4).
8. **The "born so versus deliberate" distinction is presented as prophetic or early, but the fatwas take it from late commentators.**
   - **Who the commentators are.** The fatwas cite it from *Fatḥ al-Bārī* and *Irshād al-Sārī* (YIMEL pp. 10, 12). These are by Ibn Ḥajar al-ʿAsqalānī (d. 1449) and al-Qasṭallānī (d. 1517), both Mamluk-era and long **after 1066**. The death dates are general reference, not from Noralla.
   - **A mistranslation.** In Noralla's own translation of Gad al-Haq, the gloss becomes the Prophet's speech: "The Holy Prophet says there is no sin on those born Mukhannath" (YIMEL p. 10). Skovgaard-Petersen's translation of Tantawi presents it correctly as commentary (p. 12).
   - **The al-Ṭabarī inference** (no blame until the *mukhannath* described a woman) is attributed to "At-Tabari" (YIMEL pp. 10, 12). He is probably Ibn Jarīr al-Ṭabarī (d. 923), relayed by Ibn Ḥajar. Not checked.
   - **For 1066.** A Sister may argue from the hadith's own facts (§3 row 1), which are in al-Bukhārī. She must not cite Ibn Ḥajar or al-Qasṭallānī. The earlier, attested nature-versus-choice materials are the Mālikī *khalqa* and Ibn Buṭlān (Cairo note §2.7).
9. ***Mukhannath* and *khunthā* conflated in the modern translations.**
   - Skovgaard-Petersen renders *mukhannath khalqī* as "hermaphrodite" (YIMEL p. 12; ch. p. 206).
   - Noralla's translation of Gad al-Haq has "Those born Khuntha are not blamed" where the commentary concerns the *mukhannath* (YIMEL p. 10).
   - The same slip is noted for Bouhdiba and Schimmel (`isl_mukhannath_recent_studies.md` §1.4).
   - **Never quote these translations as evidence about the *khunthā*.** A related open question, beyond Noralla: the medieval form of "changing God's creation". Classical exegesis of Q 4:119 applied it to castration, but that is from general knowledge and needs a checked *tafsīr* (al-Ṭabarī's *Jāmiʿ al-bayān*) before any loc uses it.
10. **Gad al-Haq's title in 1981.** YIMEL p. 9 calls him "the grand Imam of Al-Azhar at the time", but its own n. 49 gives his term as Grand Imam as 1982–1996. In June 1981 he was Grand Mufti (general reference, not checked). The fatwa appears in the Dār al-Iftāʾ collection (p. 9 n. 52), which fits the office of Mufti. Internal inconsistency.
11. **The date of Tantawi's fatwa.** YIMEL pp. 11–12 and ch. p. 206 give **8 June 1988**, replying to a Syndicate letter of 14 May 1988. Ch. p. 205 says the fatwa was issued "In 1987". FA p. 84 says "in 1986" and calls Tantawi "Grand Imam", although its own n. 3 (p. 105) says he was Grand Mufti 1986–1996. **Use 8 June 1988 if it is ever needed. It is not needed in-world.**
12. **The committee's suspension in 2014.** YIMEL p. 18 says Al-Azhar's representative refused to sign off on cases. Ch. p. 212 says he "withdrew an objection", which looks garbled. The 2020 statement is credited to ABC News at YIMEL p. 19 and to the Associated Press at ch. p. 212; the ABC item was an AP wire story, so both are consistent. Modern, and not needed for the mod.
13. **A slip outside the topic.** FA p. 88 dates Giulio Regeni's murder to 2019. It was 2016. Noted only as a sign that FA's background claims need checking.
14. **The Khomeini petition story** (ch. pp. 207–208) rests on a blog post and a human-rights report, and Noralla flags it as "debatable". Modern Iran. Its *move* (petitioning the highest jurist in person) is used in §3 row 10. Its details, and the person, are not.
15. **What she does not claim.** Noralla makes no claim about Fatimid, Ayyubid or Mamluk practice, the *ḥisba* or the medieval *mukhannathūn* of Cairo. The Cairo note's medieval evidence stands untouched by these works.

**Net relevance.**
- **High** for guardrails and for the shape of the Sisters' answers.
- **Nil** as historical evidence for 1066.
- Her insider account of survival strategies (FA) is the most valuable part for writing the Sisters' voice. Her medieval history is thin and secondhand.
