# Peacock, "Urban Agency and the City Notables of Medieval Anatolia" (2021): design reading for akhīs, town notables and sworn surrenders

Source: A. C. S. Peacock, "Urban Agency and the City Notables of Medieval Anatolia", *Medieval Worlds* 14 (2021), pp. 22–34. Themed section "Urban Agencies: Reframing Anatolian and Caucasian Cities (13th–14th Centuries)", guest editors Bruno De Nicola and Matthew Kinloch. eISSN 2412-3196. DOI 10.1553/medievalworlds_no14_2021s22. **Verified** from the article's first page, running footers ("medieval worlds • No. 14 • 2021 • 22-34") and DOI line in the text extraction (`sources_pending/akhi_ribat/src3.txt`, gitignored). Page numbers are the journal's own, printed at the head of each page.

**What was read.** The whole article, pp. 22–34, with all footnotes and the reference list. Quotations are short and verbatim; translations of Persian and Arabic are Peacock's.

**Scope and bias.** A short argument against a long Turkish historiography that made the akhīs into an independent "akhī republic". Peacock argues that futuwwa groups were often tied to the sultans, and that **town notables (*a'yān*)**, not akhīs, made the binding decisions about war, surrender and succession. The cases are Konya, Ankara, Sinop, Sivas, Kayseri and Aksaray, 1190s–1380s. Dorylaion and Phrygia are not mentioned. The main sources are Ibn Bibi (whom Peacock calls "notoriously problematic", p. 27), the anonymous Konya chronicle, Aflaki, Astarabadi and Ibn Battuta.

**Relation to other notes.** It corrects the rosier akhī picture in `byz_blessing_goshgarian_anatolia.md` (Selçuk and Goshgarian). De Nicola, `byz_denicola_chobanids_kastamonu.md`, shows the same towns from the side of a frontier dynasty.

**Relevance:** high for how a town under a contested lord bargains its own surrender, and for the akhīs' real political place. Moderate for akhī membership (one Ibn Battuta passage). Nil for women, eunuchs or gender crossing.

## 1. Ibn Battuta's akhīs (1330s)

- **Who.** The akhī is a man whom "artisans and other unmarried, single young men make their leader" (Ibn Battuta, Peacock's tr., p. 23). Members are the *fityān*; their leader is the akhī (p. 23).
- **The round.** He builds a lodge (*zāwiya*) with furnishings and lamps and serves his companions by day. In the afternoon they bring him their earnings to buy food. A traveller arriving that day is put up "and they do not leave him till he departs". If none comes, they eat, sing and dance (p. 23).
- **Their fame.** "No one in the world like them for great kindness to strangers". They are also quick "to admonish the oppressors, kill the police and their evil accomplices" (p. 23).
- **The akhī as lord where no sultan is.** "In places that do not have a sultan, the akhī is the ruler." He gives the traveller a mount and clothes of honour, and "in commanding and forbidding and riding, he is like a sultan" (p. 24).
- **Rivalry.** Ibn Battuta also stresses violent rivalries between futuwwa groups, leading to public violence (p. 24).
- **Akhī hosts named.** At Aksaray he stayed in the lodge of **Sharif Husayn**, deputy of the amir Eretna (himself deputy of the Ilkhan), "one of the fityān" with "a large following" (p. 26).

## 2. What akhīs actually did, by Peacock's evidence

- **Defenders when the sultan was away.** In 1278 the akhīs joined the sultan's deputy, the town headman (*ra'īs*) and notables to demand that the Mongol governor act against Karamanid, Eşrefid and Menteşe Turkmen attacking Konya (p. 25). In 1279, with the sultan absent, the akhīs led the opposition to the Turkmen besieging Konya (p. 25). The notables, allied with them: "the sultan is occupied with the Turks and is unable to help us" (p. 25).
- **Armed numbers.** Akhī Ahmad Shah of Konya commanded "several thousand soldiers and irregulars" (*junūd wa runūd*). *Runūd* is a negative term often used of futuwwa members (p. 25 and n. 19).
- **The sultan's men.** In 1290 "all the *jawānān*" (members of the brotherhoods) put on armour "at the sultan's deliberation", and the sultan honoured the akhīs (p. 26). In 1299 the sultan ordered the killing of the *sharābsālār*, carried out by **Akhī Jarūq** (p. 26).
- **Go-betweens.** When the Mongol governor Fakhr al-Din Qazwini regulated salt and confiscated sheep, riots broke out. Akhī Ahmad Shah went with officials and *jawānān* to complain of the "wrong innovations" (p. 26).
- **Tied to Mongol power too.** Sharif Husayn of Aksaray was Eretna's deputy (p. 26). Akhī 'Isa served Burhan al-Din of Sivas and Kayseri (r. 1381–98) as ambassador and deputy (p. 26). One akhī was military governor (*shihna*) of Malatya (p. 27).
- **Lists, not decrees.** The qadi of Konya kept lists of futuwwa members. No decree appointing a chief akhī survives (p. 26).
- **The other body, the *akādisha* (Turkish *iğdiş*).** A "mysterious parallel urban organisation" whose chief **was** appointed by the government (p. 26, n. 27).
- **Feared by townspeople.** In 1289–90 futuwwa groups (*runūd*) won a riot, "seizing houses and tormenting the people", so that people feared to visit their gardens (p. 27).
- **Peacock's conclusion.** Futuwwa was "organisationally autonomous, up to a point, but was frequently allied to state power" (p. 26). It is "something of a red herring" in the search for urban agency (p. 32).
- **No akhī republic.** Ankara as an "akhī republic" has "no evidence"; Wittek disproved it in 1932 (p. 24).

## 3. Town notables and sworn surrenders

The core of the article. **The notables (*a'yān*, *buzurgān*, *mu'tabarān*) decided whether a besieged town fought or surrendered, and on what written terms.**

- **Konya, after 1192.** In the war between Qilij Arslan's sons, Konya resisted Rukn al-Din for four months because it had sworn oaths to Ghiyath al-Din. Then the town's "leaders and iğdişes whose words were respected" offered him a choice: a huge payment to withdraw, or surrender if he guaranteed the deposed sultan's safety and exile. He chose surrender and sent a written oath (*sawgandnāma*), with grants of land and posts to the town's leaders (p. 27). The deposed sultan played no part; the result was "presented to him as a fait accompli" (p. 27).
- **Konya, 1204.** Ghiyath al-Din returned from exile in Constantinople. The townsmen refused him, citing their sworn oath to Rukn al-Din's son. He burned the orchards outside the walls. After talks they surrendered for safe conduct for the boy (p. 28).
- **Ankara, 1211.** The townsmen, tired of the siege, surrendered 'Ala' al-Din to his brother on sworn terms: no harm to "young or old, noble or humble, Turk or Tajik"; no punishment for their partisanship. Written contracts (*'ahdnāmas*) were handed to the messenger (Ibn Bibi, Peacock's tr., p. 28).
- **The oath ran both ways.** Townsmen swore allegiance at an accession, with land grants and posts renewed. The sultan in turn gave written guarantees (p. 29).
- **Sinop, 1214: a Christian town.** Sinop belonged to Trebizond. The Seljuks captured its ruler Alexios out hunting. The townsmen refused: he has elder sons; "we will not surrender this kingdom to the Muslims" (p. 29). Alexios was tortured and hung upside down before the walls (pp. 29–30). The notables then offered surrender if the sultan swore not to kill the *tekfur*, let him go home, and let the townspeople leave "with our possessions, children and wealth" to any place they wished (p. 30). The sultan swore before his amirs; the *sawgandnāmas* went into the city; the notables handed over the keys (p. 30).
- **In Byzantium**, bishops often played the notables' role for a city (p. 29 n. 43).
- **Sivas, 1380s.** Burhan al-Din appointed a governor before an assembly (*anjuman*) of the townspeople, with oaths on both sides. When he later executed him for treason, he first showed the *'ulama* and notables the oaths the governor had written and broken. They "held the sultan excused" (pp. 29–30).
- **Oaths could be broken** under duress, but "were not to be broken lightly" (p. 30).
- **Who the notables were.** Mostly religious families: qadis, imams, *sayyids*; also merchants, like the Tabrizi émigrés who founded a caravanserai at Konya; perhaps the Christian doctor Abu Salim, who endowed the Hekimhan caravanserai near Malatya with an Arabic, Syriac and Armenian inscription (p. 31). The qadi of Sivas negotiated light terms with the Mongols after Köse Dağ (1243) (p. 30). Kayseri's sharif, qadi, jurists, ulama and Sufis welcomed Baybars in 1277 (p. 31). The notables must have had armed force to make such decisions (p. 31).
- **Many inhabitants were not Muslim.** Probably the majority in Konya, Kayseri, Ankara and Sivas (p. 22 n. 1).

## 4. Women, eunuchs, gender crossing: absences

- **Women:** none as actors. Women appear only as "your women and children", whom Alexios warns will become slaves (p. 30).
- **Eunuchs:** none.
- **Gender crossing:** none.
- **Membership:** the akhī's followers are "unmarried, single young men" (p. 23). The article says nothing of women's branches.
- ***Iğdiş*:** a townsmen's body whose chief the government appointed (pp. 26–27). The Turkish word also means a gelded animal; that is general knowledge, not in this article, and the article never connects them. **Do not build a eunuch mechanic on it.**

## 7. Risks

- **The akhī republic is a myth** (p. 24). Don't give akhīs their own state in Phrygia.
- **Ibn Bibi is unreliable**, though his audience found his town politics credible (p. 27).
- **Sinop's torture scene** is not event content.
- **No women anywhere** in this article. Any women's role comes from other sources.
