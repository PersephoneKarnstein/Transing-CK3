Quotes are verbatim and short.

**Read this book as ethnography, not history.** Blain is a practitioner ethnographer: "I am implicated in the construction of seidr" (p. ix). She says outright: "I am not regarding the sagas of Icelanders as 'historical'" (p. 90). Most of the book describes **North American and British neo-pagan practice of the 1990s**, chiefly the Hrafnar group's "oracular seidr". Each claim in §1 is tagged:
- **[MED]** medieval text or archaeology she cites;
- **[MOD]** modern practice;
- **[BLAIN]** her own interpretation.

The mod should present only [MED] material as history.

## 1. The argument in brief

### The Greenland rite and its modern rebuild (ch. 3)

- **[MED] The saga core is thin.** The *Eiríks saga* passage (Gundarsson's translation, quoted p. 33) gives:
  - a high seat with a hen-feather cushion;
  - preparations at sunset;
  - a search for women who know the *varðlokkur*;
  - Guðríðr: "Halldís, my foster-mother, taught me that chant in Iceland";
  - "The women made a ring around the seat, and Porbjörg sat up on it";
  - the powers satisfied by a fair voice.
  
  Blain stresses what the saga leaves out: "The song Gudrídr sang is, unfortunately, not given us" (p. 33). How the seeress entered trance "[is] not evident in the account" (pp. 33-34). We do not know what the preparations (*umbúningr*) were. They could be anything from extra clothing to a smudge to mushrooms, "Nor are we likely ever to know" (p. 59).
- **[MOD] "Oracular" or "high-seat" seiðr is a 1990s US construction.** "The account of Porbjörg the Seeress is the basis for today's practice of oracular seidr" (p. 33). It "was first practised within today's Heathenry in the US when Diana Paxson went looking for 'something for the women to do'" while the men played Viking games (p. 143). The Hrafnar rite (pp. 34-36, 39-42) has these elements:
  1. warding the room by singing a rune-row;
  2. invoking Freyja and Óðinn;
  3. drumming;
  4. a guided meditation down Yggdrasill to Hel's gate, which the audience reaches in light trance;
  5. the seer going on alone in deep trance, often veiled;
  6. questions from the audience;
  7. the guide "calling" the seer back.
  
  The journey is assembled from Baldrsdraumar, Völuspá, Hyndluljóð, Snorri's Hermóðr, Saxo's Hadingus and folklore (pp. 41-42). Practitioners themselves dispute it (p. 42).
- **[MOD] Other modern variants.**
  - The Swedish Yggdrasil group (after Lindquist): the völva holds "her staff between her legs as if riding it" and raises it when ready to speak (p. 43).
  - Annette Høst's circle: singing alone carries the seer (p. 44).
  - Some Hrafnar seers have no staff. A person stands behind the seer, "shadowing" her, which one participant called being "like the staff" (p. 45).

### Trance, the cloak, and sitting out (ch. 4)

- **[MED] No drugs in the sagas.** "The sagas, however, do not mention any entheogens in any context that I can discover" (p. 55). The archaeology is the henbane in the Fyrkat pouch and a few cannabis seeds at Oseberg (p. 55, from Price).
- **[MED] Going "under the cloak" decided the conversion.** At the Alþing, the heathen lawspeaker Þorgeirr "lay down, and spread his cloak over him, and he lay all day, and the night after, saying no word". He then declared Iceland Christian, with heathen practice in one's own house left private (Íslendingabók, pp. 60-61). Blain follows Aðalsteinsson in reading this as *útiseta*-style divination (pp. 61-62).
- **[MED] Sitting out was outlawed but rarely punished.** Thirteenth-century Icelandic law banned "sorcery and spae-working ... and sitting out to wake up trolls". "While the penalty was death, no-one was convicted until Iceland's small witch-craze in the seventeenth century" (p. 62, citing Hastrup).
- **[MED] Respect fell along gender lines.** The *spákona* is "spoken of with respect, for the most part, whereas the seidkona or seidmadr is often regarded ambivalently". Men called seiðr-workers were "deemed to be 'ergi'" in "the late, Christian accounts that we have" (pp. 59-60).

### Saga women and the foster line (ch. 6)

- **[MED] Magic runs through foster-lines.**
  - Ólöf Kjannok charms her **foster-son** against weapons (Heiðarvíga saga).
  - In Grettis saga, Þuríðr, **foster-mother** of Grettir's killer, curses a log that leads to his death. The saga ends with a law outlawing "all old-lore practitioners" (pp. 91, 93-94).
  - Katla hides her son as a distaff, a goat and a pig (Eyrbyggja, p. 92).
  - Þórdís spákona "was chosen to arbitrate in important cases" (Vatnsdæla, p. 91).
  - Gunnhildr learned seiðr from two "Finnish" (Sámi) seiðmenn and then betrayed them (pp. 94-95).

### Men, ergi and seiðr (ch. 7)

- **[MED] A king's son burned for seiðr.** Haraldr Finehair's son Rögnvaldr réttilbeini, whose mother was Sámi, was a *seiðmaðr*. Vitgeirr answered the king's ban in verse: "There's no harm / if we do seidr, / children of ordinary men / and ordinary women, / when Rögnvaldr / ... does seidr". Eiríkr Bloodaxe then "burned his brother, Rögnvald, in his house with eighty seidworkers, and this work was much approved" (Heimskringla, p. 112). Óláfr Tryggvason is also said to have killed eighty, and older sources count women among them (p. 112-13, after Jochens).
- **[MED] The key texts.**
  - Ynglinga saga: *ergi* followed Óðinn's seiðr, "and therefore the priestesses were brought up in this art" (p. 115).
  - Lokasenna 24: Óðinn on Samsey "beat on the drum as witches do" (*völur*) (p. 123).
  - Helgakviða Hundingsbana I: one warrior calls another a völva (p. 124; Old Norse in n. 4, p. 164).
  - Grágás on the three killing words (p. 125).
  
  "There is no male equivalent to Porbjörg" (p. 114).
- **[MED, contested] Hyndluljóð 32.** The verse derives *völur* from Viðólfr, *vitkar* from Vilmeiðr and *seiðberendr* from Svarthöfði. Solli reads *seiðberendr* as a third-gender, "two-spirit" class, from *berendi*, a coarse word for a female animal's genitals that Gulaþing law forbids using of a man. Blain allows the insult but offers another reading: three kinds of magic, or "bringing seidr forth from within oneself" (pp. 132-33).
- **[MED/BLAIN] Staffs and "foreign" seiðr.** Staffs come mostly from women's graves, "but one in Norway is of a man" (p. 137). The sagas' habit of making seiðr-workers Sámi or Hebridean "may be read as the author's distancing him or herself from such 'queer' practices" (p. 137).
- **[MOD] Who the modern "gender-variant" seiðworkers are.** They are gay and straight **men**:
  - Jordsvin, a gay seiðmaðr who gets homophobic hate mail (p. 122);
  - Bil, a straight man told by his spirits to drop his "macho attitude" (p. 127);
  - Ybjrteir, who learned to "switch forms" as a closeted gay teenager (p. 127);
  - "James", who in Freyja-possession experiences being "female, or male, or both" (p. 134).
  
  One man, after his first high-seat trance, said "I felt extremely ergi" (p. 141). **The book has no trans women or trans femme seiðworkers.**
- **[BLAIN] Her argument for "queer" seiðr.** She uses *queer* in Halperin's sense, "whatever is at odds with the normal". In that sense "the ergi person is one who moves between the worlds ... not necessarily gay, but certainly 'queer'" (p. 126). She gives two readings:
  - From watching seers in trance, she proposes that *ergi* attached to "the vulnerability of the one in deep trance, who willingly rejects all means of protection and defence". The seer "has abrogated manhood" by depending on others (p. 140).
  - Politically, kings suppress shamanic practice because it is unpredictable and local (p. 113).
  
  She brings in third-gender models (Czaplicka, Saladin d'Anglure, Roscoe's "berdache") and critiques them. She concludes: "Seidr therefore is political practice, and 'queer' practice" (p. 141).
