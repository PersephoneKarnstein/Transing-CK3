**Correction to the brief.** The dissertation is **not** about bodily anomaly, sex change or *renyao*. It studies female sexuality, medicine, possession and religious women in Song anecdote (*Yijian zhi* above all). Sex change gets one paragraph, in the Conclusion (pp. 200–201), as future work. Its real value for Yutai is ch. V: how Tang–Song women used Daoist rhetoric to leave marriage, how neighbours called them *yao* 妖, and how officials handled them.

**Chapters (one line each).**
- Introduction (1–17). Method: read male-authored sources as multivocal; agency inside norms.
- I. Traveling Stories (18–50). Hong Mai's *Yijian zhi* as "faithful recording" of hearsay from many social levels.
- II. Circulating Knowledge (51–81). Song medicine as one resource among many; women patients defy doctors.
- III. Medicine, Exorcism, and Beyond (82–123). Song doctors pathologise "women without men"; possessed girls vs. desiring women.
- IV. Deviant Viewers and Gendered Looks (124–156). Women's erotic responses to icons; Dunhuang yaksha figures (Caves 254, 248, 288, 428, 290, 100).
- V. Untold Desires (157–195). Nuns, Daoist women and female transcendents who refuse marriage; a "potential queer space".
- Conclusion (196–202). Includes the sex-change paragraph.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **A "potential queer space" for women.** Ch. V names "a space where women's sexual bodies are not available to their husbands and one that allows women not to (or not simply to) desire men" (p. 159). It grows from circulating stories of religious models, not from convent walls alone (p. 157).
- **Daoist registers took every kind of woman.** The Celestial Masters' *Zhengyi fawen taishang wailu yi* lists five classes of women who may receive registers, including 出家女 "daughters who are unwilling to marry and leave home", 寡婦 widows and 歸居女 women returned home after divorce (p. 168). The order is a shelter: 不自存立，還依本家，憂患相纏，歸命師門, "[if] they cannot support themselves ... and are still trapped in difficulties, they may follow a master and join the order" (p. 169).
- **But the vow is self-abasing.** The parent's vow for a daughter who will not marry: 先緣罪重，生受女身，尫弱愚頑，不願出嫁, "[she] carried a deep sin and therefore was born a woman ... weak, foolish, and stubborn, not willing to marry" (p. 169). Fathers and husbands file the vow; the master does not outrank them (p. 169).
- **Pei Xuanjing (d. 854), the guarantor's question in period.** Her parents refuse her the Way: 儻入道不果，是無所歸也, "If you enter the Way and do not succeed, there will be no way back" (pp. 180, 181). She marries, and within a month tells her husband "the gods do not allow me to be your wife"; she lives apart with "immortal companions from Kunlun" (pp. 180–181). Cheng reads a woman withdrawing "step by step" (p. 184).
- **Neighbours call such a woman *yao*.** Qi Xiaoyao (*Xu xian zhuan*, 10th c.) reads the *Nü jie* and says "it concerns only the mundane"; her husband's family and neighbours 悉以為妖狂, "all considered her evil and crazy", and suspect she may "plan something else" (pp. 177–179).
- **Women choosing women.** A lady-immortal meets the village girl Xiaoyao: 「汝乃愛我，可同之所止否？」 "So you love me. Why don't you join me?" The girl throws down her basket and follows (p. 176). In Song *Yijian zhi*, the divorced Daoist Wen lives with the young Daoist Yang in Wen's father's house, and Yang moves with the household (pp. 190–191).
- **Song women's new tools: dreams and ritual work.** The Perfected Wu dreams she is a relegated Jade Woman, and her mother cancels the betrothal (pp. 187–188). In 1088 the nun Fawu shaves her head in her parents' shrine; the family summons a monk to talk her out of it, and she out-argues him (pp. 188–190). Wu then earns her keep as a healer-exorcist (p. 190).
- **The official's response: fear of *yao*, expulsion.** The Daoist priestess You (976–984) at Chengdu and Qingcheng drew crowds; the prefect Xin Zhongfu 恐其妖，遣出城, "feared that the priestess was demonic and so sent her out of the town", and her followers went with her (pp. 192–193).
- **The report chain and a local cult the court never answered.** Female Transcendent Zheng (1249–50): the village head reported to the county, the county to the prefecture, the prefecture to the court (鄉保轉聞之縣，縣聞之州，乞奏於朝), asking a temple. 久之未報: no answer came, so villagers built the shrine themselves; the chief minister sent a clerk in secret, who found the girl sold as a maid (pp. 193–194). Cheng: court recognition both won and policed local cults (p. 193 n. 341, citing Hansen 1990).
- **Song medicine reads all-women houses as sick.** Xu Shuwei: 師尼寡婦，獨居怨曠，獨陰而無陽, "nuns and widows live alone and resentful, single yin without yang"; their treatment must be kept apart from other women's (p. 102). Chen Ziming (1237) generalises it to every *guafu* "without a husband" (pp. 84–85).
- **Sex change (pp. 200–201).** *Yijian zhi* has two women who became men, one by a netherworld clerk's error, one by Daoist elixir. Both are "told in a morally neutral tone", and both are "accepted by their families and communities as soon as their bodies are confirmed truly and completely male". Xu Deng (*Hou Han shu* 82), a woman become a man and a wonder-worker, is executed for 惑眾 "misleading the people", yet miracles continue at his shrine. **Correction (checked against *Hou Han shu* 82B on Wikisource):** the text says of Xu Deng only 本女子，化爲丈夫。善爲巫術 ("originally a woman, he changed into a man; skilled in shamanic arts") and that he died (物故). The one killed by the Zhang'an magistrate for 惑眾, with a shrine raised afterwards, is his partner and disciple Zhao Bing. Cheng conflates the two. Confucian commentary reads such events "as signs (but not the cause) of political turbulence". Zhou Mi records a maid killed for resisting her master and then found 身具二形 "to have both forms".
- **The strange becomes political.** After the Mongol conquest a 14th-century reader took *Yijian zhi* as proof of 國家將亡，必有妖孽, "before a state falls there must be prodigies" (p. 200). "The strange then becomes the queer, and the queer body is associated with the body of the state."

Relevance: medium. Not a source on omens or sex change, but strong on how officials, kin and doctors read women who left marriage through the Way, which is exactly how Yutai's daughters would be read.

## 3. In-world loc lines (faith's or characters' voice)

- Guarantor: "If you enter the Way and fail, there is no road back. Will you go?"
- Intake: "She could not stand on her own, and her own house would not keep her. So she came to the Way."
- Neighbours (hostile): "Demonic and mad. She sits in that room and talks to no one."
- Prefect (hostile): "I fear she is a prodigy. Send her out past the gate, and let her wander the temples."
- Physician (hostile): "Single yin without yang. A house of women breeds fevers."
- Sister (reply): "We were not born women for a sin. We were born women. The rest is a name."
