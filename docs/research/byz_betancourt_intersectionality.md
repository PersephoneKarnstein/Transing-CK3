**Relevance, candidly.** The book's trans evidence is overwhelmingly trans-masculine. Betancourt says so: the trans monks are "persons assigned female at birth who ... live most their lives as monks, usually presenting and passing as eunuchs" (p. 89), and "Transgender women, for example, are all but absent from the historical record" (p. 205). What the mod can use is his method, his two trans-feminine cases (Elagabalus in Byzantine chronicles, Psellos), his reading of eunuchs, and his diagnosis of *why* the record is lopsided.

## 1. The argument in brief

**Method for "trans" readings.**
- Treat literary figures as possible lives: "I will take their existence for granted and treat them as real, because they were real" (p. 16); whether the trans monks existed "is beside the point" (p. 16).
- Reject "transvestite" and "cross-dressing": the figures lived full-time, "theirs were not temporary choices" (p. 90). Use "transgender" as an umbrella (after Valentine, Halberstam, Spade), which may include people who would not have claimed it (pp. 90–91).
- Pronouns follow the text's commitment: "he/him" or "she/her" as the figure commits, "they" for the fluid (p. 91). So Matrona is "she" (she returns to womanhood, pp. 93–95) and Marinos "he".
- The record is shaped by privilege: rank lets an Elagabalus or a Theodora survive in invective while the poor vanish (pp. 14–15, 206). Read invective, medical handbooks, chronicles and rhetoric for what they attack (pp. 106–18).
- Read against "trans denialism", which "operates by insisting on the 'trans-' prefix in order to fundamentally deny, qualify, or somehow input a person's gender" (p. 119).

**Why the trans-feminine record is thin.** The monks' ascent was praised as women "becoming male" (Gospel of Thomas, Philo, Basil of Ancyra, Sarah: "It is I who am a man, you who are women", pp. 96–99). Following Bynum, men "could have gained nothing socially by it except opprobrium" (p. 97). The schema in one sentence: "women become men, who are praised for their transgender ascent ... But, within the same schema, men cannot become women lest they be corrupt effeminates" (pp. 199–200). Trans women survive "only filtered through screeds of transmisogyny" (p. 119).

**Basil of Ancyra (mid-4th c.).** Through ascesis "women were to become men; men, in turn, were to join the rank of angels" (p. 99). The virgin "must make herself look masculine and her voice hard" (p. 99).

**Canon law against crossing.** Deut. 22:5; Gangra canons 13 and 17 (345) anathematise a woman who "under pretense of asceticism" takes men's clothes or cuts her hair; Trullo canon 62 (692): "no man should wear feminine attire" (p. 98).

**The trans-feminine cases.**
- *Elagabalus* (r. 218–22): Dio has her take the titles "wife, mistress, and queen" and plan "to cut off his genitals altogether" (pp. 105–7). The surgical request survives in **Byzantine** chronicles: Zonaras, "asked the physicians to contrive a woman's vagina in his body by means of an incision"; Kedrenos, "make him bisexual [*diphuē*] by means of an anterior incision"; verbatim in Symeon Logothete (mid-10th c.) and Glykas (pp. 106–7). Aelian's lost "Indictment against the Little Woman" (*katēgoria tou Gynnidos*) (p. 107). These are often "the only details provided about the Roman emperor's reign in Byzantine chronicles" (p. 107).
- *Michael Psellos* (11th c.): "with regard to nature I am feminine [*thēlys*]"; "my soul is indeed simply feminine"; he will not "despise the women's chamber" (pp. 112–13). On an imperial birth: "What does it matter if the child has been stamped in this way or that, more feminine or more masculine?" (p. 109). Betancourt reads "the schematics for a marginalized transgender identity" (p. 113), using they/them.
- *Gregory of Tours* (Frankish, 6th c.): a eunuch "who lived much of her life as a woman", castrated as a boy for a groin disease by a doctor working "in the way I had once seen physicians do in Constantinople" (pp. 70–71). Only a mention here; his fuller treatment is in the 2021 essay (`hist_trans_historical.md` P68).

**Eunuchs.**
- Byzantine castration "did not involve radical castration": the penis was usually left (p. 108). Paul of Aegina's penile amputation with "a leaden tube into the urethra" shows removal was within surgical skill (p. 111). (Confirms `hist_tougher_eunuch_byzantine.md` on penectomy.)
- "Byzantine authors understood castration as feminizing the body and, in a sense, transforming men into women" (p. 108); Aristotle: "close approximation to the appearance of the female" (p. 109); Meletius the Monk (c. 9th c.): cut, "they are made feminine, and come to resemble women" (p. 109).
- Betancourt's own position: "eunuchs served less as a third gender than as embodiments of genderqueer figures" (p. 108); they are "the most visible, prominent, and well-articulated trans identity in the empire" (p. 118).
- Monks dreaded beardless faces: Euthymios, "it is not right for a feminine face to be found in the laura"; Athos acts bar beardless youths (p. 125).

**Transition vocabulary in a hostile text.** Nikephoros Basilakes (12th c.) on Atalanta: "she also transitioned her gender [*parēllatte to genos*]. For one's upbringing confirms one's gender [*bebaioi ... to genos*]" (p. 116); and she might be one who, "being female, was made masculine in nature" (p. 117). Upbringing in the women's quarter is how gender is "confirmed" (p. 117).

**Penal castration.** Justinian castrated men convicted of same-sex acts and paraded them, on evidence even of a coerced slave; Theodora used the law against a slanderer (Procopius, *Secret History*; pp. 83–84).

**Consent.** From the mid-9th century, Photios and others insist the Virgin gave spoken consent (*synkatathesis*) before conception (pp. 3–4, 19–20): "even God himself asked Mary for consent before impregnating her" (p. 57).

**Race and the court eunuch (ch. V).** In psalters the Ethiopian eunuch is painted white: "gender identity has won out over racial identity" (p. 193). In the Menologion of Basil II (c. 1000) he is black and dressed as a Constantinopolitan court eunuch, not a foreigner (pp. 161–68, 190). Eustathios's 1174 oration lists the court's peoples, including captives "honoured with servitude" (p. 169). The trans monks are "blackened" by ascesis and praised; Abba Moses, a black former slave, is abused in the sanctuary ("Outside, black man!", p. 183): "White privilege thus apparently delights in the fantasy of becoming transracial" (p. 199). Western visitors (Liudprand, the Venetians of 1149) mocked Byzantine emperors as "Ethiopian"; "The Byzantines were not white" (pp. 174–75, 202). Slavery itself is mentioned only in passing; this book is not a source on the eunuch trade.
