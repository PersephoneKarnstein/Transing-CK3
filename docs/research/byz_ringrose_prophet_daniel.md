## 1. The argument in brief

### 1.1 The thesis: Daniel became a court eunuch between the 4th and 10th centuries

- **The hook is Theophylaktos.** In *In Defence of Eunuchs*, the eunuch speaker answers the charge that mutilation breaks Mosaic law: "Didn't God accord the eunuch honor in Isaiah? And what about the prophet Daniel?" (p. 74; Gautier ed. 1:301, n. 2). He also names the eunuch archbishops of Thessalonike, Pydna, Petra and Edessa, "to say nothing of many of the lower clergy" (p. 74).
  - Ringrose dates the essay to the twelfth century (p. 73). Her book says eleventh, and Mullett says c. 1100-1107 (see §4).
  - It was written for his brother, "a eunuch on the staff at Hagia Sophia" (p. 73), who was castrated "as part of his preparation for a career in the church" (p. 74). Theophylaktos "does not apologize" for it (pp. 73-74).
- **Why it matters.** Daniel was "widely regarded as the most important precursor of Christ after John the Baptist" (p. 75). By the twelfth century the Byzantines were "quite comfortable" casting him as a eunuch (p. 75).
- **Her dating claim.** The status Theophylaktos gives eunuchs "was already established two hundred years earlier in the tenth century" (p. 75). Tenth-century eunuchs "invented earlier 'historical' eunuchs" with spiritual roles (p. 75).
- **The Septuagint.** It "never directly states that Daniel was a eunuch", but the boys are handed to the chief eunuch for training (p. 86). Theodoret's commentary and some historians set the story beside Isaiah 39:7 ("shall be made eunuchs in the palace") "without comment" (p. 86).
- **The Latin side** (n. 38, p. 104). Jerome, early fifth century, says outright that Daniel and the three boys were eunuchs. Kuefler traces this to Jewish tradition. Ringrose says the Talmudic link cannot be dated before the sixth century. Early Byzantine authors make the Isaiah link but are "reluctant to openly call them eunuchs as Jerome does". Whether Jerome meant a castrated courtier or only a celibate one "is unclear".
- **Why a Byzantine reader saw a eunuch** (p. 86). Daniel was a captive in a foreign land. His name was changed. He was reared in the king's household, youthful and childless, and beautiful. He mediated between God and the king and between the weak and the powerful. He guarded Susanna and the three boys and purified sacred space. He interpreted dreams. Above all he was the king's trusted servant.

### 1.2 Gender framing (read with care)

- **A shift over time.** Late antiquity "often perceived eunuchs ... as a third sex, half man, half woman" (p. 75). "By the tenth century eunuchs were perceived to be men as far as sexual classification was concerned" (p. 75), "though men of a distinctly gendered sort" (p. 75).
- **A one-axis hierarchy, not a binary.** Only "physically whole men" could reach perfection, either as aristocrats or as ascetics (p. 76). Eunuchs could reach neither: they did not have to fight their own sexuality (p. 77). "Eunuchs were not necessarily effeminate; rather they lacked full masculine status" (p. 77).
- **Language changes after the ninth century.** Sources start to define eunuchs by the masculine qualities they lack, "rather than those feminine qualities they are perceived to possess" (p. 77). Modern translators "reflexively translate 'unmasculine' as 'effeminate'" (p. 77).
- **Her strong claim.** "There is no question that in the Byzantine world eunuchs represent a distinct gender category" (p. 77). It is marked by dress, assumed sexual behaviour, work, appearance and voice (p. 77).
- **But she hedges it.** If genitals and procreation are central to "male", eunuchs become a separate sex. If service matters more, they become "a special gender category within a larger construct that is 'men'" (p. 78).
- **And she splits it.** Court eunuchs and church eunuchs had different constructs, "differences that might support two distinct gender categories" (p. 82). There was "considerable crossover between these categories" (p. 84).
- **Two comparisons to drop.**
  - Byzantine gender reminds her of "some American Indian societies in the last century" (p. 85, citing Whitehead).
  - Her supporting article first appeared in Herdt's *Third Sex, Third Gender* (n. 18, p. 101).
  - Both are the anthropological third-sexing Bhatt attacks (`book_bhatt_third_sex.md`). Use neither.
- **Sexuality is not the axis.** Men "did not become eunuchs because they desired same-sex relationships. This is an erroneous modern assumption" (p. 83). Most were cut as children, so it "cannot have been a voluntary act" (n. 19, p. 101). She suspects "eunuch by nature" in saints' Lives was a hostile code phrase for castrated men who sought men (n. 19, p. 102).

### 1.3 Bodies, diet and look

- Galen grouped eunuchs, women and children as soft and moist: "Like women, eunuchs' flesh is cold" (p. 80). Diet was gendered. Eunuchs were probably fed the bland fare thought fit for women and children (p. 80).
- Eunuchs kept a beardless "adolescent loveliness" and stayed out of the sun like aristocratic women (p. 80). They kept their hair as they aged (p. 80). They grew tall and long-limbed (pp. 80-81). They had high voices and were thought "talkative and gossipy" (p. 81).
- At court they stood "like beautiful angels around the imperial throne" (p. 81). This and the "perfect servants" line on p. 83 are the chapter's only angel material.
- After the tenth century "eunuch" usually means *ektomias*, a "cut" man (p. 81).
- In late antiquity eunuchs were foreign slaves, renamed, and "artificially created beings, as well as a third sex" (p. 81).

### 1.4 The tenth-century change: freeborn, home-cut, sanctified

- In the tenth century more eunuchs came "from the educated, propertied, freeborn classes", were "castrated within the boundaries of the empire", and kept their family ties (pp. 81-82).
- **Leo VI's law** (n. 12, p. 100). Castration creates a creature "far different from what God intended". He still eased the penalties:
  - imperial servants who castrate lose their place at court and pay large fines;
  - others are fined, tonsured, have goods confiscated or are exiled;
  - a slave victim goes free, but a free victim gets nothing.
- **Court eunuchs: hostile image.** They were said to rise as "passive partners in sexual relationships with powerful men". They were called greedy, and they could be recognised by dress and manner (p. 82).
- **Court eunuchs: sacred image** (pp. 82-83; nn. 14-15, p. 100).
  - Only a eunuch or the patriarch could touch the crown.
  - Eunuchs were the only people outside the clergy allowed to handle the ceremonial candles.
  - The emperor takes anything into his own hand from a eunuch's hand.
  - Eunuchs guarded the emperor at night and were on hand "to discuss and interpret dreams" (p. 83).
- **Church eunuchs.**
  - They were honoured for celibacy. They were "castrated to aid them in remaining celibate and were not acculturated into the specialized gender status of the court eunuch" (p. 83).
  - Theophylaktos calls "eunuchism" "a valued and holy institution" (p. 83).
  - He values those "castrated as children and not of their own volition" (n. 20, p. 102).
- **Eunuch patriarchs** (pp. 83-84): Germanos (715-730), Niketas (8th c.), Ignatios (9th c.), "probably" Methodios (9th c.), Stephen II and Polyeuktos (10th c.).
  - Skylitzes says Polyeuktos "was castrated by his parents and lived the monastic life for a long time with praise" (n. 26, p. 102).
- **Saints' Lives.**
  - Nikephoros of Miletos was castrated by his parents and trained for court before entering the church (p. 84).
  - Niketas was castrated by his parents and served in Empress Irene's household. He became a monk "who specialized in healing men who were tormented by sexual desires" (p. 84).
  - Symeon the Sanctified left the court for monastic life in the early twelfth century (p. 84).
  - Ringrose doubts that Symeon the New Theologian was a eunuch (n. 29, pp. 102-103).
- **Eunuch-made sanctity.**
  - The court eunuch Constantine probably commissioned the Life of his father Metrios, who was rewarded by God with a son he could castrate and send to court (p. 84).
  - Euphratas, "an invention of the tenth century", converts Constantine I and builds Constantinople (p. 84). Note 32 traces the legend to ninth- and tenth-century sources.
  - By the tenth century "the virtuous ecclesiastical eunuch is almost a commonplace in Byzantine hagiography" (p. 84).
- **A discovery story** (n. 24, p. 102). The tale of Methodios's sterility is a common type. A holy man accused of fathering a child turns out to be a eunuch. A holy woman posing as a young man turns out to be a woman.

### 1.5 Chrysostom (4th c.): Daniel the holy man, never the eunuch

- In the fourth century "court eunuch and holy man ... would have been almost impossible to combine in a single individual" (p. 87).
- Chrysostom knew court eunuchs as enemies: his homily on Eutropius uses the stock anti-eunuch images (p. 87; n. 44, pp. 104-105).
- He "never suggests in any way" that Daniel and the boys were eunuchs (p. 88). But the boys' beauty earns "a long and rather tortured gloss" (p. 88): only a barbarian king wants beautiful servants.
- **Diet.** Daniel's refusal of the king's food becomes an ascetic diet of raw grain and water. God then makes the boys "plump and beautiful ... so that they would please the king" (p. 89). God is the "image maker" who can "correct the shape of a statue that has already been made" (p. 89).
- **The furnace.** The boys sing "as if from one mouth", and onlookers saw "a theater of piety" (p. 92). Ringrose asks whether he unwittingly pictures the eunuch choirs of court and church (p. 92).
- **The Persian robe.** Chrysostom's Daniel laments: "I who am dressed in Persian garments and wear a Persian headdress" (pp. 96-97). The courtier's costume is "a heavier punishment" than sackcloth (p. 97). In art Daniel is "always shown as youthful and beardless" in Persian dress (p. 97).
- **Her verdict.** Chrysostom "evades the probability that Daniel was a court eunuch" (p. 97) and prefers the prophet and holy man.

### 1.6 Symeon Metaphrastes (late 10th c.): Daniel the cut man and good servant

- Metaphrastes died about 1000 and may have been a high court official. Basil the *parakoimomenos*, exiled in 985, "may well have been a model" for his Daniel (pp. 89-90). His menologion "became the standard menologion for the Byzantine ecclesiastical world" (p. 90).
- **The castration is explicit.** "When the king had made them eunuchs", using *ektomias* (p. 90). The king, not the chief eunuch, takes charge of their castration and training, as at the Byzantine court (p. 90).
- **The diet is a holy man's.** The boys eat nothing "that had life": seeds, herbs, beans and dates (p. 90). God's miracle makes them look well fed (p. 91).
- **Daniel defers to the king.** He apologises for seeming wiser than the Chaldeans, yet rebukes the king for ordering "good and honest men to be unjustly put to death" (p. 91). The king makes him "guardian of all his kingdom" (p. 92).
- **The furnace boys.**
  - The boys refuse the image partly to "show how unjust the king was" (p. 93).
  - Metaphrastes does not call them Jews, and dresses them in Byzantine clothes (p. 93).
  - By his day the furnace was a stock image for "overcoming sexual desire" (n. 61, p. 105).
  - His St Clement ate only vegetables, "always remembering the three boys" (n. 51, p. 105).
- **Belshazzar.** Daniel refuses reward: a wise man "gets his reward from helping those who are in difficulty". The king then breaks his promise (p. 93).
- **The dragon.** Daniel kills it without sword or staff. Ringrose reads this as the Byzantine view that eunuchs fight by cleverness, not arms (pp. 94-95).
- **Courtly additions.**
  - Daniel builds a tower at Ecbatana where kings are buried (p. 94). Ringrose links this to the building and burial work of the great eunuchs.
  - The lions become "well-minded bodyguards", the stock words for palace eunuchs (p. 95; n. 65, p. 106, on the false etymology *eunoos*).
- **The closing chapter.** Daniel is "a man freed from the desires of the flesh with his three child friends" (p. 95). "In Metaphrastes' mind they are gendered as eunuchs" (p. 96).
- **Her verdict.** Daniel's "dominant image becomes that of court eunuch", giving moral guidance "to an emperor before whom he is always deferential" (p. 97). Metaphrastes offers "a model for the 'good eunuch'" (p. 97).

### 1.7 Beyond the church, and the end point

- **Skylitzes** (11th c.) says Basil II threw a court eunuch who tried to poison him "into a den of lions" (p. 98). Ringrose doubts it happened. What matters is that chroniclers thought it "an appropriate way for an emperor to discipline a eunuch" (p. 98).
- **Conclusion.** "In the earlier period Daniel was cast as a holy man." By the tenth century the courtier Daniel "made little sense ... unless Daniel were also presented as a eunuch, a perfect servant of the emperor" (p. 98).
- **The dream book** (n. 16, pp. 100-101). One of the most popular dream books of East and West was attributed to Daniel. Ringrose links this to eunuchs as night-guards and dream-readers.
- **The hostile register** (n. 34, p. 103). John of Damascus, early eighth century, keeps a letter he credits to St Basil: "The race of eunuchs is evil and hateful. Such a one is neither woman nor man". The letter also calls them "damned by the knife".

## 4. Corrections / answers to open questions in other notes

1. **beliefs.md row (c) and note 7: "Daniel read as a eunuch" at 867.** The chapter supports this for the tenth century and after, not clearly for 867. Ringrose's own claim is "as early as the tenth century" (p. 74). Her open witness is Metaphrastes (d. c. 1000; p. 89). Before that the reading only "lurks in the background" (p. 86). Treat Daniel-as-eunuch as **implicit at 867, explicit from the late tenth century**. This reverses the direction of the "decaying by 1066" arc for this one strand: the Daniel reading *grows* toward 1066.
2. **`hist_neville_byzantine_gender.md` §2.4 (line 65) and beliefs.md row (c).** Neville cites "Reconfiguring the Prophet Daniel" for "that's no eunuch, that's an angel!" and eunuchs "beautiful, shining, and pure". **Neither is in this chapter.** Its only angel material is the court eunuchs "like beautiful angels around the imperial throne" (p. 81) and eunuchs as perfect servants "just as angels were" (p. 83). Cite the angel-dream motif to *The Perfect Servant* ch. 7 (pp. 148-151, per the book note) or to Sidéris. Neville's sentence is a summary across Ringrose's work, not a page of this chapter.
3. **Theophylact's date varies across notes.** This chapter says "twelfth century" (p. 73). The book note records her book's "eleventh-century". Neville's note says "late 11th c.", and Mullett says c. 1100-1107. Use **c. 1100** and keep it out of the 1066 bookmark's contemporary voice.
4. **`isl_tolino_eunuchs.md`: Ringrose "too fixed".** Fair against her headline ("a distinct gender category", p. 77). But this chapter also:
   - makes the category a "special gender category within a larger construct that is 'men'" (p. 78);
   - splits it into court and church kinds with "considerable crossover" (pp. 82, 84);
   - grounds it in one masculine ladder (pp. 76-77).
   That is close to Tolino's context-dependent gendering, Höfert's "eunuchal masculinities" and Neville's "scale of virtue". Ringrose and her critics agree on the point the mod uses: Byzantines called eunuchs **men**.
5. **Bhatt.** The chapter's two framing moves are exactly Bhatt's target. Its supporting article first ran in Herdt's *Third Sex, Third Gender* (n. 18), and p. 85 compares Byzantium to "some American Indian societies". The editors' summary goes further than Ringrose and speaks of a "third bodily sex" (Introduction). None of this framing belongs in docs or loc. Use the chapter for sources, dates and court practice only.
6. **Errors in the chapter.**
   - Theodoret's Daniel commentary is called "fourth-century" (p. 86). Theodoret lived c. 393-c. 458, so it is fifth-century.
   - Germanos is placed "in the seventh century" (p. 83), but her own n. 21 gives 715-730.
   - The editors say western writers "saw none" of Daniel's eunuchry, but Ringrose's n. 38 shows Jerome saw it.
   - Do not copy any of these.
7. **`byz_ringrose_perfect_servant.md` row 10 (Theophylact as the Galenic splinter's book).** Add the dialogue's two concrete arguments for event text: the roll-call of eunuch archbishops (Thessalonike, Pydna, Petra, Edessa) and "what about the prophet Daniel?" (p. 74). Both are the approving cleric's talking points after c. 1100.
8. **`byz_ringrose_perfect_servant.md` primary cases.** Add Polyeuktos's parental castration (Skylitzes, n. 26), Niketas the healer of men tormented by desire (p. 84), and Basil II's lion's-den tale (Skylitzes 367; p. 98). Ringrose doubts that Symeon the New Theologian was a eunuch (n. 29); do not use him as one.
9. **Name list.** `tm_name_lists.txt` has Daniel in a Byzantine male list. No change. Daniel stays a man's name, and the Sisters do not take it.
