# Roy, "Translating Hijra into Transgender" (TSQ 2016): design reading

**Citation.** Jeff Roy, "Translating Hijra into Transgender: Performance and *Pehchān* in India's Trans-Hijra Communities," *TSQ: Transgender Studies Quarterly* 3, nos. 3–4 (November 2016): 412–432. DOI 10.1215/23289252-3545131.

**What was read.** The whole article, notes and references included (plain text of the PDF). Page numbers below are the journal's.

**Quotes.** Verbatim, each under 25 words. Everything else is paraphrase. The article names several public activists it interviewed; this note does not need them and leaves them out.

**Hard rule.** Hijras are a living community. Nothing here becomes a faith, a rite, a House form or an in-game group. The guru-chela system is already ruled out as a model for the Houses (`book_bhatt_brown_trans_les.md` §7).

## 1. Relation to existing notes

- **Bhatt, plainly.** Roy describes the very move Bhatt condemns, and he describes it with sympathy. Bhatt calls affluent trans women who distance themselves from "the abjectified hijra" traitors (`book_bhatt_third_sex.md` §1, "Respectability is betrayal"). Roy's subject is a middle-class troupe that drops hijra song and blessing for staged dance and an "individual" identity. He calls it "a shift from socially transgressive, devalorized modes of hijra social identity toward an embrace of respectable, middle-class transgender ones" (p. 415). He names the class cost himself (pp. 425–426) but ends hopeful (p. 427). So the facts support Bhatt; the verdict does not.
- **Where they agree.** Roy treats "age-old hijra tradition" as a modern political tool: a "strategic essentialism that frames hijra through a lens of authenticity and tradition" (p. 416). The lawyers' case behind the 2014 NALSA ruling used it, citing Koovagam and Mughal court eunuchs (pp. 416–417). He compares the word *gharānā* itself, which became common in Hindustani music only in the 1980s, as tradition named under modernity (p. 418). This supports Bhatt's "no golden age" and her charge that NALSA turned ethnography into law.
- **Bhatt's targets in this piece.** Roy leans on Reddy's ethnography (pp. 413–414) and repeats the "neither male nor female" / "third gender" self-description as the common ground of hijras (n. 1, p. 427). That is the line of scholarship Bhatt attacks (Nanda, and the NALSA framing).
- **South reading list.** Confirms `south_reading_list.md` §1 finding 4: Koovagam and Bahuchara Mātā as "trans" sites are modern. Roy reports the Aravan festival only through modern fieldwork and media (n. 17, p. 430).

## 2. Argument in brief

- In Hinglish, "transgender" means two things: an umbrella over the "koti family" of local terms, and a newer word for individual self-understanding (pp. 413–414). NGO funding for HIV work since the mid-1990s drove the second sense (pp. 414–415).
- *Pehchān* ("recognition", "identity") names both a national HIV programme and a personal identity; Roy says the two feed each other (p. 415).
- Badhai (blessing song and dance at weddings and births) is the economic and social core of the hijra household. Service to the guru, as *izzat* (respect, honour), is a kind of currency, as in Hindustani music schools (pp. 417–418).
- The colonial Criminal Tribes Act of 1871 defined "eunuchs" by three marks: suspicion of kidnapping or castrating children, dressing as a woman in public, and dancing or playing music in public or for hire (p. 417).
- The new troupe performs for awareness, not for patrons; it drops the hijra voice and badhai entirely (pp. 422–423). A reforming guru treats her disciples as equals and lets them own their labour (pp. 419–420).
- Roy compares the change to the 20th-century "sanitising" of hereditary women's dance into "classical" forms, following Weidman and Soneji (pp. 424–425). He calls it "the domestication of the hijra voice as new codes of transgender respectability supplant older ones based on servitude to the guru and badhai patrons" (pp. 425–426).

## 3. What is usable

Little, and only as framing.

1. **The slander was law.** The 1871 Act bundled child-stealing with dress and public dance into one legal definition (p. 417). This confirms Bhatt's child-lifter pattern (`book_bhatt_brown_trans_les.md` §5 row 8). Use the pattern only. Do not quote the Act in game: it is a modern colonial law about a living community.
2. **"Tradition" is often new.** Roy's point about *gharānā* and NALSA supports the mod's habit of marking reconstructions and refusing a golden past.
3. **Labour as tribute.** The *izzat* economy (service to a senior as the price of advancement) is a general pattern. It is the same shadow Bhatt finds in seniority (§5 row 17). It can sharpen that event's text in generic terms. It must not be modelled on the gharana.
4. **The "classical" trap (Worn Garland).** Roy, through Soneji and Weidman, says modern "classical" South Indian dance was made in the 20th century from hereditary temple and court dance, under upper-caste and nationalist pressure (pp. 424–425). Anything the mod shows of temple women's song should come from the Chola inscriptions and Orr, never from modern classical-dance imagery.

## 5. Cautions

- Roy's subjects are living people in a living community, and the article is about their art and livelihood. Use nothing of badhai, the Mother Goddess songs, the guru-chela bond or the gharana in any faith.
- Roy's frame is ethnomusicology, not theory of sex. He takes no view on whether hijras are women. Do not cite him for or against the mod's claim that daughters are women.
- Roy's account of the troupe is warmer than Bhatt's politics would allow. If the article is ever cited for the mod's respectability theme, cite its facts, and say the judgment is Bhatt's.
