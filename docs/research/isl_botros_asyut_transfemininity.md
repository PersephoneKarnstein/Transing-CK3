# Botros, "Portraits of Transfemininity in Asyut" (2023): design reading

Source: Beshouy Botros, "Transition and Trans\*lation beyond Binary History in Mid-century Upper Egypt; or, Portraits of Transfemininity in Asyut", *WSQ: Women's Studies Quarterly* 51.3–4, "Nonbinary" (Fall/Winter 2023): 133–155. ## 0. Framing warning

- **1958, not 1066.** This is a reading of one magazine spread about four transfeminine people in Asyut, Upper Egypt. **It is not evidence for medieval Egypt.** Botros himself rejects "the ethnographic impulse and transhistorical reflex" that links them to pre-Islamic Egypt or to Sufi dancers (p. 136). The mod should follow him.
- **Whose voice.** Botros writes from inside the broader trans and genderqueer Arabic-speaking community: he speaks of "our broader communities" (n. 9, p. 152). On how these lives should be read, his reading outranks the magazine's four experts. The magazine itself is hostile: voyeuristic, Cairo looking down on the Ṣaʿīd (pp. 135–136).
- **Private people.** The four subjects were private individuals put in a magazine. **This note does not name or quote them.** It describes them by role ("the wedding dancer", "the *zār* drummer"). The experts are also given by role.
- **Pronouns.** Botros writes around pronouns on purpose (pp. 136–137). The mod's rule stays: daughters are she/her.
- **The sensitive detail.** One subject's repeated punishment during military service, which Botros reads as likely sexual violence (p. 139), is recorded here only so it is not used. **No proposal draws on it.**

## 1. Findings

### 1.1 The spread and its question

- **The headline.** Four "men donning dresses and living as women" in Asyut, presented as "problems" (pp. 133, 139). The subtitle asks whether they do it "for a morsel of bread" (*luqmat ʿaysh*). In Egyptian colloquial, *ʿaysh* means both "bread" and "life" (p. 135).
- **Botros turns the question round.** The "why" is farcical. The better question is "how they managed to do it, to live their lives" (p. 135). He comes back to this in the conclusion (p. 147).
- **The magazine's frame.** Cairo's reading public was then about 18 percent of the population (p. 135). The spread makes the Ṣaʿīd's "queer customs" a spectacle (p. 135). More than half the spread is photographs (p. 143).

### 1.2 Four lives (described generically)

1. **A wedding dancer.** Friends asked this person to "enliven a wedding by dancing". This person went in a full feminine outfit and make-up, was admired, and from that night danced professionally. During conscription, this person kept women's clothes hidden on the base (p. 139).
2. **A *zār* drummer**, married with six children (p. 140).
   - The mother was a *zār* master and head of an ensemble. When the drummer grew too old to attend the women-only sessions, the child went on covertly in women's clothes.
   - After the mother's death, the drummer became head of the ensemble and "has not taken off feminine clothing" since.
   - Botros: the drummer "did not become a woman, but remained a devotee of *zār*", in "a unique transfeminine position" (p. 140).
3. **A person said to be married to a spirit woman "from under the earth".** She provides for this person and commands women's clothes and make-up "so that earthly women would not look upon him with desire". The town told stories of miracles and of great strength at a saint's festival (*mawlid*).
   - This person refused the journalist's request for a miracle and refused to be photographed, covering the face with a pillow. The caption said the jinn forbade it (pp. 140–141, 143).
4. **A domestic worker.** After the father's death, this person washed and cleaned for neighbours, then joined a *zār* group "after changing his clothing completely to women's dress". The outfit was a *ṭarḥa* headdress with a spangled scarf (p. 141).

### 1.3 The experts (by role)

- **A psychiatrist.** "Sexual deviance, the result of a mental illness". He separates those who "cross-dress" for pleasure or money from the truly "afflicted". He also says "Nero the Roman Emperor was afflicted by it!", the reflex to historicise, as Botros notes (pp. 144–145).
- **A social scientist.** He blames mothers who dress and spoil boys as girls, and imitation of a sister (p. 145).
- **The provincial education director.** He blames "the social environment" (*al-biʾa*). He wants them under surveillance "to prevent them from infecting others" (p. 146).
- **A lawyer.** He speaks of an upbringing that was "*mukhannatha*" (p. 146).

### 1.4 Context Botros supplies

- ***Zār* is modern in Egypt.** It came "from Ethiopia to the Red Sea and the Arabian Gulf through the nineteenth-century slave trade". Its leaders "are almost always women" (el-Hadidi 2016, via p. 140).
- **Dressing baby boys as girls.** Upper Egyptians dress infant boys as girls, pierce their ears and put kohl on their eyes, to ward off the evil eye (*ḥasad*) (p. 145). Lane described it in the 19th century (n. 12, p. 153; secondhand). Botros asks, "Are Upper Egyptians bringing up their kids trans? No" (p. 145).
- **Medical transition.** The article appeared "shortly after the first clinic offering gender-affirming care was established in Cairo" (p. 149; no source given).
- **Pronoun practice today.** "In Arabic spoken among gender queer and trans\* people", some use a neutral plural, some the dual. "The general rule is more about good manners than grammar": people ask one another (n. 9, p. 152).

### 1.5 Botros's argument

- **"Nonbinary" as a way of reading.** He uses it as a reading technique that interrupts tidy, goal-directed stories of transition, not as a label for these four people (p. 136).
- **Experts always find a cause.** Searching for causes "would always yield results" (pp. 144–145).
- **Transition as continuity.** Each of the four transitions "involved more continuity than change". The dancer kept dancing, the drummers stayed in *zār*, and the spirit's spouse kept faith with her. Transition is "a way of staying in and inhabiting one's gender, body, and lifeworlds" (p. 149).
- **Refusal.** The pillow photograph shows someone refusing "the conditions of visibility proffered by the magazine" (p. 143).

## 2. Cross-checks

- **Botros glosses *mukhannath* as a fiqh word for intersex people.** He calls it "used in Islamic jurisprudence (*fiqh*) to describe intersex people" (p. 146). **This is wrong for medieval law**, where the intersex category is the *khunthā*. The Baghdad note §1.1 table keeps *mukhannath*, *khunthā* and eunuch apart, after Rowson 1991a and al-Sarakhsī. `isl_mukhannath_recent_studies.md` records the same slip in Bouhdiba and Schimmel ("hermaphrodite"; the Almarri triad). The 1958 lawyer's own word was *mukhannatha*, describing an upbringing, so the gloss is Botros's, not the lawyer's.
- **Pathology as the experts' frame.** Noralla (`isl_noralla_egypt_trans_law.md` §1.2, §2 rule 11) finds that today's trans Egyptians use "born so" and "sick" to survive. Botros shows the same expert frame in a popular magazine in 1958, before the 1981 fatwa. **They agree, and it confirms the rule:** experts ask "why"; the narrator never does.
- **The "environment" theory is old.** The education director's "social environment" (p. 146) echoes the claim al-Maqrīzī quotes about c. 1050: Tinnīs people have natures inclined to "moistness and femininity", and "two hundred *mukhannath*s used to be born there every year" (Cairo note §1.3). This is a parallel, not continuity evidence: nine centuries lie between them.
- **Weddings.** The 1958 wedding dancer does **not** answer Baghdad note §5 Q7 (11th-c. weddings). Keep "a feast".
- **Terminology.** `isl_arab_queer_trans_feminisms.md` §2.2 dates *khawal* to the 19th century. Botros does not use it. Nothing changes.

## 4. Quotable lines for loc (in-world; never attributed to the scholar)

Lines are invented in the spirit of Botros's argument. None quotes the magazine's subjects.

- "You ask why. Ask how: how I eat, how I sleep, who keeps my door." (a daughter to a physician; after p. 147)
- "Bread is life. Ask anyone who has gone without." (a troupe elder, answering "she does it for bread"; after p. 135)
- "I did not leave my work. I stayed in it, and it made room for me." (a daughter on her becoming; after p. 149)
- "Draw the others if you like. My face is not yours to take." (a daughter refusing a painter; after p. 143)
