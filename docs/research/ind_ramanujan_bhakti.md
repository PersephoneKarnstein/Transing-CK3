## 0. Framing warning

Ramanujan uses **"bisexual"** for the bhakta who is sometimes man and sometimes woman ("double-edged, bisexual", *Speaking of Śiva*, Introduction; n. 28 "The bisexuality of the bhakta"). He means two-sexed, not sexual orientation. In "On Woman Saints" he leans on Mead and Chodorow to say that women are "secure in their identities" and need not "change their sex" (p. 324). That is a 1970s psychological frame. Use his data and translations, not these categories.

**Living communities.** The Lingāyats (Vīraśaivas) and Śrīvaiṣṇavas are living and politically active. The vacana numbers and texts below are for the Garland's **reaction** and **kinship** layer, not for building a Vīraśaiva faith (Candidate C stays "do not build").

## 1. *Hymns for the Drowning* (1981)

### The argument in brief

- **Dates and canon.** The twelve āḻvārs flourished in Tamil country between the 6th and 9th centuries (p. xi). Nammāḻvār lived c. 880–930 by historians' dating; "some would date him a century earlier" (p. xii). The first full lives are Garuḍavāhana's *Divyasūricarita* (12th–13th c.) and Piṉpaḻakiya Perumāḷ Jīyar's *Guruparamparāprabhāvam* (13th c. here; 14th c. in Narayanan) (p. xii n. 5).
  - His poems "have been chanted in temple services and processions since the eleventh century". At Srirangam the *araiyar* "sings and enacts the hymns for the listening image" (p. xiii).
  - "Inscriptions as early as the 11th century mention endowments of land for the maintenance of reciters" (p. xiii; sources given are Gnanambal and Hari Rao's *Koil Olugu*).
- **The worn garland.** Āṇṭāḷ was called *cūṭik-koṭutta-nācciyār*, "the lady who gave (flowers to god) after wearing (them)" (p. 148 n. 5). She wore flowers in her hair "dreaming of love for the divine lover Krsna", and sent them to the temple, "where the sacrilege was discovered". Ramanujan: "Giving god one's leftovers is to treat him as a lover" (p. 148 n. 5).
  - The hagiography does not, in this note, say who discovered it. The Periyāḻvār-and-the-hair detail is not in Ramanujan.
- **Barriers broken "only in theory".** In later bhakti cults the impulse to merge results, "however briefly and often only in theory, in a breakdown of barriers and distinctions" between castes, touchable and untouchable, and "between male and female" (p. 148).
- **All devotees are feminine (a reported view).**
  - The story "often cited": sixty thousand sages want to unite with Viṣṇu as Rāma. Rāma, sworn to monogamy, promises that as Krishna they may be reborn as cowherd women (*gopīs*) and unite with him (pp. 152–153, after Vaudeville). "In this view, all devotees are felt to be feminine; the Lord is the supreme male, the lover" (p. 153).
  - The date and source text of the sages story are not given (*unverified*).
- **How much of the poem is a woman's voice.**
  - Nammāḻvār gives 27 decads (270 poems) of the *Tiruvāymoḻi* and all 100 poems of the *Tiruviruttam* "to the theme of love" (p. 153).
  - He is the girl who pines, but "the poet is also the girlfriend who consoles and counsels, and the mother who restrains her". He "speaks in many feminine roles—as women in love, mothers, girlfriends" (p. 153).
- **The 13th-c. commentarial rule.** The *Ācāryahṛdayam* of Aḻakiya Maṇavāḷa Perumāḷ Nāyaṉār (13th c., 234 sections in Maṇipravāḷam) explains the alternation of voices: "In knowledge, his own words; in love, a woman's words" (p. 153 and n.). This is **the in-period theological warrant** for the girl's voice.
  - The same commentary allegorises the mother, the friend and the girl in love as three stages of knowledge, and the messenger birds as teachers (pp. 154–155 n.).
  - Ramanujan objects that "the commentary undoes the poem by reversing the direction" (p. 155 n.).
- **Separation over union.** Most love poems are "preoccupied with absence, not presence, not the bliss of union but the pain of separation" (*viraha*) (p. 155).
- **Possession.** In *Tiruvāymoḻi* 5.6 the mother hears her daughter speak as the Lord ("I made the world", "I am the earth you see"). She asks whether "our lord ... has come and taken her over?" (pp. 70–75; Afterword p. 154). The notes call these the "mother's verses" (*tāypācuram*): the girl's "hallucinations of identity with her lover" fused with "the devotee as a mouthpiece of the god" (Notes, on p. 70).

### Lines usable in the faith's voice (paraphrase; not Ramanujan's wording)

1. **The mother's defence** (from TVM 5.6, pp. 70–75): "My daughter speaks with his voice now. How shall I explain her to people who see only this world?" Use it for a Garland mother, or companion, answering a hostile kinsman.
2. **The dowry** (from *Tiruviruttam* 12, p. 63): "My pale skin and my long nights are the dowry I bring him." Use it at gate step 2, on a missed dawn or a stress hit.
3. **The foster mother's doubt** (from *Tiruviruttam* 60, p. 65): "She is only learning to say the name of his hill. Will she ever get there?" Use it for a failed vow: "next Mārkaḻi".
4. **The mother to other mothers** (from "The Dark One" 3, p. 35): "You have daughters too; how can I tell you about mine?" Use it as the faith's line to a court of the girl's own family.

## 2. *Speaking of Śiva* (1973)

### Dates

- Dēvara Dāsimayya is "probably the earliest of the vacana poets", said to be born in the 10th century at Mudanūru, a weaver (Dāsimayya section introduction).
- Basavaṇṇa's community at Kalyāṇa lasted "probably the span of one generation". It ended with the persecution after an outcaste–brahmin marriage, and he died c. 1166/1168 (Basavaṇṇa section introduction).
- Mahādēviyakka was "a younger contemporary of Basavaṇṇa and Allama in the twelfth century" (Mahādēviyakka section introduction).

### The gender vacanas, verified

- **Basavaṇṇa 703. Verified.** The poet tells the Lord of the Meeting Rivers that he wears "these men's clothes only for you". Then: "Sometimes I am man, sometimes I am woman." It ends: "I'll make wars for you but I'll be your devotees' bride."
  - Quoted in the Introduction, where Ramanujan calls it "double-edged, bisexual", and in the Basavaṇṇa section, with n. 28: "The bisexuality of the bhakta - cf. Mahādēvi 68, Dāsimayya 133."
  - The reading list's from-memory wording ("men's clothes 'only for you'", "sometimes man, sometimes woman") is correct. The number is **703**.
- **Dāsimayya 133. Verified, with a correction.** People call it woman if they see "breasts and long hair coming", and man if "beard and whiskers". "But, look, the self that hovers in between is neither man nor woman" (Introduction; Dāsimayya section).
  - Ramanujan's gloss: Dāsimayya "rejects also the differences between man and woman as superficial" (Introduction).
  - **The subject is the self (the soul), not a person in a third social state.** The reading list's "the self 'in between'" is right. It must never be glossed as a third sex or a third kind of person.
- **Mahādēviyakka 68. The "all men are wives" line.** Mahādēvi sees Śiva as "the haughty Master for whom men, all men, are but women, wives". n. 47: "'All men are His wives' gives another turn to Akka's attitude to human males." This is the soul-as-woman formula in a **woman** saint's mouth, 12th c.
- **Dāsimayya 96.** "Did the breath of the mistress have breasts and long hair?" Breath, like the soul, has no sex or caste marks. Ramanujan lists it as Dāsimayya's attack on caste (Introduction).
- **Dāsimayya 128.** Can father and mother display "the future breast and flowing hair in the little girl about to be bride?" "Only ripeness can show consequence." The image is of a womanhood present but not yet visible. It is about ripeness of knowledge, not about gender, but it can be claimed **in the faith's voice**.
- **Basavaṇṇa 111.** The poet speaks as a woman who goes to an assignation, is caught, stripped by the watchman and beaten by her husband, and fined by the king. This is the male poet's woman's voice in **shame**, for the soul's failed infidelity. Ramanujan cites it as an "extended metaphor" (Introduction).
- **Mahādēviyakka 328.** The allegorical bride: Māyā is her mother-in-law and the world her father-in-law. She will give the household the slip "and go cuckold my husband with Hara, my lord" (Introduction). The husband is Karma. The Lord is "the Illicit Lover" against "the man-made" (Introduction).
- **Mahādēviyakka 283.** "So my lord, white as jasmine, is my husband. Take these husbands who die, decay, and feed them to your kitchen fires!" (Mahādēviyakka section).
- **Mahādēviyakka 183.** Her answer to Allama on why she covers herself with hair after throwing off her clothes: "Till the fruit is ripe inside the skin will not fall off" (Mahādēviyakka section introduction).
- **The six phases (Appendix I).** In the fifth phase the devotee "suffers only as a loving woman suffers her lover's absence", and many of Mahādēvi's vacanas belong there (79, 120). The system is Cennabasava's.
- **The *śaraṇasati–liṅgapati* formula was not found** in *Speaking of Śiva*, including Appendix I. It stays *unverified*.

### For the design

- The śaraṇa evidence is in period (10th–12th c.) and strong: a male poet says he wears men's clothes only for God's sake and will be the devotees' bride (703). But it is **Kannada Śaiva**, not Tamil Vaiṣṇava, and it belongs to a living, politically active community.
- In the Garland, it is **kin, cited by the faith's scholars**, and a source of **dissent**. Dāsimayya 133's "neither man nor woman" is the voice of the faith's opponents within bhakti: the soul has no sex, so why change the body? The faith answers in Bhatt's direction: the body is not the soul, and a man's clothes worn "only for you" can be taken off.

## 3. "On Woman Saints" (1982)

**Title and pages confirmed.** The contents and notes give "On Woman Saints"; the running heads say "On Women Saints". The essay runs **pp. 316–324**, notes pp. 365–366. The reading list's "On Women Saints" and "pages *unverified*" can be corrected.

### The argument in brief

- **Bhakti is "feminine".** Its chief mood is the erotic (*śṛṅgāra*), "seen almost entirely from an Indian woman's point of view". Male and female saints alike adopt "such feminine personae as wife (*kanta*), illicit lover (*parakiya*), trysting woman (*abhisarika*), even Radha herself" (p. 316).
- **The reversal.** Against Manu, in the saints' lives "'the last shall be first'": "men wish to renounce their masculinity and to become as women" (p. 316).
- **Sources and method.** About a hundred women saints' lives, mostly from Ta. Su. Samaraya's Kannada *Śivaśaraṇakathāratnakośa* (1967), which gives "most of the saints" no dates. Ramanujan reads them "not for the truth of historical fact, but for patterns in what is presented by the narrators" (n. 5, pp. 365–366).
- **Five phases of a woman saint's life** (pp. 317–322; flow chart pp. 318–319):
  - **(A) Early dedication.** God is her first love, and she needs no conversion (p. 320). The options include Āṇṭāḷ's single-minded love, and Rēkavve, who completes the Lord's garland with a piece of her own flesh. Tilakavve is transformed "into a male by God's grace". Goggavve refuses even Śiva in disguise (p. 320).
  - **(B) Denial of marriage** (pp. 320–321). A widowed saint refuses widowhood because "she cannot be truly widowed, being married to God". This is compared with the modern Newar custom of marrying girls first to Nārāyaṇa (p. 321; a 1978 lecture, not a medieval source).
  - **(C) Defying norms.** Mahādēvi walks naked (p. 321).
  - **(D) Testing and initiation by a male figure.** "This seems to be a concession to the general normative Hindu pattern" (p. 321).
  - **(E) "Marries the Lord".** For Mahādēvi, "a new, second family of saints arranges the marriage to the divine bridegroom and gives her away". "Typically the woman saint merges with God in a temple or holy place, as does Mahadevi in Srisaila, or Antal in Srirangam" (p. 322).
- **Women saints are not public gurus.** Āṇṭāḷ and Mahādēvi "never become gurus in the public realm as Basava, Kabir, and Nammalvar do" (p. 324). They replace their families with "a second family composed of saints" (p. 323).
- **The key sentence.** "The males take on female personae: they are feminine, yearning, passive toward a male god. Before God all men are women. But no female saint ... takes on a male persona" (p. 324).
- **Ramanujan's explanation (not to be used).** Women "do not need to undergo a conversion or change their sex", after Mead and Chodorow (p. 324).

### For the design

- **Verified gloss:** "Before God all men are women" (p. 324). With Mahādēvi 68 and *Hymns* p. 153, this is the soul-as-woman reading the reading list wanted to check. It is Ramanujan's summary of the bhakti saints' legends. The narrator may attribute it ("as the saints' legends have it"). The faith may assert it.
- **Rēkavve's flesh in the garland** (p. 320) is self-wounding devotion. **Do not use** (it is near the no-self-harm line, and is not the Garland's way).
- **Tilakavve** (female to male by God's grace, p. 320) is **reaction data only**. The Garland's opponents can cite her: "God changes women to men, not men to women." The faith answers with Bhaṅgāśvana and the sages reborn as *gopīs*.
- **The "second family of saints" gives the bride away** (p. 322). This is the social gate again: the companions (*tōḻi*) of gate step 1 are the family that marries her to the Lord.
- **Dissent material.** Women saints are barred from the public guru role (p. 324), and male testing is a "concession" to norms (p. 321). These are the faith's internal critique of its own hierarchy.

## 4. Narayanan, "The Goddess Śrī" (in the same volume): the soul as bride

- Hawley's preface: for Vedānta Deśika (13th–14th c.), "Visnu is understood as married not only to Sri, but also to human souls, who approach him through her" (p. xiii).
- Narayanan: the āḻvārs "pictured the soul as the beloved woman and the Lord as the lover". Śrīvaiṣṇava theologians "went so far as to portray the soul as the bride of Visnu", with "no enmity between the bride-to-be and Visnu's eternal bride" (pp. 232–236).
- A Tirumaṅkai Āḻvār verse in the mother's voice marvels that her daughter knows Śrī lives on the Lord's chest "and yet she does not stop loving You" (pp. 232–236).
- Deśika, quoting Parāśara Bhaṭṭar (12th c.), says we love the Lord as "the bridegroom of all creation" *and* as Śrī's husband (pp. 232–236).
- **For the design.** In-period (12th–14th c.) theology of the soul as Viṣṇu's bride, with Śrī as mediator, not rival. This is the "one Lord, souls as brides" doctrine with a goddess in it. It answers part of the hierarchy risk: the bride goes to the Lord through a woman.

## 5. Corrections to the candidate and reading list

- **Vacana numbers verified:** Basavaṇṇa **703** (men's clothes, man and woman, the devotees' bride) and Dāsimayya **133** ("the self that hovers in between is neither man nor woman"). The wording in finding 1 is accurate. Add Mahādēvi **68** ("men, all men, are but women, wives").
- **Dāsimayya 133 is about the soul, not a third kind of person.** Gloss it so, or not at all.
- ***Śaraṇasati–liṅgapati*** is not in Ramanujan 1973. It is still *unverified*; try Ben-Herut (item 11).
- **"On Woman Saints"**: correct the title (singular "Woman" in the contents) and the pages, 316–324. The common summary ("male saints become women; women saints need not become men") is accurate (p. 324). But Ramanujan's evidence is undated legends from a 1967 encyclopaedia (n. 5). Treat it as **pattern, not history**.
- **The soul-as-woman gloss is attested in period**, in three places:
  - the 13th-c. *Ācāryahṛdayam* rule, "in love, a woman's words" (*Hymns*, p. 153);
  - Mahādēvi 68 (12th c.);
  - Deśika and Bhaṭṭar on the soul as bride (Narayanan, *Divine Consort*, pp. 232–236).

  The blunt "God alone is male" is Ramanujan's modern summary (*Hymns*, p. 153: "the Lord is the supreme male"; "On Woman Saints", p. 324).
- **The Āṇṭāḷ hagiography detail.** Ramanujan says only that the sacrilege "was discovered" (p. 148 n. 5). The reading list's "Periyāḻvār found the hair in the garland" still needs Venkatesan (another reader's item).
- **"All devotees are feminine" has a myth behind it.** The sixty thousand sages reborn as *gopīs* (*Hymns*, pp. 152–153; also Doniger, p. 298) is a better scriptural anchor for the Garland than anything in the reading list: men who wished to be women for the Lord and were reborn so. Its text and date are *unverified*.

## 6. Design implications

- **Creed and doctrine screen.**
  - Add "In knowledge, our own words; in love, a woman's words", after the *Ācāryahṛdayam*, p. 153. It is the faith's in-period proof text, quoted as the commentators' rule.
  - Second line: "Before God all men are women" (Ramanujan's summary, p. 324). It goes on a doctrine screen only as "as the saints' legends have it".
- **Doctrine tenet, "The Sixty Thousand".** The faith's myth: the sages who longed for Rāma were promised birth as cowherd women (*Hymns*, pp. 152–153). It is the Garland's answer to "why the body?". It goes beside Bhaṅgāśvana's choice, and the narrator flags its source as later tradition.
- **Gate step 1 (companions).** Add the "second family" beat: when she succeeds, her companions "give her away" to the Lord ("On Woman Saints", p. 322).
- **Gate step 2 (dawn bath) flavour.**
  - Missed dawn: "My long nights are my dowry" (from *Tiruviruttam* 12).
  - Failure: "She is only learning to say the name of his hill" (from *Tiruviruttam* 60).
- **Family reaction event, "The Mother's Verses".** A converting character's mother (or companion) defends her to the court in the voice of TVM 5.6: "Has the Lord taken her over? How shall I explain her to you?" Options: the court accepts (piety), or a kinsman calls it possession (stress, a hostile reaction).
- **Hostile and dissenting lines.**
  - Śaiva śaraṇa (Dāsimayya 133's argument): "The self in between is neither man nor woman; why trouble the body?"
  - Orthodox Vaiṣṇava: "The commentators made the girl an allegory; you have made her flesh" (from Ramanujan's complaint that the commentary "undoes the poem", p. 155 n.).
  - Rival citing Tilakavve: "God makes women men; you ask the reverse" ("On Woman Saints", p. 320).
- **Kin or reaction, Kalyāṇa (c_kalyani).** A śaraṇa poet character may cite Basavaṇṇa 703 approvingly. The faith may claim "the man who wore men's clothes only for the Lord". Keep c_kalyani at most an optional holy site. Never name Basavaṇṇa as a member.
- **Do not:**
  - use "bisexual", "neither man nor woman" or "in between" for the faith's daughters;
  - use Rēkavve's flesh-garland;
  - present the women-saint "phases" as medieval fact.
