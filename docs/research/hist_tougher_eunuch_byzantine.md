**Design takeaway:** the historical legal regime is *prohibition on paper, tolerated practice*, with a medical loophole, a canon-law distinction based on consent/intent, and a civil regime that progressively grants eunuchs wills (4th c.), freedom (558), adoption (c. 900) but never marriage. A mod faith that makes the "medical" loophole its official theology (Aristotelian minority medicine) is well-grounded.

### 2.2 Who performed castration, where, and the supply chain

- **Abasgia (east Black Sea) as the main 6th-c. source**; the kings castrated their subjects and sold them to the Romans; Justinian sent the Abasgian eunuch Euphratas to stop it: "Procopius observed that most eunuchs at the imperial court in Constantinople were Abasgian slaves." (p. 26); "the eunuch was charged by the emperor to go to Abasgia to tell the kings of the country to give up the practice of castrating their subjects and selling them to the Romans." (p. 40); "Justinian I commanded a halt to the creation of Abasgian eunuchs, and since they formed the majority of castrated servants in the empire, an alternative source of supply became necessary." (p. 64)
- **Lazica**: John/Mithridates, godfather of Peter the Iberian, "was a eunuch from Lazica" (App. no. 94, p. 149).
- **Armenia / Persarmenia**: Aelius Donatus: "in the east eunuchs came from Armenia" (p. 45); Narses the general "from Persarmenia" (App. no. 142, p. 157); Mamas "was from Armenia, and was castrated as a child due to illness" (App. no. 128, p. 155); Manuel (7th c.) and Artakios the koubikoularios were Armenians (pp. 61, 155).
- **Gaul**: Donatus says "most eunuchs in the west were from Gaul" (p. 45).
- **Persia**: Antiochus (praepositus of Theodosius II) and Stephen the Persian (sakellarios of Justinian II).
- **Slavs / "Scythians"**: Damianos the Slav, Niketas the Slav (patriarch 766); "Psellos reports that a corps of youthful Scythian eunuchs formed the personal bodyguard of the emperor Michael V." (p. 61) — "The Scythians were bought as slaves." (n. 50, p. 191). Basil Lekapenos's mother was a "Scythian" woman.
- **Arabs**: Samonas the Arab, Constantine the Saracen; "Arabs themselves were a source of eunuchs for Byzantium." (p. 64)
- **Muslim children kidnapped**: "In the ninth century Jahiz accuses the Byzantines of kidnapping Muslim children and castrating them." (p. 61); Jabbar says they then discarded them (n. 48, p. 191). Muqaddasi: "when Muslims raided the Byzantine empire they would release the castrated boys from the churches. One may suspect that they carried off such boys as booty." (n. 12, p. 194).
- **Verdun → Spain (and Byzantium)**: "eunuchs who were manufactured in the west by merchants in Verdun for the Spanish market, and had both testicles and penis removed." (p. 31) — Liudprand gifted four such "carzimasians" to Constantine VII in 949-50, "more precious to the emperor than anything" (p. 117).
- **Ephesus/Sardis slave trade (Herodotus' Panionius)** as the archetype of the castrated-boy trade (pp. 9, 26).
- **Home-grown Byzantine eunuchs** (the key transformation): "it is evident that in the Byzantine empire eunuchs could be supplied from the native population. This is explicitly acknowledged by the tenth-century Arab writer Masudi, who observes matter-of-factly that the Byzantines, like the Chinese, did castrate their own children." (p. 61). Natives attested already in the 6th-8th c. (Narses of Smyrna, bishop; Leontios of Syria; Leo of Sinope; Aetios; Niketas the patrikios of Paphlagonia b. 761/2; the statue of Plato set up "at the request of the parents of Plato" (p. 63)).
- **Paphlagonia** as the eunuch-producing region: "It is related that Constantine was the son of Metrios, a farmer in Paphlagonia, and that Metrios castrated his child for the sake of a career in Constantinople. This action is said to have been customary amongst farmers in the region." (p. 61). Magdalino's seven Paphlagonian court eunuchs 906-1042: Constantine the Paphlagonian, John the orphanotrophos, the Gongylios brothers, Theodore, Joseph Bringas, Symeon the New Theologian (p. 61). Plus George and Constantine (brothers of John), Constantine Phagitzes, Niketas the patrikios. Kedrenos: "during a famine in Pontos in the reign of Theodosius II Paphlagonians were forced to castrate and sell their own children." (p. 62). Hostile sources: Constantine Gongylios "an effeminate (literally 'reared in the shade') little man from Paphlagonia"; Bringas "a pitiable castrate from the Paphlagonian wastes" (p. 103).
- **Other native regions**: Boukellarion theme (Nikephoritzes; Nikephoros of Miletos from the village of Basileion), Pisidia (Niketas), Pergamum (Stephen), Lesbos/Mitylene (Thomas), Smyrna (Narses), Sinope (Leo), Constantinople itself (Polyeuktos "Born and raised in Constantinople, he was castrated by his parents", App. no. 174, p. 162).
- **Who held the knife**: (a) **doctors under duress** — Paul of Aegina "relates that he sometimes performed the operation under duress from 'certain more powerful people'" (p. 30); (b) **parents** (Metrios; Polyeuktos's parents; Nikephoros of Miletos's parents Eustathios and Maria; Niketas the patrikios's parents; Basil Lekapenos's father Romanos I); (c) **uncles / relatives** (Theophylact's eunuch castrates his nephew; Symeon's koitonites uncle brings him to court); (d) **masters** castrating slaves (Nicaea's exemption; Plautianus castrated 100 noble Romans for his daughter, p. 43); (e) **emperors / regimes** on political victims (see 2.5); (f) **the Chinese "knifers"** as comparative professional castrators (p. 30); (g) **the hijra dai ma** (midwife) (p. 30); (h) **Verdun merchants** (p. 31).
- **Training/education after castration**: Constantine the Paphlagonian passed from Basil the magistros to Samonas to the empress Zoe as a gift (pp. 55, 63); Nikephoros of Miletos "was sent to Constantinople, where he was educated, and he lived in the house of the magistros Moselle. He was enrolled amongst the imperial clergy" (App. no. 151, p. 159); Symeon/George "was sent by his parents from Paphlagonia to Constantinople to find employment in the imperial service" under his koitonites uncle (p. 62). Tougher: "There are allusions to the education of eunuchs before or after they were sent to Constantinople ... but nothing that indicates intensive induction in court tasks and behaviour." (pp. 109-10); the Ottoman palace school and the Ming palace school for eunuchs are the comparanda (p. 110, n. 127).

### 2.3 Age at castration, techniques, mortality

- **Age**: "Most eunuchs would have resulted from the castration of prepubertal boys, though castration could also occur after puberty." (p. 2). Court/clerical eunuchs were made as children ("children, still of a tender age"); adult castration is typical of religious self-castrates and punishment: "Castration after reaching adulthood is more usual in instances where the act is willingly embraced (for instance in a religious context: galli, early Christians, Skoptsy and hijras), or imposed as a form of punishment." (p. 32). Michael V castrated relatives "both mature and pre-pubertal" (p. 28). Rousselle: only pre-pubertal castrates are "true eunuchs" physically (p. 32).
- **Paul of Aegina, Epitome 6.68 (7th c.) — the two methods** (p. 30): "That by compression is performed thus: children, still of a tender age, are placed in a vessel of hot water, and then when the bodily parts are softened in the bath, the testicles are to be squeezed with the fingers until they disappear, and, being dissolved, can no longer be felt. The method by excision is as follows: let the person to be made a eunuch be placed upon a bench, and the scrotum with the testicles grasped by the fingers of the left hand, and stretched; two straight incisions are then to be made with a scalpel, one in each testicle; and when the testicles start up they are to be dissected around and cut out, having merely left the very thin bond of connexion between the vessels in their natural state. This method is preferred to that by compression; for those who have had them squeezed sometimes still desire intercourse, a certain part, as it would appear, of the testicles having escaped the compression."
- **Penectomy rare in Byzantium**: "Paul also reveals that in the Byzantine context the removal of the penis as well (penectomy) was not common." (p. 30); "since most eunuchs in Byzantium retained their penises" (n. 77, p. 202). Contrast: hijras, Chinese, carzimasians, Muslim preference (n. 49, p. 181: "Like the Chinese, Muslims seem to have preferred eunuchs who had both penis and testicles removed").
- **Terms reflect technique**: ektomias "cut" (excision, the usual term); thlibias "from 'to press'"; thladias "from 'to crush'" (p. 31).
- **Mortality**: Novel 142's "out of ninety males castrated, only three survived" (n. 93, p. 193); Theodosios son of Leo V "died after being castrated" (App. no. 42, p. 140).
- **Physical effects**: "primary hypogonadism" — no facial hair, unbroken voice, infantile penis, elongated limbs, pale wrinkled skin, "Body shape could be more akin to that of a female, due to the increased development of subcutaneous fat and fat deposits concentrated on the regions of the breasts, hips and buttocks" (p. 32). Post-pubertal castrates "would not be as visually obvious" (p. 32). Sexual function: pre-pubertal "next to no sex drive", post-pubertal "much reduced or absent, though they could (if they has a penis) experience erections and ejaculations" with variety (p. 34); Wille & Beier: decrease in 75% within 6 months (pp. 33-4). Eunuch monk Eutropius ejaculated over his godson (pp. 33, 79). Hijra taking female hormones to feminise further (p. 34).
- **Medical castration (the loophole)**: hernia treatment — Miracles of St Artemios; Gregory of Tours' doctor who learned castration in Constantinople (p. 29); John the Faster and John of Heraclea "castrated as children on the grounds of ill health" (p. 29); Mamas (Armenia) likewise. Lemerle doubts the "ill health" story for Nikephoros of Miletos (n. 13, p. 194).

### 2.4 Motives: punishment vs career vs piety vs sex

- **Career strategy (parents)**: Metrios; "the rewards that court eunuchs could win during their careers ... may have provided Byzantine families with a strong economic incentive to castrate a relative. The parallel of Italian families castrating sons and male relatives for potentially lucrative careers as castrati springs readily to mind." (p. 64). The Suda: "the example of Eutropius' successful career in the fourth century led to an increase in the number of eunuchs and even caused some post-pubertal males to castrate themselves." (p. 65).
- **Piety (parents / relatives)**: "Some Byzantine texts indicate sons or nephews could be castrated on the grounds of Christian sentiment." (p. 27). Muqaddasi: Byzantines "castrated their own children and dedicated them to church careers, 'in order that they would not be occupied with women and harmed by lust'." (p. 70). Jahiz "reports that this was done for religious reasons." (p. 63). Nikephoros of Miletos "His Life records that his parents intended him to have a religious career, and thus had him castrated when he was a child." (p. 70). Tougher's conclusion: "castration of one's children could be seen as a positive Christian act." (p. 129).
- **Theophylact's eunuch's justification** (the clearest in-world theology): "although voluntary castration by adults was abhorrent, undertaken for sexual purposes, the castration of a consenting boy by his own family, was a respectable Christian act. Theophylact's eunuch further justified castration on the grounds that it was akin to pruning a vine and that ascetics also altered their bodies through their tough regimen, thus countering the accusation that those who embraced castration were mutilating the image of God." (p. 75). Tougher adds: "One wonders, though, how much say the children would have had in their castration." (n. 72, p. 196). Parallel: Yahya al-Ghazzal told the empress Theodora circumcision "was like pruning a vine to make it more vigorous" (n. 73, p. 196-7).
- **Self-castration for purity**: Justin Martyr's Christian; Origen; Leontius of Antioch "castrated himself as he associated with a woman called Eustolia, and he wanted to demonstrate the purity of their relationship. He was defrocked, but subsequently was made bishop of Antioch by Constantius II." (App. no. 124, p. 154). Mr Seaton of Edinburgh, 1707 (p. 28). Basilakes' Bagoas castrated himself "in order to keep his lover and his good looks" and is reviled for the self-castration, not for being a eunuch (n. 123, p. 203).
- **Punishment (sexual crimes)**: Middle Assyrian Laws (adultery, male-male sex); Malalas under Justinian (rapists, homosexuals); Ekloga (bestiality); Abelard (p. 28). Modern parallel the author notes: "a wider debate about the use of castration as treatment for sex offenders such as rapists and paedophiles" (p. 28); Nazi Germany used forced castration for "sexually motivated murder, sadistic physical injury ... and sexual offences" (n. 17, p. 180). **Sergios the Arab** who insulted the eunuch ambassador Andrew as "neither man nor woman": "When Sergios was later captured he had him castrated then hanged." (App. no. 10, p. 135).
- **Political castration** (see 2.5).
- **Sex/gender**: Nero's Sporus; Juvenal's women lovers of post-pubertal eunuchs; male-to-female transsexuals (p. 27-8); the Reimer case (p. 29).
- **Music**: Byzantine eunuch singers (Odo of Deuil 1147; Anthony of Novgorod 1200); St Lazaros possibly a castrati training house (p. 76).

### 2.5 Castration as political weapon (dynastic use)

- Leo V castrated Michael I's sons Theophylact (→ monk Eustratios) and Niketas (→ monk Ignatios, twice patriarch) in 813 (pp. 28, 74).
- Michael II castrated Leo V's four sons (Symbatios/Constantine→Basil, Basil, Gregory, Theodosios — the last died) and exiled them to Prote (App. no. 42, p. 140).
- Constantine VII castrated Stephen Doukas (913) and Romanos Lekapenos (Stephen's son); Romanos I castrated his bastard Basil "so that he would not be able to usurp the rights of Romanos' legitimate heirs" (n. 23, p. 180).
- Joseph Bringas ordered the castration of the Bulgarian hostage prince Romanos, who later became patrikios, praipositos and strategos of Abydos (App. no. 182, p. 163).
- Michael V (1041-2) "castrated all his males relatives (those who were not already eunuchs), both mature and pre-pubertal" (p. 28).
- Rationale: "The idea seems to have been to bar the victims from ever holding imperial office again, as eunuchs could not attain the position of emperor." (p. 28). Symbolism: "In the case of the castration of opponents, the masculinity of the castrator is enhanced and that of the victim is nullified." (p. 52).
- Germanos (patriarch 715-33) allegedly castrated when his father was executed for the murder of Constans II (p. 70).

### 2.6 Comparative material (galli, Skoptsy, hijra, China, Ottoman)

- **Galli of Cybele**: "The eunuch priests of Cybele were known as galli, and their leader bore the title archigallus. Particular behaviour and rites were associated with the galli. They would dress as women, wear makeup and jewellery, and grow their hair long. Annual rituals developed, most memorably the Day of Blood on 24 March, on which the priests would lacerate themselves, and perhaps castration occurred too." (p. 10). Catullus 63: "once this gallus has become castrated he is no longer termed a 'he' but a 'she'." (p. 35). Not all galli were castrated (p. 23). Rousselle on galli: post-pubertal, sexually active but infertile (n. 77, p. 182). Martial 3.81: a gallus performing cunnilingus (n. 89). Mango: shrines of the archangel Michael in Asia Minor took over sites of Attis (p. 107). The Catterick "gallus" burial (p. 1).
- **Skoptsy (Russia, 1770s-1930s)**: "Male Skoptsy could undergo the removal of just the testicles ('minor seal') or of the penis also ('major seal'). Unusually female members of the community could also endure genital mutilation, encompassing the removal of nipples, breasts and external parts of the vagina." (p. 12). "Charismatic prophets played an important part"; peasant origin; persecuted, exile to Siberia (p. 12). Some castrated as children at parents' wish (p. 27). "The Skoptsy believed that self-castration transformed mortal beings into angels." (p. 107); icons of Michael "mounted on a white horse, which symbolised the purity of castration" (n. 114, p. 203). Skoptsy as bankers: "Pour une caisse comme pour un harem, un eunuque est le plus sûr gardien" (n. 101, p. 183). Self-presentation via photography (pp. 24, 111).
- **Hijras (India)**: "The hijras are castrated voluntarily, both the testicles and the penis being removed ... dress and act like women, taking female names and using female kinship terms to describe the relationships between them. They form well-organised hierarchical communities, mostly in northern India, living in houses headed by gurus" (p. 12); power to bless fertility and to curse (pp. 12-13); the castration rite "performed by a dai ma, meaning midwife, which indicates the symbolism of the act as a form of rebirth ... The severed genitals are put in a pot and interred under a tree. The period after the operation also has symbolic significance, being equated with the recovery after childbirth." (p. 30). "the hijras hold that it is through emasculation that they acquire their ritual power, and that only those who are castrated are real hijras" (p. 69) — but "the intersex are the real hijras" too (n. 5). Asha Devi unseated as mayor because male (p. 13). Hijra as MPs valued for "lack of family connections" (p. 44).
- **China**: Ming palace school; "knifers" inside the imperial city; "the precious" preserved in jars for promotion and burial (p. 30); foreign supply from Annam and Korea plus domestic (p. 45); eunuchs married, adopted, kept nephews — "The strength of the family among eunuchs debunks the myth ... that eunuchs had no family ties" (Jay, pp. 45-6); Confucian literati vs eunuchs as a "dualistic system" (p. 47); distinctive dress (Plate 13).
- **Ottoman**: black and white eunuchs; the chief black eunuch (Plate 9); palace school "where they began their career at the palace" (p. 110); silver quills in turbans for urination (n. 71, p. 182); the sacred eunuch society at the Prophet's tomb in Madina (mid-12th c.) and Mecca — 17 and 14 still serving in 1990 (n. 1, p. 194); Taranges commanded Alp Arslan's forces at Mantzikert 1071; Orhan had a freed-slave eunuch Şerefeddîn Mukbil running a Bithynian hospice (1324) and eunuch envoys Khatzes and Mertzianes (p. 126).
- **Castrati**: Italian families castrating sons for careers; cover stories (boar bites, falls); excommunication for castrators; marriage controversies (Sorlisi 1667, Tenducci 1766); angelic voices, "puttini castrati"; Moreschi "the angel of Rome"; Farinelli as Spanish court politician and Knight of Calatrava (pp. 11-12, 44, 107, 112).
- **Norman Sicily**: the one Western court to adopt eunuchs (p. 11).

## 3. Gender dossier

### 3.1 How eunuchs were gendered: the three co-existing models

Tougher's overall thesis: "in Byzantium opinion on eunuchs was diverse rather than homogeneous. Multiple identities co-existed. Byzantines had a choice in how to understand eunuchs." (p. 109); "As well as being acknowledged as other, eunuchs could be considered as masculine or feminine." (p. 111); "As for eunuchs being considered a third gender, it seems that this was a possibility, but not the only one." (p. 118).

**(a) Feminised / female**
- Aristotle, *Generation of Animals*: "Eunuchs, too, do not go bald, because of their transition into the female state ... This deformity constitutes a change from the male state to the female." (p. 35); "In his Generation of Animals, Aristotle explicitly states that eunuchs are transformed by mutilation into the female state." (p. 34). Greek medical frame: "men's bodies were hot and dry, whilst women's were cold and damp" (p. 34). Aristotle was a friend of the eunuch tyrant Hermias of Atarneus and married his adopted niece Pythias (n. 93, p. 183; p. 46).
- Catullus' Attis becomes "she" (p. 35).
- Claudian on Eutropius: "as a eunuch Eutropius has become feminised, if not female ... Claudian describes Eutropius as an Amazon, and asserts that he should have devoted himself to the female art of spinning instead." (p. 97).
- Ammianus: Cylaces "was good at cajoling like a woman because he was a eunuch." (p. 97).
- Basil of Caesarea Ep. 115: eunuchs "unmanly (ἄνανδρον) and effeminate (θηλυδριῶδες)" but also "unwomanly (ἄθηλυ)" and "mad for women (γυναικομανές)" (pp. 97-8).
- Leo Choirosphaktes: "it is as if he had been born of two women, since he was womanish (θηλυνόμενος)" (p. 103).
- Skylitzes: "little castrated men who had been raised in the shade of ladies' chambers (ἀνδράρια ἐκτετμημένα θαλαμευόμενα καὶ σκιατροφῆ)" (p. 103); Agathias on Narses: "a puny little man, a eunuch of the bedchamber, used to a soft and sedentary existence" (n. 69, p. 202).
- Theophylact's critic: palace eunuchs in the women's quarters "will become feminised, due to spending so much time with females" — but the defender "voices the more traditional view that eunuchs are indeed inherently feminised." (p. 108).
- Nikephoros Gregoras on his uncle John of Heraclea: "as a eunuch he was a feminised being, and women had less ability to control themselves than men did." (p. 79); "because as a feminised eunuch he was more prey to passions than ordinary men" (p. 103). Theodore Prodromos: "eunuchs naturally tend to cry" (n. 10, p. 206).
- Theophilos rebukes Theodore Krateros as "effeminate and unmanly (θηλυδρία καὶ ἄνανδρε)" before Theodore unseats the Arab (p. 105).
- Liudprand: "soft, effeminate, long-sleeved, hooded, veiled, lying, neutral-gendered, idle creatures" (p. 116).

**(b) Third sex / neither**
- Euripides' Phrygian: "'neither man nor woman', a descriptor for eunuchs that became common in antiquity." (p. 21).
- Claudian: Bellona says "another sex opposes her husband as Rome is led by eunuchs"; Roma calls them "an 'unfortunate band', rejected by one sex and not embraced by the other." (p. 97).
- Claudius Mamertinus: "eunuchs were exiled from the fellowship of human kind since they belonged to neither sex." (p. 98). Epiphanius: "neither men nor women". Historia Augusta: Severus Alexander "asserted that eunuchs were a third type of human." (p. 98).
- Photios on John Angourios: "an 'androgyne'" (p. 103).
- Leo VI Nov. 98: "some strange sex (ξένον τι γένος)" (p. 111).
- Sergios insults ambassador Andrew (667) as "neither man nor woman" (App. no. 10, p. 135).
- Liudprand: the eunuch admiral "was not a real man, but only a sort of man (hominem quandam) as he had ceased to be male but was not female"; another "of neither gender (qui neutrius erat generis)" (p. 116).
- Balatri asked by the Cham of Tartary if he was male or female (n. 21, p. 200).
- PBE's database category: Sex = M / F / E (p. 21).

**(c) Angelic / luminous / pure**
- "In dreams and visions angels were often mistaken for eunuchs of the earthly court." (p. 106) — John the Almsgiver; Bardas's dream (chamberlains and praipositoi as angels; St Peter flanked by two praipositoi); Narratio de S. Sophia; Vision of the monk Kosmas; the Timarion (p. 106-7).
- Oneirocriticon of Achmet: "if one dreams of a graceful and tall (εὐειδῆ εὐήλικον) eunuch, the eunuch should be considered an angel, on account of the fact that a eunuch is pure (καθαρόν), angel-like (ἀγγελοπρεπές), and not given to fleshly desires." (p. 107).
- Corippus on Narses the spatharius: luminous appearance, beauty, height (p. 101). "This luminosity had connotations of divinity, both for angels and for eunuchs." (p. 107).
- "white or bright clothing being associated with eunuchs" (p. 110). Leo the sakellarios wears a white chiton and red chlamys (p. 112).
- Dumitrescu: "angels were in some sense the patrons of eunuchs" (p. 114); the beardless grapheus of Coislin 79 has the archangel Michael intercede for him; Constantine the proedros's pendant adds Michael and Gabriel to the Deesis; Basil Lekapenos's Limburg reliquary — "Basil saw himself in this celestial angelic guard" (Pentcheva, p. 114).
- Monastic life as "the angelic life"; mystical castration by angels in dreams (p. 78). Kallikrenites styled an ἄγγελος/messenger (n. 46, p. 206). Byzantines could consider Daniel a eunuch (n. 46).

**Ringrose's thesis and Tougher's critique** (pp. 98-9, 108-11): Ringrose argues a positive third-gender construct became normative by the 10th c., via acculturation (training) and distinctive dress, keyed to Theophylact's *In Defence*. Tougher: positive and negative views were both ancient topoi ("To treat negative views of eunuchs in Byzantium as the survival of outdated topoi but positive views of eunuchs in Byzantium as authentic sentiment is a questionable position. Both were equally stereotypical." p. 102); the positive etymology (eunuch = εὐνοοῦν "well-minded") already in Epiphanius (p. 102; also Nikon of the Black Mountain's nomocanon, n. 58); Theophylact wrote to console a brother "fed up with the hostility eunuchs had to face" — "If there had been a general shift to a positive perception of eunuchs in Byzantium in the middle period, it is highly unlikely that Theophylact would have had to produce the text." (p. 108). Dress marks status, not gender: "Status was key rather than gender: eunuchs were not being singled out." (p. 110). Ringrose herself floats "more than three genders in Byzantine society" (p. 111).

### 3.2 Physiology (Aristotle vs Galen) — what the book offers
The book gives Aristotle, not Galen, as the authority for "transition into the female state" (pp. 34-5, Gen. an. 4.1 766a and 5.3 783b-784a; Hist. an. 8.50), plus Mayhew (2004) on Aristotle and women. The medical frame is humoral (hot/dry vs cold/damp). Byzantine physicians: Paul of Aegina (7th c.) castrates under duress; Basil of Ancyra was "one-time physician" (p. 33); Michael the eunuch was doctor to Alexios I (p. 121); the Kecharitomene typikon wants a eunuch doctor for the nuns (p. 78). **For the mod's "Aristotelian minority medicine" framing, the Aristotle baldness passage is the exact hook; Galen is not discussed by Tougher.**

### 3.3 Dress, beardlessness, voice
- Beardlessness as the identifier in art (Theodora panel, Leo Bible, Coislin 79, Ignatios mosaic), with caveats: before the 7th c. beards were not de rigueur; some known eunuchs are drawn bearded (Madrid Skylitzes) (pp. 23, n. 158).
- Philotheos' insignia: nipsistiarios — linen kamision with a basin; spatharokoubikoularios — gold-handled sword; ostiarios — golden baton with precious stones; bearded protospatharios — golden collar with gems; eunuch protospatharios — the same **plus pearls**; eunuch patrikios ranks above bearded patrikios (pp. 57-8, 110). Kalamara suggests an 11th-c. eunuch dress code (n. 129).
- Voice: Odo of Deuil 1147 — "the mingling of voices, the heavier with the light, the eunuch's namely, with the manly voice (for many of them were eunuchs), softened the hearts of the Franks" (p. 27). Anthony of Novgorod 1200 heard eunuch singers at Hagia Sophia (p. 122). "the angel of Rome".
- Height and softness: tall, long-limbed, pale (p. 32); Farinelli's exhumation (p. 32); "the tallest princess anyone had ever seen" (n. 72).
- Insult: "Klou klou" shouted by troops at John the protovestiarios — "a phrase customarily directed against eunuchs" (p. 104).

### 3.4 Eunuchs and women's spaces
- Imperial women always attended by eunuchs: Jerome's matron with her "semiviris" (p. 43); Basilina taught by Mardonius; Eusebia communicates via eunuchs; empress acquires her own grand chamberlain only in the 5th c. — "the direction of borrowing was from male to female" (p. 43). Pseudo-Kodinos: at coronation "the empress is escorted either by two of her closest relatives or two eunuchs" (p. 123). Zoe's "eunuchs of the bedchamber" (Stephen the Pergamene, John the Philosopher, Sgouritzes). Eirene Doukaina's retinue (Constantine, Michael). Theodora Palaiologina's John of Heraclea. Niketas (patriarch 766) "worked in the women's quarters" (App. no. 155).
- Nunneries: Justinian wants eunuch or old stewards; Kecharitomene typikon: "the posts of steward (oikonomos) and assistant-steward were always to be held by eunuchs. Further, the two priests in the nunnery were to be eunuchs, as was the spiritual father. The doctor for the nunnery was to be a eunuch too, or at least an old man." (p. 78) — yet "access to the nunnery was generally forbidden to eunuchs as it was to other men" (n. 114). Athanasia sent her eunuchs to male monasteries on becoming a nun (n. 101).
- "Transvestite nuns": women entering male monasteries "did not pretend to be whole men, but rather eunuchs, whose features they more closely resembled, especially the lack of a beard." (p. 72). Manuel II's Athos ban explicitly targets women disguised as eunuchs (n. 82).
- Eunuchs as teachers of royal girls abroad: Elissaios for Rotrud (782), eunuchs for Hadwig; Anna Komnene educated by a eunuch (pp. 115, 121).

### 3.5 Marriage, sex and eunuchs
- Byzantium "firmly rejected marriage for eunuchs" (p. 46); Leo VI Nov. 98 upholds the ban; Tougher knows "no examples of Byzantine eunuchs who were married" but suspects illegal marriages (p. 66, n. 126). Chinese and Islamic eunuchs could marry (p. 45-6). Castrati marriages controversial (Ancillon).
- Eutropius' "sister" who is also called his "spouse" — "a subintroducta, a woman brought into his household in a chaste union sometimes compared to sisterhood" (Long), or "even ... another court eunuch" (p. 46, n. 132).
- Sexual stereotypes: "commonly thought of as passive sexual partners for men" (p. 75); satirical song on Polyeuktos and Basil Lekapenos "for having shrivelled penises and large anuses" (p. 103); Theodosius II "lusted after the eunuch [Chrysaphius] who was exceedingly good-looking" (p. 40); Eutropius had male lovers (Ptolemy) per Claudian (p. 97); Juvenal's women lovers (pp. 28, 33); Basil of Ancyra's warning to virgins (p. 33); the eunuch monk Eutropius and his godson (pp. 33, 79). Chaste-by-nature counter-image: Theophylact says eunuchs "cannot experience erections or ejaculations" (p. 78); the eunuch monk who travelled with a girl "because he believed that Satan did not send temptation to eunuchs" (p. 77).

### 3.6 Eunuch saints, holy men, bishops, patriarchs, monasteries
- **Patriarchs of Constantinople**: firm — Ignatios the Younger (847-58, 867-77), Niketas the Slav (766-80), Stephen (925-7), Polyeuktos (956-70), Eustratios Garidas (1081-4); probable — Germanos (715-33), Theophylact Lekapenos (933-56); suspected — Constantine Leichoudes (1059-64); Methodios (843-7) a "natural eunuch" by St Peter's miracle; Macedonius (496-511) "it emerged that he had been castrated" (pp. 70-2).
- **Bishops**: Leontius of Antioch (self-castrate), Narses of Ascalon, the eunuch successor of Heraclides at Ephesus, Leo of Nicaea in Thrace (martyred 815), a bishop of Kyzikos (846), Nikephoros of Miletos (saint), the bishop of Leukas (968), Antony Paches of Nicomedia, John of Side, Theophylact's contemporaries (archbishop of Thessalonike; bishops of Pydna, Petra, Bulgarian Edessa), Solomon of Laodicea (executed by Kilidj Arslan), Niketas of Chonai, John of Heraclea (pp. 70-1, 122).
- **Saints/martyrs**: Dorotheus and Gorgonius (Nicomedia), Ousthazades and Azades (Persia), Indes, Hyacinthus, Protus, Theodore; Byzantine saints Ignatios, Nikephoros of Miletos, John the Faster, John of Heraclea, Niketas the patrikios, (Germanos; Symeon the New Theologian disputed) (pp. 73-4). Metrios the castrating father himself became a saint (n. 54, p. 191).
- **Eunuch-only monasteries** (p. 72-3): (1) the "monastery of eunuchs" near Jericho, 6th c., founded by Juliana Anicia's freed household eunuchs who **asked** for segregation; (2) **St Lazaros, Constantinople**, built by Leo VI (Theoph. Cont. 364-5; Patria 4.33 says Basil I built, Leo enriched) — abbot Hierotheos refused Leo's third wife burial there (n. 52); perhaps trained castrati singers; (3) **Mt Galesion**: St Lazaros converted the monastery of the Saviour into a eunuch house after "scandals" between eunuch and bearded monks (p. 76); (4) **Attaleiates' hospice of the All-Merciful Christ / chapel of the Prodromos**, Constantinople, 1077 Diataxis: eunuch monks required because they possess *apatheia* and the house was "in the middle of Constantinople and in close proximity to the agora, and thus dangerous for bearded monks" (p. 77); bearded admitted under conditions (n. 54); (5) **Symeon the Sanctified's monastery for eunuchs in Thessalonike** (p. 73). Doubtful: the Katharoi ("The Pure") of Narses; "the monastery of eunuchs" at Pandemos on Mt Olympos; the Agauroi/"Augaroi" ('Effeminates') on Olympos (p. 73). Tougher: "there is no doubt that monasteries reserved for eunuchs existed" (p. 73), but mixed houses were the norm.
- **Exclusion**: Euthymius and Sabas barred beardless youths and "a eunuch with a face like a woman's" from the lavra; the Sinai rule of Mar Saba opens with this ban (p. 75); yet Euthymius admitted the born-eunuch Gabriel (Cappadocian, trilingual) confined to his cell 25 years, later abbot of St Stephen (p. 76); Sabas sent eunuchs to the coenobium of Theodosius for training (p. 76). Athos: Symeon the Sanctified chased off in 1078 "together with his three beardless disciples", restored by the Paradosis of 1089 as "the only eunuch allowed to live on the holy mountain" (p. 76).

### 3.7 Collapse of the eunuch ideal (11th-15th c.)
- Nikephoritzes (d. 1078) "the last example of the all-powerful eunuch ministers" though John the protovestiarios follows (p. 119). Komnenian "aristocratic family rule" replaces meritocratic government; Lemerle: the paradynast ends with Alexios, whose right hands were his mother and wife (p. 125).
- Tougher's own view: eunuchs' value "had become compromised ... when it became especially clear from the example of the Paphlagonian dynasty that eunuchs could have agendas of their own" (p. 125); Alexios relied on family and foreigners; eunuchs return under Andronikos I and the Angeloi; the real break is **1204** and the Nicaean "household government"; loss of Anatolian recruiting ground; rise of the Ottomans who used eunuchs themselves (pp. 125-6). Last named significant court eunuch: Andronikos Eonopolites (co-commander at Berat 1281). Pseudo-Kodinos (14th c.) no longer singles out eunuchs (p. 123). Fulcher of Chartres 1097: "about 20,000 eunuchs are kept there" (p. 117). Late hostility: Manasses' viper, Kedrenos' aphorism "If you have a eunuch, kill him; if you haven't, buy one and kill him." (p. 103). Positive late image: Vetanos in *Livistros and Rhodamne* (p. 124).

## 4. Offices and institutions

### 4.1 Late Roman (4th-6th c.) — Latin titles
- **praepositus sacri cubiculi** (grand chamberlain), head of the **cubicularii**; rank *illustris* from 384, equal to prefects/masters of soldiers in 422; could be count, patrician, consul (Eutropius, 399 — the only eunuch consul) (pp. 36, 41).
- Subordinates: **primicerius sacri cubiculi**, **castrensis sacri palatii** (first eunuch castrensis: Amantius of the empress Eudoxia), **comes domorum per Cappadociam**, **comes sacrae vestis** (pp. 41-2); **spatharius** (chief bodyguard: Chrysaphius, Narses, Cosmas, Hylasius); **sacellarius** (treasurer: Narses, Paul, Callinicus, Andrew; PLRE: "The post of sacellarius was held only by eunuchs" p. 59 n. 30); **chartularius sacri palatii** (Smaragdus); **tribune** (Elijah); **exarch of Italy** (Eleutherios, Eutychios, Olympios, Smaragdus).
- Western court too: Ravenna (Acolius, Lauricius), Ostrogothic Theoderic's Seda and Triwila (p. 42).
- Empress acquires her own praepositus/castrensis/primicerius in the 5th-6th c. (Amantius, Stephen, Calopodius, Andrew).

### 4.2 Philotheos' *Kletorologion* (899) — the eunuch hierarchy
**Eight eunuch titles (dignities), bottom up** (pp. 57-8): 1 nipsistiarios (ablutions attendant; linen kamision with basin) — 2 koubikoularios — 3 spatharokoubikoularios (armed chamberlain; gold-handled sword) — 4 ostiarios (doorkeeper; jewelled golden baton) — 5 primikerios — 6 protospatharios (chief guard; collar with gems *and pearls*) — 7 praipositos ("most illustrious praipositos") — 8 patrikios (eunuch patrikioi rank above bearded patrikioi). Eighteen titles existed for the bearded (n. 28). Later additions: **proedros** (created for Basil Lekapenos by Nikephoros II), **nobelissimos** (first eunuch: Constantine, brother of John the orphanotrophos, under Michael V — a title "usually reserved for bearded men, and for members of the imperial family" n. 60), **sebastophoros** (Stephen the Pergamene, Nikephoritzes, Romanos Lekapenos the eunuch), **raiktor**, **hypertimos**, **protoproedros**, **pansebastos/sebastos** (Kallikrenites).

**Ten eunuch-only offices, top down** (pp. 58-9): 1 **parakoimomenos** ("the one who sleeps beside"; chief eunuch; appears late in Maurice's reign, individual post by 9th c.) — 2 **protovestiarios** (wardrobe; seals from 8th c.) — 3 **epi tes trapezes** (master of the emperor's table) — 4 master of the Augusta's table — 5 **papias of the Great Palace** (keys, security) — 6 **deuteros** — 7 **pingernes** of the emperor (waiter/cupbearer) — 8 pingernes of the Augusta — 9 papias of the Magnaura — 10 papias of Daphne. "all these offices ... convey close physical proximity to the emperor and empress, and especially care for their bodies and their space." (p. 59). Below them "a host of lowlier chamberlains" (koitonitai, "eunuchs of the bedchamber", epi tou koitonos).

**Open to eunuchs**: "almost all other posts in the imperial administration too, including the strategoi of the themes" (p. 59); frequently sakellarios, logothete of the drome (Staurakios, Aetios, Theoktistos, Niketas Xylinites, Nikephoritzes), kanikleios (Theoktistos, Eustathios Kyminianos), droungarios of the fleet (Constantine Gongylios, Bringas, Niketas, Eustathios Kyminianos), droungarios of the watch (Damianos, Symeon, Manuel), grand hetaireiarch (Constantine the Saracen, Eustratios), orphanotrophos, mystikos, chartoularios of the drome (Sinoutis), notary (Elissaios), stratopedarch, strategos autokrator, doux of Antioch/Iberia/Paristrion/Cyprus, krites/praitor of Peloponnese and Hellas, exarch of monasteries (Anthimos), oikonomos of Hagia Sophia (Calopodius), megas ekklesiarches (Pepagomenos), tatas of the court, megas droungarios, megas doux (Trebizond).

**Closed to eunuchs**: "eparch (city prefect of Constantinople), quaestor (judge and legislator), and domestikos (military officer, of which there were several variants)" (p. 59) — but Gregory was eunuch eparch in 652, and in the 11th c. eunuchs became domestic of the scholai (Nicholas, Constantine nobelissimos, Theodore); a eunuch played a mock eparch at Alexios III's court (n. 33). **Eunuchs could never be emperor** (p. 28).

**The bearded / eunuch divide**: "there was a separate set of titles available to eunuchs, distinct from those available to other men (whom Philotheos defines as 'the bearded')." (p. 57); Assyrian parallel ša rēši vs ša ziqni "the bearded ones" (p. 22). "Paradoxically, they were both set apart from their bearded colleagues and integrated amongst them" (p. 60). Non-eunuchs sometimes held eunuch posts (Basil the Macedonian as parakoimomenos, p. 59).

### 4.3 Military commanders who were eunuchs

### 4.4 Eunuchs as regents and kingmakers
- Eusebius in the succession talks after Constantius II (p. 37); Amantius tried to engineer Anastasius's succession, executed by Justin I (p. 40); Callinicus "played a crucial part in the accession of Justin II" (p. 40); Chrysaphius plotted Attila's assassination (p. 40).
- Eirene's Staurakios and Aetios ("Aetios had a brother Leo, whom he allegedly planned to make emperor", p. 65; Staurakios "planned to seize power", App. no. 197); Leo of Sinope deserted Eirene for Nikephoros I.
- Theoktistos effectively ran Theodora's regency (842-55); Constantine the Paphlagonian and the Gongylioi ran Zoe's regency (913-20) — Symeon of Bulgaria mocked Byzantium "for being governed by eunuchs" (p. 20); Basil Lekapenos dominated under Basil II until 985; Joseph Bringas headed Romanos II's government; John the orphanotrophos made his brother Michael IV and nephew Michael V emperors, arranged Zoe's adoption of the nephew, and himself wanted to be patriarch (pp. 56, 65, 82); Symeon the proedros "was instrumental in Romanos Argyros being chosen as Constantine's heir"; Ergodotes and Constantine Phagitzes sent to fetch Dalassenos as heir; Stephen the Pergamene fetched Constantine Monomachos to be emperor (1042); Manuel, Niketas Xylinites and Theodore proclaimed Theodora sole ruler (1055) and chose Michael VI; Leo Kydoniates talked Eudokia out of marrying Botaneiates; Constantine Philoxenites re-proclaimed Isaac II (1203) and helped Mourtzouphlos seize power (1204); Michael Kallikrenites mediated the Palaiologan civil war; eunuchs influential under Anne of Savoy's regency (pp. 121-2).
- Structural explanation: access — "The key factor explaining its political significance ... is access to the emperor." (p. 41); Hopkins: scapegoats, unassimilable, "lightning conductors"; Wittfogel: "an entirely trustworthy control group within the absolutist bureaucracy" (p. 47); Patterson: "The absolute ruler . . . requires the ultimate slave; and the ultimate slave is best represented in the anomalous person of the eunuch" (p. 48); Tougher: "the fundamental reason for the use of eunuchs was that they were symbols of royalty" (p. 52). Note Eirene: "It is thought that Eirene depended especially on eunuchs because they could not aspire to her imperial position" (n. 4, p. 189).
