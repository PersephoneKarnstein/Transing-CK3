## 1. The argument in brief

**Conrad.** In the *kuma koro* ("ancient speech", p. 189), the bards' name for the Sunjata corpus, men are the instruments of conquest and women are the *sabu* ("sources, providers") of all that men achieve (p. 191). The name Son-Jara itself praises the mother: "Sogolon's Jara" (p. 190). Conrad sorts the epic's women into two rough kinds (p. 192): sorceresses who act through *dalilu* and *nyama*, and women who act without sorcery (the *femme fatale* who betrays a ruler's secret, the hostess, the alliance-bride). His evidence is mostly his own recordings of Maninka bards in Upper Guinea, 1991–94 (p. 190), above all the Fadama bard Djanka Tassey Condé, read against Brett-Smith. He warns that today's worldview is not that of 13th–14th-c. Mali (p. 191) and that it is "too early" to judge historical content (p. 190). He closes by asking whether women's war leadership was hidden by a convention that credited it to husbands (pp. 221–223).

**The rest of the volume, on dating.** No contributor treats the epic as a reliable source for 13th-c. events (Austen, p. 3).
- Wilks argues that the fixed **songs** are older than the narrative and may go back to the 14th-c. court (pp. 44–49). The only externally validated bard is Dūghā (pp. 44–51). He reads Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's "Sāriq Jāṭa" as the first documentary reference to Sunjata, Mūsā's great-uncle (p. 47).
- Austen argues that what Ibn Baṭṭūṭa heard was **panegyric**, short praise of many royal ancestors, not epic (p. 70). Before the late 19th c. no Arabic or European source mentions Sunjata except Ibn Khaldūn (pp. 72–73). The epic's classic form arose in the 18th-c. Mininjan chiefdom around Kangaba and its seven-yearly Kamabolon re-roofing (pp. 72–75).
- Camara: the Kela version rests on a 19th-c. Maninka manuscript in Arabic script, kept secret by the Jabate bards (pp. 64–66).
- Johnson supplies the badenya/fadenya frame and the bard's licence to comment on power (pp. 16, 21).

## 2. What matters for the design (page refs)

### Women as *sabu*, and the price of power

- **The jinn's price is kin, and a sister pays it herself.** In the fullest Upper Guinea variants, Sumaworo's spirit guide is Jinna Maghan, "king of the jinn" (p. 206). The price of the four objects (the Soso balafon, magic arrows and others) is one human being each, "members of his own immediate family" (p. 206). He refuses and tries to substitute others. His sister **Kosiya Kante** comes to the cave of her own will and enters it: "I prefer your success to my life" (p. 207). By her act she becomes the *sabu* of Soso; he is only its instrument (p. 207). Conrad notes many variants of "the nature of the transactions involved" (p. 207 n.).
- **Other versions of the same bargain.** Belcher: Sumanguru "obtained the balafon by giving his sister to a djinn", hence his nephew Fakoli's supernatural birth (p. 101 n.; versions of Bamba Suso, Dembo Kanuté, Wa Kamissoko). A woman's society, the Nyakuruni, is described by a Beledugu midwife in 1979: "if you don't give your husband, you will give your first child" (p. 206). In Fa-Digi Sisòkò's version a king sacrifices an unborn child for Sunjata, which releases nyama that destroys the king (Johnson, p. 20).
- **The Buffalo Woman.** Do Kamissa, the buffalo of Do, gives the hunters the secret of her own death and makes them take her ugly younger "sister" Sogolon as reward (pp. 198–200). Johnson makes her the local ruler's "disgruntled aunt" and Sogolon her "grandniece" (p. 18); Conrad's Upper Guinea bards call the Condé women "sisters" (p. 198 n.). Conrad reads her as an elder woman who protects a junior's fertility (p. 200, after Brett-Smith). Her own speech blames herself for Sogolon's deformities: she put her "far-seeing mask" on the girl too young (p. 200).
- **Sogolon** is a sorceress, not Niane's weeping bride (pp. 200–201). In D. T. Condé's version she meets her husband with a sorcerer's plaiting needle shot from her eye, scalding breast-milk and an iron-tipped staff (p. 202). She lights the dance circle with two fingers stretched through the roof (pp. 203–204). She carries Sunjata for seven years in the co-wives' "women's war" (p. 205). Her "masculine side" returns in age, and she is buried "as if she were a man" (p. 201). She is a *musofadi*, a woman "strong as a man" who is usually past childbearing and in one region may join the komo (p. 201). **FtM-adjacent: reaction only.**
- **The foster-mother as source.** Kosiya Kante leaves her infant Fakoli with her barren co-wife **Ma Tenenba Condé**. She takes him round the power-places of the Manden (p. 205): to the Kulubali ancestor for washing in medicine; to Sibi, where the mansa carries him "through the great natural archway of Tabon mountain" to meet Jinna Maghan; and to **Krina**, where its ruler "rolls Fakoli in the dust of the sacred cave" (p. 205). Then to Nema and to Do and Kri. "It was a Conde woman who gave him his dalilu" (p. 205, D. T. Condé). Most bards do not tell this episode; it is one Upper Guinea version.
- **The sister as guide.** **Kolonkan** (Sogolon's Kolonkan) reveals Soso to Sunjata in a vision from her opened wrapper and calls him back from a sorcery contest (pp. 207–208). She speaks in their mother's name (pp. 208–209). Sisters' marriages make alliances: Kolonkan waits unmarried in exile until a politically useful match in the Manden (p. 215).
- **The sister's curse on a line.** Her brother **Manden Bori** shames her when her wrapper comes loose. She curses his descendants never to agree on one mansa (pp. 211–213). Modern elders in Hamana still cite the curse. The remedy D. T. Condé prescribes is for each village to give a cow, the sisters' share of the meat, and a wrapper to its sisters "so they can bless them" (p. 214). This is 1990s testimony.
- **Women's nyama and the wrapper.** A woman's exposed sex carries nyama stronger than any ritual object (p. 212, after Brett-Smith). The sorceress **Nyana Jukudulaye**, with 333 bells at her waist, stopped battles by baring her buttocks toward them (p. 196). Amulets fail before her; this is the "mooning" of the title (pp. 212–213).
- **Women in Sunjata's government.** A bard's lines: "It was Maghan Sunjata who first put a woman in government in the Manden" (p. 197). He counts eleven such women, drawn from the nine *suba* women and nine *nyagbaw* (p. 197). Conrad notes that nothing in the epic matches Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's Qāsā as "partner in rule" (pp. 220–221). The first woman at the head of troops he can find is Macarico, 16th c. (p. 221).
- **The *femme fatale* is killed by the man she served** (pp. 193–194): Sijanma executed by Faama Da, Kagbe given to Samori's army. These are Segu and Samori cycles, 18th–19th c. **Do not use** the killings.

### Bards, names, and the court

- **Period anchor (Wilks).** Al-ʿUmarī (late 1330s) puts two men before the mansa on the *banbi*: an executioner or sword-bearer and a *shāʿir* ("poet") who was also *safīr*, the "intermediary" between king and people (pp. 44–45). Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's Dūghā is the interpreter at the gate and, on Fridays and feast days, the xylophone-singer with wives and slave women who "sing with him and perform with bows" (pp. 45–46). After him come the jeli in bird-like masks with their admonition (pp. 46–47). Wilks treats them as **separate performances**, which confirms the correction already made.
- **Women in the founding of the bardic line.** Of the three "first-singers" the bards name, one is a woman: **Tumu Manian**, the Kuyate "ancestress" or "matriarch" (pp. 34, 38). In Fa-Digi Sisòkò and Kele Monson she sings "The Bow" at the start and end of Sunjata's exile (p. 38).
- **A king renames a bard.** Sumanguru cuts the tendons of Sunjata's bard Duga, shaves his head and gives him the new name Bala Fasseke Kuyate (p. 36, after Fa-Digi Sisòkò and Dembo Kanuté). The naming is a seizure, done with maiming. Sunjata later feeds the starving bard flesh from his own body, which "is that which is between the Kuyates and the royal family" (p. 49).
- **"Nyama is in speech"** (*Nyama be kuma la*, a bard's aphorism, Bird p. 281). Bird describes the female singer Nantenegwe Kamissoko filling with energy as she rose to perform (p. 281). He reports bards' belief that a jinn enters them while they perform (p. 281). The song "Nyama, nyama, nyama" may come from the Komo (Camara, p. 63); **do not use it**.
- **Bards as mediators.** The bard stands outside the authority structure and is "licensed to comment on it" (Johnson, p. 21). Casted groups are bards, smiths, leatherworkers and Muslim praise-singers (*fune*); they hold no office (pp. 10–11).
- **Smiths and the founder.** Sumanguru is the smith-king; the smiths' control of iron put them at the "economic and political apex" (Bird, p. 290). The bards sing that Sunjata organised society into freeborn, casted and slaves (p. 290).
- **Kirina** has cults of regalia associated with the epic's heroes but no school of history (Camara, p. 60). The teaching centre is Kela, near Kangaba (pp. 59–62).

### Hunters and a female deity

- The hunters' bards (*sere*) serve "the children of Saane and Kontoron" (Traoré, p. 178). In every telling Saane is female and holds the knowledge; Kontoron, her son, brother or husband, benefits from it "at the price of unconditional allegiance" (p. 180). The hunter's ascetic code (no bathing, Traoré p. 179) matches the sorcerer's (Conrad p. 196). Traoré reads Sunjata as a charter of **badenya**, harmony, against the hunters' violence (p. 180 n.). Hunters' associations are living institutions; use only this published outline.

### Gender, lightly

- Johnson calls the crippled young Sunjata "symbolically castrated", after Hays's *Limping Hero* (p. 19). That is a critic's metaphor, not a Mande category. Do not build on it.
- A bard's line on womanhood (p. 197): "It is a foolish woman who degrades womanhood. Even if she were a man." The context is women's equality with men in work and sorcery ("Whatever men can do, we can do", p. 197). It is not about crossing.
- Belcher records Gambian traditions that women ruled until men brought back the *mansaya* from the Manden, and then yielded power freely (p. 97). He calls them too mythic to treat as history (p. 97). LATER.

## 3. Corrections to the candidate and to existing notes

1. **The epic is LATER than "BACK" suggests for most of its content.** Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's bards sang panegyric of many kings, not a Sunjata epic (Austen p. 70). The epic's classic form is 18th–19th c. (Austen pp. 72–75; Camara pp. 63–66), and Conrad's women come from recordings made in 1991–94. Only some songs plausibly reach the 14th c. (Wilks pp. 44–49), and Sunjata's existence and the Soso war are attested by Ibn Khaldūn. Grade the women's episodes as **epic, recorded 19th–20th c.**; none is PERIOD.
2. **The self-offering instead of a kinsperson has an epic precedent, but it is a woman's death.** Kosiya Kante gives herself to the jinn so that no relative need be given (pp. 206–207). That is the same structure as Brett-Smith's sculptor who "has given himself" (p. 76), and it strengthens the gate's logic. But Kosiya's gift is her life. **No suicide content:** the faith may name her as the first who stood in the cave in a kinsman's place, but no event may offer a life, and the daughter's gift stays her manhood, never her death.
3. **"Claimed as her mother's child" fits the epic's foster-mother and sister better than *sinkò*.** The epic's model is Ma Tenenba Condé, the barren co-wife who becomes Fakoli's *sabu* and gives him his *dalilu* (p. 205), and the sister-guide Kolonkan (pp. 207–209). Badenya, affection between children of one mother, is the frame (Johnson p. 16). Recommend that step 2 be done by a woman who claims her as **sister** (the Kolonkan model) or **fosters her** (the Ma Tenenba model). Either way the claimer becomes her *sabu*.
4. **Names in `waf_ibn_battuta_mali.md` §5, checked here:**
   - *Sogolon*: verified, with epithets Sogolon Wulen Condé, Sogolon Kedjou, Sogolon Kuduma ("short Sogolon"), "Our heron-head" in bridal songs (Conrad pp. 195, 198–199, 201). Also Sugulun Konde (Traoré p. 178) and "Sugulun-of-the-Warts" (Johnson p. 18).
   - *Sassouma Bérété*: the Upper Guinea form is **Sansuma (Sansun Tuman) Berete** (Conrad pp. 194–195, 204); Johnson gives **Saman Berete** (p. 18).
   - *Nana Triban*: verified only as Sunjata's **half-sister** in Niane, taken prisoner by Sumanguru, who learns his secret, the white cock's spur (McGuire p. 261). Konaré Ba's 1983 literary version makes her the sister and has Sunjata give her part of his kingdom (Bulman p. 244). "Sassouma's daughter" remains *unverified here*.
   - *Sugulun Kulunkan*: Conrad's form is **Kolonkan / Sogolon's Kolonkan** (pp. 195, 207–215). Johnson names Sunjata's full siblings as "Sugulun Kutuma and Manden Bukari" (p. 16).
   - *Do Kamissa*: verified (pp. 198–200).
   - *Djamarou*: **not found** in this book; still unverified.
5. **New names (BACK unless marked)**, with grade and source, for the naming list:
   - Ma Tenenba Condé (p. 205);
   - Kosiya Kante (pp. 193, 205–207);
   - Keleya Konko(n), Fakoli's wife, who cooks as much as Sumaworo's hundreds of wives (pp. 195, 216);
   - Tumu Manian (Wilks pp. 34, 38);
   - Nyana Jukudulaye and the *sumusow* Kounkouba, Songoubetolo, Nyagarigassa, Siga and Solen Sendja "of Ton Mountain" (p. 196);
   - the co-wives Maramajan Tarawele, Jonmusoni Manyon and Tombonon Manyan Berete (pp. 202, 204);
   - Niagale Missane, keeper of Sunjata's armoury in Nioro variants (p. 221);
   - Mema Sira, Sunjata's lover in exile in one version (Belcher p. 101 n.);
   - Saane, a goddess, for invocation only (Traoré p. 180).
   - **Do not use** the 19th-c. historical women (Soghoni, Mama Siada, One-Breasted Denba, Kagbe, Sijanma; pp. 193–194, 209–222): they are real people or near-real, recent, and outside the period.
6. **The line: one claimed proof is disputed.** Conrad (p. 214) and Wilks (p. 47), following the Levtzion–Hopkins translation and Levtzion 1973, read Ibn Khaldūn as saying the throne passed from Mārī Jāṭa's line to his **brother** Abū Bakr, Mansā Mūsā's grandfather. Conrad identifies this Abū Bakr with Manden Bori (p. 214 n.). De Slane's French, read in `waf_ibn_khaldun_mali.md`, has "un fils de sa sœur" (p. 112). In Arabic the two words differ by a letter (*akhīhi*/*ukhtihi*, my inference, not checked against a manuscript). **Correct `waf_ibn_khaldun_mali.md` §3.2:** "Mūsā descends from the founder's sister" is a translation-dependent reading, and the standard modern English reading makes it a brother's line. The undisputed matrilineal facts remain the first Abū Bakr as a **daughter's** son (Ibn Khaldūn p. 111), Ghana and Walata.

## 4. Design implications

### Gate, step 1 (the smith's road): what the epic adds

- **The faith's precedent text** (faith voice): "Kosiya Kante went into the cave so that her brother need give no one; I gave the djinn my manhood so that no one of mine is given." This joins the epic (pp. 206–207) to the sculptor (Brett-Smith p. 76), and it is what the gate needs: substitution of oneself for a dependent. The narrator must mark both as later sources.
- **The jinn's name and place.** Jinna Maghan, "king of the jinn", is met at Tabon mountain's natural archway at Sibi (p. 205) and in a cave (p. 206). Holy-site text for `c_tabon` / `c_sibi_mali` can use the archway. Keep the djinn of the contract a *personal* female familiar, as in Brett-Smith; female genies guide men in the epic too (p. 209 n.).
- **Price menu.** Unchanged from `waf_brett_smith_bamana.md` §4. The epic confirms that the kin-price is the norm and that refusing it is honourable (Sumaworo first refuses, p. 206). Hostile rumours can say a rival smith "gave a person".

### Gate, step 2 (the mother's house), revised

- Two routes, both from the epic:
  - **Sister-sabu** (Kolonkan): a woman of the faith claims her as "my mother's child". She then guides her as Kolonkan guided Sunjata. Event text: "This is not what our mother told you to do" (after p. 208).
  - **Foster-sabu** (Ma Tenenba): an older, often childless woman takes her round three power-places (a washing, an archway, a cave's dust) and gives her *dalilu*. This fits a short travel chain of three sites, e.g. Kirina's cave (p. 205).
- Either woman can **curse** if shamed, through the wrapper, as Kolonkan cursed Manden Bori (pp. 211–213). That gives a strong, attested penalty for a brother or husband who humiliates the claimed daughter: a modifier on his dynasty, "they will never agree on one heir". The narrator should flag it as 1990s testimony about a 13th-c. setting.

### Gate, step 3 (the word), revised

- **Who sings.** PERIOD: the *shāʿir*/intermediary before the throne (al-ʿUmarī) and the bird-masked jeli at the feasts (Ibn Baṭṭūṭa), as Wilks reads them (pp. 44–47). Dūghā's wives sing with him (pp. 45–46), so a **jelimuso** may sing the name, with Tumu Manian as the faith's first-singer (BACK). Design: allow a court poet of either sex; the faith prefers a woman.
- **What is sung.** The 14th-c. form was panegyric that named past kings and their deeds (Austen p. 70). So the rite should **place her name in a praise-list** of the faith's women, not tell a story. Example: Sogolon, Kolonkan, Kosiya, then hers.
- **The inverse of Bala Fasseke.** A tyrant king renamed his bard by shaving and maiming (p. 36). The faith's rite reverses it: the bard names, the name is a gift, and nothing is cut. Use this as doctrine text, faith voice: "Sumanguru took a bard's name with a razor; we give a name with a song."
- **The bard's pay** stays gold dust (Ibn Baṭṭūṭa p. 412). The feeding bond, the Keita–Kuyate flesh gift (p. 49), can be a one-time "bond of the first meal" between the daughter and her jeli: a mutual opinion modifier, with a curse for betrayal on either side (p. 50).

### The line

- **Sisters' share and sisters' blessing** (p. 214, 1990s): at a succession, a ruler of the faith who gives each sister the back part of the sacrifice and a wrapper gains "blessed by her sisters" (stability). One who shames a sister risks Kolonkan's curse. Ties to Koné's aunt's share (`waf_kone_male_female_mande.md`, p. 6).
- Keep the preferred heir as a sister's child (Ghana, Walata, Ibn Khaldūn p. 111). Do not claim Mansā Mūsā as a sister's descendant in narrator text.

### Holy sites and seat (text)

- `c_sibi_mali` / `b_kirina`: in the epic, the cave at Krina whose dust holds "the essence of the original creation" (p. 205), and the field of Sumanguru's fall. Kirina keeps regalia cults (Camara p. 60).
- `c_tabon`: the natural archway of Tabon mountain at Sibi, where children are introduced to the king of the jinn (p. 205).
- `c_kangaba` (if used): the Kamabolon and its seven-yearly re-roofing, but this is **18th c. or later** (Austen pp. 73–75). Use only as a late-game flavour or not at all.

### Reaction lines

- Hostile Mande elder (after p. 203, a hunter's complaint about Sogolon): "A woman with more power than men; the child will not flourish." Use it for a court that fears the faith's women.
- Faith (after p. 191): "Men are the instruments; we are the source."
- Faith, on the mansa's line (after pp. 213–214): "The house that shamed its sister has never agreed on one king."

### Do not use

- Any event in which a woman or daughter gives her **life** (Kosiya Kante, Do Kamissa's surrender, the Wagadu virgin, p. 209). Use them only as remembered myth.
- The *femme fatale*'s execution or rape (pp. 193–194).
- The Komo, the Nyakuruni society and its price (p. 206), and the song "Nyama, nyama, nyama".
- The *musofadi* or Sogolon's man's funeral as models for the daughters: they are masculine women, reaction only.
- Johnson's "symbolically castrated" Sunjata.
- The informants' names attached to their testimony in game text.
