An earlier version appeared in *Mande Studies* 4 (2002): 21–29 (Koné's n. 1). Open access, https://sites.cortland.edu/wagadu/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2014/02/kone1.pdf.

## 1. The argument in brief

Bamana public ideology is patrilineal and male-dominant, but gender is "not an absolute but a relative one" (p. 1, again p. 8). Language and kin roles let men and women "assume or have imposed upon them each other's roles" in context (p. 1). The patrilineal "public transcript" (*kulusijala*, "the two ends of the belt", i.e. descent through the penis) is undercut by a "hidden transcript" in which everyone values *badenya* ("motherchildness") and *sinjiya* ("breastmilkhood") more (pp. 2–3, after Scott 1990). Men become "female" as mother's brothers, as joking "wives" of their older sisters' husbands, and as husbands of pregnant women; women become "male" as paternal aunts, as husbands' younger sisters, as girls born during a men's festival, and after menopause (pp. 4–8).

Koné's examples are present-day usage. He gives no dates and no history. Everything here is LATER evidence in the reading list's sense.

## 2. What matters for the design (page refs)

### Manhood, womanhood and their insults (pp. 1–2)

- *Cèya*, "manhood", is defined by the male organ and by behaviours instilled from childhood and sealed at circumcision; joining the men is *ka taa cèw fè*, and it ends in taking up trousers, *ka kulusi ta* (p. 1).
- *Musoya*, "womanhood", runs parallel; joining the women is *ka taa musow fè*, through excision (p. 1).
- Women's categories: *muso kisè* ("kernel woman", a dynamic, brave woman), *muso tafetigi* ("woman with a loincloth"), and *muso kulusitigi*, "woman with pants", "the quintessential manly woman" who heads a family, farms, or practises men's sorcery (p. 1). Proverb: a woman may bear a *soma* (sorcerer) but does not become one (p. 1).
- Men's negative categories: *cè fugari* (worthless man) and *cèmusonin*, "little manwoman", a man whose behaviour resembles a woman's (p. 2). There is no "man with a loincloth" category; the worst insult is a woman's "Exchange your pants against my loin cloth" (p. 2).
- Older word for the male homosexual: *cètèmusotè*, "not male not female", which "means both homosexual male or female and the hermaphrodite"; now replaced by *muso filanan*, "the second female", or the Wolof loan *gorjigèn* ("male female"), which Koné reads as pushing homosexuality onto the Wolof as foreign (p. 2).

### Badenya and the hidden transcript (pp. 2–3)

- The saying "Kulusijala ka surun sinji ye", "the two ends of the belt get closer than the breast milk" — in effect, "Sperm is thicker than breast milk" (p. 2).
- *Sinjiya*, breastmilkhood, is the relationship of "children who have replaced each other on the same mother's breasts and their descendants" (p. 2).
- Privately "everyone thinks more highly of badenya/sinjiya than of fadenya/kulusijala" (p. 2).
- **Sinkò**, "after-breast", is "the physical resemblance people may have when they have been breastfed by the same mother" (p. 2). It names a likeness, not a bond one enters.
- *Kònòwolo*, "the womb", the tie through the same womb, is "an underused category" because of the patrilineal weight (p. 3).
- **Naming rights:** the father's side names the first two children; the mother's family names every third child (third, sixth, ninth) (p. 3).
- As the father ages and depends on his grown children, his wives' power rises (p. 3).
- **Curses:** a father curses through his genes, or failing that through the food that made the mother's milk; a mother curses "through her womb and/or through her breastmilk" (p. 3; n. 3, p. 9: "If I have borne you in my womb for nine months... If I have fed you with my breast milk...").
- Crying: *cèninkule* ("little man's crying") is male only; *musoninkule* ("little woman's crying") is for women and for weak men (pp. 3–4).
- *Ka furu*, "to marry": only a man marries; a woman "gets married" (p. 4). "Did you marry me?" puts a wife in her place (p. 4).

### When a man becomes a woman (pp. 4–6)

- **The mother's brother.** *Bènkè*, from *ba* "mother" + *kè* "male": "the male mother/maternal male", an extension of the mother, less authoritative than anyone on the father's side (pp. 1, 4). The nephew may ask him for anything, even his daughter; "when your uncle refuses to give you his daughter, he is no longer your uncle but your mother's brother" (p. 4).
- **The joking "wife".** An older sister's husband may introduce his wife's younger brother as "my wife" and joke with him as husbands joke with wives; the younger brother may play the jealous wife ("he's my husband") (p. 5).
- **Women do not kill.** Girls are told not to kill even insects "because they are mothers to be"; adult women never kill the chickens for meals (p. 5).
- **The husband of a pregnant woman** must refrain from killing: "the husband of a pregnant wife is by extension pregnant himself" (p. 5).
- **Impotence is not womanhood.** An impotent man "has left manhood", *ka bò cèya la*, but "This definition does not mean that a man who becomes impotent becomes a woman. He is an incomplete man" (p. 5). His "back is dead" (p. 5).
- Fat men are likened to women (p. 5).
- **Women's war.** Childbirth is "women's war" (*muso kèlè*), especially when the mother dies; men who die in war and women who die in childbirth are buried alike, above the earth under leaves and stones (pp. 5–6).

### When a woman becomes a man (pp. 6–8)

- Proverb: "It is the worthless woman that makes womanhood something to regret. It is the woman that gives birth to the powerful and the wealthy" (p. 6).
- **The paternal aunt**, *tènèmuso* ("the sacred or taboo female"), counts as "father" (*fa*) to her brothers' children; the husband's younger sister counts as "husband" to her brothers' wives (p. 6). Among the Maninka the aunt gets the lower back of slaughtered animals as "the backbone of the family" (p. 6).
- **Sisters' curses** and aunts' curses are the most feared, lasting generations (p. 6); because a sister's bridewealth paid for her brothers' wives (n. 7, p. 9).
- **Girls born during a Komo or Do celebration** are carried into the men's space and become "full-fledged" members (p. 6). They bear names such as Kòmòtènè, Kònse, Gwanse, Gwankura (p. 6). FtM-adjacent: reaction only.
- Bamana children are "androgynous" until circumcision or excision (p. 7). Koné's word; see §3.
- **Shared names.** Ungendered ritual names: Bugu (born during the Do), Filifèn (after stillbirths), Sajo or Koninba (after twins). Namesakes call each other *n'tògòma*, "my namesake", and the tie passes to descendants (p. 7).
- A wife avoids her husband's name until the first child, then calls him "X's father" (p. 7).
- **After menopause** women take on men's affairs and may enter places once barred; their speech takes in male insults; "most females are not perceived as females anymore but just as mothers and grandmothers" (pp. 7–8). Men's behaviour weakens but does not become female (p. 7). Old wives often hold more power than their husbands, "exercised in the background" (p. 8).
- Note 10 (p. 10): among the *woloso* (descendants of slaves), men and women trade cross-gender insults to embarrass nobles.

## 3. Corrections to the candidate

1. **Pages.** *Cèmusonin*, *cètèmusotè* and "exchange your pants" are p. 2, not pp. 1–2 (only *muso kulusitigi* is p. 1). Badenya/sinjiya and the hidden transcript are pp. 2–3. The joking "wife" is p. 5 (introduced p. 1). "Women's war" and the burial are pp. 5–6.
2. ***Sinkò* is a likeness, not a rite.** The candidate has the claimed daughter and her mother "become 'of one afterbreast' (*sinkò*)". In Koné, *sinkò* is the resemblance between people nursed by the same mother (p. 2). A claimed child cannot literally have it. In the faith's voice it can be a hope ("may she come to have the afterbreast of my daughters"), not a status conferred.
3. **"Badenya" is "motherchildness" in Koné**, one word (p. 3), and it is set beside *sinjiya*. The name of the faith is sound. Bird and Kendall 1980 remain the classic source for the badenya/fadenya model; Koné takes it as known ("As students of Mande, we know", p. 2).
4. **Koné explicitly denies that impotence makes a woman** (p. 5). This contradicts Brett-Smith's informant (`waf_brett_smith_bamana.md`, pp. 75–76). The design should say both views exist: the sculptor's "he has made himself a woman" and the common view "he is an incomplete man". The faith takes the first; hostile Mande voices take the second.
5. **The "androgynous" child (p. 7)** is Koné's English gloss of *bilakoroya*, the uncircumcised state. Do not build doctrine on it: it is exactly the "third thing" the faiths refuse, and it applies to every child, not to the daughters.
6. **"Not male not female"** remains forbidden as the daughter's identity, as the candidate already says. Add *muso filanan*, "the second female" (p. 2), to the list of words a hostile modern voice uses; it is post-period and should not appear in game text at all.

## 4. Design implications

### Gate, step 2 (the mother's house), revised

- The claiming woman should be able to curse or bless "through her womb and her breastmilk" (p. 3). The rite's words, in the faith's voice: "If I have fed you with my milk..." rephrased to "I feed you as my mother fed us." A blessing modifier on the daughter; the same mother's curse, if the bond breaks, is the faith's most feared penalty.
- **Naming right:** in Koné the mother's family names every third child (p. 3). The faith can claim that right for every daughter: the mother's house names her, not the father's. A clean, attested hook for "the mother names the daughter".
- **The *bènkè*, "male mother"** (p. 4): a daughter's maternal uncle is already, in Bamana words, a mother. A positive event: her *bènkè* is the first man to accept her, because he "cannot refuse" his sister's child (p. 4).
- **The paternal aunt as "female father"** (p. 6): the natural hostile or gatekeeping figure on the father's side; her curse lasts generations. An event where the aunt's acceptance is needed, or where her curse must be lifted.

### Line

- Badenya over fadenya as the faith's doctrine name and its claim: "the hidden transcript spoken aloud." Doctrine text (faith voice): "Everyone says the belt is closer than the milk. No one believes it."
- The joking "wife" (p. 5) gives an in-culture, non-sacred precedent for a man being called "wife" in the family. Use as a light event between a daughter and her older sister's husband, joking turned earnest.
- Women's war (pp. 5–6) and the shared burial: the faith can honour a daughter who dies in battle *or* a mother who dies in childbirth with the same funeral. Careful: no suicide content, no glorifying a death.

### Reaction

- Hostile Bamana-voice insults for a daughter: *cèmusonin*, "little manwoman" (p. 2); "Exchange your pants against my loin cloth" (p. 2, inverted by the faith as her choice). Use sparingly and only in hostile mouths.
- Common-sense hostile line: "He has left manhood; that does not make him a woman" (after p. 5).
- Post-menopausal women's licence (pp. 7–8): older women of hostile courts may be the first to accept her, as "just mothers and grandmothers" who have gone where they were once barred.

### Names

- Ritual names for daughters (from p. 7, ungendered): Bugu, Filifèn, Sajo, Koninba. Komo-birth names (p. 6) assert membership in a living society: use only for NPC cis women, never as a faith name.
- The namesake bond *n'tògòma* (p. 7) is a neat social tie: a daughter who takes a name from the bards becomes the "namesake" of every woman who bears it.
