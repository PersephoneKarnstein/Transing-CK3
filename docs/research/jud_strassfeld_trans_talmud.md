## 1. The argument in brief (what is new against the dissertation)

- **Two interventions** (Intro, opening): centring eunuchs and androgynes changes how rabbinic gender looks, and the book contributes to trans and intersex history. The title is not a claim that the Talmud is "essentially subversive or trans" (Intro).
- **"Transing"** as method (Intro, "Transing the Talmud"), after Currah, Moore and Stryker: how gender is assembled from other bodily traits, how that assembly disciplines, and where it leaves "paths of fugitivity". Strassfeld adds a "bad/literal" reading strategy: Jews were cast as bad literal readers of the body, as trans people (and trans women above all) are cast as bad literal readers of sex. He names transmisogyny directly: the logic that replaces "transsexual" with "queerer" identities "simultaneously renders trans women themselves anachronistic" (Intro, "Transing the Talmud").
- **One category for intersex and trans bodies.** The rabbis' *saris* covers both a person born with variant anatomy and a person whose genitals change, so modern distinctions "do not mesh with the local taxonomies of the rabbis" (Intro, "Transing the Talmud").
- **The aylonit gets her chapter section.** The dissertation postponed her to the book (diss. p. 63 n. 108). There is no chapter with the provisional title "The Female as Blemish on the Male". Her material is in ch. 4, "The Aylonit: Procreative Femininity and Levirate Marriage", and ch. 5, "Diagnosing the Eunuch". See §2.2.
- **The conclusion's new thesis.** The rabbis' interest in the mutability of sex "establishes the rabbis' expertise in interpreting the body" (Conclusion, opening). The same mutability makes bodies hard to regulate: there are "visible cracks" in their ability to assign sex (Conclusion). He still finds no "gender-subversive core" (Conclusion).
- **Still against redemption.** Bodies that refuse the rabbis' timelines "do not point to transcestors in any recoverable sense" (Conclusion). He calls subversion something that "may be bought at too high a price" (Conclusion).

## 2. What matters for the design

### 2.1 R. Yose's "created being of its own": the revised reading

The dissertation read R. Yose's coda only as **exclusion** (diss. p. 162). The book keeps the exclusion and adds a theological reading, so the line now has two sides.

- **Translation.** The book renders *beriyah le-atzmo* as **"a unique creation"** (ch. 2, "'A Unique Creation'"). Note 81 explains rendering the Hebrew "him" as "them".
- **Attribution** (ch. 2 n. 80). Rabbi Yose in the Vienna print, Berlin fol. 1220 and Zuckermandel's Erfurt-based edition. Rabbi Meir in the Parma Mishnah (De Rossi 138), and in the scholars who place the list in the Mishnah. Two Geniza fragments of the *koy* list lack the coda. Strassfeld uses R. Yose but stays "agnostic".
- **Exclusion, kept.** For R. Yose, the sages' "failure" to assign one legal gender means "the androgyne must be excluded" (ch. 2).
- **New: a claim about creation.** The noun *beriyah* comes from the verb in Genesis 1:1. So "although Rabbi Yose is ejecting the androgyne from halakhah, he is also explicitly associating the androgyne with the order of creation", and the line is "a powerful theological assertion" (ch. 2, "The Genesis of Transphobia"). Note 102: the phrase is rare in tannaitic texts (also t. Kil'ayim 1:9, of animals), and the root usually means God's creations. Note 103 adds t. Berakhot 6:3, the blessing over seeing an unusual creature, and says these sources make no distinction between natural and theological.
- **New: the list and the coda "collude".** Most scholars, Fonrobert included (n. 85), set the list (inclusion) against the coda (exclusion). Strassfeld reads both as making gender "central to halakhah". The list folds the androgyne into gendered law; R. Yose thinks gender too central for the androgyne to fit (ch. 2).
- **The cost of reading subversion here.** Strassfeld will not police activists' use of the line, but a being called "unique but unassimilable" is in "a precarious social position indeed", and he does not wish "to buy queer subversion using androgynes as currency" (ch. 2).
- **Contrast with modern anti-trans law.** Neither the tannaitic androgyne nor the hybrid animal "is told, 'Don't exist'" (ch. 2). This is the chapter's point against "Judeo-Christian" binary claims (Conclusion, "Imagining a 'Judeo-Christian' Binary Gender").
- **Kessler** (ch. 2 n. 83): R. Yose's sentence, not the list, is what lasted in later rabbinic literature.

**Effect on the built content.** `faith_design.md` §13 already forbids "a created being of its own" as the sect's self-description, and the faith refuses it as a compromise. The book **supports that refusal**. A being that is "unique but unassimilable" is set outside the law. And the Nehpakhot are women, not a third thing. The new reading allows one narrower use: the sect may take the *word* (*beriyah*, a thing God made) while refusing the *exile*. See §4.

### 2.2 The aylonit (new material)

- **The parallel breaks.** m. Yevamot 8:5 frames the born saris and the *aylonit* in parallel ("so too the aylonit"). But when the born saris wrongly has levirate sex, the widow is disqualified from marrying a priest. When the *aylonit* has it, she is disqualified herself: "the woman's status [is] at stake in both cases" (ch. 4, "The Aylonit").
- **"She is the zonah."** R. Yehudah (m. Yevamot 6:5): a priest may not marry an *aylonit* even if he has children, because she is the *zonah* of Leviticus. The anonymous first opinion allows it to a priest who already has wife and children. The sages hold that the *zonah* is only the convert, the freedwoman and the woman who had illicit sex (ch. 4 n. 100). Strassfeld: in R. Yehudah's view "the aylonit herself is somehow inherently a harlot", and the *aylonit* is "made to embody the illicit nature of women's sexuality" (ch. 4). Note 101 links this to Roman ideas of the hypersexual "mannish woman".
- **Not the same as a barren woman.** The rabbis already had the barren woman (*akarah*) and invented the *aylonit* anyway. Her infertility "is written across her body", while the barren woman's is invisible (ch. 5, "Diagnosing the Eunuch"). Greco-Roman background: the infertile "mannish" dancer, the *tribas* (ch. 5).
- **Sarah as aylonit** (ch. 5 n. 63): a tradition at b. Yevamot 64b says the matriarch Sarah was an *aylonit*, with no "place" for a child. The passage distinguishes the *aylonit* from the barren woman. *Not in the dissertation note.*
- **The ram etymology** (ch. 5 n. 63): b. Ketubbot 11a derives *aylonit* from a ram that does not bear, which "seems also to associate her infertility with a gendered deviance". Fonrobert reports that the Arukh rejects this for "tree-like" (*ilan*); see the Fonrobert note.
- **Her status can change** (ch. 4 n. 95): the rabbis discuss whether *aylonit* status must be disclosed before marriage (m. Ketubot 11:6) and "the possibility of her 'condition' changing" (m. Gittin 4:8). *Unverified: I have not checked m. Gittin 4:8.*
- **The signs** (ch. 5): the translation matches the dissertation (diss. p. 118). On whether the voice or the person is unrecognisable, n. 57 now credits the point to Lev, "Genital Trouble", pp. 321–23: the Erfurt manuscript's grammar refers "only to the aylonit herself, not her voice".
- **Conclusion of ch. 4.** "Being a eunuch is defined by masculine (un)fitness and feminine sexual deviance". In the contemporary parallel, the scrutiny of trans women is "an outsized version" of the policing of women's bodies in general (ch. 4, "Conclusion: Transing the Eunuch and Androgyne").

### 2.3 Eunuchs, "kosher" masculinity and the knife (ch. 4)

- **Kosher genitals.** m. Yevamot 8:2: a man with even a strand of the crown left is "kosher". Strassfeld reads it as a ruling on "when is sex changed?" The uncircumcised priest is like an impure one, but the over-cut priest is "not kosher" (ch. 4, "Kosher Masculinity"). This is a range of acceptable cuts: necessary cuts admit, damaging cuts disable.
- **The damaged priest's household** (m. Yevamot 8:1). His slaves eat the heave offering, but his wife does not if they have had sex since the injury. Strassfeld: "bodily damage seems to function as a sexually transmitted infection" (ch. 4). The rabbis "legislate a sexual disability" (ch. 4).
- **Marriage as the gate to the community.** Deut. 23:2 is read as a ban on marrying a born, free Israelite woman. "Kosher masculinity is predicated, in this case, on access to Jewish women's bodies" (ch. 4, "The Ammonite and Moabite").
- **Castration is not cast as foreign.** Greco-Roman sources tie the eunuch to the effeminate East. The Mishnah "does not directly situate castration as a 'foreign' practice" (ch. 4). This matches the dissertation (diss. pp. 83, 86).
- **The third eunuch still has no slot.** "The rabbis do not have a category corresponding to 'eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven'" (ch. 4, "Defining Eunuchs").
- **"Hands of heaven".** The book's main text no longer discusses the b. Yevamot 80b passage that the dissertation built on (diss. p. 136). It survives only in ch. 4 n. 83: the *seris adam* is made "by human hands", the born one is "both natural and created by God". The y. Yevamot gloss of *ḥamah* as "sun" is also there.
- **The acquired saris becomes a genitally damaged man.** In the Bavli, R. Ami applies Deut. 23:2 to the acquired saris, which collapses "eunuchs and 'genital damage', two frameworks that are entirely separate biblically" (ch. 5, "'Old Men Are Like Eunuchs'"). A baraita adds the old man to the same rule, so read literally "aging becomes a type of genital damage" (ch. 5).

### 2.4 Medieval reception

The book has almost none. There is nothing on Kalonymus, Dinah (b. Berakhot 60a) or Purim. Medieval commentators appear only in notes:
- Rashi on the Biri case (as in the dissertation);
- Maimonides and Tosafot on the *koy* (ch. 2 n. 50);
- Jacob Weil (15th c.) on identifying the *koy* (ch. 2 n. 45);
- the Ritva emending a text (ch. 3 n. 44);
- Maimonides and Bertinoro reading m. Yevamot 8:5's bare "saris" as the born saris (ch. 4 n. 96);
- Maimonides' invented etymology of *tumtum* from *tum'ah*, as quoted by the artist Gil Yefman (Conclusion).

The Kalonymus–Dinah link remains the mod's own route (see `jud_kalonymus_even_bohan.md`). This book neither supports nor contradicts it.

**One modern item with a medieval echo** (Conclusion, "Imagining a 'Judeo-Christian' Binary Gender", n. 22). The best-known Orthodox halakhic book on transsexuality is Idan Ben-Efrayim's *Dor Tahapuchot*. The title quotes Deut. 32:20, *dor tahapukhot*, "a perverse generation". That is the root h-p-kh of *Ha-Nehpakhot* and *hipukh*. Strassfeld notes that the book's net effect often softens the bans: the castration ban falls on the surgeon, not the patient. **Design consequence:** a hostile period rabbi has a ready-made biblical pun against the sect ("a generation of *tahapukhot*"). The modern book must never be named in game.

### 2.5 Retroactive status and bodily time (ch. 5)

These are the same texts as the dissertation (Rav and Shmuel; Rava; the infant signs; the cataract analogy). There is new framing but no new rulings:
- Freeman's "chrononormativity" and Kaye's "natural time";
- the structure of the sugya, which runs from old age back to birth and then to puberty;
- the line "eunuch adults are created by looking backward" and its question: "what must it feel like to live in that extended liminal moment?" (ch. 5, "Retroactive Childhood").

Strassfeld also notes that the stable picture of the saris and *aylonit* as "fully men and women" in tannaitic law has "only one exception": the sign about a voice that cannot be recognised as man's or woman's (ch. 5, before the baraita, and n. 48 citing Fonrobert 2006, p. 92).

### 2.6 Modern activist reception (Conclusion, "Desiring Transcestors")

- **Micah Bazant**, *Timtum* zine (1999): reclaims the Yiddish *timtum* ("simple, stupid") as "genderqueer".
- **Gil Yefman**, *Tumtum* (2012), a knitted sculpture.
- **Elliot Kukla**, "A Created Being of Its Own" (2006 HUC thesis). Kukla was the first openly trans person ordained by the Reform movement. The quoted thesis passage speaks of "infinite locations for belonging".
- **Ritual.** S. J. Crasnow on trans mikvah rituals by Rabbi Emily Aviva Kapor-Mater, who "describes trans people as partners in (re)creation with God".
- **Strassfeld's close:** "I want these activists to have their yerushah—their inheritance."

These are modern public figures and published works. None of them may appear in game, and the narrator must not imply medieval continuity with them.

## 4. Design implications

What the book **confirms** in the built content:
- **No knife; the knife makes a man** (`tenet_tm_turning_desc`, hard limits). There is still no rabbinic slot for a eunuch "for heaven" (ch. 4). The acquired saris is folded into the genitally damaged man barred by Deut. 23:2 (ch. 5). A knife can only make an acquired saris.
- **The worthies and Jewish lords as judges** (`tm_hipukh.0003`, `tm_hipukh.0040`). The conclusion's thesis is that rabbinic talk of changeable sex is what makes the rabbis the experts on sex. The built design puts the dispute where the book says the power lies: who may rule on her.
- **"What came forth male is male"** (`tm_jewish_daughter_opinion`, `tm_hipukh.0040`). A modern dayan's "Jewish law does not recognise any change in sex" (Conclusion) is the same position. Keep the line in rabbis' mouths only, as built.
- **Dry, legal tone for Jewish reaction.** The rabbinic saris is "positively measured in comparison with such authors as Philo and Juvenal" (ch. 4, "Defining Eunuchs").

What the book **adds** (suggestions, none built):

1. **Sarah, the first aylonit (b. Yevamot 64b; ch. 5 n. 63).** A matriarch the sages themselves call an *aylonit* gives the sect a female, scriptural ancestor in place of R. Yose's androgyne.
   - Use it in the faith's voice only. The *aylonit* is a congenitally infertile woman, so claiming her is still appropriation (as `jud_strassfeld_classically_queer.md` §2.4 warns).
   - Possible line for an elder in `tm_hipukh.0020` (the three lamps), *suggestion*: *"The sages say Sarah our mother was an aylonit, with no place for a child. Her lamp is the first of ours."*
   - The midrash on Sarah's lamp burning from Sabbath to Sabbath is Genesis Rabbah 60:16 (*my addition, not from Strassfeld; check before use*).
2. **The word, not the exile.** An elder may answer R. Yose in the dispute event (`tm_hipukh.0003.a`, "text against text"), *suggestion*: *"Rabbi Yose called the androgyne a creature, a thing God made. We take the word. We are not a third thing; we are daughters."* This uses the book's creation reading (ch. 2 n. 102) and keeps the built refusal.
3. **A hostile pun: *dor tahapukhot*** (Deut. 32:20; §2.4). Give it to a worthy in `tm_hipukh.0003.desc` or the *niddui* branch, *suggestion*: *"The Torah has a name for you already: a generation of turnings, children in whom is no faith."* This is period-plausible because it is plain scripture. Do not name the modern book.
4. **The aylonit as zonah: a kohen event.** R. Yehudah's ruling (m. Yevamot 6:5) against the sages' reading is a ready court split. It is the "A Kohen and a Turned Daughter" event already proposed in the dissertation note (§4.2). The book adds the sages' counter-text (ch. 4 n. 100), so the lenient side now has its own quotation. *Design suggestion; medieval practice unverified.*
5. **Avoid the ram.** b. Ketubbot 11a's ram etymology for *aylonit* is animal imagery. The hard limits forbid it. If an etymology is wanted, use the Arukh's "tree-like" (see the Fonrobert note), which also answers Isaiah 56:3, "let not the eunuch say, I am a withered tree".
6. **Narrator text for an encyclopedia entry.** The book's balanced formula is usable. The rabbis brought eunuchs and androgynes into the law at length; that made the sages the judges of sex; and it did not make the law gender-free. Do not write "the Talmud is trans" (Intro).
