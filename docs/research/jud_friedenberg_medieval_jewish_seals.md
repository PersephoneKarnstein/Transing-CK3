# Friedenberg, *Medieval Jewish Seals from Europe* (1987): design reading for Ha-Nehpakhot's arms

**Citation.** Daniel M. Friedenberg, *Medieval Jewish Seals from Europe*. Detroit: Wayne State University Press, 1987; open-access edition on Project MUSE, 2018 (doi:10.1353/book.61491), CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 except where marked.

**What was read.** In full: "Jewish Symbols and Legends on Medieval Seals" and "The Heraldic Shield on Jewish Seals" (pp. 25–31 and following, by the index); the entries for Dulcie of the Portal (No. 23, pp. 79–80) and the Spanish women's rings (Nos. 62–65, pp. 137–39). Skimmed: the contents, the Narbonne and Provençal entries (Nos. 19–27, 40–41). Not read: "The Jew as Moneylender" (left aside: the project has no moneylending content) and the German, Hungarian and Polish catalogues. Pages come from the book's index; the text conversion has no page markers.

Also checked: the *Jewish Encyclopedia* (1901–06) article "Seal", which agrees on the devices and dates the priestly hands and the Star of David on community seals to the 16th and 17th centuries.

**Purpose.** To choose a banner and a faith icon for Ha-Nehpakhot from what medieval Jews of the Midi actually put on their seals, not from later or modern Jewish symbols.

## 1. What appears on medieval Jewish seals

- **Shared devices.** Lion, ox or bull, and eagle, used by Jews and Christians alike. In medieval Jewry the bull's head stood for Joseph, the lion for Judah (p. 25 ff.).
- **Crescent and star.** On fourteen known seals, 12th to 15th century, in a band from Languedoc to Austria. "The device of the crescent moon and star was identified by Jews and Christians as standing for Jews throughout the late medieval period in Europe."
- **Hebrew legends** are the real mark: "when Hebrew script is used in this period, the seal without question is Jewish." In southern France one face of a matrix is often Hebrew and the other vernacular. Legends name the father ("son of", or *bat*, "daughter of").
- **Influence of the Christian neighbour.** Lilies on seals from southern France and Spain follow the Marian cult (p. 28). The Midi favoured shield-shaped seals in the 13th and 14th centuries.
- **Avoided.** Pigs and boars; Christian symbols on any seal with Hebrew; the whole human figure.

## 2. What does not appear

- **The menorah.** "There are certain symbols omitted. A prime example is the temple menorah" (p. 28). Except for one questionable French signet ring (No. 44), no temple implement, shofar, etrog or lulav appears on a medieval Jewish seal.
- **The priestly hands and the Levite's jug**: common only after the 16th century.
- **The hexagram as the sign of the Jews**: the star had no fixed number of points; a Graz seal of 1482 still has five.

## 3. Women's seals

- **Dulcie of the Portal** (No. 23, Narbonne, matrix; pp. 79–80). "The letters are engraved around a portal to a castle, with what seems a portcullis above." Within the doorway is the front of a bull. The portal puns on her name (*armes parlantes*). Friedenberg ties the bull to her father, Joseph, and identifies her, tentatively, with Dulcia, daughter of Jucef de Elna of Perpignan. The legend is Latin and opens with a cross, which is unusual for the Midi.
- **Astruga** (No. 65, gold ring from the Montjuïc cemetery, Barcelona; pp. 138–39). Its Hebrew legend abbreviates Judges 5:24, the praise of Jael: "blessed shall she be above women in the tent", with Astruga's name in place of Jael's (Cecil Roth's reading).
- **Dulce** and **Jochebed** (Nos. 62–64, Teruel rings): a woman's name in Hebrew between floral bands.

## 4. For the mod

- **Never the Jew's hat.** Friedenberg lists it among the devices of Jewish seals, but it was a badge imposed on Jews ("This representation would seem abhorrent to Jews"). The index files it under "Badge of shame". It must not be used, nor the *rouelle*.
- **The menorah is not medieval seal usage.** It is a fair faith icon (the menorah stood for Judaism on objects and in manuscripts), but not a period banner.
- **The crescent and star is period-true but misleading in game**: players read it as Islamic. Not recommended.
- **Recommended banner** for Bonadonna's camp, *the Lamp*: Dulcie's model, a portal with a portcullis (vanilla `ce_portcullis` or `ce_castle`) on the faith's lavender. It is a Midi Jewish woman's own seal device, and the doorway also suits a faith whose daughters must be let in by their street. The bull is left out: it names Dulcie's father, not the faith.
- **Recommended faith icon** (new art): a round seal with a Hebrew legend from Judges 5:24, as on Astruga's ring. It is a woman's seal, it praises a woman by name in Scripture's words, and a Hebrew legend is the one feature that marks a medieval seal as Jewish. A Hebrew-literate check of the lettering is needed before it ships.

## 5. Legends, read from the page images (2026-10-23)

The Hebrew is set as images, so the text conversion lost it; these were read from the PDF pages (PDF pp. 45–47, 122, 134, 149, 151, 256–260).
- **Order:** a star, the name, *bar* or *bat*, the father's name, a pious contraction for the father (pp. 45–47). Crosses "are never present, however, when the seal has a Hebrew inscription" (p. 45).
- **Contractions.** Father alive: יצ״ו (ישמרהו צורו וגואלו), the most common; י״א (ימים ארוכים); ש״י (שיחיה); ש״י (שמרו יוצרו). Father dead: ז״ל (זכרונו לברכה); נ״ע (נוחו עדן); זקל״י (זכר קדוש לברכה).
- **Midi examples.** No. 19: קלונימוס בר טודרוס, reverse "S': MOVMET IVDEV D'NERBO". No. 21: דוד בר שמואל צב״י, reverse "S' MIELET DEL PORTAL". No. 26: טובי בר יצחק ז״ל, reverse "S. CRESQ S PF ANC". No. 27: קלונימוס בר שלמה יצ״ו on the left of a shield, "CRESCAS DE MASELA" on the right, "each legend preceded by a star".
- **Women.** No. 62: בשם יוכבד, "In the Name of Jochebed". No. 64: דולסה, the name alone. No. 65: מ׳ ב׳ / אשתרוגה / ת׳ = מנשים באהל אשתרוגה תבורך, "Above Women in the Tent Astruga Be Blessed".
