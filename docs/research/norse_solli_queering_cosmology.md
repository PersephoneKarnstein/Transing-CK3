# Solli, "Queering the Cosmology of the Vikings" (2008): design reading for the Bædling faith

**Citation.** Brit Solli, "Queering the Cosmology of the Vikings: A Queer Analysis of the Cult of Odin and 'Holy White Stones'," *Journal of Homosexuality* 54, no. 1–2 (2008): 192–208. Haworth Press / Routledge. DOI [10.1080/00918360801952085](https://doi.org/10.1080/00918360801952085).

**Verified** from the Taylor & Francis cover sheet ("Journal of Homosexuality, 54:1-2, 192-208"; published online 11 October 2008), the article's first page ("Journal of Homosexuality, Vol. 54(1/2) 2008", © The Haworth Press) and Crossref. It appears in the issue's "Part IV: Queering the Gods", with the running head "Queering Norway". Solli was then Professor of Medieval Archaeology at the University of Oslo.

**What was read.** The whole article, references included. Extracted text is in the scratchpad only.

**Overlap with existing notes.** Price (`norse_price_viking_way.md`, p. 176–177) and Blain (`norse_blain_nine_worlds.md`, row 4 and the Hyndluljóð entry) already assess Solli's reading of *seiðberendr* in her 2002 book. This note adds only what the 2008 article argues beyond that: the hanging, the Uppsala sacrifices, *ergi* for women, Norse purity, and the white stones.

## 1. The argument

**Framing.** Nineteenth-century nation-building made the Vikings a story of "sameness, not difference" (p. 193), and the Nazis and today's neo-Nazis exploit it as "heroism, white power, blood and honor" (p. 194). Researchers have "under-communicated" the gender-crossing in Norse religion because it spoiled that continuity (p. 194). This agrees with Wade row 10.

**Óðinn's paradox.** He is "the manliest god of warriors, but he is also the unmanly master of seid" (p. 195). Freyja taught the Æsir seiðr (Ynglinga saga). Because of the *ergi* attached to it, "men could not practice it without shame" (p. 195).

**On *ergi*.**
- *Ergi* had connotations of what we call homosexuality, but "must be understood contextually and not as a synonym for homosexuality" (p. 195).
- "Both men and women could be accused of ergi", with "somewhat varying connotations" (p. 195, citing Steinsland 1991a). She then sets women aside.
- The man thought passive was "a worse nidingr than the active partner" (p. 195).

**Seiðr as shamanism.** She follows Hultkrantz: spirit helpers are needed, a soul journey is not (p. 196). In Eiríks saga, Guðríðr sings the *varðlokkur* for Þorbjörg, but "it is not clear whether Torbjørg is in an ecstatic trance" (p. 196). Óðinn's seiðr, by contrast, is "classic shamanism", with soul journeys to Hel (p. 196).

**Third sex (pp. 197–198).**
- She writes that "the existence of a third sex is well known" in shamanism. She cites the "berdache" literature and then "two-spirit/two-spirited people" (p. 197).
- From Hyndluljóð's three kinds of seiðr-folk (*völur*, *vitkar*, *seiðberendr*), she argues for "a class of people belonging to a third sex" who practised seiðr (p. 197). Her basis is that *berendr* is "a very coarse word for female genitalia". Price and Blain have already weighed this.

**Why seiðr was *ergi*: three answers.**
1. **Gender-crossing.** Male shamans who crossed "were allowed into a female cosmology from which ordinary men were excluded" (p. 198). By "changing sex, maybe in combination with passive homosexuality, and becoming the wife of an ordinary man", the shaman made his power permanently visible. "The queer shaman or seid man was more powerful than the straight shaman" (p. 199). The model is Siberian (Bleibtreu-Ehrenberg, Czaplicka).
2. **Loss of control as feminine** (Strömbäck). She rejects this as anachronistic: "Conceptions of female impurity do not exist in Old Norse mythology" (p. 199, citing Steinsland). She locates such ideas in Judaeo-Christian and Islamic cosmology.
3. **Pain and sexual ecstasy.** She compares Óðinn's nine nights on the tree to modern erotic asphyxiation (p. 199).

**Adam of Bremen's Uppsala.**
- In Adam's account the feast lasts nine days, males of every kind are hanged in the grove, and the songs are indecent.
- She reads it as a contest: "Maybe these men were aspiring shamans?" Those who survived the hanging became "the tribe's new shaman" (p. 200).
- Her conclusion: "Odin had to be a queer god, because only by his doing seid and being queer could the world continue to exist" (p. 200). She draws on Eliade's divine androgyne (pp. 200–201).

**The "holy white stones" (pp. 201–204).**
- *The finds* (Solberg 1999). There are 56 phallus-shaped stones in Norway, from Telemark to Helgeland. Twenty-one are known from graves and three are closely associated with mounds; many lack a find context. At least 15 bear cup-marks, which Solli says "are considered a female symbol, symbolizing the vulva" (p. 201). Solberg calls them "symbols of ambiguity" (p. 202).
- *Date.* The Early Iron Age, about AD 200–600 (p. 202).
- *The name* comes from Guðrúnarkviða III, where Guðrún swears "by 'the white holy stone'" (p. 201).
- *Readings.*
  - The consensus, from Petersen (1906, 1924) on, is a fertility cult of Njörðr/Nerthus. Some have read Njörðr/Nerthus as a hermaphrodite (Elgqvist, Solberg).
  - Steinsland reads the stones as a "death wedding" with Hel (pp. 202–203).
  - Solli's own reading: the stones' period matches the rise of Óðinn's cult (Hedeager). The graves "may be the graves of shamans", perhaps the *seiðberendr*, "that is, two-spirited people" (p. 204). She calls this a "tentative hypothesis" that a future analysis of the grave goods must test (p. 204).

### Third-gender framings (rejected by the design)

1. **Two-spirit and berdache (pp. 197, 204).** She notes that the Indigenous conference rejected "berdache" (p. 197), then applies "two-spirited" to a Norse category. That is an appropriation, and it is also third-sexing. The term should never appear in the mod.
2. **The *seiðberendr* as a third sex.** The basis is a speculative etymology (Price p. 177). Blain row 4 has already ruled that it may be used only as hostile vocabulary. Confirmed.
3. **The Siberian "sex-changed shaman who becomes a wife".** This model makes the crossing a tool of a male shaman's power. Its subject stays a man who uses femaleness to become "more powerful" (p. 199). That is the opposite of the faith's premise (daughters are women, Bhatt revision). Women appear only as "the female equivalent of the male shaman, the volva" (p. 204), even though her one detailed rite (Eiríks saga) is led by women.
4. **Divine androgyny (Eliade).** "Universal bisexuality" as the principle of being (p. 200). This is the coincidence-of-opposites theology the Bhatt revision rejects for daughters.
5. **Stones as both sexes in one.** A phallus with a vulva (the cup-mark), read as an emblem of the hermaphrodite or the in-between (pp. 202, 204).

### Claims the evidence does not support

1. **Stones linked to Óðinn.** The link is a temporal correlation only (p. 203). No find ties a stone to Óðinn. The stones are Early Iron Age (200–600). Hyndluljóð, her source for *seiðberendr*, is a much later poem. The gap is centuries.
2. **The graves are shamans' graves.** Nobody had done the analysis. She says so herself: "tentative hypothesis" (p. 204). Her proposed test, "graves ... that cannot easily be attributed to either the male or female gender", equates mixed grave goods with a third gender of persons. That is the method Price warns against.
3. **Cup-marks as vulva.** She asserts it ("considered a female symbol"). Cup-marks run back to the Bronze Age, and their meaning is unknown.
4. **Adam's victims as shaman candidates who were meant to survive.** Nothing in Adam supports this. He says the bodies hang in the grove.
5. **Erotic asphyxiation as an analogy for Óðinn's hanging.** It is a modern sexological comparison with no Norse evidence.
6. **"Snorri never mentions the hanging" as suppression.** This is an argument from silence (p. 196). Snorri's Ynglinga saga ch. 7 does give Óðinn's lordship over the hanged. Check before relying on it.
7. **"No female impurity in Norse myth."** Taken from Steinsland. It is plausible as a statement about the mythology and was not tested here. Use it only in the faith's voice.

### What Solli gets right, or what is usable

- Óðinn learned seiðr from Freyja, and men practised it "with shame" (Ynglinga saga ch. 4 and ch. 7).
- The *ergi* insult was deadly. The passive partner bore the worse shame.
- Scholarship has suppressed gender-crossing, and nationalists have captured the Vikings.
- Solberg's catalogue of the stones is real data.
- Guðrún's oath by the white holy stone is real poetry.

**Relevance overall:** moderate to low. The article is useful on two points: the warning about nationalism, and Steinsland's "no female impurity". It is also an exhibit of the third-sexing and male-centred "queer shaman" model the design rejects. It is not reliable for the archaeology.

### Primary-source cases flagged

- **Guðrúnarkviða III**: Guðrún's oath by the white holy stone, and the false accuser punished (row 8). Verify the stanza.
- **Lokasenna 24**: Loki says Óðinn beat the drum on Sámsey "like the völur" and calls it *args aðal*. A divine precedent for the shouted *ergi* (`tm_seidr.0003`), in which the god keeps working seiðr. Faith voice only, and verify the stanza in an edition.
- **Ynglinga saga ch. 4 and ch. 7**: Freyja teaches seiðr; men work it with shame; it is taught to the priestesses (row 2; this already underlies the tenet).
- **Solberg's stones**: Glein, Helgeland (Wik 1991: "Fallosen på Val'haugen på Glein"); Rogaland (Skjølsvold 1963). Artifact sources if row 7 is built.
- **Not for use**: Adam of Bremen's hanged at Uppsala; Hávamál's self-hanging (row 4).
