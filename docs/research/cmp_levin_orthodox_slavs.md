## 0. Framing warning

- **The period is long.** "900–1700" runs well past the game. Many of Levin's best cases are later than 1453: the Stoglav (1551), the Moscow Synod of 1667, Avvakum, the tale of Frol Skobeev, Samuel Collins, Olearius. Her manuscripts are mostly fourteenth- to seventeenth-century copies, though she argues the norms were stable throughout (p. 9). Mark anything taken from the late material as *late* in docs.
- **She uses "homosexuals" as an identity** (e.g. p. 290, "encouraged homosexuals to seek a monastic vocation"). Her sources classify acts, not persons, as she herself shows (p. 77). Use her act-based findings, not the identity label.
- **Skomorokhi.** She calls them "the surviving remnant of the class of pagan priests" (p. 86). That rests on Rybakov, whose interpretations she elsewhere calls "often open to dispute" (p. 39 n. 5). Do not repeat it as fact.
- **No third-gender framing.** She never treats eunuchs as a third sex. In fact she barely treats eunuchs at all (§2.C), which is itself a finding.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **Sex was public and religious.** The Orthodox Slavs treated sexuality as "a public rather than a private matter, and a religious rather than a secular one" (p. ix). The church enforced it almost alone, through confession and penance (pp. 4, 21–35).
- **The church had sole charge.** "Unlike Byzantium, the Slavic states granted complete authority over morality and intrafamilial matters to the church" (p. 297). Princes rarely enforced canon law, even when prelates asked (pp. 23, 298).
- **Enforcement was by penance and community.** Penance meant exclusion from communion, "dry" fasts on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays, and prayers with counted prostrations (p. 31). It could be commuted:
  - by attending the liturgy (thirty times for a year of exclusion);
  - by reading the Psalter;
  - by alms, "ninety nogaty or sixty dinars" for a year of penance;
  - by taking monastic vows (p. 32).

  The last resort was excommunication and ostracism (p. 34). The community, more than the priest, made people admit the norm (pp. 34, 300).
- **Slavic canonists chose the lighter Byzantine penances.** Where St. Basil and St. John the Penitent differed, Slavic codes chose John's lighter rules ("but we say ...", pp. 30–31). There is a conversion table, e.g. Basil's 15 years = 3 years' fast with 250 prostrations (p. 31).
- **The core thesis: sins were graded by the social order they upset.** Acts were graded by how far they disturbed family, hierarchy and **gender roles**, not by "nature" (pp. 199, 210, 302). "Unnatural" sex was sex that "inverted accepted social relationships" (p. 199).
  - Male homosexual anal intercourse was condemned because "Slavic clerics felt it was important for men and women to retain their designated gender roles" (p. 203).
  - Non-penetrative sex between men was a minor sin, because "neither took the place of a woman" (p. 203).
- **The standard was practical and durable.** It fitted popular sensibilities, so it lasted through the Mongol and Turkish conquests (pp. 13, 302).

### B. Same-sex acts and the vocabulary of breach

- **The penances.**
  - Male anal intercourse (*mužebludie*, *muželožstvo*) counted as adultery: Basil's 15 years, or in practice 2–3 years of fasting (p. 200).
  - Intercrural intercourse counted as masturbation (*malakia*): 80 days with 50 prostrations. It did not bar a man from the priesthood (pp. 202, 254).
  - Being the passive partner was "defiling" even when forced, because the man had lowered himself "to the position of a subordinate or a woman" (p. 254).
- **Leniency.** Russian hierarchs were "less hostile ... than their Western European counterparts", treating these acts as "heterosexual adultery, at worst". Neither the Code of Jaroslav nor the Code of Stefan Dušan mentions homosexuality (p. 203).
- **Women.** Sex between women was *malakia*, with a year's exclusion from communion (p. 203).
  - The Zakon Sudnyj Ljudem, art. 59, prescribes flogging for a woman who mounts another (pp. 203–204).
  - Clerics linked women's same-sex acts to pagan rites: such women were "God-insulting grannies" (*baby bogomerzskie*), accused of "praying to vily" (p. 204).
  - Bishop Nifont set a light penance for adolescent girls (p. 204; Voprosy Ilji arts. 23–24).
- **"Sodomy" was a floating pejorative.** It was applied even to marital positions, and was often blamed on "foreign, non-Christian influences" (pp. 197–199).

| 2 | **Penance for castrating a son to sell him as a slave: three years** ("Nekotoraja zapoved'", art. 41). Other canons treated self-castration as suicide. | 73 n. 114 |
| 3 | Self-castration was "strongly condemned": the usual penance was three years on bread and water. | 209 |
| 4 | **A self-castrated man could not be ordained, but this did not apply "to someone who had been made a eunuch in childhood, or was born with a genital defect".** | 257 |
| 5 | For clerics, the early penance of 3 years was later replaced by "treating self-castration as analogous to murder", with loss of rank. A scribe crossed out the old penance. | 260 |
| 6 | Bogomil-type preachers Lazar and Kirill paraded naked in Trnovo, "encouraging castration and the abandonment of marriage" (Life of Theodosius of Tărnovo, Zlatarski ed., pp. 19–21). Slavic clerics accused heretics of excessive asceticism and "self-castration". | 45 |

Levin has **nothing** on a Slavic court eunuch office, on Byzantine eunuch prelates in Rus', or on hermaphrodites. The only body-variance clause is "born with a genital defect" (p. 257). Read cautiously, and as an inference *unverified* beyond this book, this means the Byzantine eunuch institution did not take root in Slavic canon practice. In Slavic Orthodoxy, a cut person is a *penitential* problem (who cut whom, and why), not a court rank.

### D. Women's roles in ritual and church

- **Proskurnicy**, the women who baked the communion bread. They had to be:
  - widows of good character, married only once, never remarried;
  - over forty;
  - not menstruating while baking.

  Their rules "seem to have been adapted from early canons related to deaconesses", that "vanished class of female clerics" (pp. 274–275). This is the one sacral office for laywomen in the book.
- **Nuns** could enter the sanctuary to light candles and decorate the altar, but all sacraments were male (p. 282). Convents also served as refuges for widows and divorced women, and as places of detention (p. 283).
- **Pagan women.** The *baby bogomerzskie* ("God-insulting grannies") were the clerics' term for female pagan leaders (p. 204). Clerics paired "evil-tongued" and "pagan" in one word, *zlojazyčny* (p. 42). Pre-Revolutionary scholars held "that paganism was primarily female", and Levin rejects both that view and its feminist inversion (p. 38).
- **Ritual sex.** At festivals such as Kupalo or Rusalia, ritual sex was "carefully structured and directed by the officiating priests", not licence (pp. 40–41).

### E. Kinship that the mod's lines can use

- **Adoption.** Slavic canon law recognised adoption, chiefly to give a childless couple an heir. It "could be confirmed through a church ceremony", and adopted children were "legally the equals" of born children, with full inheritance (pp. 148–149). Metropolitan Kiprian upheld an adopted son's rights in 1404 (p. 149 n. 67). **Serbian law forbade adopting a son if one had daughters, to protect their inheritance** (p. 148).
- **Adopted sisters.** *Pobratim* (adopted brother) and **posestrima (adopted sister)** usually meant blood-brotherhood or sisterhood, older than Christianity (p. 149). Some clerics set a year's fast for the pagan bloodletting rite. Others accepted the bond if made "through Christian rites designed for this purpose" (p. 149).
- **Emotional bonds outside marriage.** Strong friendships were "confirmed through ceremonies of adoption and spiritual kinship" (p. 301).
- **Sponsors.** A baptismal sponsor, required for adults too, became "as a parent". Russian hierarchs required the sponsor to be **of the same sex as the godchild** (p. 150).

## 4. Design implications

1. **A Slavic Orthodox penance ladder** (new event chain, `tm_slav.*`). Root = a daughter in a realm whose liege is Orthodox and of Slavic culture. The bishop's court acts, not the prince. Rungs:
   - (1) the confessor's questions, which in the later form ask "Did you not...?" (p. 29);
   - (2) a dry fast on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays with prostrations (stress; small piety gain if borne);
   - (3) exclusion from communion (opinion loss with clergy and county);
   - (4) excommunication and ostracism.

   Each rung offers commutation by alms, "ninety nogaty" (gold cost, p. 32); by liturgy attendance (time); or by taking vows in a convent (Dorleyism's Sisters' House may count, as a jab). There are no burned books and no voided wills. Loc for the prince: "That is the bishop's business. I have a war."
2. **The beard, not the dress, is the charge** (Bædling and Nehpakhot daughters in Rus', Bulgaria, Serbia). Event "Made to Resemble a Woman": a priest threatens anathema because she has shaved. Options:
   - "I did not shave. Nothing grows." (re-embodied daughters);
   - accept the anathema;
   - go before the bishop (diplomacy check: he rules it a carnival offence, 7 weeks and 150 prostrations, pp. 195, 202).

   Loc for the priest, after p. 202: "A man is made in God's image. Why would you take his face away?"
3. **The carnival rhyme with Ha-Nehpakhot.** Levin's penitentials set the dress in "carnival festivals" (p. 280 n. 195), as Kalonymus sets it at Purim. The README's Nehpakhot line ("She keeps it on when the joke is over") has an Orthodox mirror. Event for any daughter at Koljada or Rusalii in a Slavic county: the priest gives the seasonal penance. She can do the seven weeks and keep the dress; or ask why the dress is a sin only after the feast. Loc: "Seven weeks for the season's dress. And for the rest of my life?"
4. **The knife sorted by consent** (Dorleyism, by House). This is the most useful finding for the existing doctrines. Proposed Orthodox Slavic opinions toward daughters of the knife:
   - **Grandmother's House** (captives cut without consent): the victim is blameless, since a eunuch "made ... in childhood" may even be ordained (p. 257). The cutter has sinned, at least the 3 years for castrating to sell (p. 73 n. 114). So: opinion **0** toward the daughter, and **−20** toward the Dorleyist *ruler* who ordered it.
   - **Gift Alone** (adults who ask): her own choice reads as self-castration, penanced like suicide and later like murder (pp. 73, 209, 260). So: **−15** toward the daughter. The irony: the most consensual House is the most condemned. The README should say so plainly.
   - **Aunt Bea's House** (violent men cut under sponsors): reads as punitive castration of another, 3 years for the cutter. So: **−10** toward the ruler, **−5** toward the daughter.

   Implementation: there is currently no Christian `triggered_opinion` on the daughter traits (only Islamic, Eastern, Jewish). A parameter for Eastern Christian faiths, or a culture-and-faith scripted trigger, is needed. Whether vanilla has a usable parameter is *unverified*.
5. **Bulgarian reaction: "the sisters of Lazar and Kirill."** A Dorleyist preacher in Bulgaria after c. 1350 is read through the Trnovo heretics who preached castration and the abandonment of marriage (p. 45). The label is Bogomil, and Bogomils were condemned for denying marriage (pp. 43–45). Event for a Dorleyist ruler or courtier in `k_bulgaria`. Loc for the hesychast monk: "You are what Lazar preached naked in the square." The Sister's answer: "We marry. We adopt. Lazar would have hated us."
6. **The Bædling "grannies".** In Rus', a seiðr woman of the faith is a *baba bogomerzkaja*, a "God-insulting granny" (p. 204), the local slur for a pagan woman leader. It plays the role *ragr* plays in the north. Add it to the *ragr* answers as an Orthodox variant: "Grannies, they say. Then let the grannies sing." This is especially apt for the **Kyiv** holy site (`konugardr`).
7. **Same-sex marriage reaction** (all four faiths allow it). An Orthodox Slavic observer should be *milder* than a Catholic one: "equivalent of heterosexual adultery, at worst" (p. 203), and women's acts lighter still (p. 203). Suggest half the Catholic penalty, if a Catholic penalty is ever added.
8. **Adoption and sworn sisters (confirmations).** Take as Daughter matches Slavic canon practice: adoption by church rite, with full inheritance (pp. 148–149). The Bædling "Two may bind themselves as sisters by the old oath" has a Slavic twin, *posestrima*. Event in Rus': the priest will bless two Bædling sisters' oath if they do not cut their palms (p. 149). Options: Christian rite (no penance, small opinion gain with clergy) or the old blood rite (a year's penance, prestige with the hall).
9. **Serbian heir clause.** A Serbian lord may not adopt a son if he has daughters (p. 148). Flavour for The Sisters Choose under a Serbian liege: "Serbian law already guards a daughter's inheritance. We ask only that it count ours."
10. **Proskurnica, an office for elder sisters in Orthodox realms** (*proposal*). An elder Dorleyist or Bædling widow, over forty, once married, may be asked by the parish to bake the bread (pp. 274–275). The canons on menstruation do not touch a re-embodied daughter. The text should be dry, not a joke: "The canon wants a woman past her courses. The parish wants good bread." A small piety and opinion modifier; a later bishop's inquiry could remove her.
11. **Monastery visit** (*proposal*). A daughter visiting a Slavic monastery meets Nil Sorskij's rule against "beardless and effeminate faces" (p. 279) and the Athonite exclusion of women (p. 290). The abbot must decide in which category to refuse her. Loc: "The rule bars women, and it bars faces like mine. Pick one, Father."
12. **Castration for sale** (`tm_islamic` / "Boys for Tarsus", El-Azhari note row 2). Orthodox canon priced the sin of castrating a son for sale at three years' penance (p. 73 n. 114). A Dorleyist Sister's line: "Even the Slavic priests set three years for cutting a boy to sell him. Rum sets none."
