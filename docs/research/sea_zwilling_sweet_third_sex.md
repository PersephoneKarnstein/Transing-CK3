## 0. Framing warning (Bhatt)

- The authors use "third sex" as a heuristic. They say the sex/gender distinction "was not … made in pre-modern India" except by some later Jain doxographers (p. 99).
- **Their conclusion supports Bhatt's position:**
  - The third sex is "a residual category" (p. 123).
  - The data "give little support to those who posit a romantically affirmative view of a special spiritual or social role for third-gender people". The *napuṃsaka* "was marginalized and mainly regarded with contempt, at best with wary tolerance" (p. 124).
  - Reading hijras back into Śikhaṇḍin is called "inappropriate reading back" (p. 125 n. 85).
- **Use.** This chapter is the reaction source for Indic courts. It is not a model for any faith.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **Vedic origins** (pp. 101–104):
  - Androgynous creation imagery (Prajāpati with a womb and breasts).
  - A late-Vedic "virility obsession".
  - Rites against impotence. The first *napuṃsaka* is a **sacrificial victim**. Maitrāyaṇī Saṃhitā 2.5.5: "this man in the form of a woman has been seized by Misfortune" (p. 102). Indra's sin, cast off, "became a napuṃsaka".
- **Castration was disapproved of.** Their evidence (p. 104 n. 10):
  - Aśoka's fifth pillar edict bans gelding for part of the year.
  - The *Arthaśāstra* (13.5.3) tells a conquering king to prohibit castration.
  - An 1823 report says bulls were never castrated in pre-Muslim Bengal.
- ***Klība* and *paṇḍaka*** first meant "impotent man". They came to mean a man in women's hair and ornaments who dances (pp. 105–106).
  - At weddings klības are "doing evil with wailing"; only women and klības danced (p. 106).
  - The klība is "false", absorbs the impurity of the intermediate realm, and is the victim offered to *Pāpman*, "Sin" (p. 106).
  - The penalty for killing a *ṣaṇḍa* is "a load of straw and a measure of lead" (Gautama Dharmasūtra 22.25, Manu 11.132; p. 106 n. 16).
- **"Neither woman nor man."** Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 5.4.1.1–2: the long-haired man is not a woman because he is male, and not a man because he is long-haired (p. 107).
  - **Every Vedic case is "a result of misfortune, magic or injury"**, not congenital (p. 107).
- **The grammarians** (pp. 108–110):
  - Patañjali: "the neuter is that which resembles both without being either" (p. 108).
  - By the 2nd century BCE *napuṃsaka* is a third sex with the subtypes *ṣaṇḍa* (impotent) and *paṇḍaka* (gender-variant).
  - **Pāli Buddhism uses only *paṇḍaka*; *klība* "seems not to be found in Buddhist literature at all"** (p. 109).
- **Medicine** (pp. 110–112):
  - Caraka and Suśruta make the napuṃsaka a true biological third sex, born of equal parental seed.
  - The texts give "no instructions … for preventing the conception or birth of a napumsaka" (p. 112).
  - The term *tṛtīyaprakṛti* is first attested at Caraka 4.2.25b, and is in the *Amarakośa* by the 4th–5th century (p. 112).
- **The Jains** (pp. 112–116):
  - They replace *liṅga* with *veda*, meaning sex and sexual feeling.
  - Napuṃsaka-veda burns "like the conflagration of a great city" (p. 113).
  - The *Bhagavatī* adds a fourth class, the "male-napuṃsaka". He looks and dresses like a man but desires men, and is ordainable (p. 114).
  - By the 5th century they have "severed the connection" between *dravyaliṅga* (body) and *bhāvaliṅga* (sexuality). Any body can have any of the three sexualities (p. 116; Niśītha Bhāṣya 3571, n. 58).
- **The Buddhists** (pp. 117–119):
  - They held a two-sex model from the *Aggañña Sutta*.
  - They refused paṇḍakas both ordination and recognition as lay followers.
  - Vasubandhu holds that bodhisattvas are always male, and that advanced practitioners are never reborn as ṣaṇḍas (n. 70).
  - Contrast: the Jains held that third-sex persons could reach extrasensory knowledge and even be perfected souls (*Bhagavatī* 9.31.23; "Dasavaikalika 36.49, 51", p. 119).
  - **Cite check:** the *Daśavaikālika* has no ch. 36. The list of liberated beings "of the napuṃsaka sign" is probably *Uttarādhyayana* 36.49–51. *Unverified*.
- **The *Kāma Sūtra* 2.9** splits the *tṛtīyā prakṛti* into a "female" and a "male" form. Both are defined by giving fellatio as a trade (pp. 119–120).
- **Society** (pp. 120–122):
  - The androgyne deity is venerated; human third-sex persons are despised.
  - Arjuna's disguise as a dancer is "despised by the world" (Mahābhārata 4.18.11; p. 120).
  - Manu 9.201 disinherits klības and bars them from rites (p. 121).
  - Jain exceptions:
    - napuṃsakas who dress as men and are not licentious may be laymen;
    - later, those who can control their sexuality may be ordained (6 of 16 types, Niśītha Bhāṣya 3561–2, n. 80);
    - candidates were admitted "when they were known to be socially well-connected, or if they had special expertise in an area such as medicine or administration" (p. 122).
  - Religious orders "may have mitigated the lot" of some (p. 122).

## 2. What matters for the design

1. **Three Indic laws and three readings of a daughter.** They should not share one modifier:
   - **Brahmanical (Hindu courts):**
     - A daughter, of any gate, is a *klība*: "a man in the form of a woman".
     - The condition comes from misfortune, a curse or injury.
     - She is maintained but disinherited (Manu 9.201–203, checked in `comparanda_reading_list.md`) and inauspicious at weddings.
     - Her death weighs "a load of straw and a measure of lead".
     - There is no karmic-fall narrative of the Buddhist kind, and no ordination question, because the Brahmanical order has no nuns.
   - **Jain courts:**
     - She is judged by *veda* (desire) rather than body.
     - A daughter of the knife is a *kṛtrimaklība* and, in Śvetāmbara law, "essentially a male" (Jaini, via the reading list).
     - The door is narrow but real: the **Śvetāmbara** canon allows ordination to a napuṃsaka who controls desire, and expedient admission for useful skill.
     - **Digambara** courts use the napuṃsaka as the agreed case of who cannot be liberated (Jaini p. 112, via the reading list).
   - **Buddhist courts:** see `sea_gyatso_cabezon_buddhist_law.md`. A spontaneous change is a karmic fall; the knife makes a paṇḍaka; infertility bars her from the nuns.
2. **The knife repels all three.** Brahmanical and Mauryan law disapproved of castration even for animals (p. 104 n. 10). This confirms an extra penalty for daughters of the knife in Indic opinion.
3. **"Reborn"/"karmic fruit" is a Buddhist and Jain idiom, not a Brahmanical one.**
   - The Vedic and legal texts attribute the condition to misfortune, curse, the wrath of a god or injury (p. 107; compare Nārada's curse-impotence in Cabezón ch. 7).
   - **A separate Hindu reaction is needed.**
4. **The klība's dancing, hair and ornaments are a hostile period stereotype** (pp. 105–106). Use it only in hostile speakers' mouths. It must never be the narrator's description of a daughter.
5. **The Southeast Asian expansion.** The Mahābhārata's Bṛhannaḍā episode reached Java as Wṛhannalā (*Wirāṭaparwa*, 996; reading list §2). The authors show that the source frames the role as "despised by the world" (p. 120). A Javanese Hindu court's literary reference point for a man in women's dress is therefore a disguise and a disgrace, not an office. This strengthens the reading list's "poor fit" verdict for Majapahit.
6. **An embryo comparandum for Ha-Nehpakhot.** The *Abhidharmakośa* (3.15a–b) holds that an embryo is first male or female and "may become napuṃsaka" in development (p. 118 n. 64). Ha-Nehpakhot's Dinah precedent (Berakhot 60a) is a change of sex in the womb. A comparative loc line could note that Indic scholastics also imagined the womb changing sex, but towards the neuter, not towards a daughter. Use only in a scholar's voice, if at all.

## 4. Design implications

- **Hindu reaction event "Not at the Wedding"** (`hinduism_religion` ruler; a daughter at court is invited to a marriage).
  - A priest invokes the Vedic image of klības "doing evil with wailing" at weddings.
  - Options:
    - (a) She is kept away: +opinion with the priest, −opinion from her.
    - (b) The ruler overrules: −piety, +opinion from her.
  - This pairs with Bhatt's bride-contamination taboo (`book_bhatt_third_sex.md` row 8).
- **Hindu inheritance rule (event-applied).** If a Hindu-faith ruler names a daughter as heir, a `tm_kliba_disinherited` flag makes vassals cite Manu 9.201. This could be an opinion malus on the heir from same-faith vassals only. The daughter remains "maintained", so no exile event.
- **Jain door (Śvetāmbara only).** A Jain ruler may employ a daughter with stewardship or learning ≥ 12 as a court physician or steward under an "expedient admission" (p. 122), which removes the eastern opinion malus for that ruler.
  - Digambara rulers get no such option.
  - Faith keys for Śvetāmbara and Digambara in vanilla are *unverified*; check `common/religion/religions/` before scripting.

- **Hostile loc lines** (in-world speakers only; short):
  - Brahman priest: "Neither woman nor man: a man by birth, not a man by the hair." (after Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 5.4.1.1–2)
  - Jain monk: "Her desire burns like a city ablaze; the Order will not house it." (after *Bhagavatī* 2.5.1, p. 113)
  - Court jester at a Hindu court: "Bṛhannaḍā, despised by the world." (Mahābhārata 4.18.11)
- **Never** use "tṛtīyā prakṛti" or "third nature" as an honorific, a doctrine name or a faith name. The source shows it was a dramatic and erotic-manual label for prostitutes and entertainers (pp. 112, 120).
