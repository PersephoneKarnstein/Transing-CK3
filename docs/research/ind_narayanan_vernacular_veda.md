## 0. Framing warning

The book is mostly **ethnography of 1983–1991**: the Srirangam Festival of Recitation as Narayanan watched it, with interviews of named hereditary performers and reciters. The *araiyar* families and Śrīvaiṣṇava reciters are **living communities**. Use the book for structure, calendar and in-period anecdote. Never present the *araiyar*, the Śrīvaiṣṇavas, or the Āṇṭāḷ cult as the faith, and do not name living informants in loc. Where Narayanan reports something from a medieval source (commentary, *The Splendor*, *Koyil Oḻuku*, an inscription), this note says so. Everything else is modern observation.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **A vernacular Veda.** The *Tiruvāymoḻi* of Nammāḻvār (9th c.) was "the first 'vernacular' work within the Hindu consciousness to be considered as revealed" and the first in a mother tongue taken into home and temple liturgy (p. 2). Unlike the Sanskrit Veda it "has been recited by men and women of all castes" (p. 2). Accessibility meant "women and lower-caste people had the authority to recite it" (p. 46).
- **The girl's voice is a quarter of the poem.** "In about one fourth of the poems Nammalvar talks of his separation and talks from the stance of a lovesick 'heroine'", a persona from the classical Tamil love poems; "in thirty-three verses Nammalvar identifies himself as a cowherd girl" (p. 3). He speaks as "a young girl in love, for example, or the mother or friend of the young girl" (p. 14). Narayanan calls him "the suffering hero (or 'lovesick heroine,' if one adopts the terminology of classical Tamil poetry)" (p. 37).
- **The term "Parāṅkuśa Nāyakī" does not occur.** Narayanan uses "lovesick girl (*nayaki*)" (p. 172) and "lovesick heroine". She does **not** state the gloss that God alone is male and every soul a woman. Her nearest point is that the Lord is "beloved of the human soul" (p. 14).
- **The commentaries.** Written commentary began in the late 11th century with Piḷḷāṉ, Rāmānuja's cousin. Five classical commentaries (11th–13th c.) became authoritative (pp. 4, 82).
- **Recitation in ritual is itself a commentary**, alongside the written one: "the oral (ritual) and written (commentarial) elaborations" differ in emphasis but not in message (pp. 4–5).

### The Festival of Recitation (*Adhyayana Utsava*), ch. 8

- **When.** Mārkaḻi (15 Dec–13 Jan), for 21 days, from the new moon. The first ten mornings recite the first 2,000 verses. Ten nights are given to the *Tiruvāymoḻi*. The 21st day is a fast recitation of the third thousand (pp. 116–117).
- **Founding, by tradition.** *The Splendor* (Piṉpaḻakiya Perumāḷ Jīyar's *Guruparamparāprabhāvam*, 14th c., p. 21) says Nāthamuni began the festival (p. 116). *Koyil Oḻuku* says instead that Tirumaṅkai Āḻvār petitioned the Lord of Srirangam to have the poem chanted as a Veda (ch. 2 n. 54). Narayanan calls the *Koyil Oḻuku* "a document that was compiled over several centuries" and that sometimes "fill[s] in the gaps" (ch. 2 n. 54).
- **The invitation.** A month earlier, in Kārttikai, the Lord "speaks" through a priest. A scribe writes an invitation to Nammāḻvār, sent with "the cloak that I have worn, sandalwood fragrance, and flower garlands" as marks of favour (p. 117). **The Lord sends his own worn garland to the poet.** This is the mirror of Āṇṭāḷ's sacrilege.
- **The gates of heaven.** The northern "gates of heaven" open only for the ten nights of *Tiruvāymoḻi* recitation: "for these ten days heaven is contiguous with earth" (pp. 117–118).
- **The Lord dressed as a woman.** "A special favorite with the pilgrims is the Lord dressed as a woman, an enchantress, or Mohini" (p. 117). At Triplicane the tenth-day costume is *Nācciyār tirukkōlam*, "sacred dress as a woman or Mohini" (ch. 8 n. 3).
- **The *araiyar*.**
  - Hereditary performers at **three temples only**: Srirangam, Srivilliputtur (Āṇṭāḷ's birthplace) and Āḻvārtirunagari (p. 123; ch. 8 n. 13). The dance tradition at Melkote "came to an end a long time ago" (ch. 8 n. 13).
  - "Only male members of these brahmin families have the authority to perform." They trace the art to Nāthamuni (10th c.) and their rights to the 11th century (p. 123).
  - The title is usually glossed "king": the *araiyar* is "the monarch of the alvar verses" and "assumes the roles of the alvar as well as the roles assumed by the alvar" (pp. 123–124).
  - *Koyil Oḻuku* calls the *araiyar* "the limbs of the alvar, the limbs with which he can act" (p. 124).
  - The service may be done only before the processional image. A senior *araiyar* said it is "only meant for 'our Lord' and 'our alvar'" (p. 124).
- **How the girl is performed.** "Nammalvar speaks as a lovesick girl, the girl's mother, or friend, and the araiyar enacts all these roles," and also a Gypsy fortune-teller who reads omens that the Lord will come to the girl (p. 130). He dances with facial expression (*abhinaya*), repeating a single line in many readings (pp. 124–125). Only ten of the 1,102 songs now get the full sung-danced-commented service (p. 125).
- **In-period evidence of girl-voice performance.**
  - **Empār**, Rāmānuja's cousin (12th c.), watched an *araiyar* play Kulaśēkhara's angry cowherd girl clawing at Krishna. He proposed that she should instead hide her face from him, "who could not survive without her"; the *araiyar* accepted (p. 126).
  - **Nañjīyar** (early 12th c.) said he learned more from seeing his teacher, Tirunaṟaiyūr Araiyar, weep over a verse than from three readings of it (p. 132, citing the 13th-c. *Thirty-six Thousand* commentary). Narayanan: "the actor becomes the character he plays" (p. 132).
  - So the *araiyar*'s performance of the girl's part, and audience debate about how a girl should feel, are attested by 12th–13th-c. commentarial anecdote. The present choreography is modern observation.
- **The seventh night: Nammāḻvār as a woman.**
  - On the seventh night the festival image of Nammāḻvār "is dressed as a beautiful woman (*mohana alankaram*), pining for her beloved", while *Tiruvāymoḻi* 7.2 is chanted (p. 127). 7.2 is the only decad addressed to Srirangam (p. 172).
  - The Lord is carried forward in the priests' hands (*kaittala cēvai*) "almost as if he wanted to approach Nammalvar", who is "dressed as a woman" across the hall (p. 173). It draws the largest crowds of the festival (p. 173).
  - The same afternoon, male devotees chant hymns to Śrī's grace. Narayanan reads the ritual as saying that "it is by the Goddess' intervention that the Lord softens and appears before Nammalvar" (p. 173).
  - 7.2 is spoken by **the mother**: "Night and day, her eyes know no sleep," and she asks the Lord "what are you doing with her?" (pp. 173–174).
  - **Date of the dressing rite: unknown.** It is reported from 1990 observation only.
- **The tenth night: liberation.** Nammāḻvār, in white with a basil garland, is covered with *tulasī* leaves (liberation). A representative asks the Lord to return him, and the Lord replies through a priest, "We give him back to you" (pp. 127–128). The saint returns as teacher: liberation "ends not with his being at the side of the Lord but, instead, with his return to humanity" (p. 128).
- **The lovers' quarrel (*praṇaya kalaham*)** at Āḻvārtirunagari. *Araiyars* voice both Śrī and Viṣṇu. Śrī locks the Lord out, and Nammāḻvār reconciles them. In the Srirangam version Śrī yields because she respects Nammāḻvār "as if he were her father" (pp. 128–129).
- **The poem as garland.** The *antāti* form links the last words of each verse to the next. The last verse leads back to the first, "forming a garland of words for the Lord" (p. 128). Nammāḻvār calls the poem a garland, a "garland of words" and a "Tamil-garland" (p. 41).

### Women in the recitation

- **Āṇṭāḷ is kept out of the hall.** All the āḻvārs face the Lord in the Hall of a Thousand Pillars "with the exception of Antal, the only female saint". Her *Tiruppāvai* is sung in "almost every Srivaisnava home" all month, "yet she is not brought in to the court of the male alvars" (p. 123). Narayanan doubts that this is discrimination (ch. 8 n. 9).
- **Women may not be *araiyar*.** "Women were traditionally not allowed to perform in the araiyar tradition." Her suggested reasons are menstrual and birth pollution on fixed festival days, and the art's association with the *devadāsī* tradition, so that "women of high-class families would have been prevented from learning anything that was associated with suspected prostitution" (ch. 8 n. 14). The male line was kept by adoption: "only a son, a direct male descendent or child formally adopted, could claim to be an araiyar" (p. 85).
- **Medieval women reciters (inscriptions).**
  - A 13th-century inscription at the Kanchipuram (Varadarāja) temple records an endowment by the Telugu chieftain Gaṇḍagopāla "for the maintenance of women reciters" (*perumāḷ muṉ pāṭum peṇṭukaḷ*, "women who sing before the Lord") (p. 55, citing K. V. Raman, ch. 3 n. 22).
  - A 1535 inscription there gives them a share of temple food (p. 55).
  - Tirupati inscriptions mention women singing in praise of the Lord (ch. 6 n. 8).
  - Today women recite at home, not in temple choirs (p. 55).
- **"Servants of god" and the loss of temple song.** Some singing "came under the purview of certain women who were called 'servants of god' (*emperumāṉ aṭiyār* or *deva dasi*)", who "in time came to acquire a reputation as courtesans". Narayanan suggests that the brahmanical temple "tried to distance itself from this group of singers" (pp. 83–84). This is her speculation, not a source.
- **One woman commentator.** Tirukkōṉēri Dāsyai wrote the only known woman's commentary, *Tiruvāymoḻi Vācakamālai*, "The Garland of Words". It covers a hundred verses and is illustrated with her own drawings. It was "for centuries ... marginalized" and stayed in manuscript until 1950 (pp. 108–109; ch. 7 n. 25). Its colophon says she finished it in Mārkaḻi, the festival month (p. 109). **Narayanan gives no date for her** (*unverified*).
- **Nathamuni and the courtesans.** *The Splendor* (14th c.) has Nāthamuni judge a music contest between two courtesans. He takes the side of the one skilled in "divine music" and sets the canon to it (p. 80). This is hagiography.

### Srirangam history (*Koyil Oḻuku*, as Narayanan reports it)

- **The Sultānī story.** A "Tulukka" king carries off the processional image, the "Handsome Bridegroom". A woman from a nearby village follows it to Delhi, where the king's daughter, the "Sultani", rescues it from a storehouse. Temple musicians sing it home while she sleeps. In the sequel she follows the image south and dies of grief (pp. 82–83; ch. 6 n. 7).
  - Narayanan calls the story anachronistic (it is placed before Nāthamuni) and "generic". "Most of these temples have a shrine for a *bibi nacciyar*, or 'Muslim consort' for Visnu" (p. 83).
- **Raids and disruption.** The Muslim invasions were c. 1311 and c. 1323. The Vijayanagara kings restored the deity in 1371. Worship was "never abandoned, except for fifty-nine years in the fourteenth century" (pp. 83–84).
- **The *araiyar* bell (family lore, unattested).** In one attack hundreds of *araiyars* of East Uttara Street were killed and "their cymbals were melted and recast into a large bell". Narayanan found no written account (p. 84).
- **The Hall of a Thousand Pillars.** *Koyil Oḻuku* says it was built by two Hoysala generals under Rāmanātha (1263–95), which dates it to the 13th century at least (p. 123).

## 2. What matters for the design

1. **The girl's voice is liturgy, not private feeling.** A quarter of the scripture is in a woman's voice (p. 3). It is sung by everyone, of all castes, in temple and home (p. 2). Male performers are named in 12th-c. commentary anecdote enacting the girl and arguing about how she should feel (pp. 126, 132). This is the strongest in-period support for the Garland's premise that men sang, and were heard, as women before God.
2. **Nammāḻvār is dressed as a woman on the seventh night, and the Lord comes towards her** (pp. 127, 173). This is the closest thing in the source to the Garland's "answered" bride. But it is **modern observation of unknown date**, and the image is dressed, not the poet. Use it only as a character's claim, or as "the temple's custom" in the faith's mouth.
3. **The worn garland runs both ways.** Āṇṭāḷ gave the Lord a garland she had worn (Ramanujan, see `ind_ramanujan_bhakti.md`). The Lord sends Nammāḻvār "the cloak that I have worn ... and flower garlands" as an invitation (p. 117). A gate step can carry both: the seeker wears the god's garland, and the god's worn garland comes back as the summons.
4. **Mārkaḻi is doubly right.** It is Āṇṭāḷ's vow month and the Festival of Recitation month. The gates of heaven are open for the ten nights (pp. 116–118). The gate's timing is confirmed.
5. **Śrī mediates.** Narayanan reads the seventh-night ritual as the Goddess softening the Lord towards the girl (p. 173). The quarrel rite makes Nammāḻvār the reconciler of Śrī and Viṣṇu (p. 129). The Garland's theology can have the bride approach through Śrī, not against her (see also Narayanan's Śrī essay, in `ind_ramanujan_bhakti.md` §4).
6. **A men-only performance of women.** Women are barred from the *araiyar* role, and Āṇṭāḷ, the one woman saint, is kept out of the hall where men perform the girl (p. 123; ch. 8 n. 14). This is the hierarchy-in-heaven risk the reading list flags, in its sharpest in-world form. It is a gift for dissent: the Garland's daughters can say that the temple lets a man play the girl but will not let the girl in.
7. **Medieval women did sing before the Lord, for pay.** The 13th-c. Kanchipuram endowment for "women who sing before the Lord" (p. 55) is the in-period counterpart to Rājarāja's temple women. The Garland's daughters can be reciters with endowed maintenance. That is a *line* with an income, not alms.
8. **A woman's commentary called "The Garland of Words"**, finished in Mārkaḻi and marginalised for centuries (pp. 108–109). The image is right for the faith's name, but the author is a historical Śrīvaiṣṇava woman of unknown date. **Venerate the book and its form; do not claim its author.**
9. **Calendar and space for events.** The Kārttikai invitation, the new-moon start, the opening of the gates on Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī, the seventh-night dressing, the tenth-night liberation and return, the 21st-day close (pp. 116–128). A Garland festival event chain can follow this skeleton without copying the living rite.

## 3. Corrections to the candidate

- **Nāthamuni's founding of the *araiyar*** is tradition, as the reading list says. Narayanan adds that the families trace "their rights back to the eleventh century" (p. 123). In-period evidence of *araiyar* performance comes from 12th–13th-c. commentary anecdotes (pp. 126, 132), not from inscriptions read here. The c. 1085 Srirangam inscription is still *unverified*. Ramanujan (*Hymns*, pp. xii–xiii) says only that 11th-c. inscriptions endow reciters.
- **"Parāṅkuśa Nāyakī" and "God alone is male" are not in Narayanan.** She says "lovesick girl (*nayaki*)" and "lovesick heroine". Source the soul-as-woman gloss to Ramanujan (*Hymns*, p. 153; "On Woman Saints", p. 324; Mahādēvi 68) and to Narayanan's Śrī essay (Deśika: the soul as the bride of Viṣṇu), all in `ind_ramanujan_bhakti.md`.
- **The *araiyar* do not perform at Kanchipuram.** Only three temples have them (p. 123). If the candidate gives the *araiyar* court position flavour from a place, use Srirangam, Srivilliputtur or Āḻvārtirunagari.
- **The Lord's Mohinī dress is a pilgrim favourite** (p. 117). In myth Mohinī is a **disguise** (Doniger, `ind_doniger_splitting.md`). The Garland must not read the Mohinī costume as the Lord being a woman, and must not let its own change read as Mohinī-style disguise.
- **Bībī Nācciyār.** Narayanan calls the Sultānī story anachronistic and generic. Bībī Nācciyār shrines are a widespread type ("most of these temples", p. 83), not Srirangam's alone. The story comes from the *Koyil Oḻuku*, a document "compiled over several centuries" (ch. 2 n. 54). The reading list's "*unverified*" stands. The flavour is usable only as later temple tradition.
- **Do not cite Narayanan for "daughters of God" or temple-women property.** Those are Orr's (another reader's). Narayanan's "servants of god" (*emperumāṉ aṭiyār*) passage is a speculation about singers (pp. 83–84).

## 4. Design implications

- **Gate step 3 ("the worn garland"), add a reply beat.** If the seeker owns the worn garland, a later event can bring "the Lord's" worn garland back to her: a temple summons, as the Kārttikai invitation sends "the cloak that I have worn ... and flower garlands" (p. 117). Narrator: the temple sends a garland. Faith voice: "He has worn it first, and sent it."
- **Gate step 4 ("the dream of the wedding"), staging.** Model the success dream on the seventh-night *kaittala cēvai*: she is held up across the hall, and the Lord is carried towards *her* (p. 173). Option text in the faith's voice: "He came across the hall." Never claim this rite is medieval.
- **Gate timing.** Keep Mārkaḻi. Add a minor bonus if the vow's tenth dawn falls on "the night the northern gates open" (Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī, pp. 117–118).
- **Court position, "Reciter Before the Lord".** Base it on the 13th-c. Kanchipuram endowment "for the maintenance of women reciters" (p. 55), not on the *araiyar*. It is a salaried temple office a Garland daughter can hold, which keeps the faith's women owners and earners (Bhatt), not performers kept by a male line.
- **Marking role, "One Who Sings the Girl's Part".** Keep the reading list's idea that a man who performs the girl's part is first Marked. Frame it as the faith's reading of a practice it borrows, and give it the Empār beat: an elder in the audience corrects how "she" should grieve, and the performer accepts (p. 126). Do not call the role *araiyar* in loc. The word names a living hereditary office (p. 123).
- **Dissent event, "The Girl Kept from the Hall".** Garland daughters protest that the temple lets men dance the girl while Āṇṭāḷ's image is "not brought in to the court of the male alvars" (p. 123). Outcomes: piety and a hostile priest, or a quiet compromise. This is where the hierarchy critique lives.
- **Decision or modifier, "The Garland of Words".** A learned Garland daughter writes a commentary in Mārkaḻi (learning, legacy). Possible event: it is set aside by the men's schools ("marginalized", p. 108). Name the artifact after the genre, not after Dāsyai.
- **Era events.**
  - 1311 and 1323 raids, and the image carried off (pp. 83–84).
  - "Fifty-nine years" without worship in the 14th century (p. 84).
  - 1371: Vijayanagara restores the deity (p. 84). A good "the Lord comes home" event for the seat.
  - The Sultānī flavour only as "what the temple chronicle tells" (p. 83). Avoid her death-from-grief beat.
- **Holy-site text, b_srirangam (narrator voice).** "On an island in the Kaveri, the temple opens its northern gates for ten nights each Mārkaḻi, and for those nights, its people say, heaven touches earth." (pp. 115, 117–118).
- **Faith voice lines.**
  - "A quarter of the Veda is a girl speaking" (from p. 3).
  - "He wore it first" (garland).
  - "The temple lets a man sing the girl, and will not let the girl into the hall" (dissent).
- **Hostile line (orthodox priest).** "Women were not allowed to perform" is the temple's rule (ch. 8 n. 14). Use it as the priest's line, not the narrator's. Keep the menstrual-pollution reasoning in hostile mouths only.
- **Do not:**
  - present the seventh-night dressing as a medieval rite;
  - present the *araiyar* families, the Āṇṭāḷ temple or the Śrīvaiṣṇavas as the faith;
  - use the *devadāsī* "courtesan" reputation as flavour for the Garland's daughters (Narayanan's own speculation, and the colonial stigma Orr overturns).
