# Davis, "Crossed Texts, Crossed Sex" (2002), design reading

**Citation (verified against the downloaded PDF).** Stephen J. Davis, "Crossed Texts, Crossed Sex: Intertextuality and Gender in Early Christian Legends of Holy Women Disguised as Men," *Journal of Early Christian Studies* 10.1 (Spring 2002): 1–36. Johns Hopkins University Press. DOI 10.1353/earl.2002.0003. Read in full (pp. 1–36, with notes). Reading-list item 16.

**Relevance, candidly.** Every case here is a person assigned female who lives as a monk or is taken for a eunuch. The mod's constraint (trans men out of scope) means none of these saints can be a Sister or a playable precedent. The article's value is indirect: it shows what a Byzantine reader already associated with "eunuch", "woman" and "monk", and it supplies liturgical intertexts (Acts 8, Galatians 3.28, Joseph) that the faith and its neighbours can argue over. It contains no case of a person assigned male living as a woman.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **The corpus.** At least eleven Greek Lives of "transvestite" women saints were written in the late 5th–7th centuries: Anastasia (Anastasios), Apolinaria (Dorotheos), Athanasia, Eugenia (Eugenios), Euphrosyne (Smaragdus), Hilaria (Hilarion), Mary (Marinos), Matrona (Babylas), Pelagia (Pelagius), Susannah (John), Theodora (Theodoros) (p. 4). Patlagean lists twelve; two are later (Anna/Euphemianos, 9th c.; a second Mary, 11th–12th c.) (p. 4 n. 5).
- **Dating matters for the game window.** Via Patlagean: production "waned in the eighth century, but in the ninth, tenth, and eleventh centuries there was a revival of the genre", with more copying of the old Lives and new ones written (p. 8). That is 867–1066 exactly.
- **Method.** Davis rejects the fixed three-part plot (flight, disguise, discovery) of Anson and Patlagean as a structure imposed by interpreters (p. 14). The saint is "an intertextually constructed body" (p. 15), a "ragged patchwork" of five borrowed discourses: Thecla, Antony, eunuchs, Joseph and Potiphar's wife, and the fragmented female body (p. 15).
- **Eunuchs are built into the genre.** Eunuchs were "culturally constructed as a 'third gender'" (p. 21, citing Ringrose); Gregory of Nazianzus calls them "of dubious sex" (p. 22). Saints are routinely taken for eunuchs: Mary/Marinos because "she was beardless and of delicate voice"; Hilaria for lack of a beard; Pelagia known in Jerusalem as "Pelagius the eunuch"; Apolinaria admitted to Scetis as a supposed eunuch (p. 22). Others choose the cover: Euphrosyne enters as "a eunuch from the palace"; Matrona dresses "as a eunuch" (pp. 22–23).
- **Matrona's pierced ears.** Asked why both ears are pierced, "Babylas" says a former mistress adorned her "so that many of those who saw me said that I was a girl" (p. 23). Davis notes pierced ears were common eunuch adornment (p. 23 n. 61).
- **Eugenia as the Ethiopian eunuch.** Reading Thecla in a litter, attended by eunuchs, she is cast "intertextually in the role of the Ethiopian eunuch" of Acts 8 and becomes "eunuch for the kingdom of heaven" (pp. 23–24). Her own words: "being a woman by nature ... I became a man for a short time" (p. 24).
- **Joseph inverted.** The false-seduction charge (Eugenia, Susannah, Theodora) recasts the saint as Joseph, whose beauty in Jewish and Muslim commentary is feminine; Jerome and the Talmud make Potiphar desire him, and Sotah 13b has Gabriel castrate Potiphar (pp. 25–28). The false-paternity variant (Marinos, Theodora) has the saint cast out to raise the child.
- **Women's community re-collects the saint.** Matrona is "completely transformed into a man" (p. 29), yet her friends Eugenia and Susannah (names that echo two crossing saints) carry her to the monastery and shelter her child (p. 30); she later heads "a group of nuns who dressed in male monastic garb", and a nun of that house, Eulogia, first recorded her Life (p. 31 and n. 93). Davis cites Topping, "St. Matrona and Her Friends: Sisterhood in Byzantium" (p. 30 n. 89).
- **"I have sinned as a man."** Marinos's false confession is "double-voiced": "which gender identity ... is the real 'lie'?" (p. 32). The editors of *Holy Women of Byzantium* refused the translation "they shrieked like women" because "only women shriek anyway" (p. 33 and n. 99).
- **Christ and Galatians 3.28.** Christomimesis does not homogenise gender; read through "no longer male and female", the saint undermines "even the fundamental opposition of sexual division/nondivision itself" (p. 36).
- **Preservation.** The three Greek manuscripts of Marinos's Life survive only on Athos, which bars women (p. 3).

## 3. In-world loc lines
- "What hinders me?" (the candidate, after Acts 8.36)
- "They said of the eunuch that she was a girl. The monks wrote it down themselves." (a Dorleyaian preacher on Matrona)
- "No longer male and female, says the Apostle. The Mother says: daughter." (the main rite against the Galenic splinter)
- "Forgive me, father, for I have sinned as a man." (hostile cleric's sneer; the Sister's reply: "I have not done anything as a man for years.")
