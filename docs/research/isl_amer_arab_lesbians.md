## 0. Framing warning

- **This is about women's love for women, read through cis women.** It is not about trans women or *mukhannathūn*. Its value for the mod is threefold:
  1. how medieval Arabic texts named and judged love between women, which bears on a Sister who loves a woman;
  2. the court milieu of the refined ladies and the singing-girls;
  3. one Christian woman's monastery founded out of love.
- **Literary evidence.** Nearly all of it is erotic or literary writing, "phallocentric" and from "a male perspective", as Amer says (p. 221). The "lesbian-like" category is Bennett's frame (pp. 225–226) and Amer's proposal, not a medieval one.
- **Translations are secondhand.** Ibn Naṣr's *Encyclopedia of Pleasure* (*Jawāmiʿ al-ladhdha*, c. end of the 10th c.) is quoted from a 1977 English translation that Amer herself calls nearly untraceable (p. 234). Al-Tīfāshī is her own translation. Nothing was checked in Arabic.
- **Trans men are out of scope.** The *ghulāmiyyāt* (slave girls dressed as boys, pp. 226–227) and the cross-dressed warrior heroines (pp. 227–228) are recorded below as context only. Nothing is built on them.
- **Examination scene.** The "Galen examined his daughter" story (p. 216) is an examination narrative. It must not become an event (Cairo guardrails).

## 1. Findings

### 1.1 Words

- **The root.** *Saḥq* or *siḥāq* means lesbianism, and *sāḥiqa* (pl. *sāḥiqāt*) a lesbian. The root means "to pound" (as spices) or "to rub" (p. 216).
- **The vocabulary of the erotic writers** (p. 224):
  - *mutaẓarrifāt*, elegant courtly ladies, which Amer says was also used for lesbians (p. 230; her claim, unverified);
  - *nisāʾ mudhakkarāt*, "masculinized women";
  - *rijāl muʾannathīn*, "feminized men";
  - *mukhannath*, "male effeminate".
- **No word for the norm.** There was "no medieval Arabic word" for heterosexuality "or even for sexuality". *Jins* took the sense "sex/sexuality" only in the 20th century (p. 224).

### 1.2 Medical theories (via Ibn Naṣr, c. 1000; secondhand)

- **Al-Kindī (9th c.):** a "vapor" makes heat and itch in the labia, which only friction with another woman cools (pp. 216–217). Amer doubts the attribution, because no al-Kindī title fits (n. 4).
- **Yūḥannā b. Māsawayh (d. 857):** a nursing mother who eats celery, rocket, melilot or bitter-orange flowers passes on an itch the child carries "through her future life" (p. 217). Amer: here the condition is "innate and lifelong", and such views were "standard" (p. 217).
- **Galen's daughter.** This is not in Galen's surviving Greek (n. 3).

### 1.3 Hind and al-Zarqāʾ: the founding love story (p. 218, via Ibn Naṣr; earlier in the *Aghānī*)

- **The two women.** Hind bint al-Nuʿmān was the Christian daughter of the last Lakhmid king of al-Ḥīra (7th c.). She loved Hind bint al-Khuss of al-Yamāma, called al-Zarqāʾ, "reportedly the first lesbian in Arab history" (p. 218).
- **The monastery.** When al-Zarqāʾ died, Hind "cropped her hair, wore black clothes" and vowed an ascetic life. "She built a monastery which was named after her on the outskirts of Kufa", and she was buried at its gate (p. 218).
- **Sources.** The *Aghānī* (2:31–32) already has the story. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī repeats it (c. 1000) (n. 8).
- **Ibn Naṣr's use.** He praises it as proof that women are more loyal to women than men are to women. He quotes a poet: "O Hind, you are truer to your word than men" (p. 219).
- **A legend, but a lasting one.** Amer: its truth cannot be known, but its long circulation shows that love between women was thought "far more than a medical condition" (p. 219).

### 1.4 Named couples and lost books

- **Twelve books in the *Fihrist*.** Al-Nadīm (d. c. 995) lists twelve books named after women couples. Nothing else of them survives (p. 219). The titles include:
  - *Rīḥāna and Qaranful* ("Basil and Clove");
  - *Sakīna and al-Rabāb*;
  - *ʿAbda the Wise and ʿAbda the Treacherous*;
  - *Luʾluʾa and Shāṭira*;
  - *Hind and Bint al-Nuʿmān* (p. 219).
- **Lost earlier works** (n. 7, p. 218):
  - a legal opinion ascribed to Makḥūl (d. c. 733), "Women's tribadism constitutes fornication between them";
  - Abū l-ʿAnbas al-Ṣaymarī's *Kitāb al-saḥḥāqāt*, late 9th c.
- **Later material.**
  - Al-Tīfāshī (13th c., Tunisia and Egypt) describes a lesbian "community" with a head, and women's teaching on love-making (p. 220). This is after 1066.
  - Leo Africanus calls the women diviners of Fez *suḥāqiyāt* (p. 220).

### 1.5 Law (Amer's summary; secondary)

- ***Saḥq* was the lesser offence.** It was a sin, but "generally deemed to be a less serious offense than *liwāṭ*", and the least serious form of *zinā*, because no man penetrated (p. 223).
- **Penalties varied:**
  - one hundred lashes in some opinions;
  - a discretionary *taʿzīr* for Ibn Ḥazm of Córdoba (d. 1064);
  - none in others (p. 223).
- **Often not addressed at all.** "In most legal compendia of *fiqh* *saḥq* is not even regularly addressed" (p. 223).
- **Her school-by-school *liwāṭ* summary** (Mālikī stoning, Shāfiʿī by marital status, Ḥanafī *taʿzīr*, p. 223) is a textbook digest from secondary works (n. 21). The Cairo note's primary readings take precedence.

### 1.6 "Lesbian-like" spaces (pp. 225–232)

- ***Ghulāmiyyāt.*** Zubayda dressed slave girls as boys for al-Amīn, and the fashion spread through Baghdad (pp. 226–227, via al-Masʿūdī). Rowson warns against reading it as lesbian (n. 29). **Out of scope** (cis women in boys' dress for a man's gaze).
- **Warrior heroines in the folk epics.** In *Sīrat Dhāt al-Himma*, one heroine says her heart inclines "for the ladies" (p. 228, via Kruk).
  - **Inconsistent dating.** Amer says the *siyar* "survive from at least the early eleventh century" (p. 227), but her n. 31 says they were "mostly written down between the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries" (p. 228).
  - **Out of scope** as cross-dressed-woman material.
- **Sufi *naẓar*.** Gazing on a beautiful boy as a "witness" to God's beauty (11th c.) has no known parallel among women Sufis (p. 229). Amer only speculates that one existed.
- ***Ẓarf* (refinement).** Women held literary salons (p. 230). Ibn al-Washshāʾ (d. 936) describes refined men and women embroidering verses on "pillows, curtains, rings, shoes, belts" and clothing, in gold and precious stones (p. 230).
- **Wallāda** (d. 1087 or 1091), daughter of the caliph al-Mustakfī of Córdoba.
  - She kept a salon and is said to have had two male lovers and one female, Muhja (p. 231).
  - Two verses were embroidered on her robe, among them "By God, I am fit for greatness, and stride along with great pride" (p. 231).
  - **Rowson says the evidence is insufficient** to call her a lesbian (n. 37). **She is alive in 1066.**
- **Singing-girls (*qiyān*).** Enslaved singers were "freer to express" themselves than veiled free women. Some ran singing schools (p. 232). ʿInān, Faḍl and ʿArīb were called *mutaẓarrifāt*. None is "explicitly referred to as lesbian (*sāḥiqa*)" (p. 232).

### 1.7 Modern access (pp. 233–235)

- **Censorship in the Arab world.** The Arabic edition of Ibn Naṣr has its press name and date blacked out, and lacks its same-sex chapters (pp. 233–234).
- **Excision in the West.** The only English al-Tīfāshī, translated from French, cuts the lesbian chapter (p. 235).
- **Use:** background for the project's reading list only.

## 2. Cross-checks

- **The *muʾannath*.** Amer lists *rijāl muʾannathīn* as "feminized men" in the erotic writers' vocabulary (p. 224). This supports reading al-Qāḍī al-Fāḍil's *muʾannathūn* at the Fatimid Nawrūz as effeminates, not merely "entertainers" (`isl_moreh_live_theatre.md` row 1 and §4 item 7). The two agree.
- **Fatimid law has no *saḥq* rule in our notes.** The Cairo note §2.1 records only *Daʿāʾim* §§1597–1600, on men. Amer's general claim that *saḥq* is often not addressed (p. 223) fits that silence, but she cites no Ismaʿili text. **Unchecked for the *Daʿāʾim*.**
- **Leo's Fez diviners.** `cmp_davis_trickster_travels.md` has Leo call them *suḥāqiyāt* ("sahacat"). Amer (p. 220, via Davis p. 201) agrees.
- **Wallāda.** `later_starts/region_islamic-world.md` mentions her salon with *unverified* page cites. Amer gives her death as 1087 or 1091 and cites Ibn Bassām (d. 1147), *Dhakhīra* 1:332–335, and al-Maqqarī (n. 38). This is a source trail, still secondhand.
- **Singing-girls are enslaved.** The Cairo note §3.2 and `isl_kapitaikin_palatina_dancers.md` §0 already require the loc to keep the singing-girls' slavery in view. Amer's "freer to express themselves" (p. 232) must not soften that.
- **"Born so" medicine.** Ibn Māsawayh's innate-and-lifelong theory (p. 217) is the women's counterpart of al-Sarakhsī's "soft by birth" (Baghdad note §1.1, event 4). In both, "born so" is a doctor's or jurist's frame. Under the Cairo and Noralla rule (`isl_noralla_egypt_trans_law.md` §2 rule 11), it is a tactic, never the narrator's truth.
