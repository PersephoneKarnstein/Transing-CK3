# Research dossier: *Exploring Gender Diversity in the Ancient World* (eds. Allison Surtees & Jennifer Dyer, Edinburgh UP 2020)

Source file: `scratchpad/books/surtees_dunn_gender_diversity_ancient.txt` (12,180 lines; print pagination preserved via `6254_Surtees & Dyer.indd NN` footers; page numbers cited below are the print pages shown in those footers).

Purpose: raw material for the fictional CK3 faith/culture "Transbian Maximalism" (galli-modelled ritual castration, castrated men regarded culturally as women, adoptive matrilineal inheritance, trans-woman-centred priesthood, trait-based "transgender" mechanic). The brief is a *veneer of non-anachronism*: everything below is tagged by chapter so that each tenet can be traced to an ancient source the book actually cites. Where the book says something that cuts against the mod's premises, that is recorded too (Section 4).

**Important caveat up front (from the Introduction, p. 20):** the editors say "One important area of study largely omitted from this volume is that of cult practice (while Adkins does address religion broadly, she does not explicitly examine cult practice). Religious rituals and practice provide particularly rich sources for the study of gender variance and would undoubtably yield valuable discussion." So this book is NOT a galli monograph; the galli material lives mostly in Adkins (ch. 11, Apuleius' Syrian-goddess priests) plus scattered references. The galli dossier (Section 2) is therefore exhaustive of *this book* but would need supplementing (Roller 1999, Latham 2012, Beard 1994) for a full picture.

## Appendix A. Chapter-by-chapter extracts (working notes, written while reading; Sections 2–6 synthesise these)

### A.0 Introduction (Surtees & Dyer, pp. 1–25)
- Athena: "Athena takes on both a man's gender role and a masculine gender expression, despite being assigned female at her birth" (p. 9); "While Athena identifies as male, Dionysus vacillates between male and female gender performances and roles" (p. 10); "Athena remains a virgin goddess, without romantic attachments or interest in domestic activities (save weaving)" (p. 9).
- Dionysus: "he dresses in a long chiton, which has been interpreted as a female garment" (p. 10); "the twice-born god, having been torn from his dead mother's womb to be born again from the immortal thigh of Zeus. Further, initiates to the mystery cult of Dionysus are able to find new life through death" (p. 11); "Dionysus is his own maternal trace" (p. 11); "Dionysus . . . has only one consort, Ariadne" (p. 12).
- Maenadic rites as women's space: "In order for men to participate in these rites, they must also become or at least present as women. Cadmus . . . and the seer Tiresias (himself a figure who switches gender . . .) accept the worship of Dionysus and accordingly dress as women for the rituals. But when King Pentheus rejects Dionysus . . . He convinces Pentheus to dress as a woman in order to spy on the women's secret worship. But while Pentheus appears to be a woman, his male identity is seen by the women, who . . . tear him literally limb from limb as punishment for his attempted deception and male intrusion into female space. His transformation was not complete." (p. 12) **[Useful: an ancient model of a women's rite that admits men only on condition of becoming women, and punishes false/incomplete transition with death.]**
- Priapus/phallus (Barnett 2012): "phallic imagery was not limited to male bodies"; "It had less to do with what was between one's legs than it did with who was in a position of power in Roman society"; "gender roles were more attached to class in ancient Rome than to sex or sexuality" (p. 14).
- Divine vs mortal intersex (Weismantel 2012; Ajootian 1995): "while the divine dual-sexed body was worshipped, non-conforming mortal bodies were not necessarily desirable and were at times treated with violence"; "two people identified as women but with non-conforming bodies who were reportedly burned alive in the early first century bc, and another of a series of 'androgynous' births in the mid–late Republic seen as sinister portents, drowned at sea as an act of expiation" (p. 15). "there was no connection between the divinity and the human 'monsters' (monstra), before Ovid's account" (p. 18).
- Kinaidoi/cinaedi (Voss 2008): "their identity was less determined by their sexual acts than by their gender liminality" (p. 17).
- Translation policy: *androgynos* = "androgynous", never "hermaphrodite" (p. 19). Pronouns: "Adkins uses feminine pronouns when she discusses the priests of Lucius' narration because they identify as 'girls', even though Lucius himself uses derogative terms and masculine pronouns to describe them" (p. 19). Groves (2016: 350) on grammatical gender: "the words used to describe the god and intersex people in both Greek and Latin (ἀνδρόγυνος, androgynus, semimas) are unfailingly masculine in gender . . . just as a group of mixed gender defaults to the masculine in Greek and Latin, so an individual of mixed gender does as well" (p. 19). Editors use "ze/hir" for Hermaphroditus (p. 20).
- Scope limit: cult practice "largely omitted" (p. 20) — see caveat at top.

### A.1 Penrose, ch. 1 "Gender Diversity in Classical Greek Thought" (pp. 29–42)
- Frame: Feinberg's *Transgender Warriors*; Greek gender gauged by "intelligence and courageousness" as well as dress/physiognomy (p. 29). "the Scythians, who . . . refreshingly seem to have revered gender-diverse individuals as shaman-type religious figures" (p. 29).
- Xenophon *Oec.* 7.25: Zeus "measured out a greater portion of fear to her than to the man" (p. 30); 10.1 "By Hera, Ischomachus, you demonstrate what a masculine intelligence your wife has" (p. 31). Plato *Rep.* 5: men and women can have the same virtues incl. courage; Aristotle disagrees (*Pol.* 1260a; *Poet.* 1454a) (p. 30).
- Clytemnestra *androboulos* "manly-minded" (*Ag.* 11); Antigone "called a man by Creon (484–5)" (pp. 31–2). Artemisia I fought at Salamis "due to her andreia (masculinity or manly courage) and lēmatos (daring)" (Hdt 7.99.1) (p. 32). Parthenos as *adamatos* "untamed" (p. 31).
- **Hippocratic *On Regimen* 1.28–9 (c. 400 BC) two-seed theory** (p. 33): both parents emit male or female seed; combos yield "most feminine and graceful" girl; bolder girl; "more audacious (tolmērotera)" girl "called masculine (andreiai)"; "brilliant with respect to the soul and strong-bodied" boy; "less brilliant" but "manly (andreios)" boy; and "'If male seed is emitted from the woman, and female from the man, and the male seed wins the battle . . . These become androgynoi and are rightly called so' (1.28.4)". "The term androgynos here has generally been understood to refer to a feminine man rather than an intersex individual" (p. 33). **[Ancient "natural" theory of a third category of male-bodied feminine person, produced by the mother's seed winning — a possible in-world rationale for a randomly assigned trait.]**
- Intersex infants: "Greek children who were intersex were probably exposed, as the ancient Greeks perceived such infants to be ominous prodigies of ill-fate to come"; "In at least one case, an intersex infant was burned alive at Athens (Diod. Sic. 32.12.2)"; Garland "suggests that the practice of burning intersex children was Roman, not Greek" (pp. 33–4). Brisson: "By the Roman imperial period . . . more tolerance towards intersex individuals developed" (p. 34).
- Cowardice = femininity: Aegisthus "called a 'woman' (gynē) and accused of 'having a cowardly soul' (psychē kakē)" (*Ag.* 1625–7) (p. 34); Spartan coward "could be forced to shave half of his beard (Plut. Ages. 30.3)" (p. 34); Eupolis' play *Astreteutoi* alternatively titled *Androgynoi* (p. 35); *Clouds* 680 Cleōnymus written as "Cleōnymē" with feminine ending (p. 35); Plato *Tim.* 90e–91a "the cowardly man who lives a bad life will be reincarnated in the second generation as a woman" (p. 35).
- *Kinaidos*: Aeschines 2.151 "ten thousand hoplites like Philon . . . or thirty-thousand kinaidoi like you?" (p. 35). Ps.-Aristotle *Physiognomics* 808a signs of the kinaidos: "weak eyes, but also knocked knees, a tilted head, limp wrists, his gait (either wagging his hips or else holding them still), and finally his roaming eyes" (p. 36). Etymologies: "kinein ta aidoia (to move one's shameful parts) or kenos aidous (empty of shame)" (p. 36). Agathon in *Thesmophoriazusai* 111–60 "dresses as a woman in order to feel the roles of the women characters"; "Euripides, who does drag very badly and is called a gynnis, a 'feminine man', Agathon is called a gynē or 'woman'" (p. 37). Demosthenes "accused of wearing feminine clothing, particularly undergarments" (Aeschines 1.131) (p. 37). Sapsford 2015: Hellenistic Egyptian inscriptions where "kinaidoi self-identify. In Egypt, it would appear that being a kinaidos was an occupation that involved singing, dancing or entertaining" (p. 37). **[Self-identified kinaidoi as an entertainers' guild — a precedent for a recognised queer occupational class.]**
- **Scythian Enarees / Anarieis** (pp. 37–40): "Herodotus asserts that androgynoi are to be found among the Scythians, who call them Enarees (4.67.2)". "The Enarees were androgynous shaman-type priestesses who took on a role similar to that of two-spirits in North America". Herodotus 1.105.4: femininity is "a female disease that had been cast down upon them by the goddess Aphrodite after they pillaged her temple at Askalon". Hippocratic *Airs, Waters, Places* 22.1: "'Most of the Scythians become impotent [eunouchoi] . . . and are called Anarieis'"; Penrose translates eunouchoi as "impotent" since the cause given is horse-riding, moistness, vein-cutting behind ears — "The author lists neither castration nor intersexuality as reasons". "The Anarieis 'speak like women' (22.1), wear women's garments, act like women, and work with other women doing women's work (22.1, 7)." "these individuals are revered by others in Scythian society, who prostrate themselves before the Anarieis and attribute the cause of their femininity to divine ordinance"; "Herodotus (4.67.2) notes that the Enarees said that Aphrodite endowed them with the gift of prophecy" (p. 38). Chukchi shaman parallel (Bogoras). "Scythian society created a safe space for transgender and/or intersex individuals that ancient Greek society of the classical period did not" (p. 38).
- **Galli mention in Penrose:** "Gender-queer eunuch priestesses of the goddess Cybele do appear in a Greek context in Apuleius' second-century ce The Golden Ass (8.24–30; see Adkins in this volume). They wander from town to town, seemingly without a home, and Apuleius writes of them in a pejorative manner." (p. 38)
- Burials: possible Enaree at Tilya Tepe (Afghanistan), "male to female, not intersex" (Davis-Kimball); Siberian permafrost burial with weapons + fertility amulets, "XY chromosomes . . . intersex, a male-to-female transgender woman, or otherwise 'gender non-binary'. The grave goods suggest that the remains are of an elite warrior" (p. 39).
- **Mare's-urine HRT hypothesis:** "the Enarees may have been transgender women who drank mare's urine to help them transition from male to female. Pregnant mare's urine contains high levels of oestregen and is still used today to make a hormone replacement therapy drug called premarin . . . Thus the Enarees may have transitioned to womanhood by drinking an extract of pregnant mare's urine" (Taylor 1996) (pp. 39–40). **[Flavour gold for a steppe-adjacent CK3 culture: a non-surgical "potion" transition path.]**
- Persian/Babylonian: Ctesias — satrap Nanarus "lived in luxury wearing feminine clothing, shaving himself and using cosmetics, but nonetheless took offense to being called an androgynos" (p. 40). Carlà-Uhink: Greeks/Romans held the "'widespread idea that cross-dressing' was 'practiced by other barbarian cultures'" (p. 40). Greek cross-dressing "often described or portrayed in a ritual context, or on the stage . . . rather than as part of an ongoing, transgender-type identity" (p. 40).
- Conclusion: "The Greeks understood male femininity as a defect of character or 'illness' of sorts of the soul . . . In ancient Greek sources, we see early vestiges of the Western medicalisation of intersexuality and transgender identification as diseases" (p. 41). "the Scythians not only tolerated, but celebrated sex/gender diversity in a way that the Greeks did not" (p. 40).

### A.2 Sukava, ch. 2 "Blending Bodies in Classical Greek Medicine" (pp. 43–53)
- Laqueur's "one-sex body" critiqued by King 2013: "both one-sexed and dual-sexed concepts of the body have existed concurrently since antiquity" (p. 43). Laqueur relied on "the relatively late physician Galen (second century ad)".
- Humoral physiology over anatomy: "They envisioned the body primarily as a receptacle for fluids"; "an emphasis on bodily fluids in medical theories encouraged physicians to imagine sex, and along with it gender, as a process of blending" (p. 43). *Anthrōpos* "encompasses both sexes" (p. 44). *On the Nature of Man*: four humours "are the essence of" the body; "there is virtually no trace of a sexualised body" (pp. 45–6).
- King 1998: "In Hippocratic gynaecology, to be a woman is to menstruate" (p. 47). *Diseases of Women* 1.26: female body "araiosarkoterē (more spongy) and apaloterē (more soft)" — "the distinction here is one of quality, not of kind" (p. 47). *Places in Man* 46: "'The so-called women's sicknesses: [their] wombs are the cause of all of these sicknesses'" (p. 48).
- *On Regimen* 1.27: sex of children "can therefore be altered prior to conception by both the male's and the female's intake of food and drink"; "An increase of the 'fire' element within a parent promotes a male child; that of 'water' promotes a female" (p. 49). 1.28: "both the female (thēlu) and the male (arsen) can be fused together (synistasthai . . . pros allēla), since defining elements of both sexes are contained within each" (p. 49). Hanson 1992: outcomes "'masculine boy' and the 'feminine girl', or in the 'wimpy boy' and the 'manly girl'"; "this spectrum of potential outcomes allowed for medical intervention to prevent and correct aberrant outcomes" (p. 51).
- Phaëthousa of Abdera (*Epid.* 6.8.32): stopped menstruating, "her body became masculine (to te sōma ēndrōthē): she became hairy and grew a beard, and her voice became rough"; Nanno of Thasos similar; King: "she still must have been considered a woman by physicians because she possessed a womb and had menstruated" (p. 51).
- Plato *Rep.* 5.451c ff.: "a female can possess a physician's soul (iatrikē psychē) just as a male can (454c–e)" (p. 52).
- Modern parallel: Oudshoorn 1994 on hormones; "both ancient and modern Western medical models of sexual spectrums are products of individual bodies' resistance to being pigeonholed" (p. 53).

### A.3 Uhlig, ch. 3 "Birth by Hammer: Pandora" (pp. 54–66) — first half
- Explicit trans-studies motivation: "The complex role of body modification, particularly through medical technologies, in the lives of many (though by no means all) trans individuals invites reflection on the way that categories such as 'natural' and 'artificial' . . . shape . . . our understanding of the way that sex and gender now function" (p. 55). Barad: "'Holding the category "human" fixed' . . . 'excludes an entire range of possibilities in advance'" (p. 55).
- Sophocles' satyr play *Pandora or Hammerers* (Πανδώρα ἢ Σφυροκόποι); fr. 482 "moulding clay (πηλὸν ὀργάζειν)" (p. 58). Jane Harrison's *Prolegomena* (1908): Pandora originally "a powerful chthonic goddess . . . born from the earth itself", displaced by a "'bourgeois, pessimistic'" Hesiodic refashioning — "the displacement of the more ancient (and genuine) matriarchal structure by a new (artificial) patriarchal order" (p. 57). *Anodos* vases: female figure rising from earth attended by hammer-wielding satyrs (Ferrara T 579) (pp. 57–8). Aeschylus fr. 369: "'mortal woman [born/created] from a seed of moulded clay' (ἐκ πηλοπλάστου σπέρματος θνητὴ γυνή)" (p. 59).
- Satyr costume *perizoma* "providing them with fabricated genitals and equine tail"; "a naked body that is at once more 'natural' and more 'artificial'" (p. 60). "If we take seriously the expanded definition of the body put forth by contemporary theorists, the fabricated objects that theatrical satyrs wield and wear are no less a part of their somatic identity" (p. 61).
- Hammer-births: Helen from the egg (Apulian krater, Bari 3899); Athena from Zeus's head freed by Hephaestus' hammer/axe (Pindar *O.* 7.36 "χαλκέλατος πέλεκυς"); "the new-born Athena is marked as a craft product, fully clad in the metal armour . . . that signals not only the goddess's martial nature but her unsettling masculine qualities"; Aeschylus *Eum.* 737 "τὸ δ' ἄρσεν αἰνῶ πάντα" (she sides with the masculine in all things) (pp. 62–4). Pandora's birth carved on the base of Pheidias' Athena Parthenos (Paus. 1.24.7) (p. 64).
- **[Relevance: "womanhood as made, not born" — a first woman who is literally manufactured by craftsmen's hammers; Harrison's old matriarchal-substrate theory is itself a historical ideology the mod can mimic as in-world doctrine.]**

### A.3 (cont.) Uhlig, ch. 3 — second half (pp. 65–6)
- Parthenon: Pandora on the Parthenos base as "a 'visual articulation of the male-female antithesis', a reminder that the city of Athens, like Athena herself, was defined by the subjugation and exclusion of women (Hurwit 1995: 185)"; "Pandora stands as a correlate to the Amazons so conspicuously subdued on the temple's west metopes" (p. 65). Alternative: "a celebration of Athena Ergane, an artificially crafted mortal body replicating the corporeal artifice of Athena herself" (p. 65).
- Key thesis line: "a body whose artificiality is celebrated as a source of power, whose construction through tools is not a sign of dependence but a deliberate means of bringing a new form into the light" (p. 65). "we could redeem the artificial woman of his accounts, reclaiming the beauty so demonised there as something truly and authentically valuable, not despite but because of its fabricated nature" (p. 66). Erichthonius born from the earth, "the skilled hands of Athena, the mother who gave him form, aid his passage" (p. 65) — **non-gestational motherhood of an earth-born child; adoptive-style maternity**. Prometheus "the creation of both men and women from clay" (Vernant); Ovid *Met.* 1.79–83 (p. 66). Aristotle *GA* 789b8–12 "many things are worked by breath, as by a tool (hōs organō); . . . like the hammer and anvil in metalwork" (p. 66).

### A.4 Shannon-Henderson, ch. 4 "Life after Transition: Spontaneous Sex Change" (pp. 67–78)
- Scope: "reports of girls and women who spontaneously acquire anatomical markers of maleness, not in myth but in real life" (p. 67); intersex births as prodigies: "Livy 27.11.4–5, 27.37.5–7, 31.12.6–10; Julius Obsequens 46, 81, 92, 94, 96, 107, 108, 110"; "the birth of intersex children inspired fear in a Roman population that viewed them as signs of severe divine displeasure" (p. 67). Brisson's terms: intersex = "'simultaneous dual sexuality'"; sex change = "'successive dual sexuality'" (p. 67).
- Thesis: stories "broadly confirm rather than challenge three widespread ancient ideas: (1) that there are two distinct sexes, male and female; (2) that a person must belong to one sex or the other; and (3) that of those two sexes, the male is definitively superior" (p. 68).
- *Epidemics* 6.8.32 Phaëthousa quoted in full: "her body became manly (ἠνδρώθη) and grew hairy all over, she grew a beard, her voice became harsh . . . she later died"; Nanno: "one hope of becoming feminine (γυναικωθῆναι): if normal menstruation occurred" (p. 68). "both she and Nanno remain grammatically feminine in the text (βιώσασα, ταύτῃ)" (p. 69). "The boundary between male and female cannot be crossed, and even attempts to approach it are deadly" (p. 69).
- Pliny *HN* 7.36 "Arescon, who had been called Arescusa and had actually gotten married, but . . . eventually a beard and a penis came forth (barbam et virilitatem provenisse) and he took a wife" (p. 70). Phlegon *Mir.* 6–9: Philotis AD 53 "became a man when male genitals appeared on her" (μορίων αὐτῇ προφανέντων ἀρρενικῶν ἀνὴρ ἐγένετο); Aitete→Aitetos AD 116; Antioch girl AD 45 "suddenly male parts burst forth on her and the girl became a man" (p. 70). "women who spontaneously grow penises are not intersex, but male" (p. 71).
- Diodorus 32.10–12: Heraïs (145 BC) — "There burst forth from Heraïs' female parts . . . a male organ with testicles attached"; doctors "had to make an incision and cicatrise the perforated area, and after putting the man's organ in good order (τὴν . . . ἀνδρὸς φύσιν εὔκοσμον ποιήσαντας)" (32.10.7) (p. 71). Grammatical gender changes only with the name: "Heraïs, changing her name to Diophantus . . . enlisted in the cavalry" (32.10.8) (p. 72). **Kallo (115 BC)**: "Kallo's tumor must be cut by an 'apothecary' (φαρμακοπώλης), who also operates on Kallo's new penis to perforate it and performs catheterisation" (p. 72–3). **[Both are ancient *surgical* sex-confirmation precedents, albeit F→M.]**
- Social transition: name changes (Heraïs→Diophantus, her father's name; Kallo→Kallon; Arescusa→Arescon; Sympherousa→Sympheron); "Heraïs exchanges her female clothing for masculine dress (32.10.7) and joins the military"; "Kallo similarly adopts male costume, gives up wool-working, and discards her weaving equipment (32.11.4)"; Sympheron "works as a gardener . . . since women's menstrual blood was thought to harm plants" (p. 73).

### A.11 Adkins, ch. 11 "The Politics of Transgender Representation in Apuleius' *Golden Ass* and *Loukios, or the Ass*" (pp. 157–68) — **THE GALLI CHAPTER, transcribed closely**
- Opening (p. 157): "Lucius is sold to a group of priests of the Syrian Goddess. Like the priests of Cybele with whom they are frequently linked, these figures are infamous in Greek and Latin literature for ecstatic music and dance, transvestism, self-castration, and suspect sexuality. In both the Greek and Latin novels, the first-person narrator Lucius calls them cinaedi, Graeco-Roman scare figures of gender and sexual deviance. The priests, however, never use this derogatory term, instead calling themselves 'girls' (puellae: Met. 8.26; korasia: Onos 36) and using feminine grammatical forms. In their own words, they construct feminine identities, adding to the evidence that some of the followers of Cybele and the Syrian Goddess – commonly referred to as galli – were transwomen and other assigned-male-at-birth individuals with diverse gender identities and sexual orientations (Roscoe 1996; Taylor 1997: 336, 371; Adkins 2014: 35–55; Blood 2015; Blood forthcoming; Carlà-Uhink 2017: 16–19)."
- Thesis: "the primary locus of the priests' contested identities is their speech" (p. 157). Both novels derive from the lost *Metamorphoseis* of Lucius of Patrae (Photius *Bibl.* 129) (p. 158).
- **Onos 36 / Met. 8.26 "girls":** "'O girls, I have purchased for you a slave, beautiful and strong and of the Cappodocian race.'" (Ὦ κοράσια, δοῦλον ὑμῖν ἐώνημαι); Latin "'Puellae, servum vobis pulchellum en ecce mercata perduxi'" (p. 158). "This is supported by other literary texts that apply feminine grammatical forms to galli (Catull. 63; Hephaestion 12.3; Stat. Theb. 12.226; Anth. Pal. 7.223). Thus the priests' uses of feminine grammatical forms in the novels are not authorial invention, but most likely represent the real speech of some, if not all, Greek and Roman galli." (p. 158) **[Core citation for in-world feminine self-reference.]**
- **Cinaedus stereotype** (pp. 158–9): "kinaidos/cinaedus seems to have referred originally to a man who performed a style of dance associated with the Near East"; "cinaedi are portrayed as men who wear make-up and long, brightly coloured clothes, curl their hair, depilate their legs, walk with a shorter or swaying stride and speak in high-pitched voices"; "The cinaedus serves as a scare figure, a symbol of failed masculinity".
- **Galli defined** (p. 159): "Galli is the Greek and Roman term applied to some of the followers or priests of several cults of Anatolian mother goddesses, including Atargatis (the Syrian Goddess) and the more famous Phrygian goddess, called Meter in Greece, Magna Mater in Rome and Cybele in Greek and Latin literature (see especially Roscoe 1996; Roller 1999; Lightfoot 2003: 1–85). Their cults were quite different in Greece and Rome than in Anatolia, especially in the perceived nature of the galli. Though the origins of the term are debated, it appears first in the Hellenistic period and likely derives from the Greek name for Gauls who settled in Asia Minor in the third century bc . . . For Greeks and Romans, 'gallus' thus evoked a sense of uncivilised alterity (Roller 1999: 187–234)."
- **Castration chronology** (pp. 159–60): "The Greeks and especially the Romans, who conceived of masculinity as phallocentric and physically inviolable (Walters 1997), became increasingly obsessed with the concept of the gallus as an effeminate easterner: in literary contexts, galli are frequently described as eunuchs. While the Greeks and Romans attributed voluntary castration to the eastern origins of these cults, there is limited evidence for it in the pre-Hellenistic Near East (Roller 1998, 1999: 105, 253–6). Our earliest references to eunuch followers of Cybele appear in Hellenistic Greek literature and Roman literature of the late second/early first century bc, over a hundred years after the goddess's induction into Rome (Roller 1999: 263–326; Latham 2012). Our first evidence for the worship of Cybele's consort Attis, often portrayed as the mythological model for castration, also appears in the Hellenistic period (Roller 1999: 177–81). Thus, as Lynn Roller (1999: 318–19) has shown, the aspect of Cybele's cult that Greek and Roman authors found the most foreign and objectionable – the galli, who might perform femininity and/or castration – was a development of her Greek and Roman cults rather than an importation of Near Eastern practice." **[Important for "ancient roots" claim: castrated galli are a Hellenistic/Roman phenomenon (3rd–1st c. BC onward), not Bronze-Age Anatolian.]**
- "Despite the ancient and modern fascination with the image of the castrated gallus, we do not know how widespread this practice actually was or what it meant to those who performed it. The myths related by outsiders to explain castration and the feminine dress of the galli depict these as a result of madness (Catull. 63), divine punishment (cf. Roller 1999: 239–41) or the avoidance of sexual wrongdoing." (p. 160)
- **Lucian *De Dea Syria* 19–27, Combabos myth** (p. 160): Combabos "castrates himself in case of future allegations that he has slept with the queen and leaves the evidence behind in a sealed box"; later "reveals as proofs of his innocence both the contents of the box and his own body. ## 2. The galli/gallae dossier (everything this book says)

Scope note: the only sustained treatment is Adkins ch. 11 (pp. 157–68), which concerns Apuleius' and the *Onos*' priests of the **Syrian Goddess** (Atargatis), "frequently linked" to the priests of Cybele and read by Adkins as galli "though the term itself is never used" (p. 164). Everything else is incidental. The book contains **nothing** on the *dies sanguinis*, taurobolium, Megalesia, Hilaria, the Roman citizen ban on becoming a gallus, or late-antique Christian polemic; those will have to come from Roller 1999, Latham 2012, Beard 1994, Hales 2002 (all cited in the bibliography).

### 2.1 Who they were: definition, etymology, geography
- "Galli is the Greek and Roman term applied to some of the followers or priests of several cults of Anatolian mother goddesses, including Atargatis (the Syrian Goddess) and the more famous Phrygian goddess, called Meter in Greece, Magna Mater in Rome and Cybele in Greek and Latin literature" (Adkins, p. 159).
- "Their cults were quite different in Greece and Rome than in Anatolia, especially in the perceived nature of the galli. Though the origins of the term are debated, it appears first in the Hellenistic period and likely derives from the Greek name for Gauls who settled in Asia Minor in the third century bc . . . For Greeks and Romans, 'gallus' thus evoked a sense of uncivilised alterity (Roller 1999: 187–234). The followers of the Anatolian goddesses also inherited long-standing Greek and Roman depictions of 'easterners' as soft, decadent, savage and effeminate" (Adkins, p. 159).
- Penrose's one-paragraph summary: "Gender-queer eunuch priestesses of the goddess Cybele do appear in a Greek context in Apuleius' second-century ce The Golden Ass (8.24–30; see Adkins in this volume). They wander from town to town, seemingly without a home, and Apuleius writes of them in a pejorative manner." (Penrose, p. 38)
- "Like the priests of Cybele with whom they are frequently linked, these figures are infamous in Greek and Latin literature for ecstatic music and dance, transvestism, self-castration, and suspect sexuality." (Adkins, p. 157)

### 2.2 Castration: chronology, extent, meaning
- **Chronology (critical for the "ancient roots" claim):** "in literary contexts, galli are frequently described as eunuchs. While the Greeks and Romans attributed voluntary castration to the eastern origins of these cults, there is limited evidence for it in the pre-Hellenistic Near East (Roller 1998, 1999: 105, 253–6). Our earliest references to eunuch followers of Cybele appear in Hellenistic Greek literature and Roman literature of the late second/early first century bc, over a hundred years after the goddess's induction into Rome (Roller 1999: 263–326; Latham 2012). Our first evidence for the worship of Cybele's consort Attis, often portrayed as the mythological model for castration, also appears in the Hellenistic period (Roller 1999: 177–81). Thus, as Lynn Roller (1999: 318–19) has shown, the aspect of Cybele's cult that Greek and Roman authors found the most foreign and objectionable – the galli, who might perform femininity and/or castration – was a development of her Greek and Roman cults rather than an importation of Near Eastern practice." (Adkins, pp. 159–60)
- **Extent unknown:** "Despite the ancient and modern fascination with the image of the castrated gallus, we do not know how widespread this practice actually was or what it meant to those who performed it. The myths related by outsiders to explain castration and the feminine dress of the galli depict these as a result of madness (Catull. 63), divine punishment (cf. Roller 1999: 239–41) or the avoidance of sexual wrongdoing." (Adkins, p. 160)
- **Optional, not mandatory:** "castration is depicted as a choice, not a requirement of the cult (DDS 42–3, 50–1)" (Adkins, p. 161).
- **Eunuch status = slavery:** gallus myths "attempt to explain why men would voluntarily become eunuchs – a state associated with slavery in Greece and Rome – and wear the clothing of women, who, even if free, had nowhere near the legal, social or economic power of men" (Adkins, p. 160).
- **Roman phallocentrism:** "The Greeks and especially the Romans, who conceived of masculinity as phallocentric and physically inviolable (Walters 1997), became increasingly obsessed with the concept of the gallus as an effeminate easterner" (Adkins, p. 159).
- **Attis' self-castration cry (Ovid):** "In Ovid's Fasti, Ovid's Attis uses the same expression after he breaks his vow of chastity. 'Ah! Perish the parts that harmed me!' he cries (a! pereant partes, quae nocuere mihi! 4.240), before castrating himself." (Begum-Lees, p. 112) — note the frame: Attis castrates himself *after breaking a vow of chastity* to the goddess.
- **Catullus 63:** cited twice, never quoted: for applying "feminine grammatical forms to galli" (Adkins, p. 158) and for the manuscript oscillation of masculine/feminine endings, "see Strauss Clay 1995: 146–8 for a similar treatment of Attis in Catullus 63" (Begum-Lees, p. 108); and as depicting castration as "madness" (Adkins, p. 160).
- **Castration as sex-change, explicitly distinguished from spontaneous change:** "Our authors do not include stories of men becoming effeminised, for example by castration, a situation fundamentally different from spontaneous sex change (Flemming 2000: 152 n. 54)" (Shannon-Henderson, p. 74).
- **Elagabalus and castration:** "Dio states (80[79].11.1) that Elagabalus pondered having himself castrated, not for religious reasons (as castration was traditionally associated with some eastern cults), but because of his feminine nature. Later in his history Dio provides a similar statement, describing how Elagabalus asked the doctors to contrive a woman's vagina in his body (80[79].16.7)." (Rantala, p. 125) Dio 80[79].11 on "animals . . . fed in the temple with cut genitals . . . obviously points to eunuchs – a group indeed often associated with easterners, such as the eunuch priests of eastern cults such as Cybele (cf. SHA Heliog. 7.1; see also Rauhala 2008)" (Rantala, p. 123).
- **Adkins' own gloss (modern):** "the DDS is describing gender transition, which may include changes in body, clothing and/or social role (for castration as an avenue for changing one's body to correspond with one's gender identity, see Chakrapani 2016; Blood forthcoming)" (p. 161).

### 2.3 The foundation myth: Combabos (Lucian, *De Dea Syria* 19–27)
- Combabos, "a servant of the Assyrian king who accompanies his queen Stratonike to Hierapolis", "castrates himself in case of future allegations that he has slept with the queen and leaves the evidence behind in a sealed box"; when accused he "reveals as proofs of his innocence both the contents of the box and his own body. He returns to Hierapolis and remains as a follower of Hera" (Adkins, p. 160).
- **DDS 27, the aetiology of the galli (quoted, p. 160):** "Each year many castrate and unman themselves (τάμνονται καὶ θηλύνονται) in the temple, whether to console Combabos or to do favour to Hera . . . No longer do they wear male clothing, but don female robes and do women's work (εἵματά τε γυναικεῖα φορέουσιν καὶ ἔργα γυναικῶν ἐπιτελέουσιν). As I heard, the reason for this too goes back to Combabos. What happened was that a foreign woman came to a festival and, seeing him handsome and still in male clothing, was smitten with a great passion; but when she learned he was not a whole man (ἀτελέα) she did away with herself. At that, saddened because he was so unlucky in love, Combabos donned female clothing lest another woman ever suffer the same fate. And this is the reason for the female clothing of the galli (ἥδε αἰτίη Γάλλοισι στολῆς θηλείης)."
- "Elsewhere in the treatise, however, Combabos has not only the clothing but also the 'shape' of a woman (morphē: DDS 26)" (Adkins, p. 161). Combabos' castration and clothing "are depicted as undesirable, self-imposed punishments for excessive masculinity, in this case being too attractive to women" (pp. 160–1).
- Note the verb θηλύνονται: literally "are made female / feminise themselves" — the ancient text itself words castration as *becoming female*, and pairs it with women's dress AND women's work (ἔργα γυναικῶν).

### 2.4 Dress, cosmetics, hair
- *Met.* 8.27 (quoted p. 164): "'On the next day, when each of them had put on multicoloured clothes and hideously beautified himself, smearing his face with caked-on foundation and outlining his eyes with paint, they set out, having put on their caps and saffron-coloured linen and silk dresses, some of them in white tunics decorated all over with flowing purple designs in the shape of lance-points, gathered up with a girdle, wearing yellow shoes on their feet.'" Latin key words: *mitellis et crocotis et carbasinis et bombycinis iniecti . . . pedes luteis induti calceis*. "These features, commonly ascribed to galli and often to cinaedi, are depicted in Roman art and literature as characteristic of the 'effeminate easterner', particularly the distinctive caps and tunics." (p. 164)
- Philebus: "bald, but with long, grizzled curls hanging down" (*Met.* 8.24, p. 163); Philebus in distress imagines being "compelled to dash about with my hair loose" (*crinibus solutis*, 8.25, p. 165).
- Material record: galli depicted "wearing the religious garb interpreted as feminine in our literary sources: robes, jewellery and curls or ribbons hanging from the head (Vermaseren 1997: nos 249–50, 466)" (p. 162). Archigalli "are depicted wearing trousers, tunics and caps resembling those of Attis" (p. 162). Soterides "is depicted in the relief above wearing feminine clothing" (p. 162). Funerary monuments show "a variety of gendered or gender-blended clothing (Hales 2002)" (p. 161).

### 2.5 Grammatical gender and self-naming
- "The priests, however, never use this derogatory term [cinaedi], instead calling themselves 'girls' (puellae: Met. 8.26; korasia: Onos 36) and using feminine grammatical forms. In their own words, they construct feminine identities" (p. 157).
- Philebus to the sisters: "'O girls, I have purchased for you a slave, beautiful and strong and of the Cappodocian race.'" (Ὦ κοράσια, δοῦλον ὑμῖν ἐώνημαι); "'Puellae, servum vobis pulchellum en ecce mercata perduxi'" (p. 158). Of himself: "'for me, poor girl (egoque misera)'" (8.25, pp. 164–5). The sisters call themselves "'your dovies' (nobis quoque tuis palumbulis)" (8.26, p. 165).
- "With the single exception of solus in the Latin above . . . the priests in both novels employ only feminine grammatical forms and concepts, using speech to construct a shared feminine gender identity." (p. 166)
- **Not authorial invention:** "This is supported by other literary texts that apply feminine grammatical forms to galli (Catull. 63; Hephaestion 12.3; Stat. Theb. 12.226; Anth. Pal. 7.223). Thus the priests' uses of feminine grammatical forms in the novels are not authorial invention, but most likely represent the real speech of some, if not all, Greek and Roman galli." (p. 158)
- Counter-case in stone: Soterides "refers to himself in the inscription using masculine grammatical forms" while depicted in feminine dress; archigalli "whose inscriptions use masculine names and grammatical forms" (p. 162). So the epigraphic record is masculine while the literary self-speech is feminine.
- Editorial policy follows the priests: "Adkins uses feminine pronouns when she discusses the priests of Lucius' narration because they identify as 'girls', even though Lucius himself uses derogative terms and masculine pronouns to describe them" (Intro, p. 19).

### 2.6 Were they regarded as women?
- By themselves: yes ("girls", feminine forms, "a community of transwomen", p. 168).
- By the Roman narrator: no — "Lucius reinterprets the speech of the priests not as an expression of femininity, but as proof of effeminacy (cf. Skinner 1997: 12 on 'feminine' vs. 'effeminate')"; "'But those girls were a chorus of cinaedi' (sed illae puellae chorus erat cinaedorum)"; "in the Onos, Lucius never refers to the priests in the feminine; in The Golden Ass, he does so only once and only for the sake of grammatical agreement" (p. 166). Lucius calls them "half-men (semiviris)" (8.28, p. 168).
- By Roman culture generally (eunuchs): "it has sometimes been claimed that eunuchs were considered to be 'half-men' with no manly qualities (Isaac 2004: 338). While this might be an exaggeration, ancient sources do point out that the presence of eunuchs upset Roman cultural notions of masculine identity in the western part of the empire and that, because of their castration, eunuchs were perpetually considered to be 'other' in the Roman west (Kuefler 2001: 31–2)" (Rantala, p. 123). "The ancient Mediterranean cultures recognised a number of penis-bearing corporealities which were nevertheless differently gendered than the normative man: certain eunuchs . . ." (Åshede, p. 86).
- Voice: "Latin authors describe the voices of women, eunuchs and cinaedi as 'thin' and 'broken', while the voices of men are 'sound' or 'whole' (Quint., Inst. 11.3.19–20, 11.3.32 . . .)" (p. 166) — eunuchs grouped acoustically with women.
- In the Syrian cult per Lucian: they "don female robes and do women's work" (DDS 27) and Combabos has "the 'shape' of a woman" (DDS 26) (pp. 160–1).
- **Verdict the book supports:** galli were a *third thing* in Roman eyes (not-men, "other", "half-men"), women in their own speech and in the Syrian cult's self-description, never legally or socially *women* in Rome. "Regarded culturally as women" is defensible only *inside the cult community* and in the East (Hierapolis), not in the Latin West.

### 2.7 Begging, alms, class, legal status
- *Onos* 35: "'For he was a kinaidos and an old man, one of those who carry the Syrian Goddess around into the villages and fields and force the goddess to beg.'" *Met.* 8.24: "'one of those vulgar dregs of the common people who, making the streets and towns ring with cymbals and castanets, carry the Syrian Goddess around and force her to beg'" (*unum de triviali popularium faece . . . deamque Syriam circumferentes mendicare compellunt*) (p. 163).
- "the ritual mendicancy described above (see also Dion. Hal. Ant. Rom. 2.19; Cic. Leg. 2.22, 2.40)" (p. 164).
- Payment for self-wounding: "Lucius depicts this religious ritual as a form of street entertainment: every time the priests cut themselves, they receive payment" (pp. 166–7).
- Legal/class marking by self-flagellation: "she also whips herself, subjecting her body to a physical punishment that marks her in the eyes of the Roman reader as belonging to the social classes legally subject to corporal punishment: slaves, the lower classes and infames, people such as sex workers, gladiators and actors who were legally disenfranchised" (p. 167). Elagabalus-as-sex-worker: sex workers "akin to actors, players and singers" in status (Rantala, p. 124).
- Homelessness/itinerancy: "They wander from town to town, seemingly without a home" (Penrose, p. 38).
- Property: the priests own a slave boy and purchase an ass (p. 158); the slave is their "'shared concubine (partiarius . . . concubinus)'" (p. 163) — shared property and shared partner.
- Fraud: priests "are also portrayed as habitual liars: see the deceptive prophecy at 9.8 and the accusation of theft at 9.10" (p. 167).

### 2.8 Rites
- Music: "the 'Phrygian' musical instruments, rhythms and melodies played during religious rituals (Met. 8.30; see also Lucr. 2.618–20; Ov. Fast. 4.181–4, 4.189–90, 4.193–214)"; "cymbals and castanets" (pp. 163–4).
- Ecstasy and self-cutting (*Onos* 37): music to "'incite a frenzy'", ecstatic dance, "cut their arms and tongues with swords, 'with the result that, in a moment, everything was filled with effeminate blood (malakou haimatos)'" (p. 166).
- Prophetic possession and public self-accusation (*Met.* 8.27): "'as if filled with the holy spirit of a divine power, pretended a fit of madness . . . Shouting prophetically, he begins to attack and accuse his own self with a fabricated lie, as if he had perpetrated something against the law of his holy religion, and moreover, he himself demands just punishment for his hateful crime from himself and by his own hands'" (*velut numinis divino spiritu repletus . . . vaticinatione clamosa*) (p. 167). Adkins: "this is a public confession, a ritual shared by the followers of the Syrian Goddess and the cult of Isis to which the narrator himself will soon belong (Lightfoot 2003: 78–9)" (p. 167).
- Flagellation: the whip is "'the distinctive attribute of those half-men (semiviris)'"; "'the ground becoming wet with the filth of effeminate blood' (cerneres . . . solum spurcitia sanguinis effeminati madescere)" (8.28, p. 168).
- Carrying the goddess's image door to door (*circumferentes*) (p. 163); Philebus fears the ass will "throw off her divine image" (8.25, p. 164).
- Annual castration festival at Hierapolis: "Each year many castrate and unman themselves in the temple" (DDS 27, p. 160).
- Vow of chastity (Attis): "after he breaks his vow of chastity" (Begum-Lees, p. 112).
- Sexuality within the troupe: "aggressively pursuing sexually passive roles with a young man" (*Onos* 38 / *Met.* 8.27) (p. 163); marriage banter — "'This is not a slave, but a bridegroom for yourself! . . . may you soon birth for us foals just like him'" (*Onos* 36, p. 165).

### 2.9 The archigallus and the epigraphic galli (the "respectable" record)
- "Roman funerary monuments for Cybelean galli and archigalli (a high priesthood added in the late first/early second century ad) depict them in a variety of gendered or gender-blended clothing (Hales 2002). Archigalli, whose inscriptions use masculine names and grammatical forms, are depicted wearing trousers, tunics and caps resembling those of Attis (Vermaseren 1977: nos 261, 395, 401–2, 446–8, 462)." (p. 161–2)
- "The best known dedication for a gallus is the Soterides inscription from Cyzicus, dated to 46 bc. While Soterides refers to himself in the inscription using masculine grammatical forms, he is depicted in the relief above wearing feminine clothing . . . He makes the dedication in thanks for the safety of Marcus Stlaccius, his symbios, a Greek word meaning 'partner' or 'spouse' (Roscoe 1996: 203)." (p. 162)
- "Each of these demonstrates their subjects' status and pride in their identities as priests of Cybele, who by the second century ad had achieved a high level of visibility and a secure social position; they bear little resemblance to the frenzied, sexually depraved galli of Greek and Roman literature (cf. Latham 2012: 101–14)." (p. 162)

### 2.10 Reception and hostility
- Literary galli are the cinaedus scare-figure: "cinaedi are portrayed as men who wear make-up and long, brightly coloured clothes, curl their hair, depilate their legs, walk with a shorter or swaying stride and speak in high-pitched voices"; "The cinaedus serves as a scare figure, a symbol of failed masculinity" (pp. 158–9); "kinaidos/cinaedus seems to have referred originally to a man who performed a style of dance associated with the Near East" (p. 158).
- Voices "'broken', 'hoarse', 'effeminate', 'discordant' and 'wailing' (8.26: fracta et rauca et effeminata voce clamores absonos intollunt; 8.27: absonis ululatibus constrepentes fanatice provolant)" (p. 166).
- Power of naming: "Power is held by those who impose a definitive meaning on discourse" (p. 163); "The fact that our unreliable, asinine narrator Lucius can define the reality of an entire group of people through words alone reveals the role that gendered discourse played in the politics of representation in Imperial Roman literature and culture" (p. 168).
- Orientalism: "a set of stereotypes that, when combined with more recent Orientalist discourses, still lingers in modern scholarship (Roller 1999: 19–24 offers a well-articulated critique)" (p. 159).

### 2.11 Survival into late antiquity
- The book's latest datum is the archigallate "added in the late first/early second century ad" and the second-century "secure social position" (p. 162), plus Elagabalus (218–22) and SHA *Heliog.* 7.1 (Rantala, p. 123). Nothing on the fourth century, Julian, Firmicus, Augustine or the cult's end. **Gap: for a faith claiming continuity to 867 the mod must bridge c. 250–867 on its own; the book offers no handle.**

### 2.12 Modern trans readings assembled in the book
- Adkins: priests' self-speech "adding to the evidence that some of the followers of Cybele and the Syrian Goddess – commonly referred to as galli – were transwomen and other assigned-male-at-birth individuals with diverse gender identities and sexual orientations (Roscoe 1996; Taylor 1997: 336, 371; Adkins 2014: 35–55; Blood 2015; Blood forthcoming; Carlà-Uhink 2017: 16–19)" (p. 157); "a community of transwomen sheltered within an imported religion on the margins of Graeco-Roman society" (p. 168).
- Hijra comparison: "Hijras are traditionally assigned-male-at-birth followers of a mother goddess who wear women's clothing and use feminine pronouns. Their foundational stories are so close to those of the galli that they likely have a historical connection (Taylor 1997: 332; Lightfoot 2003: 385–6). Hijras have long considered themselves a third gender; tapping into international human rights movements, some now identify as transwomen" (p. 161).
- Havens hypothesis: "some have argued that the ancient Anatolian cults were likely havens for non-gender-conforming genetic males and feminine intersex individuals (Taylor 1997: 336, 371)" (p. 161).
- Blood's spectrum: galli "should not be viewed as a homogeneous group, but most likely included people who would have identified, in modern terms, along a spectrum of genders and sexualities, including transwomen, cisgender transvestite men, and assigned-male-at-birth individuals who preferred a passive/penetrated role during sex; we should likely add feminine, assigned-male-at-birth, asexual individuals, who might have been marginalised if they did not marry and produce children as was expected of Greek and Roman citizen men" (p. 161).
- Penrose: "Gender-queer eunuch priestesses of the goddess Cybele" (p. 38); Enarees as "androgynous shaman-type priestesses" (p. 37); the mare's-urine HRT hypothesis (pp. 39–40).
- Rantala on Elagabalus: "a step beyond merely challenging traditional gender roles and gender expression towards the actual changing of one's sex in order to become female, presumably to match a feminine expression and a female gender identity" (p. 125).

## 4. Belief-plausibility matrix

Each belief the mod wants to encode is weighed only against what this book attests. Verdicts: **STRONG** (ancient sources say roughly this), **PARTIAL** (ancient analogue exists but the fit needs a stated in-world reinterpretation), **WEAK** (little or contrary evidence; present as the faith's own innovation or borrow from outside the book).

### (a) Trans women as a distinct sex class
- STRENGTHEN: Hippocratic *androgynoi* are a natural third outcome of conception ("These become androgynoi and are rightly called so", Penrose, p. 33). Eunuchs are "differently gendered than the normative man" and "perpetually considered to be 'other'" (Åshede, p. 86; Rantala, p. 123). Hijras "have long considered themselves a third gender" and are historically linked to galli (Adkins, p. 161). Scythians have a named class, Enarees, with a divine aetiology (Penrose, pp. 37–8). Grammatical default: mixed-gender individuals take masculine, "just as a group of mixed gender defaults to the masculine" (Intro, p. 19) — language itself recognised the mixed case.
- WEAKEN: Greek and Roman writers "broadly confirm . . . (1) that there are two distinct sexes . . . (2) that a person must belong to one sex or the other" (Shannon-Henderson, p. 68). *Androgynos* is overwhelmingly a slur for a cowardly man (Penrose, pp. 33–7). The epigraphic galli use masculine forms (Adkins, p. 162).
- **Verdict: PARTIAL.** Ancient cultures had third-category *words* (androgynos, eunouchos, gallus, Enaree, semivir) but mostly as deficient-male, not as a dignified sex. The faith can plausibly *reclaim* an existing category (as hijras do) rather than invent one.

### (b) Womanhood achieved by rite
- STRENGTHEN: Lucian's verb θηλύνονται ("unman/feminise themselves") for the Hierapolis temple castration, followed by women's robes and women's work (Adkins, p. 160). Men enter maenadic rites only by becoming women (Intro, p. 12). Masculinity itself was "'an achieved state, radically underdetermined by anatomical sex'" (Gleason, in Begum-Lees, p. 115) — if manhood is achieved, so may womanhood be. Ulpian assigns legal sex by what "prevails" (Åshede, p. 89). Pandora: womanhood literally manufactured (Uhlig, p. 65). Iphis' change is ratified by a votive, a public rite (Begum-Lees, p. 111).
- WEAKEN: Lucius' counter-reading — the rite produces *effeminacy*, not femininity (Adkins, p. 166). Shannon-Henderson's sources record only F→M, "it only makes a good story when the worse spontaneously becomes the better" (p. 74). Pentheus' incomplete transformation is punished (Intro, p. 12).
- **Verdict: STRONG for the cult-internal view, WEAK for outsiders.** Encode as: within the faith a rite makes a woman; hostile faiths see a "half-man".

### (c) Castration as sacred rebirth
- STRENGTHEN: Dionysus "twice-born", initiates "find new life through death" (Intro, p. 11). Attis as mythological model (Adkins, p. 159). Annual temple castration "to console Combabos or to do favour to Hera" (p. 160). Enarees' femininity "attributed . . . to divine ordinance" (Penrose, p. 38).
- WEAKEN: The book never attaches "rebirth" language to galli; myths explain castration as "madness (Catull. 63), divine punishment . . . or the avoidance of sexual wrongdoing" (Adkins, p. 160). Attis castrates himself as penance for breaking chastity (Begum-Lees, p. 112). Eunuch status was "associated with slavery" (p. 160). "we do not know . . . what it meant to those who performed it" (p. 160).
- **Verdict: PARTIAL.** The *act* is firmly attested; the *theology of rebirth* must be supplied by syncretism with Dionysiac/Isiac mystery language (which the book does document). Say so in flavour text: "the Mother's daughters borrowed the twice-born god's promise".

### (d) Castrated men regarded as women
- STRENGTHEN: DDS 26–7: Combabos has "the 'shape' of a woman (morphē)"; galli "don female robes and do women's work" (Adkins, pp. 160–1). Priests "identify as girls" (p. 168). Latin grouping of women, eunuchs and cinaedi as sharing "thin" and "broken" voices (p. 166). Elagabalus "termed wife, mistress, and queen" (Rantala, p. 121). Hijra analogue (p. 161).
- WEAKEN: Romans saw "half-men (semiviris)" (p. 168), "a chorus of cinaedi" (p. 166); eunuchs "'half-men' with no manly qualities" at most, not women (Rantala, p. 123). Archigalli present masculine (p. 162).
- **Verdict: PARTIAL → STRONG inside the Syrian/Anatolian cult sphere.** Historically defensible for a Near Eastern/Anatolian-rooted culture, indefensible as a *Roman* norm. Recommend the culture's homeland be placed east (Syria/Anatolia/steppe), not Italy.

### (e) Women-only or transfeminine priesthood
- STRENGTHEN: Demeter's mysteries "forbidden to male eyes" with a priestess (Kallo) (Shannon-Henderson, p. 77). Maenadic rites as female space (Intro, p. 12). Galli as "a community of transwomen sheltered within an imported religion" (Adkins, p. 168). Enarees as "shaman-type priestesses" (Penrose, p. 37). Mary leads "her female entourage" (Sowers & Passaro, p. 190). Virtus disguised as a priestess "with the priestess's customary fillets of white wool" (Agri, p. 139). Thecla's allies "explicitly feminine" (p. 195).
- WEAKEN: The archigallate is masculine (p. 162). The book is silent on whether cis women served alongside galli; the Introduction says cult practice is "largely omitted" (p. 20). Kallo's case shows exclusivity enforced by *birth* sex, against the mod's premise.
- **Verdict: STRONG for women-only mysteries; PARTIAL for a *mixed* cis/trans women's clergy** (no source shows the two together; present it as the faith's synthesis of Demeter-style exclusivity and galli membership).

### (f) Separatism
- STRENGTHEN: Itinerant troupe outside civic structures, "on the margins of Graeco-Roman society" (p. 168); Thecla's community replaces "biological family with their social/religious community" (p. 190); Scythian "safe space" (Penrose, p. 38); Dionysiac "communitas" "'stripped of all social barriers'" (Hughes, p. 154); Hortensia's women's assembly "when the men were silent" (Deminion, p. 205).
- WEAKEN: Galli depended on begging from the very society they stood apart from (p. 163); 2nd-c. galli "had achieved a high level of visibility and a secure social position" (p. 162) — integration, not separatism.
- **Verdict: PARTIAL.** Marginal, mobile, self-contained communities are attested; political separatism is not. Frame as "withdrawal" or "wandering" rather than secession.

### (g) Adoptive / matrilineal heirship
- STRENGTHEN: Tryphaena's "symbolic adoption of Thecla" into "a newly converted and markedly feminine oikos" (p. 190). Hortensius "lived again in the female line" (p. 205). Maesia Sentinas apparently "had no living male kin" and acted for herself (p. 202). Artemisia inherited her husband's throne and ruled as satrap (McCoskey, p. 210). Athena as non-gestational mother of Erichthonius (Uhlig, p. 65). Isis as surrogate guardian of Iphis (ch. 7). Harrison's (outdated) "matriarchal structure" theory is itself an ancient-looking ideology (Uhlig, p. 57).
- WEAKEN: Roman adoption law is not treated; Ulpian: "'women are separated from all civic and public functions'" (Deminion, p. 199). No source shows galli passing property to adopted daughters.
- **Verdict: WEAK–PARTIAL.** Supportable only by analogy (Thecla) and by non-book Roman adoption law. The eunuch's childlessness is the genuine historical engine: a celibate/castrated clergy *must* recruit heirs by adoption, as monasteries did.

### (h) Communal property / alms economy
- STRENGTHEN: Priests "force the goddess to beg" (p. 163); ritual mendicancy in Dion. Hal. and Cicero (p. 164); paid per self-wounding (pp. 166–7); the slave boy is their "shared concubine" and they buy the ass jointly (pp. 158, 163); "They wander from town to town, seemingly without a home" (Penrose, p. 38). Thecla "barters away her valuables" (p. 186); Jesus' female followers "providing financial support for him and his entourage" (p. 189).
- WEAKEN: Soterides and the archigalli commission costly monuments — private wealth and status (p. 162).
- **Verdict: STRONG.** The best-attested socio-economic feature of the galli.

### (i) Polyamory / non-marital kinship
- STRENGTHEN: "shared concubine (partiarius . . . concubinus)" (p. 163); sisters ask Philebus to "share him sometimes with us, your dovies, too" (p. 165); Soterides' *symbios* (p. 162); Megillos "married" Demonassa (Ash, p. 177); Elagabalus "bestowed in marriage and was termed wife" while also marrying five women (Rantala, pp. 121, 124); Egypt as the land where women marry (Begum-Lees, p. 110); Lucian *Dial.* 12 woman sharing her bed with "one of her girl friends" (Ash, p. 182).
- WEAKEN: All these are hostile or comic sources; the "sharing" is Lucius' lampoon. Thecla's model is *celibacy*, not polyamory.
- **Verdict: PARTIAL.** Non-marital partnership (symbios) is epigraphically real; communal sexual sharing is attested only as satire. Offer both a celibate (Thecla/Attis-vow) and a partnered (Soterides) path.

### (j) Bodily modification as sacrament
- STRENGTHEN: Self-castration in the temple (p. 160); self-cutting of arms and tongues, flagellation, "effeminate blood" on the ground (pp. 166–8); Heraïs' and Kallo's surgeries (Shannon-Henderson, pp. 71–3); Elagabalus' vaginoplasty request (Rantala, p. 125); depilation and shaving-festival (Dio, p. 122); Hermaphroditus binding hir breasts (Åshede, p. 84); Pandora's hammered body, "artifice is her strength" (Intro, p. 22); mare's-urine hormones (Penrose, p. 40). Uhlig explicitly links to "body modification, particularly through medical technologies, in the lives of many . . . trans individuals" (p. 55).
- WEAKEN: Roman view: "masculinity as phallocentric and physically inviolable" (p. 159); self-whipping marks one as of the classes "legally subject to corporal punishment" (p. 167).
- **Verdict: STRONG.** Blood-rites and body-alteration are the most richly attested element; the Roman horror is itself usable as the hostile-faith doctrine.

### (k) Intra-queer hostility
- STRENGTHEN: Blood's spectrum — galli included "transwomen, cisgender transvestite men, and assigned-male-at-birth individuals who preferred a passive/penetrated role" and asexuals (p. 161), i.e. a coalition with internal fault-lines. "'feminine' vs. 'effeminate'" (Skinner, p. 166). Nanarus wears women's dress "but nonetheless took offense to being called an androgynos" (Penrose, p. 40). Archigalli masculine vs galli feminine (p. 162). Megillos: "'Don't make a woman out of me'" against being read as a woman-loving woman (Ash, p. 175); scholars "running roughshod over Megillos' self-identification" (p. 181) — the book itself stages the lesbian/trans-man interpretive quarrel. Pentheus torn apart for incomplete transformation (Intro, p. 12). Kallo prosecuted by her own cult (p. 77).
- WEAKEN: No ancient text shows galli fighting cinaedi or tribades; the hostility is outsider-imposed.
- **Verdict: PARTIAL.** Plenty of raw material for *boundary-policing* doctrines (who counts as a real daughter; the masculine archigallus faction), little for inter-group feuds.

### (l) Class / labour framing
- STRENGTHEN: "gender roles were more attached to class in ancient Rome than to sex or sexuality" (Intro, p. 14); galli as "vulgar dregs of the common people" (p. 163), self-punishment marking them with "slaves, the lower classes and infames" (p. 167); eunuch status "associated with slavery" (p. 160); kinaidoi as an *occupation* in Egypt (Penrose, p. 37); women's work (ἔργα γυναικῶν, wool-working) as the sign of transition for Combabos' followers and Elagabalus (pp. 160, 121); Kallo "gives up wool-working, and discards her weaving equipment" on becoming male (Shannon-Henderson, p. 73); actors and sex workers as a legal status group (Rantala, p. 124); Ash's intersectional reading of Megillos' wealth (p. 180).
- WEAKEN: Elite galli existed (Soterides, archigalli); Elagabalus was emperor.
- **Verdict: STRONG.** Labour (wool, music, dance, begging) is how the ancients *saw* gender; a tenet tying womanhood to taking up women's work is historically apt.

### Notes for the modder
- The faith's strongest "non-anachronistic" pillars from this book are (3) blood rites, (4) alms/communal economy, (5) women's-work gendering, (1)/(2) the castration rite with feminine self-naming, and (13) Ulpian's prevailing-sex rule. The weakest are adoptive matrilineal succession (7) and political separatism; both should be presented in-game as the faith's *own* later developments (just as the book shows castration itself was a Hellenistic development of Cybele's cult, Adkins pp. 159–60).
- The homeland should be Anatolian/Syrian or Pontic-steppe rather than Italian: every ancient text that calls castrated men women (DDS, Enarees) is eastern; every text that calls them half-men is Roman.
- A continuity gap of c. 250–867 exists in this book; the archigallate's 2nd-century "secure social position" (Adkins, p. 162) is the last datum. Bridge it with sources outside this volume.
