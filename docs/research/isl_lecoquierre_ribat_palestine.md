# Lecoquierre, "Ribāṭ in Palestine: life on the frontier" (2023): mostly contemporary, and mostly not for the mod

Source: Marion Lecoquierre (University of Helsinki), "Ribāṭ in Palestine: life on the frontier", *Contemporary Levant* 8, no. 2 (2023): 157–173. DOI 10.1080/20581831.2023.2246798. Published online 6 September 2023. Open access, CC BY. **Verified** from the Taylor & Francis cover page and first page in the extraction (`sources_pending/akhi_ribat/src5.txt`, gitignored). Page numbers are the journal's own, from the running heads.

**What was read.** The whole article, pp. 157–173: text, notes (pp. 169–171) and the reference list (pp. 171–173).

**Scope and bias.** This is a political geography of the **present day**, based on fieldwork in Israel and the West Bank in 2011–2019, about 35 interviews (p. 158). Medieval material is limited to a two-page summary of the scholarship (pp. 159–161) and two sentences on Palestinian ribāṭ buildings (pp. 160, 165). Its subject is ribāṭ as a contemporary repertoire of Palestinian resistance, including the *murābiṭūn* and *murābiṭāt* at al-Aqsa (2013–2015). It is a sound source for that, and almost unusable for a medieval game.

**Relation to other notes.** The historiography section duplicates and is less detailed than `isl_negre_ribat_andalus.md` §1. The Jerusalem hostels are covered better by Burgoyne (cited here, not read). See `isl_mortel_ribats_mecca.md` for the hospice phase.

**Relevance:** **low.** Usable: a short summary of how scholars defined ribāṭ, and the fact that Mamluk Jerusalem had hospices of the Mecca type at the gates of the Haram. Not usable: everything contemporary (see §4).

## 1. Definitions in the scholarship (pp. 159–161)

- **Fort with volunteers.** Doutté (1900): ribāṭs were "forts built upon the borders of Muslim empires" held by "a garrison of volunteers" (Lecoquierre's tr., p. 160). He added a second, spiritual sense, devoting one's soul to God's service (p. 160).
- **"Fortified convents"** and "maritime fortresses" (Basset and Terrasse 1927; Zbiss 1954, p. 160). The "military monastery" reading is contested: Kennedy (2011) calls the ribāṭ a "Muslim equivalent of a [Christian] monastery". Chabbi (*EI*², 1997) calls that "extrapolation and misinterpretation", though "urban residences of Ṣūfīs were subsequently known as ribāṭ" (p. 160).
- **Frontier first.** Chabbi: the *murābiṭūn* are "those who dwell on the frontier" (p. 160). Standard reference works call ribāṭs frontier forts and posts, including those of the Arab–Byzantine *thughūr*, the Mediterranean coast and Central Asia (p. 160).
- **A practice, not a building.** Picard and Borrut (2003) say Western historiography "frozen" the meaning and prefer "places of ribāṭ". De la Vaissière speaks of "a 'doing-ribāṭ'" (p. 160). Bonner: the study of ribāṭ is about "transformations in social practice" (pp. 160–161).
- **Root.** *r-b-ṭ*, "to tie", first a gathering of horses (Q 8:60, *ribāṭ al-khayl*), later "staying or … attachment to a place" (Chabbi, p. 162; note 14, p. 170).
- **Devotional sense.** Waiting in the mosque from one prayer to the next counts as an act of ribāṭ, linked to *iʿtikāf*, a pious retreat in the mosque (pp. 166–167). This is from modern interviews but rests on hadith usage the article attributes to al-Bukhārī, Muslim, Aḥmad and al-Ṭabarānī (p. 166).

## 2. Palestine's medieval ribāṭs

- **Two kinds** (p. 160): coastal ribāṭs of the 8th–10th centuries (after Khalilieh 1999, 2008, on Arsūf and coastal navigation), and Mamluk ribāṭs (13th–16th c.) round the al-Aqsa compound in Jerusalem (after Burgoyne 1987).
- **The Jerusalem hostels.** Mamluk ribāṭs were "meant to accommodate pilgrims and the poor" (after Van Berchem 1922). They stood "strictly around, and sometimes inside" the compound, often near its gates, a liminal zone between the city and holy ground (p. 165).
- Two survive in use today (note 41, p. 170). That is contemporary community history and out of scope.

## 3. Women, eunuchs, gender crossing: findings and absences

- **Medieval women: none.** The article names no medieval woman, founder or resident.
- **The murābiṭāt** are contemporary Palestinian women at al-Aqsa (2013–2015), outlawed by Israel in September 2015 (pp. 165–166). They are a **living community and a current political movement**. They must not be used as a model for anything in the mod (see §4).
- **Eunuchs and gender crossing: none.**

## 4. Why most of this article is off limits

- **Standing rule: never present a living community as a faith or its model.** The murābiṭūn, the murābiṭāt, the Afro-Palestinian residents of the old ribāṭs, the residents of Silwan and al-Araqib, and the named public figures are all living people and communities.
- **Ha-Nehpakhot risk.** The article's central scene is Muslim women confronting Jewish far-right visitors at al-Aqsa (pp. 157–158, 165). Any transposition into a medieval Jerusalem event that sets Jewish and Muslim women against each other at a holy site would import a modern conflict. It would also risk casting Jews as intruders. Ha-Nehpakhot's guardrail says Jewish characters are never the wrongdoers in harassment events. **Do not build it.**
- **Modern jihadist usage** (Dabiq, Inspire, Azan, p. 161): no use.
- **Hadith on "ribāṭ until the Day of Resurrection" in al-Shām** (pp. 168–169): quoted from a 2015 sermon. If a Muslim lord's flavour line ever needs the hadith, take it from a hadith collection, not from this article.
