# Heaney (ed.), *Feminism against Cisness* (2024): design reading

**Citation.** Emma Heaney, ed., *Feminism against Cisness*. Asterisk: Gender, Trans-, and All That Comes After (series eds. Susan Stryker, Eliza Steinbock and Jian Neo Chen). Durham and London: Duke University Press, 2024. ISBN 978-1-4780-3045-4 (paperback), 978-1-4780-2622-8 (hardcover), 978-1-4780-5943-1 (ebook). LCCN 2023033120. Dedicated "For Susan Stryker". No DOI is printed in the text, so none is given here.

**What was read.** The whole book: introduction, nine chapters and afterword, with the notes skimmed for sources. Bibliographies and the index were checked only for citations (`scratchpad/books/tr/13.txt`; the text was cleaned of soft hyphens before quotes were checked).

**How cited.** By the book's printed page, e.g. "p. 58". Chapter numbers are the book's own. Bhatt is cited by chapter and line of her text file, as in `book_bhatt_brown_trans_les.md`.

**Quotes.** Verbatim and 25 words or fewer, each checked against the text. Everything else is paraphrase.

**Left out on purpose.** Private individuals named or quoted from social media: a Twitter user Bey quotes (p. 168), a man who posted about Lavery (p. 223), the student plaintiff Wuest discusses (pp. 177, 184–86), a teenage activist Gill-Peterson quotes (p. 198), a māhū organiser and a Yaqui member of a 1970s group named in the Kristjansson and Heaney chapter (pp. 64–68). Their words and names do not appear here. Passages on suicide and self-harm, and the accounts of child sexual abuse in the introduction, are marked in §7 and not used.

**Relation to existing notes.** Bhatt names this book as an example of the view that gender is a "white invention" (`book_bhatt_brown_trans_les.md` §2.5; Bhatt ch. 4, l.358–361). §4 below tests that charge chapter by chapter. Rows that only confirm a §16 rule say so and propose nothing new.

## 2. Core arguments, chapter by chapter, with Bhatt alongside

**2.1 Introduction: two materialisms (pp. 1–27).** Heaney starts from a 1970 radio conversation between Sylvia Rivera and Marsha P. Johnson. Johnson says she learned womanhood from her mother's housework; Rivera says that work is forced on women. Heaney calls Johnson's womanhood "an identity born of skill transmission" (p. 4) and says both are right: housework is "the only necessary labors" (p. 4), and freeing it means freeing it from gender and race. The theory has two halves. First, sexual difference comes from money: the feminine is the social form of unpaid reproductive work (p. 9). Second, it is lived in the body as penetrability: "sexual difference is the partitioning of bodies into penetrator and penetrated" (p. 8). One person can move between the two places: "Your sex can change regularly" (p. 8). Cisness, by contrast, "has no material basis of its own" (p. 10). Who is outside cisness? "The literal answer: every single one of us" (p. 25). The poor, the racialised, the disabled and above all trans people are made to carry that truth.
- *Agrees with Bhatt.* Penetrability as the core of sex is Bhatt's ch. 3 (personhood goes to the penetrator, l.217–218). Heaney's "womanlike implies an inadequacy, that one is not even, properly, a woman" (p. 7) is Bhatt's degendering. Heaney's doctor who calls a 1917 street fairy's body-knowledge "a most extraordinary delusion" is Bhatt's epistemic vandalism (ch. 8); Heaney names it "the expert dismissing the subject's understanding of her body as delusion" (p. 16).
- *Differs from Bhatt.* Bhatt grounds women's oppression in gestation: women are sorted into "valued for gestation, and everyone else" (ch. 1, l.66). Heaney grounds it in unpaid care work and says cisness, the link to genitals, has no material base. Heaney's "every single one of us" is the universal move Bhatt warns about: folding other degradations into one category erases trans women (ch. 1, l.83). Heaney also dates the "claim to sexed propriety" by colonisers and elites to "the past five hundred years" (p. 1), which leans toward the colonial-origin story Bhatt rejects.

**2.2 Awkward-Rich on Sojourner Truth (pp. 37–50).** Truth's famous question appears only in Frances Gage's account, written twelve years later; the 1851 report has her claim "as much muscle as any man" instead (p. 43). White feminists from Gage to Joy Ladin have used the line as a promise that "woman" can always be widened. That use hides two things. Truth's womanhood was disputed in her life: in 1858 pro-slavery men said she was a man in disguise, and she bared her breasts (p. 42). And "Black unsexing is a resource from which sex/gender is made" (p. 45). He sets beside Truth an 1890 San Francisco arrest of a person who claimed "no sex at all" and was sent to an asylum (p. 46).
- *Agrees with Bhatt.* Racial degendering is kin to transmisogyny but not the same, and analogy erases the difference (Bhatt ch. 1). Bhatt names this chapter without attacking it (l.361).
- *Relevance.* Low for history, real for writing: a figure turned into a slogan loses her life. See §5 row 7.

**2.3 Kristjansson and Heaney on the 1970s (pp. 56–72).** STAR's members "capacitated each other's flesh by sharing hormones, food, shelter, medical access, and autonomous spaces" (p. 57). Rivera ran STAR House, fed "our other kids", and wrote to trans women in prison (pp. 61–63). Radical Queens began as a consciousness-raising group of femmes with "butch boyfriends"; "Pretty queens didn't spend Saturday nights writing manifestos" (p. 68, quoting Cei Bell). It called gender "the caste system by which male-dominated society designates women and effeminates as inferior" (p. 68) and fought "machismo" gay men who mocked queens (pp. 68–70). The Miami Transsexual Action Organization admitted only women post-op or on hormones, and its white founder printed a Yaqui member's words only as "news" in her own voice (pp. 65–67). The authors read all this as decolonial: cisness in the Americas was "cultivated as a property of white bodies through slavery and genocide" (p. 58).
- *Where Bhatt's charge lands.* Bhatt says this chapter "espouses this reductive view of all non-Western societies" (l.361). The chapter does generalise: "The historical process that generalizes cisness is the colonial dehumanization of collectivities and social forms that are not organized through cisness" (p. 58). It builds on Lugones and cites Oyěwùmí and Miranda's joyas, all of which Bhatt's ch. 5 and our notes already reject.
- *Where it is fairer than Bhatt allows.* It says "European colonizers came to Turtle Island and the Caribbean with cis hierarchies deeply embedded in their social organization and thought" (p. 58), so patriarchy is not a colonial invention in Europe. It also refuses "any claim that trans freedom would require an impossible return to a precolonial time" (p. 71). Its claims are about the Americas and Hawai'i. The overreach is in the general sentence, not the case studies.
- *What survives for us.* The practice, not the theory: shared hormones, food and roofs; "rioter, land protector, and/or mother are not mutually exclusive categories of women" (p. 64); separatism that turns exclusive and speaks over its own members.

**2.4 LaFleur on self-castration reports (pp. 83–101).** LaFleur collects short newspaper notices of men who castrated themselves, read with "speculation as a necessary condition of scholarship" (p. 86). "A historical phenomenon need not be trans to be a source of trans inspiration" (p. 86). The same newspapers explain castration through livestock: gelded cattle grow "docile", horses "gentle", chests "thin and slender" (pp. 90–93). And most castrations in the archive were punishments of enslaved and free Black men (pp. 85, 99–100), so the method needs "a clear-eyed refusal of historical innocence" (p. 85). LaFleur's best case is "trans diy" (p. 101), and admits "History is frequently unredeemable" (p. 101).
- *Bhatt.* No direct overlap. LaFleur's speculative reading against a hostile record fits Bhatt's ch. 8 counter-reading. Her refusal of innocence fits Bhatt's refusal of a golden past.
- *For us.* This is self-harm material (see §7). Usable only as method and as hostile livestock language.

**2.5 Velocci on cisness as the novelty (pp. 108–123).** The US Supreme Court's Alito argued trans people were unknown in 1964; Texas cited an article framing youth transition care as a new debate (pp. 108–109). So: "trans people have been here for a long time. ... You know who haven't? Cis people." (pp. 108–109). Trans history built a minority model in which most people simply fit; Velocci wants trans history as a method that shows how much work it takes to make anyone fit (pp. 110–118). "Legitimacy doesn't come from having always been one thing" (p. 122). The "trans" category is invented, "but so what?" (p. 123); so is cis.
- *Agrees with Bhatt.* Bhatt on the lost paradise: "So fucking what?" (ch. 5, l.500). Both refuse to rest present claims on the past. This confirms §16 rule 2.
- *Differs.* Velocci's "there are fewer cis people than we thought, and perhaps none at all" (p. 122) is the universal move again (see 2.1). Lavery's "Egg Theory" (`tsq_lavery_egg_theory.md`) warns that this kind of universalism erases what is distinct about trans people.

**2.6 Gabriel on gender abolition (pp. 135–151).** Capital uses gender as "an accumulation strategy" (p. 139): it sorts people into cheaper and unpaid work. The goal is "the abolition of gendered abjection, not—at least, not in the abstract—gendered signification" (p. 137). Two errors mirror each other: transition as conformity to be stamped out (Raymond) and transition as subversion (Preciado). "Both are moralisms, and both are equally useless" (p. 142). "Transsexual desires aren't either good or bad: they're real" (p. 143). Barred from legal work, trans women were pushed into sex work, which also founded trans community (pp. 144–145). Pleasure and beauty are part of the demand: "communal luxury" (p. 147). Gabriel cites Lugones (p. 140) but conditionally, and faults the Endnotes collective for having nothing to say about gender "anterior to and outside of" capital (p. 149).
- *Agrees with Bhatt and Serano.* No moral test of a woman's transition (Serano's post "Nobody reinforces the gender binary & nobody subverts it either", `serano_blog_essays.md` #5; Bhatt's refusal to shame women who adapt, ch. 7, l.722, l.748). Materialist about labour, like Bhatt's call to build power outside the family (ch. 7, l.813).
- *Differs.* Gabriel aims at capital; Bhatt at male supremacy, which she says cannot be analysed away (Concl., l.996). Gabriel's pleasure-as-politics sits uneasily with belief C6 ("liberating sex without liberating women" fails); Gabriel's own frame is labour first, so the two can live together (see §5 row 3).

**2.7 Bey on confession (pp. 158–169).** Confession began as a Christian duty to find and show the truth of oneself (p. 159). Gender asks the same: "When did you know you were a woman? Have you had the surgery?" (p. 161). "So, you must confess, on their terms, to their questions" (p. 161). "Gender must be placed, sedimented, known, and untroubled in order for sociality to happen" (p. 161). Bey, read as a cis man and nonbinary, also refuses the left's demand to confess male privilege (pp. 161–162), and wants "facelessness" (p. 168).
- *Agrees with Bhatt.* The surgery question is Bhatt's (ch. 3, l.245).
- *Differs.* Bey's answer is personal refusal of the category. Bhatt: "not believing in them doesn't stop others from trying to shove you in anyway" (Concl., l.1000). The mod sides with Bhatt (A3; daughters are women). Nonbinary facelessness is out of scope.

**2.8 Wuest on the coalition (pp. 175–188).** The Alliance Defending Freedom gave a small radical feminist group part of its budget (p. 176). The coalition speaks in feminist and even antiracist terms, and the right supplies the money and lawyers (pp. 176, 184–186). The pattern is old: feminists and the Religious Right allied on pornography and victims' rights (p. 175). Wuest calls the result "the form of the anxious matriarch" (p. 188): "we are all 'women's rights' advocates now" (p. 188).
- *Agrees with Bhatt.* Bhatt ch. 2: "If a feminism is found to not adequately serve the interests of patriarchy, it is twisted until it does" (l.181). Bhatt ch. 1 also warns that women of a degraded class may police the line to secure their own place (l.89). Wuest's case is that pattern, funded.

**2.9 Gill-Peterson on anti-trans mothers (pp. 197–209).** Fighting each bill by saying trans children will die locks them into a script of "endless exposure to death" (p. 198). "Trans women and children should not have to die ... to establish that their lives might be worth valuing" (p. 199, shortened). The "mom" groups defend white womanhood and the nation; part of their worry is a child's "hypothetical, future reproductive potential" (p. 207). Arguing facts with them fails, since binary-gender ideology "is largely impervious to rational critique" (p. 204). She wants care that is not motherhood: "No one is raising anyone else in this situation and, so, importantly, no one is reproducing themself in anyone else" (p. 208). She refuses to borrow ballroom kinship as a metaphor (p. 208), and recalls feeling, early in transition, "in the childhood of my womanhood" (p. 208).
- *Agrees with Bhatt.* Natalism and nation: Bhatt ch. 4 ("Owe me grandkids", l.396; the "groomer" panic, l.334). Not borrowing a living community's kinship form: Bhatt's caution on the guru-chela system (ch. 7, l.713).
- *Differs.* Gill-Peterson calls the movement a defence of *white* womanhood, though she also lists Hungary, Russia and Brazil (p. 206). Bhatt insists the same politics thrives outside the West (ch. 4, l.388). Bhatt separately attacks Gill-Peterson's *A Short History of Trans Misogyny* for third-sexing the hijra (l.364); this chapter does not repeat that.

**2.10 Lavery on deductiveness (pp. 217–232).** Mostly film criticism. Usable points: "there are not one but many cisnesses that we are up against" (p. 217), from clinic rules to the line between natural and synthetic hormones; and "'Leave us alone' is the cry of trans feminism against cisness" (p. 217). She describes anti-trans conspiracy thinking that calls queer teachers "grooming" (pp. 224–225). Her short example of reasoning backwards from the present to what one should have known as a child (pp. 218–219) is the egg logic of her TSQ essay.

**2.11 Mitra's afterword (pp. 241–247).** Feminism has proved women's worth through demography, which counts women by birth sex (pp. 243–245). She warns against theory that "casts trans people as idealized subjects, the last frontier of good radical politics" (p. 241). Her answer is "building a feminism for the living" (p. 247). She uses "third gender" neutrally (pp. 242, 245); Bhatt and A3 do not.

## 3. What the book does not cover

No Byzantium, no Norse or Anglo-Saxon material, no medieval Judaism, no Tamil or Mande material, no China (He-Yin Zhen is named once, p. 246, as an early feminist). Nothing before the 16th century except a passing line on Dionysian cults (p. 109). All case studies are the modern United States, with Hawai'i, the US–Mexico border, Canada and the UK. Indigenous roles (Two Spirit, *badé*, *māhū*, *joyas*) appear only in ch. 2. Trans men appear in Awkward-Rich's reading of Truth and in Gill-Peterson's account of the mothers' fears; out of scope.

## 7. Cautions

**No suicide or self-harm content.** Do not use, even paraphrased:
- LaFleur's whole archive of self-castration reports (pp. 83–101) is self-harm material, including deaths (pp. 95–96) and an 1891 report titled "A Frightful Suicide" (p. 96). Use only the method (row 9) and the livestock language (§6.1). No self-castrating man, ancient or medieval, becomes a foremother in any faith's voice.
- Gill-Peterson's discussion of suicidality around anti-trans bills (pp. 197–198).
- A memoir passage in the introduction about a boy who wanted "to be dead" (p. 13).

**Child sexual abuse.** The introduction discusses abuse in the childhoods of Rivera, a memoirist and Janet Mock (pp. 5–7, 12–14). Never in loc. Rule 4 already bars any confirmed child-taking.

**Never a living community as a faith.** Ch. 2 discusses Two Spirit organising, Crow *badé*, Kanaka Maoli *māhū* and Miranda's *joyas*; ch. 8 mentions ballroom kinship. None becomes a faith, rite or group. The joyas remain flagged (H27, H30). STAR House and the 1970s groups are real modern organisations: they inform the House design only as pattern, never as in-game text.

**No colonial-origin story.** The mod is set in 867, before any of the colonisation the book discusses. Do not import "cisness is colonial" into Badenya, the Worn Garland or Yutai Dao (Bhatt ch. 4–5; §16 rule 2).

**No "third gender" and no "non-man".** Radical Queens called themselves "non-men" (pp. 68–69), and Mitra uses "third gender" neutrally (pp. 242, 245). Neither becomes a word for daughters (A3). E2's coalition of all dissidents stays a coalition, not a third category.

**Trans men out of scope.** Awkward-Rich's trans masculine reading of Truth (p. 47), the 1890 arrest, and the mothers' fears about children assigned female (pp. 205–206) give nothing to build.

**Modern words.** "Cis", "cisness", "trans" and "gender" are modern. No in-world text uses them.

**Sexual content.** Lavery's chapter has explicit passages (pp. 223, 231). Not for loc.
