## 1. The argument in brief

**The book.** In Oyo-Yoruba religion the root metaphor of power is not "man and woman" but "husband and wife", together with "rider and horse" (pp. xx, 7, 211). Every initiand of a possessing god, of either sex, is a "bride" (*ìyàwó*) of the god. Male initiands wear women's dress, and mature male priests keep women's hair, earrings and eyeliner (pp. 7, 216–217). The Oyo palace built its government on the same metaphor. Royal wives (*ayaba*), possession priests (*ẹlẹ́gùn*), eunuchs (*ìwẹ̀fà*) and messengers (*ìlàrí*) were all "wives" of the king or of Ṣàngó (pp. 9–12). Matory calls the imperial era the "Age of Sango". It gave way after c. 1830 to the warrior republics of Ibadan, an "Age of Ogun" of iron, war and male solidarity in which the wifely delegates lost their place (pp. 13–17). The British revived the wifely palace under Indirect Rule (pp. 22–23). In Matory's reading the male Ṣàngó priest has crossed the line "not between 'man' and 'woman' but between consanguinity and 'mounting'" (p. 222).

**The article.** Scholars make and keep the secrets of the religions they study, and their agendas reshape them.
- Ruth Landes's "cult matriarchy" (1947) and Édison Carneiro's later writing recast Bahian Candomblé as female by tradition. In doing so they vilified the male *adé* priests (pp. 411–417).
- Oyěwùmí's "genderless" Yoruba past (1997) is a second, nationalist invention (pp. 424–433).
- Both are "inventions in the service of overlapping imagined communities" (p. 434).

## 2. What matters for the design

### A. Dating: nothing Oyo or Ife is datable before 1453

- **Oyo's foundation is undated in Matory.** The earliest dated layer is the dynasty's exile at Igboho: "four Oyo monarchs ruled from Igboho, approximately from 1555 to 1610", after Smith 1965 (p. 84; also pp. 9–10). Igboho "has existed at least since the sixteenth century" (p. 83). Tradition says the dynasty fled there after Nupe armies sacked "the previous Oyo capital" (p. 83). That implies an earlier Oyo, but Matory gives it no date or content.
- **Cavalry and castration are 16th-century by tradition.**
  - Johnson's oral sources put "the convergence of key ritual concepts" in the Igboho period (p. 9).
  - Cavalry is credited to Alaafin Orompoto, "ca. 1560" (p. 10).
  - Castration "became a requirement for the highest palace officials" under Orompoto's successor (pp. 9–10, 84–85, after Johnson 1921: 163).
- **The empire is 17th–18th century.** Matory places the "Oyo royal empire of the 16th to the early 19th century" (p. xviii) and cites Law's *Oyo Empire, c.1600–c.1836* (p. xv). Ketu was under Oyo "by the seventeenth century" (p. 237).
  - The ilari became direct political officers only under Abiodun in the last quarter of the 18th century (pp. 11–12, after Babayemi).
  - "We have no way of knowing precisely when" ilari names came to state the king's will (p. 12).
- **The possession complex is documented late.**
  - Writers have described male priests' women's dress "for nearly a century" before Matory; the earliest he cites is Dennett 1910 (p. 180). The article's note 51 (p. 437) says the same, though the OCR of that note is damaged.
  - Blood-heavy initiation is "at least as old as" the 18th–19th-century Atlantic dispersal (p. 26 n. 8).
  - Johnson's 19th-century account of initiation "differs radically" from what Matory saw (p. 224 n. 4).
  - Extant art of the Ṣàngó type "date[s] back to the eighteenth century" (p. 241 n. 4).
- **Ife is the only in-window datum, and it cuts against back-projection.** The bronzes and terracottas of "ancient Ife (eleventh to fifteenth centuries)" show none of the later kneeling, vessel-bearing women, and no horsemen (p. 240). Matory concludes that this cautions "against the imposition of 'mounting' symbolism upon ancient Ife government" (p. 240).
- **Islam.**
  - "Non-Yoruba Muslim clerics" were probably at Igboho before the 1590s (p. 134).
  - A Muslim marriage expert counselled the Igboho king (pp. 133–134, after Johnson 1921: 164).
  - Yoruba conversions are first documented in 1775 (p. 134).
  - Nupe had a Muslim ruler "by the close of the eighteenth century" (p. 134).

**Upshot.** For 867–1453 there is no Matory datum on Oyo or Ife gender practice. The "wives of the god", the cross-dressing priest, the ilari and the ìwẹ̀fà are traditions placed at c. 1555–1610 at the earliest and recorded from the 19th century on. Ife (c. 1000–1400) is in the window, but the evidence Matory gives from it is *negative*.

### B. The ìyàwó and the ẹlẹ́gùn (ch. 6)

- **Terms.**
  - *Ìyàwó* means "bride" or "wife [junior to the speaker]". It names the initiand of either sex (pp. 7, 189, 223 n. 1).
  - *Ẹlẹ́gùn* ("mount") and *ẹṣin* ("horse") name the mature priest (pp. 7, 189, 216).
  - Senior priests are *Bàbá Onísàngó* or *Ìyá Onísàngó*, "Father-" or "Mother-Owner of Sango" (p. 215).
  - The god "mounts" (*gùn*). The word joins horse-riding, possession and a "violent" kind of copulation (pp. 7, 184, 207; p. 225 n. 16).
- **Who.** "Whereas all Ogun priests are male, Sango priests are mostly female" (p. 7). Matory saw a man's and a woman's initiation at Igboho and observed "no procedural differences" (p. 188).
- **Recruitment is not chosen.**
  - Successors are usually drawn from the kin of a dead priest.
  - The priesthood may delay the corpse's burial until the family names one, "a highly feared form of coercion" (p. 189).
  - "Most adults would not voluntarily join"; the panegyrics cast the god as a slave-hunter (pp. 198–199).
  - Illness may also be read as a calling (pp. 189–190).
- **The initiation is secret and specific; use none of it.** It is "hedged in by àṣírí awo (religious secrets)" (p. 187). Matory describes:
  - shaving and an optional scalp incision;
  - the "bathing in blood" over the head;
  - the mortar seat;
  - pressing the ram's forehead to the initiand's before the sacrifice;
  - the *kele* necklaces;
  - the *ọ̀ṣù* ball and the head-painting;
  - the indigo skullcap;
  - the "carrying outdoors of the child" on the seventh day, as for a newborn girl;
  - the feet washed by the "senior co-wife" (*ìyálé*);
  - the paid striking with straws (pp. 189–197).
- **What the cross-dressing means, for Matory.**
  - The initiand is made an infant daughter, a blood kinsman of the royal house, a captive, a vessel and a bride, all at once. Matory calls this "second-order brideliness" (pp. 198–210).
  - Mature priests outgrow "bride" and become "mounts" (pp. 215–220).
  - Cross-dressing here "is not a carnivalesque mockery of women", nor a ritual inversion, nor a deconstruction of gender (p. 220).
  - It is "the very antitype of theater" (p. 183).
- **The male priest outside ritual is a man.**
  - From initiation his hair "must never be cut". Possession priests are "the only Oyo-Yoruba men I know who braid their hair", often in an archaic bridal style, *àgògo* (p. 216). They wear earrings, and at festivals eyeliner and henna (p. 217).
  - "In few other respects is the daily postinitiatic dress or conduct of male priests feminine or bridelike." They "regularly have multiple wives and children" (pp. 216–217).
  - The article says the same: they present "not as women per se but as wives of the gods" (p. 422).
- **Matory's own frame is not trans-affirming.** On Western transsexuals he writes "In no simple sense is any of the three a 'woman'", and he calls irony "key" to their social roles (p. 222). Do not import this frame. It is cited here because the mod's narrator must not borrow Matory's language for its daughters any more than it borrows his ethnography.
- **The king is a wife too.** By the present Alaafin's account, a male Oyo king "is himself regarded as a 'wife' of Sango" (p. xx). Priestesses place the first charged crown on a new king's head and threaten to let Yemọja "take over his head" (p. 227). Kurunmi of Ijaye, a warlord, styled himself not a "wife" but a "son" of the god (p. xx).

### C. The palace "wives" (ch. 1, ch. 3)

- **Ayaba (royal wives).** They headed empire-wide priesthoods and acted as advisers, as go-betweens with subject chiefs and as provincial representatives. Some were "wives" of Ṣàngó as well (p. 9). In the 19th century the king sent older wives out as long-distance traders (pp. 20–21).
- **Ìlàrí.** They served as diplomatic observers, toll collectors, messengers, cavaliers, guards and priests (p. 9).
  - Female ilari were classed as royal wives. Male ilari "cross-dressed and declared themselves 'wives of the king'", on oral evidence via Atanda, Aderele and Smith (p. 9).
  - Matory suggests male "wives" existed because female wives were secluded and could not travel (p. 9).
  - They were prepared by Ṣàngó officials. The *Ìyá Kéré*, whom Matory calls "the senior palace priestess of Sango", made them in her apartment (pp. 10–11).
  - Each king renamed them for his own will, for example *Mádáríkàn*, "Do-not-oppose-him" (p. 11).
  - They were still sent out "as late as the 1950s" (pp. 22–23).
  - In 1965 a descendant of the ilari Ààrẹ still appears to have cross-dressed to lead the royal wives' procession to the Igboho kings' graves (p. 85).
- **Ìwẹ̀fà (eunuchs).**
  - Castration was a palace requirement, and some ranking ritual officials "had only one testicle removed" (p. 10, after the Landers and Johnson 1921: 30).
  - Matory reads this as "wifeliness", not paternity control (p. 10).
  - Under Abiodun the ayaba, ẹlẹ́gùn, ìwẹ̀fà and ilari together were the king's "expanded corps of 'wives'" (p. 12).
- **Do not use the killings.** These include the crown prince killed at the king's death until 1858, the king's mother put to death (pp. 9–10, 146), King Aole's poison (p. 13) and Ṣàngó's hanging (pp. 5–6, 204). This is the brief's rule against suicide and also plain sense.
- **Orompoto (pp. 84–85).** Igboho men tell of the second Igboho king, a king's daughter who "had turned male" while dancing at her installation and "stood naked before the kingmakers to prove it". Matory's gloss is that "a penis was the sine qua non" of her rule (p. 84). Oyo palace authorities, after Smith 1965, say only that Orompoto was a woman (p. 84).
  - This is **FtM**, and only **reaction data**.
  - It is 1980s telling about a king of c. 1560, so it is **not usable in period**.

### D. Ogun and Yemọja: the other poles

- **Ogun** is not a possession god in Oyo North (pp. 6, 25 n. 4).
  - "Oyo-Yoruba priesthoods of Ogun are exclusively male": hunters and blacksmiths in men's dress (pp. 6–7).
  - An Ifá text tells how Ogun chased women who "terrorize[d] their man folk with their knowledge of spirit possession" into the bush (p. 5, quoting Euba 1985).
  - **For Badenya:** in Yorubaland the smith and hunter are Ogun's male pole. Badenya's smith's road is Mande (Brett-Smith) and must not be dressed in Ogun's iconography.
- **Yemọja** at Igboho has an all-female priesthood of postmenopausal women. They are matrilateral kin of a house that resisted Oyo (p. 146).
  - Their myth is of a wife who leaves the husband who insulted her breasts and keeps the children who stayed loyal (pp. 146–148).
  - Matory: "female dominance is implicit in the hierarchy of 'motherhood'", as against the subordination of "wifeliness" (pp. 174–175).
  - **For Badenya:** this is the closest Yoruba analogue to "the mother's child". It is reaction data only, and it is 20th-century.

### E. "Gendered Agendas" (2003)

- **Landes and Carneiro (pp. 411–417).**
  - Landes's "City of Women" made Candomblé a female tradition and diagnosed male priests as "passive homosexuals" who did not count (pp. 412–414).
  - Carneiro had described male and female priests as equals in 1936. After working with Landes he "literally reversed the course of history" (pp. 414–415).
  - Half a century on, women had in fact become the majority of chief priests (p. 417). The scholarship changed the religion.
- **The adé (pp. 417–423).** Matory explains why Brazilians assume male priests are *adés*: Brazilian penetrator/penetrated categories met the West African metaphor of "mounting" (pp. 418–420).
  - He is firm about the West African side: "I have never said or believed that the West African transvestite priests were or are in any sense homosexual" (p. 423).
  - He reports one private, unpublished eyewitness claim from 1968–73 and declines to build on it (p. 424). **Do not use it.**
  - He finds no "pre-colonial, 'traditional' Yorùbá precedents" for the homophobia that Landes and others projected onto the priests (p. 419).
- **Oyěwùmí (pp. 424–433).**
  - Matory rejects her claim that Yoruba society had no gender before colonisation.
  - Her earliest document is 1829, and she treats the time before records "beyond immediate scrutiny" as an "authentic prototype" (pp. 428, 430).
  - Yoruba marks gender in kin terms, titles, dress, residence and marriage (pp. 426–429). An Ifá verse calls women "deceitful" (p. 430, after Abimbọla).
  - In the book's preface he calls her reading "homophobic" and "nationalist" (pp. xxvii–xxviii). He denies he ever compared the priests to drag queens: "I expressly deny any likeness between them" (p. xxviii).
  - His last note adds that a "Yorubá language" as such was built by mission-educated Oyo Christians in the mid-19th century (p. 438 n. 65).
- **The warning (p. 434).** Scholars' analyses "are often employed as models of 'African tradition'". These models can raise some segments of a living community and "marginalise" others, and that holds whether the scholar is honest or is using the field as an "allegory" for a distant audience.
  - Matory does **not** write a sentence warning against using the priests as a trans or gay icon; that is an inference.
  - What the text gives is: (1) the West African priests are not homosexual in his account (p. 423) and are men outside ritual (pp. 216–217); (2) not every crossing "dressed up in 'gender'" is about men and women (p. 223); (3) every outside agenda, feminist, nationalist or otherwise, has reshaped these religions and harmed some of their members (pp. 413, 434).
  - A trans-women's faith built on Ṣàngó's *ìyàwó* would be the next such agenda.

## 3. Corrections to the candidate and existing notes

1. **"Every devotee, man or woman, is a bride of the god"** (`south_reading_list.md`, finding 4) is too broad. *Ìyàwó* names the **initiand** and junior priest. Mature priests are *ẹlẹ́gùn*, "mounts", and *ẹṣin*, "horses" (pp. 215–216; p. 421).
2. **Male priests do not live as women.** Outside ritual they keep wives and men's dress; the lasting signs are braided hair, earrings and festival cosmetics (pp. 216–217). The candidate B "possible gate" (possession plus bridehood) has no lived-womanhood content to borrow, quite apart from the living-religion risk.
3. **Ìwẹ̀fà "after 1400 at the earliest"** (`waf_johnson_yoruba.md` §4) is still too early. By Johnson's own tradition, which Matory follows, castration of the top palace officials began under Orompoto's successor in the Igboho period, c. 1560–1610 (pp. 9–10, 84–85). If ìwẹ̀fà offices appear at all, it should be after 1453, which is outside the game. Recommendation: **no ìwẹ̀fà offices**. At most, an Oyo courtier's line that the narrator marks as a 16th-century tradition.
4. **The Ìyá Kéré.** Matory calls her "the senior palace priestess of Sango" who initiated the ilari (p. 11). Johnson (pp. 63–64) has her keep the treasury and regalia and be "mother" of the ilari, while Ṣàngó worship belongs to the *Ìyá Nasó*. Prefer Johnson for titles, and do not use either in period.
5. **Johnson's page for the ilari making.** Matory cites Johnson 1921: 63, 74 for the shaving (p. 11). `waf_johnson_yoruba.md` has p. 61 from the text itself; p. 61 stands.
6. **Candidate B's grade (LATER) stands**, and Ife gives a sharper reason: its 11th–15th-century art lacks the mounting iconography (p. 240).
7. **Landes and the "matriarchy".** The reading list says Landes's thesis "helped Brazilian power-brokers marginalise male, reportedly homosexual, priests". More exactly, Landes and Carneiro, and behind them Ramos and the Northeastern Regionalists, wrote the adé out of "tradition" (pp. 413–417). Women's predominance followed the scholarship.
8. **Neither side of the Oyěwùmí debate** belongs in the faith's theory. In particular, Badenya's "mother's children" must not be sold as an "African matriarchy". That is Landes's error, which the article exposes (p. 434).

## 4. Design implications

### What Badenya must avoid

- **No possession or "mounting" in the gate**, and none of the terms *ìyàwó*, *ẹlẹ́gùn*, *ẹṣin* or *gùn*. Mounting is the "displacement of personal will" (p. 10). It is entered under coercion (pp. 189, 199), which is the opposite of a chosen womanhood under the Serano rule. And it is the core of a living priesthood.
- **None of the Ṣàngó initiation's elements** in any Badenya rite (§2B). Badenya's step 1 already has "washings like a new mother's" from Brett-Smith (pp. 229, 234). Keep it sourced there, and never add blood on the head, a mortar seat, a ram or a seventh-day "child" presentation.
- **No ilari-style renaming.** An ilari's name states the king's will; Badenya's sung name must state her own. Keep the contrast explicit, as `waf_johnson_yoruba.md` does.
- **No uncut braided hair as the mark of a daughter.** In Oyo it is the male Ṣàngó priest's mark (p. 216). If Badenya needs a hair sign, find a Mande source.
- **No Ogun iconography on the smith's road.** Ogun's male pole is guns, anvils and the war camp (pp. 6–7, 16).
- **No "matriarchy" and no "genderless Africa"** in the faith's voice or the narrator's (article pp. 412, 425–430). The faith's matrilateral claims rest on the attested sister's-son succession (al-Bakrī, Ibn Khaldūn, Ibn Baṭṭūṭa), and on nothing else.
- **No hearsay** (article p. 424) and **no suicide** (pp. 5–6, 13, 204).

### Reaction lines: Oyo-type courts (a ruler of the Orisha faith holding `c_oyo`)

- **Date gate.** Do not fire before 1453; that is, do not fire at all. The palace "wives" complex is a tradition placed at c. 1555–1610 and documented from the 19th century (pp. 9–10, 84). If the team wants an Oyo palace flavour anyway, use one event after 1400 with a narrator note marking it a back-projection.
- **Event "The King Would Make You His Wife".** An Oyo king offers a daughter a delegate's office as one of his "wives" in the political sense: envoy, toll-keeper or rider (p. 9). He offers it with a name that states his will.
  - **Refuse.** Keep her name (the Declare Before Witnesses rule: nothing takes her name back). Small opinion loss with the king.
  - **Accept the office, not the name.** She serves as his envoy under her own name. Prestige plus a diplomacy bonus, and the king's opinion is unchanged.
  - **There is no option** in which she takes his name.
  - Narrator note: "Oyo royal messengers bore names that stated the king's will (Johnson 1921, p. 61; Matory 1994, p. 11). The palace counted them, men and women alike, among his 'wives'. This is a 16th–19th-century institution; nothing like it is recorded before 1453."
- **Courtier line toward any daughter:** "Here even the king is a wife — of Ṣàngó." This rests on p. xx and is flagged as a 1988 statement by the reigning Alaafin.
