**Relevance, candidly.** The volume is almost entirely Latin-West and almost entirely trans-masculine or genderqueer. Its only Byzantine chapter (Szabo) is about a castrated *man* read as a man; its Eastern-saint chapters (Wright, Ogden, Bychowski) are about people assigned female. None of its chapters treats a person assigned male living as a woman or nun. Its use for Dorleyism is method, vocabulary, and a strong in-period figure (Ignatios).

## 1. The argument in brief

**Introduction (Spencer-Hall & Gutt).**
- "Transgender" is a broad umbrella "including genderqueer, genderfluid, and non-binary identities" (p. 11). Only two chapters leave Catholic Western Europe: Szabo (Byzantium) and Colwill (Scandinavia) (p. 13).
- Method: "Hagiography is not biography, but ... trans lives could be envisioned and, therefore, trans lives could be lived" (p. 27). The "disguise" reading is "a product of the critic rather than the text itself" (pp. 26–27), with Davis's "Crossed Texts" named among the works using it (p. 27 n. 71).
- Euphoria, not only dysphoria: "Being trans is not only about what feels wrong; it is about what feels right" (p. 15).
- It notes Ringrose's eunuch "third gender" without endorsing it (p. 27).

**Szabo, Ignatios (ch. 4).**
- Ignatios, son of Michael I, was castrated at about fourteen after his father's deposition by Leo V (pp. 117–18); patriarch twice, against Photios. He is the only beardless figure among the patriarchs in the Hagia Sophia mosaics (p. 119).
- Vocabulary: *eunouchos* the neutral term; *ek/tomias* "cut man", rare until the Crusades; *spadōn*, *kastratos* legal; *thlibias* "crushed"; *kartzimas* for orchiectomy plus penectomy (p. 112). By the 10th century a eunuch could be called *anēr*, "man" (p. 112). Patriarch Methodios was a eunuch "castrated in dreams or visions" (p. 112).
- The Fathers distrust eunuch virtue. Gregory of Nazianzus: "A virtue that comes naturally is unsatisfactory: but one that proceeds from choice is worthy of praise" (p. 114). Basil, Letter 115: "unfemale, unmale [*athēlu, anandron*], mad for women ... They were condemned to the knife at their very birth" (p. 115).
- Eunuchs served women's houses as "safer" staff, but the early Athos typika (Tzimiskes 972; Athanasios' Lavra; Constantine IX, 1045) bar eunuch novices "in all circumstances" (p. 121).
- Niketas David (early 10th c.) makes Ignatios manly: hard-won *sōphrosynē* and five uses of "father" (pp. 121–25); posthumous fertility miracles (milk restored, a barren woman conceives, a breech birth turned) (p. 124). Only after death is he called *anēr* (p. 125). Bardas's dream has angels "in the guise of eunuch courtiers"; the *praipositos* Baanes Angoures is Photios's henchman (p. 119).
- Szabo's conclusion: Byzantine masculinity was "more open and accessible to non-men than the canonical historical and textual record may suggest" (p. 111). The direction is eunuch *toward man*, not toward woman.

**Bychowski, Marinos (ch. 10).**
- Re-reads "transvestite" through Hirschfeld (1910), who cited Marinos, to argue "authenticity" over "artifice" (pp. 245–50). Proposes *imago transvesti* (the trans self as the soul's God-given image) and *imitatio transvesti* (the impact of a trans saint's lived truth on others) (pp. 250–53).
- Key text: Marinos to his father, "The one who saves the soul is like the one who created it" (p. 255); "do you wish to save your own soul and see mine destroyed?" (p. 256). She reads "I have sinned as a man" (*hōs anthrōpos eplanēthēn*, "was made to wander") as the double bind of a man unable to live openly as one (p. 260).
- Against reading trans saints as "projections of cisgender fears and desires" (Anson) (p. 254). The chapter ends on the "trap" slur against trans women today (p. 262).
- Pronoun practice: she keeps the sources' she/her for Marinos in quotations to make the inconsistency visible (p. 249).

**Wright and Ogden, Euphrosyne/"Esmarade" (chs. 6, 8).**
- In the Old French Life (c. 1200) the saint is tonsured as a nun and renamed "Esmarade", a name "common to males and females" (Wright p. 157), then arrives at a monastery saying "tell him that you have left a eunuch at the door" (*un castré*) (p. 158). Wright reads the eunuch self-presentation as genderqueer and uses they/them (p. 156).
- The monks desire Esmarade *as a eunuch* (p. 156 n. 4), against Anson's reading that they wanted femininity.
- After death the body is prepared against the saint's wishes and venerated as female; the narrator calls the saint "the body" (p. 157 n. 6). Miniatures alternate between a beardless tonsured monk and loose-haired woman (pp. 165–70).
- Ogden: the father inherits the child's cell and gives his wealth to the abbey, taking a role she reads as feminine (pp. 210–12). Gender transgression is presented as neither sin nor virtue in itself (pp. 204–5).

**Usage guide (Appendix).** "Eunuch": "an AMAB individual who has undergone surgical and social transitions (often against their will) from one form of masculinity to another", "a non-binary gender ... existing within the spectrum of masculinity" (p. 293). "Transsexual" is discouraged as reductive because it ties identity to "bodily morphology" (pp. 321–22). "Transvestite" should not be used of trans people (p. 322). "Transwoman" (one word): avoid; use "trans woman" (p. 322).
