# Gil, *Jews in Islamic Countries in the Middle Ages* (2004): design reading (Maghreb parts)

Source: Moshe Gil, *Jews in Islamic Countries in the Middle Ages*, translated from the Hebrew by David Strassler, Études sur le judaïsme médiéval 28 (Leiden and Boston: Brill, 2004). ISBN 90 04 13882 X; e-book 978-90-47-41316-5 (both from the extraction's front matter). The Hebrew original was published by Tel Aviv University in 1997 (front matter); it prints 846 Geniza documents in full (Preface, p. xiii), and Gil cites them by bold number, as I do where it matters. The Hebrew title *Be-malkhut Yishma'el* is from Gil's own cross-references ("Gil, Be-malkhut").
Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo (`sources_pending/maghreb/gil_jews_islamic_countries.txt`, gitignored). Page numbers are the book's own, from the running heads. Gil's sections are numbered (sec. 114 etc.) and I give both.

**What the book is, for our purpose.** Despite the title it has **no Maghreb chapter**. It is about Babylonia and Persia, Sicily, and the Jewish economy. The Maghreb appears as Babylonia's western diaspora (Part Two, ch. 3, "Babylonia the Diaspora Center") and through the merchant families of Kairouan (Part Four). It **stops in practice around 1070**. It says nothing on the Almoravids or Almohads (a search for Almohad, Almoravid, Murabit, Ibn Tumart and 'Abd al-Mu'min finds nothing).

**What was read.**
- In full: Part Two ch. 3, secs. 114–127, pp. 165–192 (the Maghrib and the yeshivot: Sijilmasa, Tlemcen, Tahert, Sfax, Masila, Qabes, Fez and Ashir, Kairouan, Hushiel and Hananel, Jacob b. Nissim and Nissim, the sons of Berekhiah, Judah b. Joseph, the negidim of Kairouan); sec. 128 opening, p. 193 (al-Andalus and Fez).
- In part: sec. 241, pp. 410–413 (the 10th-century flight from Iraq that brought Babylonians west); Part Three secs. 307–309, pp. 535–541 (Aghlabid and Fatimid Sicily, skimmed for Ifriqiyan dates); Part Four secs. 337–340, pp. 607–613 (slaves; read only for the gender/eunuch search); secs. 383–384, pp. 687–692 (the Berekhiah and al-Majjani families); the Tahirti family's women's letters, pp. 678, 695–696, 699, 702; the Chronology, pp. 730–742 (Maghreb entries).
- Searched but not read through: the whole text for Almohad/Almoravid, herem/ban/excommunication, women/wife/daughter/widow, Fas, Sijilmasa, Qayrawan. The merchant chapters (Radhanites, Ibn 'Awkal, Nehorai b. Nissim) were left aside on purpose: they are trade and finance.

## 2. Community authority

### 2.1 Where authority came from
- **Two masters.** By "generations-old tradition" the Maghrib belonged to the Palestinian yeshiva; by the 11th century Babylon's influence was stronger (sec. 114, p. 165). Kairouan blended the two traditions (p. 180).
- **Kairouan as relay.** Queries from Fez, Sijilmasa, Tahert, Tlemcen and Qabes went through Kairouan to Fustat and on to Baghdad; Kairouan copied and redistributed the responsa (secs. 115–118, pp. 167–173).
- **Titles came from the yeshivot.** Hushiel got the title "head of the scholars" (*resh be rabbanan*), probably from Hay (p. 180). Nissim got *resh be rabbanan* and *aluf*; after Hananel's death he was named chief judge "in the entire Maghrib" (pp. 182 n. 123, 185).
- **The house of study.** Jacob b. Nissim "was the founder and head of the bet midrash in Qayrawan" (p. 183).

### 2.2 The nagid: Gil against Hirschberg
- Gil: in this period the title nagid "was given only sporadically, as honorary title granted by the yeshivot … to the personage closest to the Muslim authority. He was generally a physician" (sec. 127, p. 189). A standing office (the *ra'is al-yahud*) arose only in Egypt from about 1200.
- Hirschberg (pp. 205–206): the ruler appointed the nagid. **For design:** both sources make him the physician at the ruler's side who speaks for the Jews. Who confers the title is disputed; the mod can let the faith's voice and the narrator differ.
- **Abraham b. 'Ata**: physician; with Badis on campaign in 1015 (p. 189); Hay's poem calls him "the nagid of the nation and head of its sedarim, and the Master and Minister of the Jews" (p. 190).
- **The yellow badge story.** al-Mu'izz sent Ibn 'Ata to the leading Maliki jurist Abu 'Imran. The jurist threw him out ("his house was like a mosque") and had the edge of his turban dyed because he was not wearing the yellow badge. al-Mu'izz then half-apologised: he had wanted to show him how great Islam's scholars were, hoping he would convert (p. 190).
- **Jacob b. 'Amram** "was not a scholar, but … supported scholars by providing food and clothing" (p. 192).

### 2.3 The ban
- Gil's Maghreb pages give one ban: during the 1057 disaster, Nissim had Labrat b. Moses b. Sughmar read a yeshiva letter in the synagogue of Sousse. Labrat asked his brother in Alexandria to "proclaim a severe ban upon anyone tampering with" the funds pledged to the yeshivot (p. 186).
- No ruler's licence is mentioned, here or anywhere in the Maghreb sections. This agrees with Hirschberg (§2.3 of that note): the Maghrebi ban was an internal tool. The case concerns money; use it only as evidence of who could ban, never as content.

### 2.4 A woman regent and the Jews
- Judah b. Joseph (d. c. 1018), scholar and merchant of Kairouan, received a sumptuous gift "from the Lady" (*al-sayyida*), "apparently Umm Malal, aunt of the ruler, al-Mu'izz b. Badis": "a saddled mule and ceremonial clothes" (p. 188). Gil notes she "de facto ruled the country, until October 1023" (p. 188 n. 126).
- Judah also interceded successfully for the Jews of Fez exiled to Ashir (pp. 187–188).
- This is a sourced case of **a woman ruler of Ifriqiya honouring a Jewish scholar with robes**. It is the closest thing in either book to the faith's patronage theme.

**What the table shows.** Arabic names of good omen (Surura "joy", Mubaraka "blessed", Najiyya "saved", Bahya "pretty", Umm al-Khayr "mother of good") and Arabic forms of address (Sitt-, Mawla-) dominate. No woman in these pages has a biblical name. This matches Shatzmiller's Provence, where vernacular names also dominate.

### 3.2 Women's lives in the letters
- **A sister's letter** from Mahdiya to her brother Isma'il b. Barhun in Fustat: she "sits near the wall, accompanying the voyagers"; she begs him home ("all you had to do was walk from home to the synagogue"); she says she has been fasting for 60 days (p. 695). This is the most vivid woman's voice in either book.
- **Engagement in the father's absence** (Mawlat, p. 695). Who arranged it is not stated.
- **Property.** Women held and inherited shares of houses in Kairouan (Surura, Zayna, p. 183 n. 123). Women also freed household slaves by will (Fustat, p. 611; context only).
- **A woman living with a man without a certain divorce**: the Tahirti elder Isma'il took the case to Hay, who answered in Arabic (p. 696). It shows a lay elder handling a woman's status case.
- **Sijilmasa's *izr***, "a big shawl worn by women", finer than Egyptian cloth (p. 167 n. 115).

## 4. Gender and the body
- **Eunuchs:** Talmud and geonim forbid castrating a slave, but some geonic responsa let a Jew buy a eunuch or have a non-Jew castrate one. Muqaddasi's report on Pechina (Spain) is ambiguous; Gil rejects Dozy's anti-Jewish reading (pp. 608–609, n. 338). This is slave-trade material. Under the stage-2 guardrail it **must not be used for the sect**.
- **Khuntha/androgynos, cross-dressing, Purim dress, mukhannathun, effeminacy:** not found in the Maghreb sections or by search.
- **Distinguishing dress:** the yellow badge (*'alam*) story of c. 1016–1039 (p. 190), the earliest dated *ghiyar* enforcement on a Maghrebi Jew in either book. In 1037 Baghdad renewed the badge orders (p. 413), outside the Maghreb.
- **Result:** as in Hirschberg, no Maghrebi gender-crossing material. The finding is absence.

## 5. Rabbanites, "Palestinians" and dissent
- Hushiel came from Bari, a "Palestinian" by custom, among the "scholars of Edom" (Christian lands) in Kairouan. In 998 Jacob b. Nissim debated shofar custom with them (p. 180). Kairouan housed two rites side by side and blended them (p. 180).
- Sherira complains of Maghribis who had cut their ties with Pumbedita (p. 166). Hay suspected Shemariah of Fustat of having gone over to Palestine (p. 176). Loyalty between yeshivot was a live issue, and it was fought over letters and titles, not bans.
- **For the sect:** a dissenting current inside the community could hold its own rite and its own patrons, as the "Palestinians" of Kairouan did, without a ban. This supports Ha-Nehpakhot living beside the Rabbanite majority in the Maghreb.

## 6. Stories and texts
- **The four captives** (Ibn Daud's tale, which Gil dates c. 960): four scholars sailing from Bari, taken by an Umayyad admiral, ransomed one each in Alexandria, Cordoba and Ifriqiya, each founding learning where they landed (pp. 177–180). It is a founding legend of Maghrebi learning.
- **Sherira's *Letter*** of 987, written for Jacob b. Nissim of Kairouan (p. 183).
- **Hay's eulogy:** on hearing of Jacob b. Nissim's death Hay eulogised him "with a great crowd present at the yeshiva" (p. 184).
- **Nissim's end:** a letter adds "the holy one" after his name, "perhaps an indication that he was murdered" (p. 186 n. 125). Too uncertain for content.

## 7. Guardrail notes
- Most of Gil's Maghreb material is **commercial** (the Ibn 'Awkal, Tahirti, al-Majjani and Berekhiah firms; the yeshiva money transfers). It may be noted as background; **no proposal below rests on it**. Avoid: the yeshiva-funds ban (p. 186), Judah b. Joseph's thousand-dinar praise (p. 188), the silver dispute (pp. 690–691).
- **Slavery and eunuchs** (pp. 607–613): context only, never content.
- **Baghdad persecutions** (pp. 410–413) are not Maghrebi.

## 9. Open questions

- Goitein, *A Mediterranean Society* vol. 3 and Krakowski for marriage, women's property and names with full dates; the Tahirti women's letters (Gil's nos. 160, 161) are printed in Gil's Hebrew volumes, which we do not have.
