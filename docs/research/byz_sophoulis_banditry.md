# Sophoulis, *Banditry in the Medieval Balkans, 800–1500* (2020): design reading for landless bands

Source: Panos Sophoulis, *Banditry in the Medieval Balkans, 800–1500* (New Approaches to Byzantine History and Culture). Cham: Palgrave Macmillan / Springer Nature, 2020. xiii + 186 pp. (bibliography pp. 143–84, index p. 185). ISBN 978-3-030-55904-5 (print), 978-3-030-55905-2 (eBook). DOI 10.1007/978-3-030-55905-2. **Verified** from the title and copyright pages of the text extraction (`sources_pending/balkans/sophoulis_banditry.txt`, gitignored). Page numbers are the book's own, read from the running heads.

**What was read.** The whole main text, chs. 1–8 (pp. 1–141), with the footnotes. The bibliography was not read, and the index is missing from the extraction. Quotations are short and verbatim. The book's own translations of primary sources are marked as such. Translations of Latin or Greek that the book prints untranslated are mine and marked "my tr.".

**Scope and bias.** The book is about the Balkans. Asia Minor appears only in passing: the Turkoman frontier (p. 8 n. 3), Turkish pirates from Aydin, Menteshe and Karasi (p. 96) and Omur of Aydin (p. 79). Dorylaion and Phrygia are not mentioned. Most of the detailed evidence is late: the Dubrovnik court records (1348 onwards, mostly 15th c.) and the Byzantine civil wars of the 1320s–1340s. For 867–1100 there are only a handful of cases, nearly all from saints' Lives. Sophoulis's working definition excludes plunder by armies on campaign (p. 18), but he keeps the soldier who turns bandit. He rejects Hobsbawm's social bandit for the Middle Ages: the medieval bandit's motive "was mainly economic" (p. 141).

**Relation to other notes.** Read with `norse_ahola_outlawry.md` (Icelandic outlawry, the three endings, sheltering) and `norse_miller_bloodtaking.md`. Ahola gives the Bædling law; Sophoulis gives the Byzantine, Bulgarian, Serbian and Ragusan law, and the clearest medieval cases of **a band turning into a landholder**. He has nothing on gender crossing (§8).

**Relevance:** high for band-to-landholder paths (Momčilo, Dobromir Chrysos, the Albanian clans), co-option of bandits as soldiers and road guards, and Serbian collective liability. Moderate for monasteries and for band structure. Nil for women in bands, eunuchs or disguise.

## 1. Who became a bandit

- **Three sources, four types.** Scholars name peasants, shepherds and soldiers as the sources of bandits (p. 24). Sophoulis adds the noble bandit, making four types: shepherd, soldier, noble and peasant. The types overlap (p. 57).
- **Hobsbawm's recruits**, which Sophoulis accepts as a description: "escaped serfs, ruined freemen, military men, runaways from state authorities", unmarried young men with no place in peasant society. Soldiers, deserters, veterans and herdsmen are "a natural material for banditry" (p. 21).
- **Shepherds are the commonest source** (p. 35). Arms, raiding skill, an honour culture, and pressure on pasture once farming spread after the 9th–10th c. pushed herders into long-distance transhumance and banditry (pp. 35–36). Vlachs, Albanians and Montenegrins dominate the record (pp. 58–71).
- **Soldiers** turned bandit on campaign, when pay or food was short (pp. 37–38, 71–72), and after service, when veterans got no pension beyond their fiscal status and some fell into poverty (p. 38). The armed men of Bokovikon hired in 1330 were probably veterans (p. 75).
- **Peasants** turned bandit in bad times: war, famine, tax flight (pp. 29–30, 35, 76). Free peasants hid to avoid taxes and tenants fled their dues in the 13th c. (p. 29). Serbian law forbade villagers to leave their manors, but charters show they ran away "in great numbers" (p. 31). Matthew of Ephesos in 1332 still thought farm-born villagers "neither capable nor willing to harm travellers" (p. 77).
- **Unpaid workers.** In the Life of Germanos of Kosinitza (9th c.) the workmen who built his monastery were not paid their 100 gold coins. They bound him and led him off to ransom him, until a passing embassy to Serbia paid (p. 76; the embassy is "tentatively dated to 886", n. 85).
- **Nobles.** Lords and officials used retainers for robbery, extortion, kidnapping and illegal tolls, and were rarely punished (pp. 77–83, 137–38).
- **The dispossessed as migrants.** When Albanian chiefs were rewarded with land and titles by foreign powers, clan ties turned "hierarchical and vertical". That pushed tribesmen into migration and nomadism (p. 69).

## 2. How bands were formed, led and kept

- **Kin at the core.** The "operational core" was blood kin, brothers, cousins and nephews, with some outsiders. The family already had "leadership, rules of conduct …, established procedures for division of income" (p. 91). Bobani Vlach lawsuits name pairs of brothers again and again (pp. 65–67).
- **Size.** Usually ten to fifteen. Up to fifty are reported (p. 84): Matthew of Ephesos heard of fifty bandits waiting for his party (1332, p. 84 n. 124). One Bobani robbery of August 1456 had forty-four accused (p. 66 n. 38).
- **Leaders were respectable men.** Vukota Nenčić, a *katunar* (head of a *katun*, a herding camp of several households under a senior member, pp. 36, 68), traded with Dubrovnik from 1404 to 1423 and was accused of robberies with his son (pp. 64–65). Another *katunar*, Miljen Bogavčić, named in many suits, was asked in June 1446 to judge a house robbery in which his own nephew was a suspect. A month later the case was dismissed (p. 65).
- **Gifts bind the network.** In October 1444 Ivaniš Dobrilović stole a horse and gave it to the Vlach *knez* Sladoj Vuković (p. 67 n. 42). Bribery, gifts, godparenthood and blood-brotherhood tied bandits to wider support (pp. 67, 91–92).
- **The bandit network.** Supporters gave information about targets and pursuers, shelter, food, and help selling loot (p. 92). Transhumant herders needed lowland kin and clients for winter grazing, and "these ties formed the backbone of the 'bandit networks'" (p. 37).
- **Bandit communities.** Where the whole locality condoned robbery, Sophoulis speaks of "bandit communities", which held cattle-lifting, robbery and murder to be deeds of honour (pp. 92, 137).
- **Local operation.** Most bands worked on familiar ground near home (p. 83). They scouted at taverns and from villagers (p. 84 and n. 123). The ambush was a log wall in a wooded pass or narrow road (p. 84).

## 3. Living off the land

- **Loot.** Bobani loot (1410–1473): oxen, cows, sheep, goats, horses, pigs, bees, chickens, cloth and clothes, boots, rings, belts, purses, knives and swords, salt, wine and money. They also "abducted and sold people into slavery" (p. 64). Through cattle-lifting they "were able to improve their economic position" (p. 64).
- **Prices.** In the Dubrovnik hinterland a horse fetched 10–30 *perpera*, an ox about 5–8 (p. 91). Specialised goods were hard to sell in the countryside (p. 91).
- **Captives.** The slave trade in captives rose in the 14th–15th c. Servants, a maid, and a mother with her three children were sold to the Turks (1465–68, p. 89 n. 152). Monks were taken for ransom (pp. 89 n. 154, 93–94).
- **A plunder economy.** Albanian and Montenegrin clans developed a "plunder economy" (p. 68). The Chronicle of the Tocco says the Albanians "robbed each others' villages and katuns" (book's tr., p. 71). In 1337 the Byzantine army recovered an estimated 300,000 oxen, 5,000 horses and 1,200,000 sheep seized by Albanian raiders (p. 70 n. 51).
- **Bands travelled with families.** In 1325 Albanians wasting Thessaly wanted to leave because they had taken no fortress. Others of their people stopped them, saying they had come "cum uxoribus et filiis" ("with wives and children", my tr.) to help (Marino Sanudo's letter, p. 69 n. 50). This is the book's only sign of women inside a moving band.
- **The roads.** Bands sat on trade routes: the Trebinje stretch of the Via Drine ("hundreds of robberies", p. 85), Konavle, Kameno Brdo (twelve robberies 1413–73, p. 85), the Strymon valley, and the Iron Gates of the Danube (p. 85). Forest was both ambush site and refuge (pp. 39–40).

## 4. Law and vocabulary

**Byzantine.**
- *Lēstēs* (bandit) against *kleptēs* (thief): Justinian's Novel 134.13 makes force the difference (p. 98 and n. 2). The Basilika repeats the Digest: those who declare public war are enemies; "All others are bandits and pirates" (book's tr., p. 98; also p. 8 n. 2).
- Penalties: the *furca* (gibbet) under the Ecloga (726), the Basilika, the Eklogadion and Armenopoulos's Hexabiblos; crucifixion in Vlastares's Syntagma (c. 1335). Aiding bandits was also punished (p. 98).
- **Applied once:** Theodore Komnenos Doukas, ruler of Epiros (1215–1230), executed "the famous bandit Petrilo". Archbishop Demetrios Chomatenos of Ohrid wrote that this was no sin but a protection of the righteous (pp. 98–99).
- Raiders and irregulars: *koursaroi* (pirates or land raiders), *rymparaioi/ribaraioi* (glossed as "mercenary units of adventurers", p. 73 n. 69), *Tzetarioi* (a brigand company at Thessaloniki, 1430, p. 74). Vlach *hoditai*, read as road or caravan guards, killed one of the Kometopouloi in 976 (p. 58 n. 2).
- Policing: *vigla*/*vigliatikon* (the watch and its tax), *kephalē* (provincial head), towers (*pyrgoi*) (pp. 105–12).
- The word "bandit" was also a slur for rebels: Thomas the Slav (824), Bardas Skleros's army (976–79), Lachanas-Ivailo, Peter the Hermit's followers, the Turkomans of the Seljuk frontier, Osman's "bandit gangs", and the Catalans (p. 8 n. 3; p. 17).

**Bulgarian.** The *Zakon sudnyj ljudem*, based on the Ecloga, was the standard code until the Ottoman conquest. Its oldest version has no clause on banditry. Later Russian versions give death to *razboinici* (p. 99).

**Serbian (Dušan's Zakonik, 1349).** About fifteen articles (p. 100 n. 15):
- Art. 118: anyone who robs a merchant pays 500 (p. 100 n. 20).
- Art. 145–146: a village where a brigand is found is "scattered", the brigand hanged, the headman made to pay for everything he did. Prefects, lieutenants, bailiffs and reeves share the penalty (p. 101).
- Art. 143: the border lord (*kraištnik*) pays sevenfold for raiders who come through his march and return with booty (p. 101 n. 27).
- Art. 149: brigands taken in the act "shall not be pardoned but blinded and hanged" (book's tr., p. 102).
- Art. 150: ordeal by hot iron carried to the altar if there is no proof (p. 102).
- Art. 157–160: prefects post guards on roads and pay victims from their own houses. A headman who refuses a merchant lodging pays his losses. The ruler repays robbed travellers, merchants and monks and recovers the sum from the officers (pp. 100–101).
- Art. 183: herdsmen who commit brigandage come before the royal court (p. 102).
- Applied: Dušan's men were killed and their horses stolen near Strumica. The compensation (*priselica*) fell on the Hilandar monastery, which owned the land (charter of 1376, p. 103).
- Officers: the *kefalija* (from *kephalē*), with civil, judicial and military powers from Milutin's reign on (p. 105); *gradobljudenije*, the guard service on towns and roads (p. 105); *b'ci*, imperial watchmen (1355, p. 106); *biglja* (p. 107). The *kefalija* office was also adopted in late medieval Bulgaria (p. 105).

**Ragusan.** Statute of 1272, Book 6: *robatores stratarum* repay eightfold, then sixteenfold, then always double. If unable to pay, punishment rises with the sum: beating and branding, one eye, the right hand, both eyes, hanging over ten *hyperpera* (p. 104). In practice few were ever tried, because the crimes happened outside Ragusan land and the *katunars* would not cooperate (p. 104).

**Wallachian and Moldavian.** Collective responsibility, with a fine (*duşegubina*) if a community did not hand over a criminal (p. 104).

**Ottoman era.** The book has no Ottoman law. It gives only *hajduk* bands (attested in Ottoman land registers from the first half of the 15th c., p. 127) and the later *armatolikia*, locally recruited armed bands used against banditry (late 17th–18th c., p. 127).

**Pardon in law: absent.** No Balkan legal text the book cites provides an amnesty, and the Zakonik forbids pardon outright. Every Balkan pardon in the book is a ruler's personal act in a civil war (§5). Western pardons for military service are §7.

## 5. From band to landholder (the key cases)

- **Momčilo (fl. c. 1330s–1345).** The best-documented case (pp. 78–81).
  - From youth a highway robber on the Serbian-Bulgarian border. At thirty he enrolled in Andronikos III's army, kept raiding on both sides, and fled to Serbia (p. 79).
  - In 1343 or summer 1344 he offered his service to John Kantakouzenos, who gave him command of the "fortresses, villages and inhabitants" of Merope in the Rhodope, which had submitted. He raised 300 horse and 5,000 foot, whom Kantakouzenos calls brigands (p. 79).
  - Gregoras says he called himself Kantakouzenos's "subject-ally and slave-volunteer" (book's tr., p. 80). He fought for profit and a "bandit salary" for his followers (p. 80).
  - He switched to the regency, and Empress Anna made him **despot**. He destroyed Omur's fifteen ships at Abdera, then asked Kantakouzenos's forgiveness and was **forgiven** and made **sevastokrator** (p. 80).
  - He declared independence and took most of Merope (p. 80). He was killed at Peritheorion in **June 1345**. Kantakouzenos let his **widow** return to Bulgaria "with all of her property" (p. 81).
  - He ran what were in effect protection rackets and was feared locally. In folk tradition he became a hero against the Turks (p. 81).
- **Rajko**, Momčilo's nephew and comrade, escaped to Serbia after 1345 and seems to have been made **kefalija** of the iron district north of Serres (p. 129). This is the band's lieutenant made a road-keeping officer.
- **Dobromir Chrysos (1190s–c. 1205).** "Never called 'bandits'", but the pattern is the same (pp. 81–82).
  - A Vlach with 500 men, first on the emperor's side. Jailed on suspicion, then made commander of Strumica c. 1196. He raided Macedonia.
  - Alexios III besieged him twice in 1197, then sued for peace. He recognised his lands between the Strymon and the Vardar, with Strumica and Prosek, and gave him a Byzantine bride (p. 82).
  - c. 1201 he raided again as far as the Peloponnese. He was tamed by marriage to the emperor's granddaughter and kept only Prosek (p. 82). The Bulgarians took his lands c. 1205 (p. 82 n. 117).
  - He likely lived on extortion and tolls on the Morava-Axios road and the Via Egnatia (p. 82).
- **The Albanian clans (1325–1416).** Raids in Thessaly (1325), Berat (1337) and Thessaly again (c. 1341). Fresh migrants came with Dušan's armies. "By the 1360s, all of Epiros and Aetolia-Akarnania were controlled by a number of Albanian clans" (p. 70). Carlo Tocco took back Aetolia-Akarnania (1405) and Arta (1416), and many Albanians moved on to Attica, Boeotia, the Peloponnese and Corfu (p. 70). This is the clearest *collective* path from raiding band to landholders, and also the clearest path back out.
- **Blok's general point**, which Sophoulis accepts: brigand life was so hard "that many of them sought to come to terms with those in power" (pp. 22–23). These conversions do not make good ballads (p. 23).
- **Frontier administration** produced such men "almost" by design: "shifting loyalties, the opportunism and the irregularity of Balkan frontier administration" (p. 81; again p. 138).

## 6. Co-option: bandits as soldiers, guards and frontier settlers

- **Rulers recruited from the margins on purpose.** It solved "both the problem of banditry and the shortage of troops, for as a rule, brigands made excellent soldiers" (p. 73; again p. 137).
  - Basil II resettled Vodena with *kontaratoi*, "wild and murderous fellows, merciless bandits" (Skylitzes, book's tr., p. 73). The date, c. 1001, is general knowledge, not from the book.
  - Thomas Preljubović of Ioannina (1367–84) hired "thieves, brigands, bandits and plunderers" against Gjin Boua Spata (p. 73).
  - Carlo I Tocco hired plunderers to raid Sgouras Boua's land (p. 73).
  - In 1422 the despots of Epiros "ordered brigands" to seize horses and goods from Roman lands (p. 73).
  - In 1430 Venetian Thessaloniki used the *Tzetarioi*, a company of brigands "brought together from different places", as wall guards (p. 74).
- **Licensed bands that went bad.** c. 1304 the Bulgarian John Choiroboskos, given a court rank with the emperor's leave, raised 1,000 men against the Catalans, and they "degenerated into a company of brigands" around Thessaloniki (p. 72). The book prints "Michael IV". The co-emperor Michael IX is meant (general knowledge). In 1341–45 the garrison of Tzernomianou robbed around its own walls by night. They were "very well practised in banditry" (Kantakouzenos, book's tr., p. 72).
- **Guards who rob.** In 1327 Gregoras's embassy met armed "Mysians" (Vlach or Slav settlers) in the Strymon valley. They had been hired as road guards by a landowner or official, but with poor supervision they ambushed travellers themselves (pp. 60, 107). The Albanian **Burmazi**, settled on the Serbian-Ragusan border in the first half of the 14th c. with "policing and garrison duties", looted often (p. 107). Sophoulis: giving violence to troublesome people shows "the weakness of Balkan states" (p. 107).
- **Herders employed by monasteries.** The "Vlachs of Lavra" grazed an imperial summer pasture secured for them by the Athonite monastery (p. 61 n. 14). Sophoulis suggests Paroria's monasteries had similar arrangements (p. 61 n. 14).
- **Conversion to shepherds.** At Paroria, Gregory of Sinai turned most of the robbers to repentance, and they "became humble shepherds" (Life by Kallistos, book's tr., p. 61; also p. 10).
- **Hired by the state against outsiders.** In 1189 Frederick Barbarossa believed the *doux* of Braničevo had set "Greeks, Bulgars, Serbs and half-barbarian Vlachs" on his army in the Bulgarian forest. Sophoulis thinks the *doux* probably hired "professional bandits" (p. 59).

## 7. Western comparanda (ch. 7): pardon, service, companies

- **Eustace Folville** led a Leicestershire band (court records 1326–33). He took the king's justice Sir Richard Wylughby for a ransom of £1,300 (1332). A year later he was "pardoned in return for the military services he had rendered" in Scotland (p. 117).
- Under Edward III at least **850 charters of pardon** were granted in 1339–40, and 260 more in 1360–61, to men who had served in the wars (p. 120 n. 26).
- Kings recruited noble bandits into peacekeeping commissions, including the arrest of other bandits (p. 118).
- **Henry the Fowler** (919–936) is said to have formed "a legion of ex-robbers" against the Magyars (Widukind, p. 120).
- **Free companies:** demobilised soldiers with no homes formed companies or robber bands and held towns and castles as bases (p. 119). Werner von Urslingen's Great Company plundered Italy in 1345–48 until Louis of Hungary hired it (pp. 119–20). The Papacy paid companies to move on (pp. 120–21). Papal bulls denied them church burial and excommunicated anyone who fed or sheltered them (p. 120).
- **The Capuchonnés** (from c. 1182) were a sworn association against mercenaries. Brigands joined it, and it turned on nobles and clergy (p. 121).
- Other remedies: the Trailbaston (1305), the Westphalian Vehmgericht, Italian *bargelli*, armed escorts (*conductus*), and clearing trees beside roads (pp. 121–22).

## 8. Women, eunuchs, gender crossing, disguise: findings and absences

- **No woman bandit, no woman band leader, and no woman in a band's operational core** appears anywhere in the book, Balkan or Western. The one hint of women inside a moving armed group is the Albanian families of 1325 (p. 69 n. 50).
- **Women appear as:**
  - plaintiffs in Dubrovnik suits: Vladisava Radovinova, robbed of two horses, a saddle and four goats (1423, p. 65 n. 30); Ljubisava, who sued for her son Jakob, a carpenter who had fled the city after a brawl and was seized and robbed by Bobani Vlachs (1467, p. 67 n. 39);
  - captives and slaves (pp. 89 n. 152, 90, 96). In 1393 three girls sold as "Patarines" won their freedom in Dubrovnik by proving they were baptised Christians of Christian parents (p. 90 n. 159);
  - the victim of a sexual assault by a Vlach, Konstantinos Avrilionis, who then beat her father with about thirty kinsmen (letter of John Apokaukos, 1221, pp. 59–60). Not usable as event content (§12);
  - brides used to tame a warlord: Dobromir's two Byzantine brides (p. 82);
  - Momčilo's widow, allowed home with her property (p. 81);
  - in folklore: Momčilo's "unfaithful wife Vidosava" and his sister Jevrosima (p. 129);
  - with young men at the *nedalai*: a 13th-c.(?) church letter on Bulgarian night "games" with thefts, arson and robbery. Clergy were to keep "young men and women" away (p. 109). Sophoulis glosses the sexual charge as "homosexuality and adultery". The Greek he prints says "corruptions of bodies and adulteries" (φθορὰς σωμάτων καὶ μοιχείας, p. 109 n. 71). "Homosexuality" is his gloss, not the source's word.
- **Eunuchs:** none in the book.
- **Disguise:** none in the Balkan material. Eustace the Monk's romance has him use "various disguises" to rob his rival (p. 123), with no detail. Sophoulis does not say what the disguises were.
- **Gender crossing:** none. The book has nothing to confirm or deny cross-dressed outlaws.
- **Design consequence.** Women in a faith's band are the mod's invention, with no support or contradiction from this source. Ahola's Icelandic material (women outlawable in law, none in the sagas, women shelter outlaws) is the nearer support for the Bædling. For the other five faiths, the honest frame is the 1325 note: the people of the band travel with it.

## 9. Frontiers, monasteries and pilgrims

- **Frontiers.** Banditry flourished "in peripheral and frontier regions which rarely remained under firm state control" (p. 39). Pachymeres says the **Turkomans** on the Byzantine-Seljuk frontier found it profitable "to live like brigands" from mountain strongholds, and calls Osman's acts those of "bandit gangs" (p. 8 n. 3). This is the book's one sentence on the Anatolian frontier where Dorylaion lay. That Dorylaion was a frontier town after 1071 and was refortified by Manuel I in 1175 is general knowledge, not from this book.
- **Pilgrims and missionaries on the road.** Gregory the Decapolite, travelling from Ephesos to Rome in the 830s, was seized by Slav river bandits, probably on the Strymon (pp. 46 n. 32, 85 n. 130). Bishop Lietbert of Cambrai met bandits in the "Bulgarian desert" in 1054 (p. 8 n. 3). Adhemar of Le Puy was robbed in Pelagonia by Pechenegs (p. 59 n. 6). In 876 Venice tried to ban buying slaves taken by pirates and bandits (p. 90 n. 157).
- **Monks as targets.** Hermits outside fortified houses were easy prey and good ransom (pp. 93–94). Gregory of Sinai left Athos c. 1328 because of Turkish raiders. At Paroria a rival monk, Amiralis, hired brigands against him (pp. 10–11). Between 1335 and 1341 he founded a monastery there. Tsar Ivan Alexander gave money and supplies and built a tower (p. 11). Romylos of Vidin returned once the tsar threatened the brigands with death (p. 113 n. 94). Bandits drove John of Rila from his first mountain (p. 87). Turkish raiders sold Athonite monks: Philotheou's abbot and monks were sold at Bursa in 1348 and ransomed (p. 89 n. 154).
- **Monasteries as police.**
  - Towers were built or rebuilt by monks on imperial request: Iviron at Komitissa, manned by peasant watchmen (Patriarch Isaias, 1323–34, p. 111); Kosmidion near Rhaidestos (chrysobull of 1342, p. 111).
  - In 1313 Alypiou received state land on Athos "on the condition" that it restore a pirate-wrecked tower (p. 111).
  - c. 1418 Despot Andronikos Palaiologos paid for a tower for "those who were going to settle" at Mariskin, a deserted village Dionysiou had acquired (p. 111).
  - Vatopedi built at least six estate fortifications in 1329–50 (p. 112).
  - Monastic peasants were drafted, or exempted, as foot and mounted watch (Zographou, 1342, p. 106). Fifteen peasant families given to St Paul's in the early 15th c. owed a thirtieth of their produce for the castle guard (p. 106).
- **Monasteries paid for crime on their land.** The Strumica compensation of 1376 (§4) fell on Hilandar (p. 103).

## 11. Design proposals for landless bands of the six faiths

Vanilla *Roads to Power* pieces checked in the local game files (`common/character_interactions/06_ep3_laamp_interactions.txt`, `common/casus_belli_types`, `common/task_contracts`):
- `negotiate_settlement_interaction` ("Pacify … by settling [her] as a vassal within your realm");
- `purchase_land_interaction`;
- `evict_adventurer_interaction` ("Expel Adventurer");
- `seize_realm_interaction`;
- `hire_laamp_mercenaries_interaction`;
- `request_laamp_invasion_sponsorship_interaction`;
- CBs `ep3_laamp_adventurer_county_invasion` and `ep3_laamp_apprehend_adventurer_cb` (loc "Resist Capture");
- contracts `laamp_join_war_contract`, `laamp_help_claimant_contract`, `laamp_raid_contract`, `laamp_prison_break_contract`, `laamp_religious_minority_contract`;
- camp purposes `camp_purpose_brigands`, `camp_purpose_mercenaries` and `camp_purpose_legitimists`, and the camp temperament value.

How each works internally (AI weights, costs) was not checked. Read the definitions before building.

## 12. Risks and cautions

- **Ethnic banditry.** Sophoulis (with his sources) treats Vlachs, Albanians, Montenegrins and "Egyptians" (Roma, the 12th-c. marginal note on "bold lad's wages", p. 71) as bandit peoples. The mod must not encode any culture as criminal. Use "herders", "a camp of the hills" or the band's own name in loc. Do not gate brigand mechanics on Vlach, Albanian or Romani cultures.
- **Bogomils as slaves** (pp. 51, 90): the book reports that enslaving "heretics" was held lawful. Usable only as hostile-world context for a heresy-flagged faith (the Dorleyaians are a heresy too), never as a mechanic that sells people.
- **Sexual violence** (1221, pp. 59–60) and **torture of monks** (Romylos, p. 88): no event content.
- **The *nedalai*** (p. 109): Sophoulis's word "homosexuality" is his gloss on "corruptions of bodies". Do not cite this page as evidence of same-sex practice, and do not attach a faith to it.
- **Social bandit romance.** Sophoulis rejects the noble robber for the Middle Ages (pp. 23, 134, 141). If a faith's band is written as Robin Hood, that is the faith's voice, not the narrator's. Momčilo the anti-Turkish hero is folklore, and the history is a feared racketeer (p. 81).
- **No women in bands, no gender crossing.** Any trans women's band rests on the mod's invention, Ahola, and the faith's own voice. This book offers nothing either way.
- **Anatolia is thin.** One footnote on Turkoman frontier brigands (p. 8 n. 3). Dorylaion facts (frontier after 1071, refortified 1175) are general knowledge and should be checked against a source on Byzantine Anatolia before going into loc.
- **Date errors in the book.** "Michael IV" for Michael IX (p. 72). Theodore Doukas as emperor of Thessaloniki "1224–1330" is a misprint for 1230 (p. 98).
