## 1. The argument

- **The dictionaries.** The OED (entry updated 2008) defines *badling* as "an effeminate or homosexual man (also used as a term of abuse or contempt)", and its etymology begins "Origin uncertain. Perhaps related to Old English bæddel hermaphrodite" (p. 1 [9]). The OED adds that "bad adj. could perhaps show the reflex of bæddel, with loss of -l" (p. 1 [9]). The *Dictionary of Old English* gives *bædling* as "effeminate man, homosexual", "with question marks before each of these terms" (p. 1 [9]).
- **The OED's own line for *bad* does not run through *bæddel*.** The OED's main etymology for *bad* is "perhaps ultimately < Old English bǣdan to force, constrain, impel" (p. 1 [9]). Sayers sets aside what he calls "the speculative tie of bad to badling" (p. 1 [9]).
- **"Who is the bad guy?"** If the root is *bǣdan* "to force", it "would evoke male rape" but would not say whether the agent or the victim is meant (p. 1 [9]).
- **Old French and Old Norse ruled out.** The Old English evidence predates the Conquest, and "Nor does Old Norse offer any leads" (p. 1 [9]).
- **His proposal: a British (Celtic) substrate word.** Gaulish *baitos* "mad, insane"; Old Irish *baíth* "mad, insane, ignorant" but also "lascivious, debauched", and *baitsech* "prostitute" (p. 1 [9]). He proposes an Old British *\*baed-* taken into early Old English as *\*baed*, "with an affect suggesting that the person designated shares characteristics with the subaltern British" (p. 2 [10]).
- **The church added the suffixes.** "The Church designates one who engages in unnatural sexual relations by adding the agent suffixes -el/ling (> bæddel, bædling)", while "the simplex form evolved toward bad", of whose meanings "'sodomite' is only one" (p. 2 [10]).
- **Acts, not persons.** Medieval texts were "principally concerned with acts", and "Whatever the ontological status of the actors, our relevant texts are drafted from the perspective of sins and their punishment" (p. 2 [10]).
- **Later life of the word.** *Badling* resurfaces in 1508, in a Scots poem printed with *Golagros and Gawane*: "A badlyng furrying parsillit wele with sable", which Sayers renders "a sodomite trimming his borders well with sable" (p. 2 [10]). In northern dialect it later means "a good-for-nothing ... a naughty child" (p. 1 [9]).

### Problems with the note

1. **Two mistranslations of the Canons (p. 2 [10]).** He renders *nytene* as "the mentally deficient". Fulk and Jurasinski and Wade both read it as "beast" or "cattle". This error suits his "mad" etymology. He renders *hi beoð hnesclice swa forlegene* as "he is lustful just like the adulterer". Fulk and Jurasinski read *hnesclice* as "effeminately" or "softly", and *forlegene* as a feminine form: "like an adulteress" (`oe_fulk_jurasinski_canons_theodore.md` §1, B 8). His version removes both the softness and the feminine, which are the features that matter most to this faith. **Do not quote Sayers's translation anywhere in the mod.** Use Fulk and Jurasinski.
2. **The Celtic etymology is thin by his own account.** He calls Old British "a very meager linguistic corpus" (p. 1 [9]). No Old English adjective *\*bæd* is attested. *Bad* first appears in Middle English. He does not explain the geminate *-dd-* in *bæddel*.
3. **He bypasses "hermaphrodite".** In his account *bæddel* and *bædling* are two parallel church coinages from one root, both meaning "one who engages in unnatural sexual relations". Fulk and Jurasinski (p. 67, after Fulk 2004a) derive *bædling* from *bæddel* "hermaphrodite". Sayers does not cite Fulk and gives no reason to prefer his derivation over Fulk's.
4. **"Subaltern British."** The idea that the English named sexual offenders after the Britons they conquered is a speculation from one etymology. It should not become lore.

### On the viral claim that "bad" comes from *bæddel*

- **Sayers does not support it as stated.** In his account *bad* is not descended from *bæddel*. Both come from a shared root, and *bæddel* is the derived form. Sodomy is "only one" of the root's meanings (p. 2 [10]). If he is right, the slur came from the insult, not the insult from the slur.
- **The OED only reports it as a suggestion.** "It has been suggested that bad adj. could perhaps show the reflex of bæddel" (p. 1 [9]). The OED's preferred line is *bǣdan* "to force".
- **The weak form survives.** "*Bad* and *bæddel* may be related" (the Bluesky form in `web_baedelism.md`, @sympolite) is compatible with Sayers and with the OED's *bǣdan* line. But it remains one speculative hypothesis among several. The strong form ("the word *bad* is sourced from *bæddel*", @melodies-stereo) has no support in this note.
- **A side effect for the "oppressed one" folk etymology.** The OED's root *bǣdan* "to force, constrain" (p. 1 [9]) belongs to the same family as Frantzen's *ge-bæd* "oppressed one" (Wade row 1). This makes the "oppressed one" reading slightly more respectable than Fulk and Jurasinski's silence suggests. It is still a minority view and stays a voiced folk etymology (F&J row 5).

### Does Sayers support or undercut the faith's name and description?

- **The name stands.** Sayers treats *bædling* as the main attested Old English word and confirms that it occurs in the Canons in the passages the faith relies on (B 6, B 8). He offers nothing in favour of *bæddel* as the better name.
- **The parenthesis stands.** "(the glossaries also keep *bæddel*)" makes no claim about derivation, so it survives both Sayers's view and Fulk's.
- **"The Old English word for such men" is too strong.** In context, "such men" means men who "went the other way", that is, toward womanhood. Sayers glosses the word "sodomite". The DOE puts question marks before both "effeminate man" and "homosexual". Fulk and Jurasinski gloss it "homosexual (?)", note that the translator did *not* use *bædling* for *mollis*, and say the word needed "some more distinctive trait" that we cannot recover (F&J pp. 66–67). The faith description is narrator text. So it asserts, in the mod's voice, a meaning that no source gives. This breaks the rule that the faith may claim, but the narrator may not (Wade row 11).
- **The README's "an effeminate man" is too strong as well.** The Canons do not equate *bædling* with effeminacy. Only the Cleopatra Glossary does, and that is a glossary. Sayers and the DOE give "sodomite" or "?homosexual".

**Relevance overall:** low to moderate. The note is short and weak on the Old English text. Its value for the mod is in two places: it shows that the strong "*bad* from *bæddel*" claim has no scholarly support, and it exposes the over-claim in the faith description and the README.
