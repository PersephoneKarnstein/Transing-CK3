# Psellos's "feminine nature": Papaioannou 2013 and Jeffreys and Lauxtermann 2017, design reading

**Citation 1 (checked against the extraction).** Stratis Papaioannou, *Michael Psellos: Rhetoric and Authorship in Byzantium*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2013. ISBN 978-1-107-02622-3.
**Citation 2 (checked against the extraction).** Michael Jeffreys and Marc D. Lauxtermann (eds.), *The Letters of Psellos: Cultural Networks and Historical Realities*. Oxford Studies in Byzantium. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2017. Part II, "Summaries of the Letters" (pp. 143–445), is by Michael Jeffreys alone; chapters 3–4 are by Jeffreys, ch. 5 by Lauxtermann, ch. 6 by Diether Roderich Reinsch.
**Edition sigla used below (both books).** S = Sathas, *Mesaionikē Bibliothēkē* V (1876); KD = Kurtz and Drexl; G = Gautier. A letter keeps its number across both books.

**Read in full.** Papaioannou ch. 5, "Female voice: gender and emotion", pp. 192–231 (the pathos, grammar of gender, first-person femininity and object-of-desire sections), and the Introduction's life of Psellos, pp. 4–11. Jeffreys and Lauxtermann: the Summaries of S 72 (p. 345), S 157 (p. 387), S 180 (pp. 400–1), KD 201 (p. 262), S 130 (p. 374), S 139 (pp. 377–8); excursuses 6 and 10 (pp. 424, 427); excursus 15.3 "Questions of gender" (p. 434); the Summaries Introduction §4 on dating ("nw"/"Nw", p. 146).
**Read in part.** Papaioannou on Theodote (p. 162), on Styliane's funeral oration (pp. 220–1), on Theodora's selention (pp. 215–16). Jeffreys ch. 3 "Michael Psellos and the Monastery" (pp. 42–57: family, tonsure, nuns, charistikē); ch. 4 on Constantine, nephew of Keroularios (pp. 74–5, 83–4); Reinsch ch. 6, p. 136; Lauxtermann ch. 5 on the "consul of the philosophers" (p. 113).
**Not read.** The rest of both books; the Greek letters themselves; Kaldellis 2006 (*Mothers and Sons, Fathers and Daughters*), which both books cite for the family texts.

**Relevance, candidly.** These two books answer the dating question cleanly. They also give the strongest guard against over-reading: Papaioannou treats the "feminine" Psellos as a rhetorical persona built for a few close readers. Nothing here concerns eunuchs beyond passing mentions; for that, use the eunuch notes.

## 1. Findings

1. **The three passages Betancourt quotes are three letters.** Betancourt's English (pp. 109, 112–13) matches Papaioannou's translations word for word, so he very likely quotes from Papaioannou (my inference; Betancourt's notes were not re-checked).
   - "with regard to nature I am feminine [*thēlys*]": **S 72**, to the kaisar Ioannes Doukas, on the birth of Psellos's grandson by his adopted daughter Euphemia (Papaioannou pp. 207–9, Greek text and translation).
   - "my soul is indeed simply feminine [*thēlys*]": **S 180**, to the *kritēs* (judge) of Philadelphia, theme of Thrakesion (Papaioannou pp. 206–7).
   - "more feminine or more masculine?", "if this pertains to a feminine soul, I do not really know", and the women's chamber: **S 157**, to Constantine, nephew of the patriarch Keroularios, on the birth of his son Romanos (Papaioannou pp. 195–200, Greek text and translation).

2. **Dates.**
   - **S 72: c. 1063–4.** Jeffreys, p. 345 (excursus 6: letters to Ioannes in Constantine X's reign). Papaioannou gives "most likely 1063–5?" after De Vries-van der Velden 1996. He also notes Volk's 1072–3 and Kaldellis's "perhaps 1066" (p. 207 n. 53). The letter mentions writing to "the emperor": Constantine X if 1060s, Michael VII if 1070s (p. 208 n. 57).
   - **S 157: c. 1074.** Jeffreys, p. 387: Constantine's second marriage, to a Georgian attendant of Maria of Alania, was probably soon after her own marriage to Michael VII (c. 1073). Papaioannou: "written late in Psellos' life (mid 1070s?)" (p. 195). The letter addresses Constantine as *epi tōn kriseōn*, an office he held after 1074 on Jeffreys's table (excursus 10). One discrepancy: Reinsch (same volume, p. 136) calls S 157 a letter on the birth of Euphemia's child. Papaioannou's note says only that the bath scene concerns her children (p. 198 n. 24). Follow Jeffreys and Papaioannou: the newborn is Constantine's son.
   - **S 180: "probably 1060–7 or later"**, Jeffreys pp. 400–1. This is a "Nw" date: it rests mainly on Jeffreys's hypothesis of a network of civil judges (p. 146). It is weak. Psellos's grey hair and the "deep old age" in the letter (Papaioannou p. 206) point late.
   - **Psellos's death:** "likely" 1078 (Papaioannou p. 11). The corpus runs "to shortly before he died in 1078" (Jeffreys p. 434).
   - **So the sourced window is c. 1063 to c. 1074, inside a life that ends c. 1078.** The design note's "c. 1066" is no longer unsourced: it is Kaldellis's date for S 72's grandson (via Papaioannou p. 207 n. 53). It is one of three proposals.

3. **Gender talk is a late feature.** Jeffreys: Psellos's experiments with gender "seem only to have increased after he lost his daughter and wife, then became a monk" (p. 434). He dates a letter as after 1060 partly because it explores gender (KD 201, p. 262). That is a circular criterion; use it only as support.

4. **Papaioannou's reading: a persona, not a confession.**
   - Gender here is "discursive": "coded signs" in writing, "rather than ... indexes of social practices" (p. 202).
   - Psellos keeps the androcentric hierarchy in his philosophy. In speaking of himself he makes pathos (emotion, passivity) central and admits "to certain addressees" that it is gendered feminine (p. 206).
   - "What is otherwise marginal or supplementary is raised, for an exclusive audience, into a fundamental feature of the author's persona" (p. 215).
   - He did this "without ever directly challenging the androcentric logic of Byzantine writing"; "his purpose was not to activate some new theoretical model of gender" (p. 231). He "appropriated femininity ... to amplify readerly desire and redirect it upon himself" (p. 231). Papaioannou calls it "rhetorical transvestism" (p. 231).
   - Still, it is "virtually unprecedented": "virtually no Byzantine writer identifies his own affects as feminine" (pp. 210, 215). The precedents are Synesios's letters 146 (c. 399) and 79 (c. 412), and Synesios keeps the femininity temporary (pp. 210–12).
   - In S 72 he is "perhaps more masculine" in learning and feminine by nature. Then he claims to be more philosophical than the kaisar, who wept (pp. 208–9). The play is a performance between friends of rank.

5. **The grammar of gender in Psellos (Papaioannou pp. 202–6).** Three levels: body (hot and dry against cold and moist), soul (dispositions and social roles), and comportment (manliness as freedom from emotion). The "one-sex" model: the female is the male altered by lack of heat at conception (p. 204 n. 39). On "more feminine or more masculine", the comparative suggests degrees of social gender, but the verb *diatypoun* ("stamped") suggests the body (p. 201). The manuscripts split on whether the boy should be like his father or his mother; Papaioannou leans to "mother" as the harder reading (p. 202). Jeffreys's summary follows "mother" (p. 387).

6. **Psellos on gender-crossing others: hostile, or clinical.**
   - *Eudokimos* (lecture *On How Some Become Intelligent and Others Stupid*, Phil. min. ii 19): a boy who keeps to the women's quarters, spins and weaves, and "fashions dolls" for a pretend wedding; Psellos's case study of a slow mind (pp. 204–5). The lecture is not dated in the book; Psellos's lectures are put "between 1047 and 1054" by Kaldellis (p. 6 n. 14).
   - *Avitus* (Elagabalus) in the *Concise History* ch. 39: "outright female in both body and soul", a slur for same-sex desire (p. 205). It shows the same "feminine" vocabulary used as invective.

7. **Women, empresses, the court.**
   - Theodora (sole empress 1055–6): Psellos wrote a Lenten selention for her to speak, in which she says her words are "loftier than the nature of a woman" (pp. 215–16). In the *Chronographia* he faults Zoe and Theodora for mixing "games belonging to the women's quarters" with rule (p. 216 n. 72). He also praises Theodora's ability to rule (p. 206 n. 49).
   - Psellos was "not ... on good terms" with Theodora after he left his monastery (Lauxtermann p. 4; Jeffreys pp. 45, 54). A document she endorsed ended Euphemia's engagement (p. 45).

8. **Family.** Mother Theodote, presented as a saint in his *Encomium* (late 1054; Papaioannou pp. 11, 162). His parents both entered monasteries when he was a teenager, after his elder sister died in childbirth; the impulse was his mother's (Jeffreys p. 42). Daughter Styliane died c. 1052 before marriage (Papaioannou p. 11; funeral oration, pp. 220–1). His wife then "disappears"; Jeffreys, after De Vries-van der Velden, thinks she entered a monastery (pp. 42–3). Adopted daughter Euphemia: engagement to Elpidios Kenchres 1053, dissolved 1056 (Papaioannou p. 11); her son is the grandson of S 72. Psellos was tonsured late 1054, aged 36, and kept the habit to his death (p. 11).

9. **Adoption at the top.** A daughter of Constantine (nephew of Keroularios) was adopted by the ex-empress Eudokia after her father's death, and Eudokia presided over her wedding (Jeffreys p. 84). Eirene Doukaina sent her own daughter Eudokia to a convent when Eudokia's marriage to Constantine Iasites failed (p. 84). Both are after 1081.

10. **The eunuch in the letters.** Very little. Konstantinos Leichoudes, Psellos's teacher, Monomachos's adviser and patriarch 1059–63, was a eunuch (Papaioannou p. 8). In S 139 (1055) Psellos lists an "inscribed eunuch" among the patriarch's men who hounded him (Jeffreys pp. 377–8). Nikephoritzes, later Michael VII's minister, is named "the eunuch" in passing, from Attaleiates (Papaioannou p. 14 n.).

11. **Consul of the philosophers.** *Hypatos tōn philosophōn*, a title made for Psellos: "likely around 1045" (Papaioannou p. 6), 1047 (Jeffreys p. 43; Lauxtermann p. 113). Ioannes Italos succeeded to it in the 1070s (Papaioannou p. 10).
