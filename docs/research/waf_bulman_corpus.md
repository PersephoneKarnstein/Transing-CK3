## 1. The argument in brief: dating and transmission

- **What the epic is.** A "literary practice" of a hereditary bardic class. It works at three levels at once: history, social formation and personal morality (pp. 2–3). Its core subject is *bádenya* ("mother-childness") against *fàdenya* ("father-childness"). Badenya is shown "by Sunjata's sister's willingness to risk all", and "in some accounts by Sumanguru's sister's sacrifices" (p. 5).
- **The written record has a 500-year gap.** Between Ibn Khaldūn and M. M. de Barros's 1882 mention of "Assinjâta" no written source names Sunjata (pp. 12, 20). Even the 17th-c. Timbuktu chronicles do not, nor the European travellers on the Gambia and Niger (p. 20). A 1450s Portuguese report of a war between "Sambageny" and "Semanagu" is judged unlikely to refer to them (p. 20 n. 105).
- **Scenario 1: the epic is post-medieval.** This is the view of most scholars: Person, Niane and Austen place its present form in 18th-c. Mininjan/Kangaba (p. 12). Niane in 1974 put it in the late 17th or early 18th c., under Mansa Saman, a vassal of Segu (p. 16). The editors add an "hourglass" model. Successor-state griots kept origins and the recent past and dropped the middle. Later, when outsiders asked, they "stitched together the elements" (pp. 14–16). Four regional centres: Kulikoro (the Sumanguru cult), Kela/Kangaba, Nioro (Soninke sources) and Fadama (p. 16).
- **Scenario 2: some continuity from the 14th c.** Ibn Khaldūn names Mārī Jāṭa, and Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's jeli recited predecessors' deeds, so a mode of transmission existed (p. 17). Details may match, but no narrative sequence does:
  - Dūghā and the epic's Doka/Doua;
  - the bird-masked jeli and the kɔ̀mɔ rites at Kulikoro (p. 17);
  - al-ʿUmarī's heroes' trousers and the epic's oversized-clothes test (p. 17);
  - terracottas from the Inner Niger Delta, 11th–17th c. (pp. 17–18), whose provenance and dating are insecure and which the editors include with caveats (p. 18 n. 99).
- **The editors' position.** They do not choose between the two. They warn against Niane's "leap of faith" of unbroken continuity (p. 13). They note that the epic is known everywhere by 1889–1919: twelve accounts from six sites across 300,000 km² (p. 18). So it is unlikely to be newly made then.
- **Why Europeans did not record it** (pp. 20–22):
  - contempt for griots: Jobson (1620s) reports dead griots buried in hollow trees "so basely doe they esteeme" them (p. 21);
  - distaste for the music, including Laing's 1822 horror at jelimusow singing (p. 21);
  - confusion over how to treat griots;
  - the bards' own restriction. At Kela only elders may perform it, and in 1954 Kangaba griots still refused to perform before Europeans (p. 22).
- **Transmission after 1960.** Niane "melded" his informants' accounts into "a seamless narrative" (p. 9). From Camara Laye's 1963 tape recorder onwards, versions became longer and transcribed line by line (p. 9).
- **The Foreword's two motifs** (Moraes Farias, pp. xiii–xv):
  - the senior traveller feeds the starving junior his own flesh, and the junior becomes his praiser. "Flesh became word and word becomes flesh" (p. xiv);
  - Sumanguru first sang his own praise on the bala. When a jeli played it, he found it "better ('sweeter') to hear the praise from another person's mouth than from his own" (p. xv). Praise must come from another.

## 2. What matters for the design (account numbers and pages)

### Women's roles: the sister who wins the secret is the oldest attested female role
The sister who seduces or befriends Sumanguru and learns his secret appears in nearly every early account, under many names:
- Hourst's Kulikoro account: his "only sister Ma" (ch. 1, 1889/95, p. 55);
- Quiquandon: Sun-Jatta's sister and the white rooster's spur (ch. 3, 1890, p. 79);
- Monteil: she "thought … of devoting herself to the common cause" (ch. 4, 1898, p. 89);
- ch. 5 (Kita, c. 1910, p. 96);
- ch. 6 (Nioro, 1891, p. 107);
- Maniamba Sukho (ch. 7, 1900/01, p. 115);
- ch. 8 (c. 1906, p. 122);
- Jigui (ch. 9, 1910/11, p. 135);
- Kili (ch. 10, c. 1907/08, p. 153);
- Sugulu Kaluka / Sogolon Konlonka (ch. 13, Kela 1922, pp. 217–219);
- Sogolon Kolonkan, "some call her Meniemba Suko" (ch. 15, 1937, pp. 246, 254–256);
- Jegue (chs 16–18, 1937–50).

**Grade the sister's role as LATER but early (1889 onwards).** It is the best-attested women's role in the epic. Note the variation in what she gives:
- most often she gives herself as a **wife** to the enemy;
- Niane and Vidal make her an unwilling gift (ch. 13, p. 217);
- in Sadji's 1930s literary version she is jealous and is married off (ch. 14, p. 236).

**Other roles:**
- **Nana Triban by another name.** Quiquandon's sister Nagana Tiriba Sukho married the buffalo-slayer Damba-Musa Ulandi Taraure, and their son is Tiramakha (ch. 3, 1890, p. 77). The editors say they know "no other accounts" of this marriage (p. 77 n.). Conrad's 1994 Fadama text has exactly this marriage: Nana Triban, Danmansa Wulanni, Turama'an (Conrad ll. 2039–48). It is an independent corroboration across a century and across regions (Kenedugu and Upper Guinea). The editors' note missed it.
- **The sister's knowledge and counsel.** In Koate's version the sister, Killikillimadjumasuko, holds back the angry hero and reminds him of the diviner's word (ch. 12, 1907–09, p. 187). She takes the hearts and livers for the guests. Her younger brother "struck his sister in the face, which tore her dress" (p. 189), and the editors link this to the curse on his line (p. 189 n. 113).
- **Captured and renamed.** When Sumanguru takes Mande, Sunjata's small sister Sigassuko is renamed: he "baptised her and named her Taffesiga" (ch. 12, p. 188). It is the female counterpart of the bard renamed by the tyrant.
- **The buffalo woman's grievance.** In Koate the sister changed into a koba antelope because "All twelve brothers abused the sister" (ch. 12, p. 173). In Kanote the buffalo is the king's sister (ch. 10, p. 142). Both match Conrad and Niane's denied-inheritance grievance: abuse by brothers.
- **Women who name.** In Kanote's version the father names the son "Sugulongburema". A smith woman, Niarhale Mbomu, comes to shave him. The baby rages that she shaves him "without giving my name". An old woman bends to whisper the name, and her sister shouts "Sugulongburema!" into his ear (ch. 10, pp. 146–147).
- **Sayings.** Sidibé's local saying: "In your family, your sister and your mother are the ones who love you most" (ch. 15, p. 256). Quénum's literary Sundiata greets "my sister, born from the same breast as I" (ch. 17, 1946, p. 303).
- **Sumanguru's mothers.** Dabi Turé and Sansum Turé, from a refrain recorded by Le Chatelier (Introduction, p. 9 n. 47). Conrad's bard has "three Touré women".

### Smith and djinn bargains: the kin-price is early, the sister-for-the-bala is not
- **The sister-for-the-bala bargain (Kosiya Kante) is not in the 1889–1959 corpus.** The editors cite it only from Conrad 2004 (ch. 4, p. 89 n. 32).
- **What the early accounts have instead is a kin-sacrifice told of both kings:**
  - Monteil's Khasso informant: "After sacrificing his sister, Sundiata consulted" the geomancers. That is Sunjata, not Sumanguru (ch. 4, 1898, p. 89);
  - the Nioro accounts: diviners tell Sumanguru to kill "your sister's son", which he does. His grieving sister then carries his secret to Sunjata (ch. 7, 1900/01, p. 115; ch. 9, 1910/11, p. 135).
  - **A sister's dependent is the price; the sister betrays the brother who paid it.** This is the reverse of Kosiya's self-gift.
- **The bala.** In the Foreword's motif Sumanguru is the bala's first player, without a djinn sale (p. xv). The Kulikoro accounts give him a magician's gri-gri (ch. 2, p. 63). Jinn bargains as such appear only in the late accounts (chs 14, 17, 20).
- **Smiths.** Smiths forge the iron staff in nearly every version (chs 1, 5, 9, 13, 15, 19, 20). Nyamakani is the head smith and "high priest" of the Sumanguru shrine at Kulikoro (ch. 2, Petrović 1930, p. 64).

### Gender inversion: none
No account has a man becoming a woman, wearing women's dress as a role, or a eunuch. The nearest items are:
- **An insult.** A griot wakes the idle king: "You are sleeping like a woman" (ch. 10, c. 1907/08, p. 156).
- **A stage taunt.** Jegue to Sumanguru: "You are not a man." He replies: "you are a woman among women, but I am also a man" (ch. 16, 1937 drama, p. 286).
- **Female nakedness as a weapon.** A jealous sister sits naked at her brother's door to curse his day (ch. 14, 1930s, p. 235). The editors call female nakedness "a potent weapon feared by men" (p. 235 n. 47).
- **A claimed status that held (status, not gender).** Under Sumanguru, freeborn lineages "chose to present themselves as griots" to escape death, "and are still griots" (ch. 4, 1898, p. 89). After Sumanguru's fall, "the only ones who were not slaughtered were those who claimed to be smiths" (ch. 8, c. 1906, p. 123).

### The mother's name in naming
- **Kanote.** "Sugulongburema", given by the father (ch. 10, p. 146).
- **The editors' standard etymology.** Sunjata is "a contraction of his mother's name (Sogolon) and Jata ('lion')". Vidal's "ox" etymology is the unusual one (ch. 13, p. 215 n. 19).
- **Mothers' names as recorded:** Sukulu-Kutuma (ch. 3, p. 73); Sungubu/Sungulu (ch. 4, p. 89); Sugulong Kotuma (ch. 10, p. 146); Sugulunkurmang (ch. 12, p. 185); Sugulu Konte (ch. 8, p. 122); Sogolon Konte (ch. 15, p. 255).
- **Name-forms by sex.** In Koate, "the Massari call the women Siga and the men Keita" (ch. 12, p. 188). That is a gendered naming practice; it is unexplained.

### Griots' payment
- **Gold.** Kanote's Sundiata fills a pit with water and "seventeen moules of gold" monthly and tells the griots to take what they wish (ch. 10, p. 155).
- **Praise of strangers.** Jajolet de La Courbe (1685–86) tried to stop twenty praising griots by paying them, and they only began again (Introduction, p. 22).
- **Flesh.** Payment can also be the flesh-gift bond (Foreword, p. xiv).

### Burial: the earliest, most widespread episode about women in the corpus
Sogolon's burial abroad, and the price of a grave, is in at least eight accounts:
- Quiquandon: "You are a foreigner … The earth here is not your own" (ch. 3, 1890, p. 75);
- Monteil: "It was customary in Mema … to buy the required land", one mould of gold (ch. 4, 1898, p. 87);
- ch. 5 (Kita, c. 1910, p. 96): Sundiata must buy the grave from his own **maternal uncles**;
- Arnaud: "when a foreigner died, his heirs had to pay something to the local people before they were allowed to bury" (ch. 8, c. 1906, p. 122);
- chs 10, 11, 12 (1907–09);
- Sidibé: Koite and Konate may each demand the grave-price at the other's funerals, an exchange custom (ch. 15, 1937, p. 255). The editors say it is found nowhere else (p. 256 n.);
- Humblot: "If my mother is not buried, we have war" (ch. 19, before 1918, p. 332).

The Wolof buffalo-woman of ch. 20 (1950) asks: "Bury me in a place where people do not go" (p. 344).

### Kulikoro: the refuge rock is real, and is a 19th-c. shrine of Sumanguru
- **The earliest reports.** In 1819 Dochard says the king of Segu may not visit the hill and that escaped slaves flee to it (Introduction, p. 20 n. 104). In 1887 Caron says the spur is sacred, and none "would dare to go there to seek captives who have taken refuge there". Keitas were barred from it (ch. 1, p. 53).
- **Jaime, 1889–90.** The mountain is "a place of asylum … for all fugitives, male or female, who have cause to complain of a master or of a spouse". There they are inviolable, "may build houses and farm certain neighbouring fields" set apart for them, and are fed by offerings (ch. 2, p. 63).
- **The shrine's other side.** Petrović in 1930 records that women were removed from the fields during the flute rite; to hear Sumanguru's flute was death to them (ch. 2, p. 64).

## 3. Corrections to the candidate (§15) and to other notes

1. **Kosiya Kante is attested only in Conrad's 1990s recordings** (and the Gambian versions Belcher cites), not in any 1889–1959 account. The §15 citation should read "Conrad 2004, ll. 2835–3099 (Fadama, 1994)" and be graded LATER (1990s). The **early** kin-sacrifice in the corpus is a sister's **son** killed by Sumanguru (chs 7, 9), or Sunjata's own sister (ch. 4). The faith should not claim the Kosiya story as "old". The narrator says when it was recorded.
2. **Kolonkan.** The *name* in that form is early 20th c.: Soolon Kulunkan in Humblot (ch. 19, before 1918, p. 331), Sogolon Konlonka in Vidal (ch. 13, 1922, p. 219). The *role* of the sister who wins the secret goes back to 1889. The meat episode is in Kita (ch. 5, c. 1910, p. 96), Sissoko (ch. 11, p. 163) and Koate (ch. 12, p. 189). The curse after the brother tears her dress is in Koate (ch. 12, p. 189). §15's Kolonkan is well grounded.
3. **Ma Tenenba is absent from the corpus.** The foster-mother episode is a Fadama speciality (Conrad 2004). Fakoli's mother appears only as Sasia, Sumanguru's sister (ch. 19, p. 332). Grade Ma Tenenba LATER (1994) and single-source.
4. **The mother's-name prefix** is confirmed for 1907/08 (Kanote) and as the editors' standard etymology. §15 is right.
5. **Koulikoro refuge.** Strongly confirmed for 1819–1930 (LATER). It was asylum for runaways of either sex fleeing "a master or … a spouse", with land to farm (ch. 2, p. 63). It belonged to Sumanguru's shrine, and its cult excluded women. The design may use **the asylum**, in faith voice as a refuge the faith keeps. It must not use the shrine, the smith priest or the flute rite. The narrator marks the refuge as a 19th-c. institution.
6. **"The House buries its own"** gains an early epic anchor (1890 onwards). A stranger's dead need bought ground. Even maternal uncles may demand the price (ch. 5). The 1620s report of griots buried in trees shows a burial exclusion of a casted group (p. 21). Together with the contemporary refusals of burial, the doctrine now has a 17th-c., an epic and a modern layer. All are LATER.
7. **Gender inversion: none** in 1889–1959. This confirms §15's opening statement that no pre-1453 or early-epic case exists.
8. **`waf_conrad_austen_sunjata.md` §3.1 (dating)** stands. Add Niane 1974's dating (late 17th–early 18th c., p. 16) and the editors' "hourglass" model (p. 16).

## 4. Design implications

### Gate step 1 (the smith's road)
- **The djinn's demand.** Offer the kin-price menu (a sister's son, a sister) as **what the djinn demands and the daughter refuses**. Both epic kings paid it in some early versions, and the Nioro versions show that paying it loses you your sister's loyalty (chs 7, 9). Faith voice: "Sumanguru paid with his sister's son and lost his sister; I paid with what was mine and kept my sisters."

### Gate step 2 (the claiming)
- **Make the claimer a sister-figure first.** The early corpus makes the sister the woman who acts for her sibling, more than the mother. Fold Ma Tenenba's rounds in as the foster route, marked single-source.

### Gate step 3 (the word)
- **The naming scene, after Kanote (ch. 10):**
  - a smith woman cuts the first lock;
  - an old woman of the claiming house speaks the name into her ear;
  - the jeli proclaims it.
- **A self-given name does not hold.** Doctrine, faith voice, after the Foreword (p. xv): "Sumanguru sang his own praise and found it sweeter from another's mouth; no daughter names herself." This forbids a self-naming shortcut and keeps the gate social.
- **Captor-renaming.** A rival ruler who captures a daughter may rename her (Taffesiga, ch. 12). That is a hostile event, reversible only by the jeli's word.

### Burial
- **"The price of the ground"** (decision or event): when a daughter dies abroad, or her lineage refuses her, her House pays the grave-price in gold, or offers the potsherd-and-feathers message as a threat (chs 3, 4, 5, 10, 12, 15, 19).
- **Reaction lines:**
  - hostile: "The earth here is not your own" (after ch. 3, p. 75);
  - faith: "If my sister is not buried, we have war" (after ch. 19, p. 332).

### Koulikoro (barony refuge)
- **Holy-site or barony text, narrator voice:** "Into the 19th century runaways of either sex, fleeing a master or a spouse, could not be taken from the hill of Koulikoro, and farmed fields set apart for them" (ch. 2, p. 63).
- **Mechanic:** a "Refuge at the Rock" for courtiers or daughters fleeing a spouse or lord. Do not depict the Sumanguru shrine.

### The fourth holy site: Do
The editors cite al-Bakrī (1068) on "Daw", "a great kingdom", and al-Idrīsī on the same name (p. 18). Fieldwork on the Niger between Niamina and Segu, at the place local tradition calls Dô Dugubani, found medieval settlement and 87 tumulus fields "without parallel in Mali" (p. 18, citing MacDonald et al. 2018).
- **This is the only epic place in this reading with a PERIOD name** (al-Bakrī) and medieval archaeology.
- It is Sogolon's, Kamissa's and Tenenba's homeland.
- **Recommend Do (the Segou-area county) as the open holy site.** Text: the buffalo-woman's land, where the Condé sisters were born.

### Do not use
- The Kulikoro shrine's rites, Nyamakani, the flute.
- Kɔ̀mɔ material, including the bird-mask parallel (p. 17). This weakens the bird-masked jeli as a gate image: keep the mask PERIOD (Ibn Baṭṭūṭa) and do not tie it to Komo.
- The sister's seduction as a playable sexual act. Keep it as told myth.
- The 1930s–50s literary versions (chs 14, 16–18) as evidence.
- The real griotte Yakaré (ch. 1, p. 53) or any named informant in game text.
