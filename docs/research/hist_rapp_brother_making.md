## 1. Book map

**Rapp's thesis** (Intro pp. 1–5; restated p. 4): adelphopoiesis is "a uniquely Byzantine way to create a relation of ritual kinship", first attested in a late-8th-c. euchologion, "commonly practiced among Byzantine men, sometimes also between men and women and between women and women, at all levels of society" (p. 1). It is the third of three church-recognised strategies of ritual kinship, after marriage and godparenthood (synteknia), and "the least onerous of the three with regard to the way it was concluded and the consequences for the next generations" (p. 2). Its origin lies in small-group (semi-eremitic) monasticism of the 4th–7th c., where prayers blessed two men "as they embark on their spiritual journey with mutual support" (p. 3). The thesis proper: "Byzantine adelphopoiesis was motivated by Christianity and depended on the collaboration of priests, yet did not enjoy official recognition by the church. It can thus serve as an example of a social institution that owed its existence to the people who engaged in it. Institutional control followed behind social practice and was largely confined to first offering and then monitoring the ceremonial" (p. 4).

**Position against Boswell** (pp. 2–3, 48, 59, 72–76): Rapp acknowledges "the debt that scholarship owes to John Boswell, who was the first to recognize the importance of the ecclesiastical tradition of brother-making" (p. 48), but rejects his conclusion "that the Byzantine ritual of adelphopoiesis between two men was structurally, and hence also functionally, comparable to marriage" (p. 48). "I was not alone in concluding that the adelphopoiesis ritual in Byzantium was not created with the purpose of sanctioning and sanctifying homosexual relationships, as he seemed to suggest, although I stand firm in my conviction that this evaluation of the historical evidence does in no way undermine the legitimacy of seeking recognition for same-sex partnerships in current societies" (pp. 2–3). She shows Boswell's six "marriage-like" gestures are mostly absent from Byzantine-period adelphopoiesis texts and argues "an even stronger argument can be made for similarities with rituals of Christian initiation, especially at baptism" (p. 72). His translations "must be used with caution as they often exaggerate certain nuances of meaning for the sake of bolstering his interpretation" (p. 59). Verdict: "the ritual of adelphopoiesis itself does not show any significant resemblance to that of marriage during the Byzantine period" (p. 76).

## 2. The rite dossier (raw material for the "Vow of the Last Vial" and "New Personal History" ceremonies)

### 2.1 Rapp's one-paragraph reconstruction (p. 48)
"Imagine the following scene: Two men enter a church together. They step in front of the table on which the Gospel is laid out, and place their hands on it, one on top of the other. The priest speaks prayers over them, asking that God may grant them his peace, love, and oneness of mind. Then they embrace and from now on are regarded as 'brothers.' What has been performed here is the ritual of adelphopoiesis, literally the 'making of brothers.'"

### 2.2 Where, who officiates, who attends
- **Place**: a church, before the *trapeza*/table on which the Gospel book lies (p. 48; Slavonic rubric "prayer in front of the table (trapeza)", p. 69). Rapp notes the prayers belong to the class said "not at the altar, behind the iconostasis, but in the congregational space of the church or in other locations" (p. 53). The 1985 Jerusalem sister-making was in a side chapel of the Holy Sepulchre after the Sunday liturgy (p. 50); 7th-c. Symeon and John were blessed by an abbot in a Jordan-valley monastery (p. 53).
- **Officiant**: a priest; some manuscripts "specify whether they are pronounced by a priest or by a deacon, at what moment they make the sign of the cross, and what liturgical objects—such as candles, censers, or liturgical fans (rhipisteria)—they might be using" (p. 53). Slavonic version opens with "an introductory invocation by the deacon" (p. 69). Jerusalem 1985: bishop, a monk, and a deacon "holding either a lit candle or a thurible with incense" (p. 52).
- **Witnesses / publicity**: "The ritual was not conducted in privacy, but assumed the presence of a community. Prayer E, first attested in a manuscript for use in lay communities, makes explicit reference to the presence of witnesses during the ritual, while Prayers B, F, G, and N emphasize that the participants have gathered in a church for the purpose of attaining the priest's blessing" (p. 86). Prayer E: the pair "want to profess, before angels and people, spiritual love in everything, and who want to call each other brothers in the church and before witnesses, and who want to be sanctified through your words by the priest" (p. 296). Unanswered: "who else was present during the ritual, the wives and children of the newly confirmed spiritual brothers, or only male friends or relatives?" (p. 87).
- **Number of parties**: all but one version name two; one post-Byzantine ms. (no. 59) "mentions that the prayers are said not for two, but for three brothers and supports this by the invocation of heavenly assistance of three military saints: George, Demetrios, and Theodore" (p. 76). Prayers invoking collective bodies "suggest that these words might have been recited in rituals involving more than two men" (p. 86).

### 2.3 Liturgical sequence (composite; each element with attestation)
Rapp: "At its root, the ritual consisted of two prayers unaccompanied by further instructions, and this is how it appears in the three earliest manuscripts. Liturgical instructions are noted only sporadically beginning with the late tenth century (no. 4), but become more frequent beginning with the twelfth century and are almost ubiquitous from the fifteenth century onwards" (p. 72). Titles vary: *euchê* (prayer), *taxis* (order of service), *akolouthia* (liturgy); 40 mss. give only prayers, 24 give rubrics (p. 63).

1. **Entry and hands on the Gospel**. "The imposition of both men's hands, usually one upon the other, on the Gospel book as the priest speaks the first prayer over them simply offers visible confirmation of the seriousness of their intention" (p. 73). Attested in at least eleven mss. (nos. 9, 13, 17, 22, 23, 27, 35, 41, 43, 48, 53?, 59, 61?, 66?) (p. 76). This gesture "is well established from the fourth century as accompanying the solemn swearing of an oath" (p. 73). Hierarchy variant: "one manuscript (no. 43) specifies that the 'older' brother places his hand on top of that of the 'younger,' thus implying a hierarchical relation in which the former literally has the upper hand" (p. 76). Crucially: "they do not exchange promises, a feature that further distinguishes this ritual from the nuptial liturgy" (p. 73 n. 59).
2. **Deacon's introductory invocation** (Slavonic Euchologium Sinaiticum, late 11th c., p. 69).
3. **First prayer** = Prayer A *Ho poiêsas ton anthrôpon* ("who has created man in your image and likeness"), late 8th c., 30 mss. Full text below.
4. **Gospel reading**: John 13:1–17, Jesus washing the disciples' feet, "the ultimate example of brotherly love" (Slavonic version, p. 69).
5. **Second prayer, before the table** = Prayer B *Ho panta pros sôtêrian*, 9th c., 41 mss. Full text below. "All manuscripts, with one exception (no. 14), contain either Prayer A or Prayer B, sometimes together, or in combination with further prayers" (p. 78).
6. **Optional additional prayers** (C–P) by region/period: 10th-c. monastic S. Italy adds C and D; Constantinopolitan court mss. use E and F; 12th-c. S. Italy adds G–L; 13th-c. M; 14th-c. N; 15th-c. Athos O; 16th-c. Athos P (pp. 78–81).
7. **Cross held over the hands**: "Only one text for the ritual for adelphopoiesis, in the twelfth-century manuscript Sinai, ms. gr. 973 (no. 14), has the priest holding a ceremonial cross over the hands of both men in order to bless and confirm their oath" (p. 74); post-Byzantine Greek and Russian use hand-cross; "the modern Greek expression stavradelphos (literally 'cross-brother') is con­nected to this practice in later centuries" (p. 74).
8. **Joining/binding of right hands**: rare and late. "Jerusalem, Metochion tou Taphou 182 (8), of the fifteenth century (no. 53) specifies that the two men should join their right hands"; only post-Byzantine no. 59 "mentions the binding of the hands of the brothers by the priest" (p. 73). Russian liturgical books "prescribe that both be 'bound with a belt' while holding each other's hands in front of the tetrapodion" (p. 73 n. 64). In Jerusalem 1985 "the bishop had us join our right hands together and he wrapped them in a portion of his garment" (p. 50).
9. **Kiss / embrace / bow**: "thirteen times to be precise, to the conclusion of the ceremony, when the newly confirmed brothers either embrace or bow to one another (nos. 9, 13, 23, 24, 27, 29b, 35, 41, 50, 53, 59, 63, 66)"; "In nine manuscripts, the brothers also kiss or bow down to the Gospel at the conclusion of the ritual" (p. 76). Slavonic: "The priest kisses both brothers, then they kiss each other" (p. 69). "the Greek verbs aspazesthai or proskynein can cover all these meanings" (p. 76).
10. **Silent prayer + Lord's Prayer** (Slavonic): "The third prayer is said silently by the priest. This prayer (o tês agapês phytourgos) addresses God as the teacher of peace who has granted us to receive each other in love as his adopted sons" — a Greek reconciliation prayer otherwise absent from Greek adelphopoiesis (p. 69).
11. **Communion**: "Then follows the partaking of the presanctified gifts, that is, the eucharistic bread and wine that had been consecrated on a prior occasion" — in Greek tradition only twice (no. 25, 12th c.; no. 59, 16th c.), common in Slavic (pp. 69–70). Serbian practice observed 1966/1977: "the priest handed the partners a cup of red wine, from which they sipped in turn, three times" (p. 69 n. 44). Latin Trogir ritual (c. 1370) "includes the celebration of the Eucharist" (p. 70).
12. **Procession and meal**: "At the conclusion of the ritual, the priest takes the 'older brother' by the hand, who then takes the hand of his 'younger brother,' and they all go to share a meal—a rare reference to the common feast that follows the ecclesiastical ritual and that underlines its public character" (p. 69).
13. **Triple circling** (the "Dance of Isaiah"): NOT part of the Byzantine core; "attested in an early fourteenth-century Serbian manuscript, in the Russian Orthodox ritual of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, and in the post-Byzantine Greek manuscript Athos Kutlumousiou 341" (p. 75). Before the 14th c. "triple circling was reserved for the baptismal ritual (the priest leading the godfather who carried the child) and the consecration of deacons, priests, and bishops. This ritual gesture is usually interpreted as a manifestation of initiation to a new status" (p. 75).
14. **Irish variant (Gerald of Wales, 12th c.)**: "First they join in compaternitatis foedera [...]. Then they carry each other three times around the church. Then, going into the church, before the altar, and in the presence of relics of the saints, many oaths are made. Finally, with a celebration of the mass and the prayers of the priests they are joined indissolubly as if by a betrothal [tanquam desponsatione]. But at the end [...] each drinks the other's blood" (p. 71). Rapp treats this as "tendentious" (p. 71).

**Absent from the Byzantine rite** (p. 76): exchange of rings; crowning; swords; exchange of promises/vows.

### 2.4 Saints and biblical exemplars invoked
- **Prayer A**: apostles Peter and Andrew; James and John sons of Zebedee; Philip and Bartholomew ("become each other's brothers, not bound together by nature, but by faith and through the Holy Spirit"); martyrs Sergius and Bacchus, Cosmas and Damian, Cyrus and John; Theotokos; John the Baptist (p. 293). Rapp: "The apostles Peter and Andrew were biological brothers, as were James and John [...] Philip and Bartholomew were also believed to be brothers, and engaged in missionary journeys along with their sister Mariamne" (p. 84). "Sergius and Bacchus were military men, and Cosmas and Damian were physicians who offered their services free of charge. The latter were twins—the closest nonhierarchical biological relation imaginable" (p. 84). Cyrus and John "came from different families and background. Cyrus hailed from Alexandria and practiced as a physician who refused payment [anargyros] [...]. John, who had completed a career in the army, attached himself to Cyrus as his disciple" (pp. 84–85). All martyred under Diocletian, giving a terminus post quem of 311; mention of Theotokos places the prayer after 451 (p. 84).
- **Prayers I and M**: Peter and Paul, "Peter from Caesarea Philippi, Paul from Tiberias", whom God "has brought together and made them brothers" (p. 298); M: "to unite [synaphênai] your holy apostles Peter and Paul from the ends of the earth, and has bound them [syzeuxas] through your Holy Spirit" (p. 300).
- **Prayer J**: James and John; and "counting them among the five wise virgins" (p. 299) — Rapp flags "cross-gendered imagery in Prayers E and J, which refer to the five wise virgins of Matthew 25:1–13" (p. 84).
- **Prayer E**: negative model Cain ("not do evil in their hearts like Cain"); lamps filled with oil (p. 296).
- **Prayer D** (long, monastic): Abraham at Mamre, Isaac, Abel, Enoch, Lot, Jacob inheriting Esau's blessing, Daniel, Elijah, Elisha, three children in the furnace, Lazarus, Zacchaeus (pp. 295–96).
- **Prayer P**: Psalm 133 "Behold how good and delightful it is when brothers live together" (p. 301).
- **Ms. no. 59** (three brothers): George, Demetrios, Theodore (p. 76).
- **Absent**: "Conspicuously absent from this list of positive examples are David and Jonathan" (p. 84) — present only in the 14th-c. Bulgarian Zaykovski Trebnik (n. 86).

### 2.5 The core prayers verbatim (Rapp's translations, App. 3)
**Prayer A** (pp. 293): "Lord God, ruler of all, who has created man in your image and likeness and has given him eternal life, who has deemed it right that your holy and most famous apostles Peter, the head, and Andrew, and James and John the sons of Zebedee, and Philip and Bartholomew, become each other's brothers, not bound together by nature, but by faith and through the Holy Spirit, and who has deemed your holy martyrs Sergius and Bacchus, Cosmas and Damian, Cyrus and John worthy to become brothers: Bless also your servants NN and NN, who are not bound by nature, but by faith. Grant them to love one another, and that their brotherhood remain without hatred [amisêton] and free from offense [askandaliston] all the days of their lives through the power of your Holy Spirit, the intercession of the All-Holy [Mother of God], our immaculate Lady the holy Theotokos and ever-virgin Mary, and of holy John the forerunner and baptizer, the holy and truly renowned apostles and all your holy martyrs. For you are the unity and security and lord of peace, Christ our God, and to you we raise up glory and thanks."

**Prayer B** (p. 294): "Lord our God, who has granted everything for our salvation, and who has ordered us to love [agapan] one another and to forgive each other's trespasses. Even now, benevolent Lord, that these your servants who love one another with spiritual love [pneumatikê agapê] have come to your holy church to be blessed by you: Grant them faith without shame [pistin akataischynton], love without suspicion [agapên anhypokriton], and just as you granted your peace to your holy disciples, grant also to them everything that they ask for their salvation and grant them eternal life. For you are a merciful and benevolent God, and to you we raise up our praise, the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit."

**Prayer C** (p. 294): "...give them gladness [euphranon], so that they may become spiritual brothers more than brothers according to the flesh."

**Prayer E** (court, Constantinople; pp. 296–97): "Lord our God, who has granted everything for the benefit humankind, who has also instated spiritual brotherhood [pneumatikên adelphotêta systêsamenos] and given the desire for love [agapês], even now that these your servants want to enter into brotherhood and want to profess, before angels and people, spiritual love in everything, and who want to call each other brothers in the church and before witnesses, and who want to be sanctified through your words by the priest: Lord our God, give them love without suspicion, faith without shame, and the light of understanding, so that they may guard the declarations of spiritual brotherhood, forgive each other's trespasses, and not do evil in their hearts like Cain. [...] lighten their lamps which are filled with the oil of their works [...] For you are the progenitor of love, Christ our God."

**Prayer F** (court; p. 297): "...as these your servants NN have come to stand in the face of your holy glory and to become spiritual brothers, preserve them in your name and sanctify them in your truth, so that, having conducted themselves according to your commandments, they may become heirs of your kingdom."

**Prayer H** (lay; p. 298): "Send your plentiful mercy on these your servants for spiritual brotherhood, so that they think alike and love each other, and send down on them your Holy Spirit, as you have done on your holy apostles and prophets".

**Prayer J** (p. 299): "preserve these your servants, NN, in peace and unity of mind [homonoia] all the days of their lives, enacting your commandments. Keep their lamp unextinguished, counting them among the five wise virgins [...] for the things of the flesh are not like those of the spirit."

**Prayer K** (S. Italy; p. 299): "Make these your servants NN and NN spiritual brothers and let there be between them no scandal from the machinations of the devil and his evil spirits".

**Prayer N** (p. 300): "strengthen them to serve and be subservient to one another in the fear of God".

**Prayer O** (Athos; p. 301): "Unite also the brothers here in holy love [philêmati], in peace, and in love without suspicion".

**Prayer P** (Athos; p. 301): "Behold how good and delightful it is when brothers live together [...] through the bond of love [tô syndesmô tês agapês]."

**Deir Balizeh papyrus** (late 6th/early 7th c., Egyptian koinobion): fragmentary prayer naming God "the giver of love (agapê) and brotherly love (philadelphia), who has granted the bond of peace (en tô syndesmô tês eirênês)" with Ps. 113:5–6 as in Prayer I — "possible that this represents the trace of an early prayer of brother-making" (p. 77).

### 2.6 Key phrases and their meaning (pp. 85–86)
- *pistis akataischyntos* "faith without shame/dissembling": "a regular expression in the Greek liturgy, not specific to brother-making [...] a prayer for Christian faith that is openly professed, not shamefully hidden".
- *agapê anhypokritos* "love without suspicion": "a love devoid of hypocrisy or pretense, a 'genuine love'"; Rom. 12:9.
- *askandalistos* "free from offense": ambiguous whether an outsider or a partner is the one not to be scandalised; Rapp: "their declared intent is that henceforth each partner has the obligation to conduct himself with regard to the other so as to give no reason for shame, suspicion, or offense".
- Interpretive summary: "the prayers were intended to replicate 'through the spirit' a relationship between brothers of the same father and nothing else" (p. 84). "The relationship is anchored in the realm of God's divine love [...] with the emphasis on a bond between men that is not 'by nature,' but spiritual" (p. 83). Footnote 85: "The same language is used by Byzantine churchmen and jurisprudents to define the role of the synteknos (godfather)".
- Functional range: "They could be used to bless a close spiritual friendship of two monks, or to help two laymen to end a quarrel—or perhaps as an encouragement of two laymen who were on the same spiritual journey or pilgrimage, or indeed to make peace between two monastic brethren. The possibilities are manifold and only confirm the value of the ritual which was founded precisely in its adaptability" (p. 87). Prayer A (and I, J, M) "would be most suitable for the affirmation of spiritual relationship between two men, while Prayer B's emphasis on salvation and its mention of peace [...] could have been applicable to relations that were potentially fraught with tension" (p. 86).

### 2.7 Comparison with marriage and adoption rites
- **Marriage**: Boswell's six gestures (hands on Gospel; binding of hands with stole; hand-held cross; swords; crowning; triple circling) examined pp. 73–75. Hands on Gospel is an oath gesture common to many rites; binding of hands rests on one post-Byzantine ms.; cross on one 12th-c. ms.; swords "never prescribed"; crowning rests on a misread wedding rubric and on Harmenopoulos' prohibition on monks "holding wedding crowns" (being best man); triple circling is 14th-c. and derives from baptism (pp. 73–75). Conclusion p. 76 quoted above. Also, no rings, no crowns, no promises.
- **Baptism/initiation**: by manuscript placement, "the most pronounced association of adelphopoiesis by proximity is established with prayers for Christian initiation" (37 occurrences: baptism, tonsure of a monk, veiling of a nun, "the first haircut of a child, or a young man's offering of his first growth of beard") vs. 15 with marriage/engagement and 8 with oaths/reconciliation (p. 63). The Slavonic Euchologium Sinaiticum places brother-making right after "four prayers for the cutting of hair or beard" (p. 69). Triple circling = initiation to a new status (p. 75). Hence Rapp: "There is thus no overwhelming reason to follow either Boswell, who associates adelphopoiesis exclusively with marriage, or Panagou, who sees a relation by proximity to filial adoption" (p. 63).
- **Adoption**: Panagou reads proximity to filial adoption; Arranz labelled the rite "adoptive brotherhood"; Frček and Holosnjaj translate the Slavonic/Bulgarian rite as "fraternal adoption" (pp. 58 n. 22, 68 n. 43, 69 n. 47). Rapp resists the label but notes the Slavonic silent prayer speaks of God granting "us to receive each other in love as his adopted sons" (p. 69).

### 2.8 The 1985 "sister-making" (the only fully described woman–woman performance) (pp. 50–52)
Robin Darling Young's account: Syrian Orthodox Archbishop Dionysius Behnam Jajaweh "remarked that since we had survived the rigors of Syria and Eastern Turkey in amicable good humor, we two women must be good friends indeed. Would we like to be joined as sisters [...]? [...] After the liturgy, the bishop had us join our right hands together and he wrapped them in a portion of his garment. He pronounced a series of prayers over us, told us that we were united as sisters, and admonished us not to quarrel. Ours was a sisterhood stronger than blood, confirmed in the outpouring of the Holy Spirit, he said, and since it was a spiritual union, it would last beyond the grave." The archbishop's own account of the pilgrim custom (Tur Abdin Syrian Orthodox): prayer at five stations of the Holy Sepulchre complex, "whether two or ten together hold hands above this holy site", e.g. "O Lord Jesus, make your grave the basis of spiritual brotherhood and faith-filled love among us [...] that we may correspond to each other as brothers and sisters in your holy name"; then "they hug each other and kiss each other spiritually and in faith and love, and then greet each other as brothers or sisters when they write to each other" (p. 51). "Gender was irrelevant, as was the number of participants, or indeed their Christian denomination" (p. 52). Rapp's gloss: "Pilgrimage is essentially a displaced monastic experience with an expiration date, and—like monasticism—is bound to transform human relations. Spiritual kinship as 'brothers' and 'sisters' seems to be the most appropriate way to conceptualize such relations, applying to lay men and women the same designation as that for monks and nuns" (p. 53).

### 2.9 Manuscript-use evidence of paired women
Vaticanus graecus 1811 (no. 13), S. Italy, 1147: diptychs on fly-leaves commemorate "individuals [...] in pairs: men and women, men and men, women and women, for example, Constantine and Eirene, Peter and Apollinarius, Kale and Zoe. The paired commemoration is striking and may perhaps be explained through the application of adelphopoiesis between members of the same religious association" (p. 68). Grottaferrata Gamma beta II (no. 9), 11th c.: "Remember, o Lord, your servant [singular!] Theodore and Philip and forgive them [!]" — joint forgiveness suggesting "the kind of committed brotherhood bond" (p. 68).

## 3. Legal and social dossier

### 3.1 The three (really four) ways to make kin in Byzantium (pp. 9–13)
"In addition to marriage, several strategies were available to expand the kingroup through the careful selection of suitable men and women: adoption, godparenthood, and brother-making. It is important to understand their similarities of purpose but difference in application and scope" (p. 9). Rapp's structural scheme, from the jurists: relations "by nature" (*physei*) vs. "by arrangement" (*thesei*); the latter "include relations by filial adoption and relations by ritual brotherhood" (p. 232), and also synteknia.

| Relation | Created by | Legal recognition | Marriage bars | Inheritance | Who may enter |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

### 3.2 What adelphopoiesis did and did not create — the jurists' answers (ch. 5, pp. 230–246)
- **Earliest legal text**: *Peira* 49.11 (11th c.): adelphopoiesis "was a relation affecting only the two individuals involved and that marriage prohibitions resulting from this relation applied only to them, but not to their kin [...] It also raises the possibility of a man and a woman becoming ritual siblings, which would—at least according to some—constitute an obstacle to a later marriage" (p. 237).
- **Anonymous appendix to the Synopsis Basilicorum maior (late 11th c.)**: "Among the relations 'by arrangement,' only filial adoption is granted recognition. Adelphopoiesis is declared to be 'against the law,' the text continues, 'and there is no good reason for it'" (p. 234).
- **Niketas of Thessaloniki (12th c.)**, to priests asking "If some wish to make adelphopoiia, should we make them, or not?": "it is superfluous and only leads to great sins, explaining that all mankind has been made brothers through baptism and that the only legitimate brotherhood relations either by nature or 'by arrangement' are those involving a father" (p. 239).
- **Demetrios Chomatenos (1216–c. 1236)**: "But brotherhood by arrangement, which is of undetermined [asystatos] and unaffirmed [abebaios] status, does not correspond to nature, and is not recognized by the law, presents no obstacle to marriage whatsoever" (pp. 243–44). To King Stephen of Serbia: "These are not recognized by the church" (p. 244). Godparenthood by contrast: "the godfather and the biological father become 'one person' (heis anthrôpos), their children are called 'brothers'" (p. 243). Rationale: godparenthood and adoption are recognised "because they imitate nature in bringing forth sons, while it is not possible to create a brother for oneself" (p. 245).
- **John Pediasimos, *Peri gamôn* (1275–1335)**: "Even if a man and a woman had been joined in brother-making, this was not an obstacle to their marriage, unless it had been concluded secretly"; he "is one of the few legal experts of the early fourteenth century who threatened punishment for the priests who performed the ritual" (p. 234); he "explicitly barred from marriage men and women who were joined in adelphopoiesis and who had engaged in sexual relations, unless they confessed to this infringement prior to the wedding" (p. 237).
- **Matthaios Blastares, *Syntagma* (1335)**: adelphopoiia "is brought about by 'no praiseworthy' motivation. The only legitimate purpose which he could imagine for adelphopoiesis would be to secure an heir, but, as he was quick to point out, the legal relationship that fulfills that function is filial adoption" (p. 235).
- **Harmenopoulos, *Hexabiblos* (c. 1345)**: mentions the relation "in the context of inheritance rights and of fraternal adoption, where brother-making was equally declared to be without consequence. Harmenopoulos's treatment of adelphopoiesis reveals the two areas where the relation was likely to generate confusion and which legal experts needed to address: marriage prohibitions and inheritance rights" (p. 235).
- **Manuel Malaxos (late 16th c.)**: "the frequent occurrence of subsequent marriage between a man and a woman who had previously contracted brother-making had led the church to prohibit the relation altogether. Any such existing relations should be regarded as null and void [...] Whoever engaged in brother-making, including the priest who performed the prayers, should henceforth be punished" (p. 238). Also cites men who "had dissolved the brotherhood that they had sworn on a Gospel book" (p. 239).
- **Lone dissenting ms.**: Codex Parisinus graecus 1384 "prohibits marriages between the sons and daughters of ritual brothers" (p. 240 n. 28).
- **Rapp's synthesis**: "The jurisprudents of all periods insisted that the relation enjoyed no legal status and had no legal consequences" (p. 239). "Only beginning with the early fourteenth century were condemnations issued and efforts put into place to curb the practice" (p. 239). "For all the references to adelphopoiesis as being unlawful, unrecognized, unreasonable, and altogether unnecessary, Byzantine legal authors were on the whole remarkably quiet about the potential of the relation to cover up or to legitimize male-male sexual relations or to facilitate larger associations of a subversive nature. To the contrary, adelphopoiesis may have been relegated to the margins of legality, but it was an openly recognized mainstay of society" (p. 242).

### 3.3 Revocability (p. 238–39)
"Marriages can end in divorce, oaths can be broken, children can be disinherited. Was it possible to dissolve a relation of spiritual brotherhood? The closest analogy of a spiritual kinship relation, baptismal co-godparenthood (synteknia), does not seem to permit an annulment of this bond. There are only the faintest glimpses of such a possibility for brother-making" — Malaxos's dissolved Gospel-sworn brotherhoods, and Ragusa 1487/1492 (notarized formation and dissolution). Monastic dissolution: at Phoibammon (634–35) David's renunciation of Jacob "required a written declaration in the presence of three other monks" plus a public prayer; "The fact that a prayer accompanied the dissolution of a paired monastic relationship would suggest that prayers and declarations of some kind had also been performed when it was formed" (pp. 113–14).

### 3.4 Inheritance and property — what people *believed* vs. what law allowed (pp. 240–41)
- "The legal sources, with a single exception, insisted that it was a lateral bond limited to the two men involved and that it carried no marriage prohibitions for the next generation. [...] At the same time, the frequent injunctions to refrain from brother-making as a means to pass on one's inheritance imply the possibility of a cross-generational extension of the original bond, from one's ritual brother to his son" (p. 240).
- "Many people were of the opinion that the property of the two brothers was somehow held in common and passed on jointly to their heirs. In this way, it would have been possible to retain property within the 'family' circle, and to counteract the imperial efforts from the late tenth century to discourage close marriage in order to prevent the land-holding aristocracy from becoming too powerful. Adelphopoiesis between distant relatives might well have been employed as an antidote to the economic effects of exogamy" (p. 241).
- Manuel II's chrysobull for Athos (1406): monks who have made synteknia or adelphopoiia with laymen "shall no longer go to their houses, nor take lunch or dinner with them, or indeed join in drinking parties with them, nor should they leave anything to them as their heirs" — "the danger of the alienation of a monk's personal property to an outside heir that would otherwise pass into the ownership of the monastery" (p. 198).
- Danelis (9th c.): "named Leo as the heir to her possessions" despite a living grandson Daniel (pp. 204–05); "The relation is interpreted as creating obligations as well as ties of property and inheritance that extend over three generations, from Danelis to Basil and on to Leo" (p. 205).
- Kievan Caves Paterikon: Ioann "entrusted the inheritance for his five-year-old son to Sergij", his spiritual brother (p. 223).
- Monastic cell inheritance between pairs: Labla papyrus (511): "after my death my cell will belong to Eulogios" (p. 111); Nikon of the Black Mountain, typikon ch. 11: "If, however, a fellow brother also cohabits with [the dying man], he has authority over whatever belongs to [the dying monk's] lot" — "firmly grants inheritance rights only to the former" (pp. 176–77); Euthymios's dotation c. 1000 deeding land to disciple John who "will designate his own heir from among his monks" (pp. 172–73); Samuel "was his heir forever" of Agathos (p. 104); Apollo's brother "had left him his virtues as an inheritance" (p. 105 n. 45).
- Western notarised *affrèrement*: "declare their intent to combine their possessions, hold them in joint ownership, and pass them on equitably to their heirs [...] sharing 'one bread and wine'" (pp. 21–22); Ragusa 1487: "to stay and to reside together, sharing the same bread and the same wine, as is the custom of good brothers [...] an agreement that also extends to their heirs and legal successors" (p. 24). Calabria 13th c.: laymen "designate a monastic community as their legitimate heir while at the same time joining the monks as a 'brother'" (p. 20). Lavra 1013: Maria and Constantine Lagoudes donate and become "united in the spirit" with the monks, "becoming one soul" with them (p. 20).

### 3.5 Godparenthood and baptism as the template of artificial kinship
"At baptism, the sponsor takes spiritual responsibility for the neophyte and thus becomes his spiritual 'father.' [...] Other such moments of offering oneself up to God were marked by the ritual of hair clipping, whether offering the first growth of beard, or the cutting of hair at monastic initiation for both women and men. In all these instances, a sponsor (abbot, spiritual father, baptismal sponsor, godfather) takes on the role of spiritual guide, who assists in the crossing of the threshold to a more committed Christian life. Such assistance, as shall be seen below, could also be offered between equals, in a nonhierarchical relationship" (p. 89). Kinship terms used as titles from the 8th–9th c.: Philaretos "grandfather of the emperor", Danelis "mother of the emperor" ("the first woman to be honored with a kinship designation as a title", p. 204 n. 65), Zaoutzes "father of the emperor" (p. 212). Olga of Kiev baptised with Constantine VII as godfather and made *zostê patrikia*, "Lady with the Belt" (p. 214).

### 3.6 Women in adelphopoiesis — all the evidence Rapp gives
1. **Programmatic statement** (p. 1): "commonly practiced among Byzantine men, sometimes also between men and women and between women and women".
2. **Woman–woman**: "The only indication of a bond of ritual sisterhood between women of which I am aware comes from hagiography: the proposed compact between Maria of Amnia and the other contestants in the bride show [...] as told in the Life of Philaretos of the ninth century" (p. 237). Text: "Dear sisters, let us make a pact [syndesmon] between ourselves of the kind called sisterhood [adelphosynê] that she who becomes empress shall assist the others" (p. 216). Rapp's gloss: "This was an environment in which male-male bonding and female-female bonding were cultivated and became a recognizable part of the social fabric" (p. 221); Maria is "one of the rare examples of a woman proposing 'ritual sisterhood'" (pp. 163–64).
3. **Woman–woman diptychs**: Vat. gr. 1811 (1147) "Kale and Zoe" paired commemoration (p. 68).
4. **Woman–woman, modern**: 1985 Holy Sepulchre sister-making (pp. 50–52); Serbian *posestrimstvo* "sworn sisterhood between two women, is also attested, but does not seem to be of concern to the authorities and the fear of sexual relations is never articulated in this context" (p. 259); Mani: "two men (more rarely two women)" (p. 254).
5. **Woman–man** (common): Peira (p. 237); Pediasimos (pp. 227, 234, 237); Chomatenos's court case of the maidservant Kale acting "on behalf of her 'brother by arrangement'" (p. 245–46); the early-16th-c. poem mocking "women who attempted to disguise their love affairs under the cover of either synteknia or adelphopoiesis" (p. 237); Tzetzes on women "making bold 'like adelphopoietoi'" (p. 227); Vision of Anastasia condemning "those who fabricate a fraternal relationship in order to commit adultery" (p. 227); Pseudo-Chrysostom on sex with the wife of one's adelphopoiêtos as incest (p. 238); Serbian *pobratimstvo pricestno* begun by a sick young woman asking a man's help "as a brother" (p. 259); Serbian church suspended the rite in 1975 after a ritual-sibling pair sought marriage (pp. 259–60). Rapp's open question: "Did women ever initiate them? In what social milieu was this deemed expedient?" (p. 227).
6. **Woman as instigator**: Danelis arranges her son's brotherhood with Basil — "Clearly, the instigator of this bond of brotherhood is an ambitious mother" (p. 203), modelled on Queen Kandake who "wished to make him her son" (p. 203).
7. **Paired women ascetics** (monastic analogue): Nonna "together with her ever-virgin daughter" (p. 111); Aurelia Theodora and Aurelia Tauris, "monachai apotaktikai", co-leasing property, 400 CE (p. 111); Marana and Cyra, Theodoret HR 29, two noble women walled in together outside Beroea with chains (p. 120); Makrina and Emmelia buried in one grave so their "experience of community [...] should not even in death be undone [diazeuchthênai]" (p. 152); Edessa (Macedonia) tombstones: "Tomb of the deaconess Theodosia and of Aspilia and Agathoklia virgins" etc. — "the women had been living together and were buried together" (p. 155); Eusebia the Macedonian deaconess and the wife of Kaisarios buried together, "characterized by charis and adherence to the same dogma and worship" (p. 156); Anna abbess of Bardaine and her "spiritual mother" (p. 166); Thebes confraternity (1048) of "clergy, laymen, and women" calling itself an *adelphotês* (p. 19); Epiros 1225 list with a nun and "women and men in equal proportion" (p. 19). Rapp: "very little is known about women who lived by themselves as hermits and even less about women who lived in pairs" (p. 119).
8. **Liturgical silence**: "What happened when a man and a woman wanted to receive the prayers? This last scenario is known from hagiographies, historical narratives, and official prohibitions, but not provided for in the liturgical texts" (p. 87). All Greek prayers are masculine-plural for two men; "could the ritual have been adapted for relations between men and women, as the narrative evidence suggests? [...] It would have been easy enough for the celebrant to adapt the text of the ritual as the situation required" (pp. 64, 237).

### 3.7 Eunuchs (all mentions)
- Marriage: "Most women and men in Byzantium were married at one point in their lives, with the possible exception of nuns, monks, and eunuchs" (p. 10).
- Adoption: "The legislation of Leo VI explicitly allowed childless women and eunuchs to adopt" (p. 13).
- Corporate solidarity: "two eunuchs purchased a property outside Rome together, and two other eunuchs made arrangements to share the same tomb, whether out of economic considerations or because of personal attachment remains unclear. These are interesting cases of the pursuit of shared interest similar to the known instances of adelphopoiesis" (p. 15).
- Philotheos and John (11th c.): "John, who was a eunuch, became the oikonomos of the monastery"; the pair "pooled their financial resources"; Symeon the New Theologian appears to Philotheos "wearing the distinguished garment of a high-ranking eunuch [...] Is it possible that this identification of Symeon as a eunuch carries a double meaning within this story, affording special validation to John's physical state?" (pp. 169–70).
- Court offices of intimacy: *parakoimomenos* "the one who sleeps next" to the emperor; *synkellos* "cell-mate" of the Patriarch (p. 216).

### 3.8 Monastic pairs and "spiritual families" — the phenomenology (ch. 3)
Rapp's indicators of a brother-making relationship (pp. 148–57, 160): a joint purpose; shared or adjacent living arrangements; "sharing of spiritual capital" via vicarious penance ("he had laid up a heavenly bank account of good deeds which was large enough to share with others", p. 148); mutual prayer; a contractual/oath element ("had sworn an oath to each other that they would never be separated from each other, either in life or in death", p. 150); a ritual moment of blessing; joint death and burial ("Not only are their souls united in Heaven, but also their bodies", p. 153). Reform typika institutionalised pairing: Evergetis ch. 24 "there should be two of you in your cells united by the law of spiritual love, being of the same mind and really living together as brothers in harmony, bearing the same yoke of Our Lord" (p. 174); Kosmosoteira 1152 "two in each cell [...] as brothers one in soul" (p. 175); Phoberos: three per cell as three generations (p. 175); Athos *skêtê*: "an elder, his disciple, and a young apprentice-servant, with the expectation that after the death of the elder, the next generation would move on to the next higher position of seniority" (p. 172). Monastic families cross gender in the Theodore Stoudite case: "the male and female members settled in separate communities on their one remaining property, in Sakkoudion" under uncle Plato (p. 163). Hierarchical pairs: father/son discipleship (Agathos/Samuel: clothing, prayer invoking "monastic ancestors", prostration, service, heirship, pp. 103–04); Athanasios the Athonite blessed two brothers "by putting their hands into his" and told them to call each other "father" (p. 172).

### 3.9 How the Church policed it
- **Monks first** (9th c. on): Theodore the Stoudite, Testament (806–09): "You who have fled from the world and from marriage, do not maintain relations of adelphopoiia or synteknia with lay people. For this is not found in the Fathers. And even if it is found, then only rarely, and this is not the rule [ou nomos]" (pp. 193–94). Typikon of John Tzimiskes 971/2: "None of the brothers is to be allowed to leave the mountain to form a bond of synteknia or adelphopoiesis with laymen. If some of them have already concluded a bond of this sort for themselves, they must still not go off to their houses or have lunch or dinner with them or join them at all in drinking" (p. 195). Nikephoros Chartophylax (11th c.): "For the law does absolutely not recognize the so-called 'brother-makings'" (p. 196). Peter Chartophylax: godfathering, brother-making, holding crowns "are against the canons (of the church) and prohibited" (p. 196). Patriarch Athanasius I (1303–09) ruled against "sworn associations [synômosiai] and drinking parties [symposia] [...] also from special friendships [philia merikê]" (p. 197); for laymen "he demanded that priests prohibit ritual brotherhood [...] he ruled that priests should prevent the formation of 'brotherhoods' (adelphosynai, plural) by imposing a penance. If penance could not be applied, he requested that the priests report such brotherhoods to the civil authority" (p. 239). Patriarch Matthew I (1397–1410): not permitted "to conclude friendships, adelphopoiia, or the so-called synteknia with them, and to conduct business in the form of loans, worldly exchanges, and trade" (p. 198). Chomatenos to an abbot: "especially you must prevent brother-makings and everything else that would make them inclined toward the world" (p. 244).
- **Reason given**: "The greatest concern was not the participation in the ecclesiastical ritual of brother-making in and of itself, but the social consequences that the relationship entailed: familiarity, ease of access, and the opportunity for socializing, especially at feasts that involve drinking" (p. 195); "Adelphopoiesis inside monasteries was never affected by any of these rulings" (p. 231).
- **Laity**: no prohibition before the early 14th c. (p. 239). Even then "We are not informed whether these rigorous demands were ever put into practice" (p. 240); post-1453 "there are no known cases of priests being defrocked on these grounds" (p. 252). 18th-c. Karpenisi: "any priests who performed prayers for it were to be defrocked, and laymen who participated in it were to be excommunicated" (p. 253). Goar's 1638 editorial note: "although this ritual has been prohibited by ecclesiastical and imperial law, we have nevertheless printed it exactly as we have found it in many other codices" (p. 58). Catholic Dalmatia/Bosnia 16th–18th c.: priests warned against rites "between men and women, between Catholics and 'Greek schismatics,' and between Turks and Christians" (p. 259). Constantine Kostenencki (15th c.) condemned blood exchange "especially between Christians and Muslims, as a heresy" (p. 256). Chalcedon canon 18 (451): "Secret unions [synômosiai] and associations [phratriai] are forbidden even by the secular laws; and much more is it becoming that they should be forbidden in the Church of God" (p. 197).
- **Rapp's structural reading**: "Why the relation came under attack at that particular time. Was it the moral impropriety of hetero- or homosexual relations that might occur under the cover of ritual brotherhood? Was it the fear of secret associations that might threaten social stability and political order? Was it because the increased role of the church in defining social relationships had become objectionable to the state?" (p. 242).

### 3.10 Social functions (summary quotations)
- Boundary-crossing: "Adelphopoiesis, as we have seen, had the potential to join men and women, Orthodox and Catholic, and Greeks and Latins" (p. 230); "to join like with like, but also like and unlike" (p. 191).
- Patronage ladder: Kekaumenos warns that "greedy and ambitious men also know to exploit other means to establish a relation of patronage and obligation with their betters, namely through adelphopoiesis, synteknia, and by offering their children as sons- or daughters-in-law" (p. 225). "In both versions, it was the socially superior who initiated the brotherhood relation by granting gifts and favors in the expectation of future recompense after a reversal of fortunes. Brother-making was not a fleeting sentiment or a spur-of-the-moment decision, it was for the long haul" (p. 210).
- Household access: "As Leo's ritual brother, he enjoyed the same liberty with the women of the household as a family member" (Nikolaos Mystikos visiting pregnant Zoe daily, p. 218).
- Treachery topos: Chronicle of Morea: "Never believe a Roman in whatever he may swear to you; when he wants and desires to betray you, then he makes you godfather of his child or his ritual brother [adelphopoieton] or he makes you an in-law so that he may exterminate you" (p. 226).
- Puchner's post-Byzantine functions list: "protection and support of those in need, the old, and the sick; as social advancement and assurance of protection by people of means and influence; as a psychological strategy of uniting fighters in times of crisis; as a strategy to supplement family relations in small communities; and as an expression of intimate friendship" (p. 229 n. 146).

### 7.1 Scripted "Sister-Making" ceremony outline (Vow of the Last Vial), built from the rite dossier
Stage directions in brackets cite the source element.

1. **Approach.** The two daughters enter the church together and stand "in front of the table on which the Gospel is laid out" [p. 48]. A deaconess with a censer or candle attends [p. 52]; the community is present as witnesses [Prayer E, p. 296; p. 86]. If a sponsor is present she stands between them, as Nikon knelt "placing Symeon on his right and John on his left" [p. 159].
2. **Hands on the Book.** Each lays her right hand on the Gospel, "one on top of the other" [p. 48]; if the pair is unequal in age or rank, the elder's hand lies above [ms. 43, p. 76]. No words are spoken by the pair: "in none of the manuscript versions of the ritual do the two men speak in their own voice" [p. 29]. The faith reads this silence as the vow being *given* rather than *sworn*.
3. **First prayer (adapted Prayer A).** The priestess recites, with the exemplars swapped to the faith's own pairs but keeping the hinge-clause: "Bless also your servants NN and NN, who are not bound by nature, but by faith. Grant them to love one another, and that their sisterhood remain without hatred and free from offense all the days of their lives" [p. 293]. Mod-specific exemplars may replace Sergius/Bacchus etc.; Rapp notes the invocation of "twins—the closest nonhierarchical biological relation imaginable" [p. 84] as the ideal.
4. **Reading.** John 13:1–17, the washing of feet, "the ultimate example of brotherly love" [p. 69]. (Optional in-game: the sponsor washes the new daughter's feet.)
5. **Second prayer (adapted Prayer B).** "Grant them faith without shame, love without suspicion, and just as you granted your peace to your holy disciples, grant also to them everything that they ask for their salvation" [p. 294]. For a sponsor–daughter vow use Prayer C's line "that they may become spiritual sisters more than sisters according to the flesh" [p. 294]; for a reconciliation vow use Prayer E's Cain clause [p. 296].
6. **The Cross and the Binding.** The priestess holds a hand-cross over the joined hands [Sinai gr. 973, p. 74] and wraps them in her stole or garment [1985 account, p. 50; Russian belt rubric, p. 73 n. 64]. The mod's "belt/girdle" and the sponsor's hood (T14) are given here [Barsanuphius, p. 110].
7. **The Last Vial.** Shared cup: red wine sipped "in turn, three times" [Serbian practice, p. 69 n. 44; Durham, p. 258] — the historical substitute for blood [p. 249]. Rapp notes a 1536 Cypriot ms. with prayers "peri pneumatikês haimomixias" [p. 249 n. 2]; the faith's "Last Vial" can be presented as the sanctified form of this. In a monastic setting substitute the presanctified gifts [p. 69].
8. **Kiss and bow.** The priestess kisses both; they kiss each other; both kiss the Gospel [pp. 69, 76].
9. **Procession and feast.** The priestess "takes the 'older [sister]' by the hand, who then takes the hand of [the] 'younger,'" and leads them out to a common meal [p. 69]; the public feast "made the relationship public and allowed associates, friends, and kin to take a stake in it" [p. 249]. Optional post-1300 variant: triple circling of the table, the "Dance of Isaiah", marking "initiation to a new status" [p. 75].
10. **Record.** Names entered as a pair in the diptychs for commemoration "Remember, o Lord, your servant[s] NN and NN and forgive them" [p. 68]; in-game, a permanent paired entry in the character's history.
11. **Afterlife clause (flavour text).** "since it was a spiritual union, it would last beyond the grave" [p. 50]; sisters "often prayed to God, that he would take the two of them together" [p. 160].

### 7.2 Notes for the writers
- Keep the rite *unspoken by the parties*: that is the single most distinctive Byzantine feature versus Western oath-brotherhood (p. 29) and versus marriage (p. 73 n. 59). The vow is a blessing received, not a promise exchanged.
- Rapp's hard "no" list for the Byzantine period: no rings, no crowns, no swords, no triple circling before the 14th c., communion rare in Greek practice (pp. 69–76). Using them is a deliberate post-Byzantine or heterodox flourish, which the faith may own.
- The chronology Rapp gives for the real rite's social career can pace the mod's hostility curve: monastic blessing (4th–7th c.) → named rite among monks, clerics, emperors (7th c.) → lay strategy and monastic bans (9th–11th c.) → legal "not recognised" (11th–13th c.) → punishments threatened (14th c.) → post-Byzantine survival, excommunications (16th–18th c.).
