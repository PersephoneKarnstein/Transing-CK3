**What was read.** Both articles in full, footnotes included. Johnson (1998), Biller (1992) and Katz (1999), which Resnick and Roguin cite, were not read. Their positions are given only as Resnick reports them.

**Relation to existing notes.** `judaism_reading_list.md` (Part B, finding 6 and §5, entry 12) set this as a hard limit before the sources were read: no Jewish character with a bloody flux, "weak as women", or melancholic. `cmp_devun_shape_of_sex.md` and `cmp_wingard_trans_middle_ages.md` give the parallel Christian link between Jews and unstable sex. This note is **reaction data the mod must avoid**. It lists the tropes, who voiced them, and where, so that `faith_design.md` §13 can name them.

**Why it matters more for this faith than for any other.** The Nehpakhot are Jews whom the law reads as men, and who become women. A Christian line about "Jewish men who become women" lands directly on a trope that says Jewish men already are womanish and bleed like women. The three lamps include "the days of separation" (*niddah*). So any text in which an outsider notices a daughter keeping the days, or bleeding, reproduces the myth.

## 1. The argument in brief

### Resnick (2000)

The myth that Jewish men menstruate has 13th-century roots. Three strands met:
1. **Theology** (pp. 244–252). Menstruation was the curse of Eve. Jews were cursed for the blood of Christ (Matt. 27:25: "his blood be upon us and on our children"). So Jewish men were said to bleed as punishment.
2. **Natural philosophy** (pp. 252–256). Scholastic science explained the bleeding as hemorrhoids, the purging of melancholic blood, caused by Jewish diet, fear and idleness.
3. **Conflation** (pp. 257–261). The pseudo-Albertian *De secretis mulierum* and others joined the two. Jacques de Vitry supplied the monthly cycle.

The myth then served as a motive for the blood libel: Jews needed Christian blood to stanch their flow (pp. 244, 261–262). Resnick knows Willis Johnson's 1998 counter-argument, and thanks him for a pre-publication copy (p. 244 n. 12). According to `judaism_reading_list.md`, Johnson holds that a gendered "menses" belief appears only around 1500, and that earlier references to a flux "were not gendered". Resnick's own evidence supports a middle position. Before c. 1300, most texts speak of a "flux" or hemorrhoids. The explicit word "menstruation" comes with Cecco d'Ascoli (c. 1324), *De secretis mulierum* (late 13th or early 14th c.) and *Omnes homines* (early 14th c.?). The monthly cycle comes with Jacques de Vitry.

### Roguin Maor et al. (2021)

A short survey by physicians that follows Resnick, Johnson and Biller. It adds:
- a date of 1219 for Jacques de Vitry (p. 2);
- the early-modern Spanish afterlife: Quiñones and Huerta in 1632, Aznar Cardona in 1612 on "lizard tails", and Torrejoncillo on Good Friday (pp. 4–5);
- the Thuringian expulsion of 1400, explained by the Jews' need for blood "to heal a wound that flowed in them perpetually" (p. 4);
- Tyrnau, 1494, where fourteen Jews were burned (p. 4).

**Reliability.** It is lower than Resnick's:
- It gives Caesarius of Heisterbach a monthly-flow quotation ("unwarlike even more than women, everywhere serfs, suffering from a flow every month", p. 2) that Resnick attributes to the *Historia orientalis* tradition, not Caesarius. Resnick's Caesarius story is about Good Friday.
- It says Albert called Jews prone to "envy, extreme hairiness, and *luxuria*" (p. 3) without a citation.
- It reads the Jewish name "Raquel" in the *Cantar de mio Cid* as feminising (p. 3). That reading is *unverified* and contested.

Use Roguin only for the early-modern and Nazi-era afterlife (pp. 4–5), and Resnick for everything medieval.

| 9 | **Idleness and fear** cause cold, womanish bodies: "they are in idleness"; "in fear and anxiety" | Bernard of Gordon, *Lilium medicinae* 5.21 (Montpellier, c. 1303); *Omnes homines* | Resnick pp. 257, 260–261; Roguin p. 4 |

| 16 | The woman with the issue of blood (Luke 8) as the figure of the **Jew healed by conversion** | Radbertus and others | Resnick pp. 249–250 |
| 17 | Cain's "trembling head", a "quaking heart" | Jacques de Vitry | Resnick p. 259; Roguin p. 2 |
| 18 | Tails, smell, pallor (early modern) | Vincent Ferrer (as Cardoso cites him); Spain 1612–32; England 1648 | Resnick pp. 262–263; Roguin pp. 4–5 |

What **Jewish** medicine said: Maimonides' *Medical Aphorisms* and his treatise on hemorrhoids, and Avicenna's *Canon*, treat hemorrhoids without calling them commoner in Jews (Resnick p. 252 and n. 46; Roguin p. 3). The first known Jewish rebuttal is Isaac Cardoso's *Las excelencias de los Hebreos* (1679) (Resnick p. 263; Roguin p. 5). Resnick found "little evidence" that medieval Jews responded to the canard at all (p. 263).

## 3. What matters for the design

- **Both of the faith's physical marks sit on trope ground.** The faith turns men into women, which touches tropes 1, 2, 10 and 11. Its women keep *niddah* ("the days"), which touches tropes 1, 2 and 15.
- **The Purim setting.** Trope 14 names "Purim cakes" with Christian blood. The Purim feast in `tm_hipukh.0011` and `.0002` must never show baked food in a way a hostile Christian event could gloss. Never pair Purim food with blood or with a Christian child. This includes any Christian "they say the Jews at Purim…" line.
- **Conversion.**
  - **Christian side.** Tropes 4 and 16 make conversion the "cure" of a Jewish body. A Christian event in which a daughter of the Turning is "healed" or "made whole" by baptism must not exist.
  - **The sect's own voice.** Its "turning" must never be phrased as the stopping of a flow, or as healing.
- **Melancholy.** Trope 7 makes Jews pale, timid and solitary, and trope 9 idle and fearful. So do not give daughters of the Turning, or any Jewish character, the melancholic, shy or craven traits as a faith or event default, and do not write pallor into a description. (Individual random traits are vanilla's business.)
- **Montpellier.** Bernard of Gordon taught at Montpellier, a holy site of this faith, and his *Lilium* carries trope 6 and trope 9. Shatzmiller (1994, p. 53) notes that the *Lilium* was translated into Hebrew. Never make it the book in `tm_hipukh.0030`, or quote it anywhere.
- **Good Friday.** Trope 3 sets the flux on Good Friday. Purim usually falls in Lent, about a month before Passover and Easter. Avoid any Christian event that ties the Turning to Holy Week or to Easter, for example a Christian lord who reacts to the dress "in the week of the Passion".

## 4. Corrections

- **`judaism_reading_list.md`, Part B, finding 6.** The Jacques de Vitry quotation is confirmed in Resnick (p. 259) as well as Roguin (p. 2). Resnick reads it "weak even as women"; Roguin gives "weak as women". The Latin is *imbelles et imbecilles facti sunt quasi mulieres* (Resnick p. 259 n. 64). Date it c. 1219–21. The reading list's attribution "quoted in Roguin Maor et al. 2021, p. 2" stands.
- **Who said "menstruation" first.** Before c. 1300 the medieval texts mostly say "flux" or hemorrhoids. The finding is better put as: "a monthly flux from Jacques de Vitry (c. 1220); 'menstruation' by name by c. 1300–1324 (pseudo-Albert, Cecco d'Ascoli)". This also keeps Johnson's objection in view.

## 5. Design implications (hard limits to add to `faith_design.md` §13)

Proposed wording for the §13 hard-limits paragraph:

> **The male-menses myth** (`research/jud_male_menses_myth.md`). No text in any faith, in any voice, may:
> - give a Jew, or a daughter of the Turning, a "flux", hemorrhoids, monthly bleeding, or "the curse of Eve";
> - call Jewish men womanish, unwarlike, weak "as women", pale, timid, melancholic, idle or fearful;
> - mention Matt. 27:25 ("his blood be upon us"), Ps. 78:66 ("smitten in the hinder parts"), the woman with the issue of blood as a figure of conversion, or the red heifer as proof of Jewish impurity;
> - pair Purim, Passover or a wedding with blood, or with a Christian child;
> - show baptism "healing" a Jewish body;
> - have an outsider notice or comment on a daughter keeping *niddah*;
> - quote or name Bernard of Gordon's *Lilium medicinae* (Montpellier) on Jews.
>
> Christian hostility to the Turning attacks the law and the doctrine (Deut. 22:5, "a man maimed", "one does not cite miracles"), never the Jewish body.
