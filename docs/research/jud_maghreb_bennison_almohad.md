## 2. The legal status of Jews

- **The *dhimma* (box, p. 165).** It was "an unwritten contract". Non-Muslim monotheists could practise discreetly and had their lives and property protected, in return for obedience and the *jizya*. Optional extras were bans on building, on bearing arms and on posts with power over Muslims, and rules on clothing or marks. These rules were "more honoured in the breach than the application".
- **Numbers and place.** The Jewish population was smaller than the Christian one. It was spread "from Ifriqiya through the Maghrib and western Sahara to al-Andalus", was mostly urban, and had Judaeo-Berber villages in the west. Late reports of whole Berber tribes practising Judaism are "late and unreliable" (p. 167).
- **Almoravids (c. 1050–1147).** Orthodox Maliki dhimma practice (p. 169). Two Maghrebi acts are recorded: the Aghmat levy of c. 1071, and the exclusion of Jews from Marrakesh at night under ʿAli b. Yusuf (p. 168). The Jews of the Marrakesh region lived at Aghmat Ilan, "a pleasant town … with full prosperity" (al-Idrisi, p. 168). In Seville the jurist Ibn ʿAbdun wanted Muslims barred from serving Jews and Christians "as bath-house staff, latrine cleaners or grooms". That implies non-Muslim masters who employed Muslims (p. 169).
- **Almohads (1147–1229).** At the start they "rejected the dhimma in toto", on the grounds that all monotheists should accept Ibn Tumart as *mahdī* (p. 171). Al-Nuwayri's line is that there was no church or synagogue left in ʿAbd al-Muʾmin's lands (p. 172). Bennison qualifies this in three ways:
  1. The Almohads tried to convert *all* monotheists, Maliki Muslims included, so minorities were not singled out (p. 172).
  2. They probably could not enforce it "except in isolated incidents designed to act as exemplary warnings" (p. 172).
  3. Treatment varied. Towns that submitted (Sousse, Sfax) were left alone. Resisting towns (Tunis) suffered general confiscation (p. 173).
- **The converts' second generation.** Ibn ʿAqnin reports that "new" Muslims of Jewish origin were barred from owning slaves and from marrying "old" Muslim women (p. 175). Al-Mansur's decree followed from his suspicion that "God only knows what their hearts conceal or their homes contain" (al-Marrakushi, quoted p. 175). Bennison ties it to the exile of Ibn Rushd to Lucena in the same years (p. 175).
- **Aftermath.** After the empire fell, Jews "reverted to Judaism", and in "more isolated locations, conversion had probably never been enforced". Jews were again important in commerce and government in the successor states (p. 176). Bennison calls the Almohad interlude "transient, albeit harsh" (p. 176).

## 3. Jews at court: service and its dangers

- Rulers often preferred "Christian or Jewish advisers, physicians, bodyguards and tax collectors". Muslims could read that as a breach of the dhimma, and it could provoke "popular hostility" (p. 167).
- At Granada, Samuel b. Naghrila and his son Joseph served as viziers to the Zirid amirs, until Joseph's murder in 1066 and the massacre that followed. Bennison puts its roots in palace faction fights and resentment of tax collection. She stresses that the Sanhaja Zirids themselves employed the Naghrilas: "not … an example of 'Berber fanaticism'" (pp. 167–168).
- Andalusi Jews served Christian kings as ambassadors. Al-Muʿtamid executed Alfonso VI's Jewish envoy Ibn Shalib (p. 168).
- Physicians to the Almoravids are in Kraemer, not Bennison (see `jud_maghreb_maimonides_fes.md` §3).

## 4. Gender and the body (the main finding)

**The veil as "men dressed as women".** Saharan Sanhaja men wore the face veil (*lithām*). It was a mark of honour, "severely compromised by its removal" (p. 123). Hostile sources depicted it as "an inversion of Islamic gender norms" (p. 123). Ibn Tumart's opening attack on ʿAli b. Yusuf in the great mosque of Marrakesh was: "Where is the amir? I see only veiled slave girls" (al-Baydhaq, quoted p. 64). Bennison: in Almohad rhetoric the veil "became a mark of Almoravid inversion of gender norms, of men dressed as women" (p. 65). That rhetoric matched the "inappropriate freedom" of Almoravid women (p. 65). Ibn Tumart presented the Almoravids as the people of a hadith about "men of dubious manhood" whose women go bareheaded (p. 66 n. 18). The Almohads themselves, by contrast, "did not wear 'effeminate' garb such as the lithām" (p. 136).

**The Almoravid answer.** The court's own myth made the veil a sign of ancient devotion to Islam. Their Himyarite ancestors "veiled themselves in the manner of their women" to escape persecution (al-Hulal, quoted p. 124), so the veil was not an inversion at all (p. 124). The Almoravid qāḍī Ibn Rushd al-Jadd ruled that wearing it in the mosque was permitted (p. 126). Non-Sanhaja soldiers took up the veil as a mark of rank, and Ibn ʿAbdun wanted different veils for different ranks (p. 126). Some Almoravids took their veils off when they submitted to the Almohads in the 1140s (p. 124).

**Women of the Sanhaja.**
- Inheritance was often matrilineal. Divorce was common and not stigmatised (box, p. 157).
- Almoravid amirs were known by their mothers' names (Muhammad b. ʿAʾisha, Sir b. Fatima, Yahya b. al-Sahrawiyya). Bennison: "only the Almoravids regularly identified elite men by reference to their mothers in a non-derogatory way" (p. 158).
- Zaynab al-Nafzawiyya was the daughter of a Qayrawan merchant at Aghmat, wife first of its lord, then of Abu Bakr and then of Yusuf b. Tashfin, and his chief adviser (pp. 33–35, 158–159). She herself probably asked Abu Bakr for the divorce (p. 35).
- Ibn Tumart harangued ʿAli b. Yusuf's sister for riding unveiled in Marrakesh, and her horse threw her (pp. 65, 159).
- Hawwaʾ bint Tashfin held literary salons in Seville (p. 159).
- Al-Marrakushi blamed Almoravid women for disorder: every important Lamtuna or Masufa woman "acted as the protector of rebels, evildoers, bandits, and tavern and brothel keepers" (p. 158).
- Under the Almohads Bennison finds "a retrenchment of such power and a reassertion of the domestic and child-bearing roles of women" (pp. 163–164). The exception is Ibn Tumart's sister Zaynab, a trusted adviser (p. 159).

**Fannu bint ʿUmar.** She defended the citadel of Marrakesh in 1147 and fought to the death "in the guise of a man" (al-Baydhaq, p. 159). This is a woman in men's dress, the reverse direction. It is **not** material for the sect (trans men are out of scope), and the mod should not read her as anything.

**Eunuchs, homosexuality, intersex.**
- Eunuchs served as chamberlains: Kafur under al-Mansur, Rayhan and Mubashshir under al-Nasir. Sandal the eunuch stood beside Tashfin at his last stand. They were "favoured as men with no familial bonds" (p. 138). The sources' word *khādim* may just mean servant (p. 138). The slave-castration route across the Sahara is mentioned at p. 208. Guardrail: not for the sect (`jud_phelan_slave_trade_motif.md`).
- Homosexuality was "practised with varying degrees of toleration". Ibn ʿAbdun attacked male prostitutes. Al-Hakam II's concubine Subh supposedly appealed to him by her "boyishness and short haircut" (p. 161). That is Umayyad Córdoba, before the period.
- **Absences.** Bennison has nothing on *khuntha* (intersex) law, the *mukhannathūn* as a social group, Jewish dress inside the Jewish community, Purim, or Jewish women. She mentions bathing only through Ibn ʿAbdun's bath-house rule (p. 169) and as one of women's "respectable" outings (p. 151).

**Women's ordinary life.** Marriage was a contract between the groom and the woman's male guardian. Widows of independent means had more say (p. 155). Ordinary women worked, sold embroidery in the market, washed linen, went to the judge, and met at cemeteries (pp. 151, 161–162). Seclusion was a mark of wealth (p. 161). Concubinage was common at the top, and an *umm walad* could not be sold (p. 156).

## 5. Fes, Marrakesh and the urban quarter

- Fes was a loyal Almoravid town. The Almoravids enlarged the Qarawiyyin in 1135, and work on it began again in 1203 (pp. 340, 344).
- Towns had quarters "defined by occupation and religion". There were "no ghettos as such", but people clustered with co-religionists. Synagogues worked as neighbourhood centres, as mosques did (pp. 149–150). Quarters locked their gates at night (p. 150).
- Ibn Tumart lodged in Fes mosques, including a tower room in the Masjid Taryana where he slept and taught (p. 150).
- At the fall of Marrakesh, ʿAbd al-Muʾmin refused to enter because its mosques' *qiblas* "were too far east, a practice not for Muslims but for the Jews", and rebuilt them (al-Baydhaq, p. 307). This is polemic. Do not use it.

## 6. Design implications

- **Gate content by regime.** Before 1147 (Almoravid), the Maghreb matches the sect's "under another lord" model: dhimma, Jewish physicians and merchants at court. From 1147 to 1229 (Almohad) there was no dhimma in principle, enforcement was patchy, and Jews lived in a "decorous hush" (Stroumsa). After 1229 (Hafsid Ifriqiya) and 1248 (Marinid Fes), the old pattern returns. A Ha-Nehpakhot *kingdom* of the Maghreb is a game outcome that replaces all of this. Its texts should speak of the neighbours, not of a history it erased.
- **Persecution must not become a mechanic.** Bennison is the best counterweight to a "fanatic Berbers" reading. Use her framing in any loc that mentions the Almohads: they tried to convert all monotheists, Muslims included; enforcement was exemplary and patchy; and it passed.
- **The veil quarrel is the period's own fight over gendered dress.** It is about men in a "woman's" veil, used as an insult between Muslims. It is the closest thing in the Maghreb sources to the sect's subject. It must be handled so that no Muslim party becomes the sect's villain, and so that the insult is never voiced by the narrator.

## Open questions

- Who led and judged the Jews of Fes, Sijilmasa and Aghmat under the Almoravids, and whether a *ḥerem* needed the amir's leave? Bennison does not say. Hirschberg, *A History of the Jews in North Africa* 1, and Gil are needed.
- Jewish dress under the Almoravids, and the *ghiyār* in the Maghreb before al-Mansur: Stillman, *Arab Dress* (cited in India Traders p. 516 n. 14) and Cohen, *Under Crescent and Cross*.
- Bennison and Gallego, "Jewish trading in Fes on the eve of the Almohad conquest" (cited pp. 172, 204) has the Fes letter of 1140 and the Sijilmasa figures. It is not in the repo.
