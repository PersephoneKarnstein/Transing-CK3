## HEADLINE FINDING ON THE AL-JAHIZ "REALM OF MALE CHARACTERISTICS" LINE

**The sentence "castration transfers eunuchs from the realm of male characteristics to that of females" does NOT occur in this book in those words.** I searched every OCR variant of Jahiz/Hayawan and every gender word (male, female, feminine, womanly, realm, characteristics, transfer). What Ayalon actually gives from al-Jahiz's *Kitab al-Hayawan* is:

1. **Hayawan I, 116 (Ayalon pp. 268-269, Appendix A)** — the closest thing to the paraphrase. Al-Jahiz says the eunuch acquires, as a result of castration, the *women's* set of aptitudes — skill and patience in domestic service "and that is what typifies women" (*wa-dhalika ya'rid lil-nisa'*). Ayalon's summary: "the eunuchs, like the women, were very proficient in, and showed a strong inclination towards, all kinds of service (*khidam*), including menial ones. According to him, an emasculated person would show a much greater ability in this kind of duties than any of his virile relatives, including his twin brother." Full quotation and Arabic below (§2.1).
2. **Hayawan I, 124-125 (Ayalon pp. 104-108, Chapter 7)** — the long passage on castration, Byzantium, eunuchs' hatred of their castrators, their lust for women, horsemanship and archery. Ayalon says his translation is "rather free" but "the purport of the whole passage remains absolutely clear" (p. 108 n. 8).
3. **Hayawan I, 135 (Ayalon p. 108 n. 9)** — the eunuch as a fighter better than Turk or Khariji.
4. **Hayawan I, 124 ll. 3-9 (Ayalon p. 306, Appendix E)** — the Byzantine/Christian castration of sons dedicated to churches; eunuchs' alleged sexual prowess.
5. **Hayawan I, 117-118 and 293; Mufakharat al-Jawari wal-Ghilman (Ayalon pp. 255-269)** — terminology (khadam = khisyan) and the beauty of expensive eunuchs.

So the "realm of male characteristics to that of females" wording is almost certainly Marmon's or Pellat's (EI2 "Khasi") rendering of **Hayawan I, 116**, the *khidma* passage, in which al-Jahiz explicitly says the eunuch's new aptitudes are the ones that "befall women". Ayalon's version of that passage is the primary evidence to cite. Note also that **al-Mas'udi (d. 956), Muruj VIII, 147-150 (Ayalon pp. 210-211)** records, and *refutes*, the claim circulating in the 9th-10th c. that eunuchs "are men among women and women among men" and "more similar to women" — the strongest attestation in this book that *contemporaries regarded castrated men as (quasi-)women*, strong enough that a major historian felt obliged to argue against it theologically.

## 1. Book map and Ayalon's thesis

(Chapter titles from the Contents, pp. v-vi; page numbers are printed pages.)

**Introduction (pp. 1-12).** "The State of the Study of the Eunuchs": the eunuch institution is "one of the most retarded fields of research" in Islamic history; Ayalon traces his own path from Mamluk studies (1940s) through "On the Eunuchs in Islam" (1979) to this book, originally planned as *Harun al-Rashid and his Eunuchs*. "The Decisiveness of the Terminology": the whole study "hinges on the correctness of the identification of *khadim* with *khasi*. If this claim of mine is mistaken, then there is hardly any eunuch history in the medieval and in a good part of the modern Muslim periods" (p. 8). "The Wider Aim" (p. 10): "The eunuchs, once they were rooted in Islamic society, became essential not only for the upkeep of the harem and all that is related to it, but also for the success of the Mamluk socio-military system. Thus a great and formidable triangle was created in Islam, consisting of three elements: women, eunuchs and Mamluks, with the eunuchs forming the connecting link between the two other elements." He adds that the Mamluks "postponed for a good number of centuries the victory on land of Christian Europe over Islam."

**PART ONE: GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS**
- **Ch. 1 The Eunuchs in Islam: Basic Characteristics (pp. 13-38).** Trustworthiness and high prices (Herodotus: eunuchs "fetch a much higher price than whole men, because they are trustworthy in every respect"); the three special conditions of Islam — (a) extreme seclusion and great numbers of women (Hilal al-Sabi: 11,000 eunuchs, 7,000 black and 4,000 white, against 4,000 women at al-Muqtadir's court, "almost three eunuchs per one woman!", p. 16), (b) the Mamluk slave institution and the eunuch as barrier against pederasty in the military schools, (c) the wide boundaries of Islam and the ethnic variety of eunuchs. Ends with Xenophon's *Cyropaedia* on why Cyrus trusted eunuchs, which Ayalon calls "outstanding in its insightfulness".
- **Ch. 2 Eunuchs and Mamluks: The Eunuchs' Impact (pp. 39-60).** The eunuch as educator (*ustadh*), the eunuch's "womanly traits" and love of children, eunuchs as guardians of princes (Samanid Nuh b. Nasr attaching his three sons to three eunuchs), the barrier role; eunuchs as commanders.
- **Ch. 3 No Opposition to the Introduction of the Eunuchs (pp. 61-64).** No Muslim ruler ever tried to abolish the institution; Muslim jurists forbade castration but the ban was evaded by importing ready-castrated slaves; castration centres at the frontiers (Pechina/Baljana in Spain by Jews, Washalaw in Abyssinia, Byzantium); al-Jahiz's *al-Radd 'ala al-Nasara* blaming Christians, not Jews, as "the castrators" (p. 66 n. 9).
- **Ch. 4 The Establishment of the Eunuch Institution in Islam (pp. 65-70).** Unanimous attribution to Caliph Mu'awiya (Ibn al-Faqih: he introduced "the prayer niches, the stalls in the mosques reserved to the rulers, the gendarmery, the bodyguard, the eunuchs and the confiscation of money and property"); the Fakhita anecdote; Sayf b. 'Umar's "4,000 eunuchs" threat (36/656).

**PART TWO: THE 'ABBASID CALIPHATE IN ITS HEYDAY**
- **Ch. 5 The First 'Abbasids (up to Harun al-Rashid) (pp. 71-79).** Sabiq al-Khwarizmi; the eunuch sent to check whether Abu Ja'far would kill Abu Muslim; al-Mansur's Arab eunuch from Khawlan dismissed because "an Arab will not enter my palace in order to serve my womenfolk"; al-Hadi beheading two slave-girls caught in lesbian love after a khadim informed on them; the qati'as of al-Mahdi's eunuchs in Baghdad.
- **Ch. 6 The Role of the Eunuchs in Harun al-Rashid's Reign (170-193/786-809) (pp. 80-95).** Masrur, Husayn, Rashid, Kawthar, Faraj al-Khadim (builder of Tarsus and Adhana), etc.; eunuchs as executioners, jailers, envoys and frontier commanders.

**Ayalon's thesis in one paragraph.** *Khadim* in the medieval sources almost always means eunuch; eunuchs were far more numerous and powerful in Islam than anywhere else because of three Islamic peculiarities (harem seclusion, the Mamluk system, the vast slave catchment); they formed the hinge of the "Great Triangle" women–eunuchs–Mamluks; they were recruited from Byzantium (above all), the Slavs, the Sudan/Abyssinia and India; castration was done outside Islam by Christians and Jews (and some Muslims) at frontier centres; eunuchs were prized as "trustworthy" because they had no family, and were given the most intimate and the most violent tasks (harem, treasury, prisons, execution, fortress command, the training of Mamluk boys). Ayalon separates "our obvious abhorrence of the inhuman practice of castration" from "our judgment about the eunuchs' contribution to the civilizations of which they formed an integral part for very long periods" (p. 38).

## 2. The al-Jahiz dossier

Abu 'Uthman 'Amr b. Bahr al-Jahiz of Basra (c. 160-255 / 776-868/9). Ayalon uses four works: *Kitab al-Hayawan* (Cairo 1938 ed.; vol. I pp. 116, 117-118, 124-125, 135, 293), *Kitab Mufakharat al-Jawari wal-Ghilman* (ed. Pellat and ed. Harun, Rasa'il, Cairo 1965), *al-Radd 'ala al-Nasara* (in *Three Essays of al-Jahiz*, Cairo 1926), and *Kitab al-Bighal* (on the mule and the barid). Ayalon's assessment (p. 105): "Al-Jahiz, with his unique kind of interest and insight, paid great attention to the eunuch phenomenon. He has some very revealing passages relating to the subject of our discussion." On p. 22 (Chapter 1, speaking of the harem passage): "The importance of al-Jahiz's evidence lies in its earliness, and in the fact that it is not confined at all to the rulers' courts: this reflects the widely spread practice of the Muslim woman's seclusion, especially in urban society." In Appendix A Ayalon treats al-Jahiz as decisive proof that *khadim* already meant "eunuch" in the mid-3rd/9th century, and notes (p. 262) that al-Tabari "was also his contemporary. When al-Jahiz died in 255/869 al-Tabari was thirty years old. They support each other."

### 2.1 Hayawan I, 116 — castration gives the eunuch the women's aptitudes (Ayalon pp. 268-269, Appendix A)

This is the passage behind the second-hand "realm of male characteristics to that of females" line. Ayalon's words:

> "Very illuminating is al-Jahiz's view, expressed on several occasions in his *Kitab al-Hayawan*, that the eunuchs, like the women, were very proficient in, and showed a strong inclination towards, all kinds of service (*khidam*, sing. *khidma*), including menial ones.[202] According to him, an emasculated person would show a much greater ability in this kind of duties (or jobs) than any of his virile relatives, including his twin brother.[203] [Arabic as given by Ayalon:] *wa-ta'rid lahu [lil-khasi] duna akhihi li-ummihi wa-abihi wa-duna ibn 'ammihi wa-jami' rahtihi al-basar bil-raf' wal-wad' wal-kans wal-rashsh wal-tarh wal-bast wal-sabr 'ala al-khidma wa-dhalika ya'rid lil-nisa'* [= 'and there comes to him (the eunuch), unlike his brother by the same mother and father, and unlike his cousin and all his kin, skill in lifting and setting down, sweeping, sprinkling, spreading and laying out, and patience in service — and that is what comes to women'] ... [generally speaking] patience and perseverance in service (*khidma*), and this is what typifies the women.[204]"
>
> Footnotes: [202] Jahiz, Hayawan, I, 116. [203] Ibid., pp. 117-118. [204] Ibid., p. 135. "The translation into English is partly free. Cf. also the following evidence: [a eunuch] as a result of castration became sagacious and well adapted to the various kinds of service (*kharaja kayyisan mutahadhdhiban fi anwa' al-khidma*), and he rose to the highest positions (Marwazi, p. 300). Another eunuch is described by the same author as eager to give service (*raghib fi al-khidma*); ibid., p. 299."
>
> "The 'marriage' between the *khasi* and the *khidma* is expressed here by al-Jahiz in the most convincing terms and gives us the best explanation, together with that of al-Sam'ani (and al-Marwazi), why the term *khadim* became the most preferred and the common appellation replacing *khasi*. It serves also as a corroborative proof (if such a proof is necessary at all) to my claim that al-Jahiz used *khadim* in the sense of *khasi* on several occasions." (p. 269)

Note: Ayalon renders *akhihi li-ummihi wa-abihi* as "twin brother"; the Arabic literally says "his full brother". The force of the passage is that castration alone, not birth or upbringing, produces the female aptitude: the same parents produce a man and a eunuch, and the eunuch comes out with the women's gifts. Al-Jahiz's framing is of *khidma* (service) as a female trait that castration confers on a male body. Ayalon adds (p. 269) that Moussa cited "two passages of the Book of Animals, relating to the effects of castration on slaves" — i.e. al-Jahiz treats castration as a transformation with bodily and behavioural *effects*, within a zoological treatise.

Ayalon on the eunuch's euphemisms (pp. 269-270): "in Arabic the three commonest euphemisms for *khasi* were *ustadh*, because he used to teach small children; *hurami*, because of his connection with the Harem; and by far the most frequent of them all *khadim*, because of his particular aptitude for performing all kinds of services, including the menial ones."

### 2.2 Hayawan I, 124-125 — the main passage: Byzantium, hatred, lust, horsemanship, archery (Ayalon pp. 105-107)

Ayalon (p. 105): "He has some very revealing passages relating to the subject of our discussion. In the main one he says, amongst other things:"

> "Every castration in the world has its origin in Byzantium (*wa-kullu khisa' fi al-dunya fa-innama asluhu min qibali al-Rum*) [i.e. the Byzantines are Christians who ...]. It is astonishing that they pretend to be compassionate, merciful, kind and gentle hearted more than any other kind [of people pretends to be]. Suffice it, however, to mention how mutilating castration is and how cruel is the act of the castrator. There is no doubt that they [the Byzantines] brought upon themselves the unforgettable rancour and revengefulness of the eunuchs (*khisyan*) against them ... a thing which they [the Byzantines] did not expect or fear. The Byzantines are wary of shooting arrows against the eunuchs, and the eunuchs do not turn their backs cowardly on the Byzantines (*fa-la hum yanzi'un wa-la al-khisyan yankilun*). For archery (*al-rimaya*) is widespread among the eunuchs. If the eunuch is just a horseman distinguished by his marksmanship (*uswar*) [?], even then he will cause the Byzantines much harm (*balagha minhum*). But if he combines wealth with his marksmanship and acquires estates (*diya'*) in Tarsus and Adhana and recruits soldiers (*istana'a al-rijal*) whom he pays from his remunerative estates (*al-'uqad al-mughilla*), then the damage he causes the Byzantines will be as big as that caused by a mighty commander (*qa'id dakhm*). There is no enmity which surpasses the eunuchs' enmity to the Byzantines. This proves how excessive is their lust for women, and how strong is their desire to have sexual intercourse with them. It also proves that the eunuchs know full well what they have been deprived of."[8]
>
> "In another passage al-Jahiz says: One of the characteristics of the eunuch is his endurance in horse riding, and his ability to gallop continuously on horseback. In this respect he surpasses the Turks and the Khawarij. Whenever his patron (*mawlahu*) hands over to him his riding animal in order to enter a prayer house or a public bath or in order to visit a sick man, the eunuch will make the animal gallop back and forth without a break, until the return of his patron. Another characteristic of the eunuch is his love of archery, and that is because of his ardent desire to attack the Byzantines (*wa-ya'ridu lahu hubb al-ramyi bil-nushshab lilladhi yadur fi nafsihi min hubb ghazw al-Rum*)."[9]
>
> [8] "Jahiz, Hayawan, I, 124-125. There may be, here and there, a mistake in my translation (or interpretation), but the purport of the whole passage remains absolutely clear. See also Canard, 'Relations,' p. [?]5, and Jahiz, al-Radd 'ala al-Nasara, cited in chapter 3, note 9. The eunuch is classified here by al-Jahiz as belonging to the highest category of fighters. Cf. his Manaqib al-Atrak, in Tria opuscula, Leiden 1903, pp. 1-56, where our author speaks very highly about the military ability of the Turks and the Khawarij. The eunuch, according to him, is better than either!"
>
> [9] "Jahiz, Hayawan, I, 135. Bayhaqi seems to echo al-Jahiz when he says that a eunuch, when dedicating himself to God, would go to the Byzantine frontier, together with his property, and fight there (*wal-khasi idha tanassaka ghaza wa-lazima al-thughur wa-badara bi-malihi ila Tarsus*) (Mahasin, II, 390). According to Marwazi (p. 300) the purpose of the Byzantines in castrating their children was to dedicate them to the church and to high offices (*bayt al-'ibada wal-kidkhuda'iyya wal-niyaba*); the castration, as he states, improved their service. See also Jahiz, al-Radd 'ala al-Nasara, cited chapter 3, note 9. The deep hatred of the castrated for his castrator is also well illustrated in the murder of 'Imad al-Din Zengi (see chapter 12, as well as Ayalon, 'Eunuchs in Islam', pp. 78-79)."

Ayalon's own commentary (pp. 104-105, 108-110): "As far as source evidence is available now, the Byzantine example can be shown to have been at the origin of the Muslim eunuch institution. The (mistaken) claim of al-Jahiz that the Byzantines or Romans were the inventors of castration (see below) strengthens that assumption." "Quite probably the Persian-Sasanian example had its no mean share in shaping the eunuch institution in Islam, although specific proof to that effect is still lacking ... The living example of Byzantium must have been much stronger than that of an empire which had ceased to exist." "This would mean that if al-Jahiz's assertion about the eunuchs' unbounded hatred for their Byzantine mutilators is correct, [it] would have been quite easy for those eunuchs to move closer to the frontier, with the permission or even at the instigation or order of their patrons." But he adds (p. 110) "two weighty obstacles ... the first is the absence of any clear cut and definite acceptance of al-Jahiz's evidence; the second is that neither can it be proved indirectly," because the sources "mention only rarely the ethnic affiliation of the individual eunuchs."

### 2.3 Hayawan I, 124 ll. 3-9 — Byzantine castration leaves the penis; eunuchs' alleged sexual prowess (Ayalon p. 306, Appendix E, passage III)

> "III. Al-Jahiz (circa 160-255/776-868): The castration (*khisa'*) of the Rum-Byzantines. 'There are among people of certain creeds those who castrate (*yakhsu*) their sons and dedicate them to the house of worship and make them sacristans, as the Rum do. But they [in carrying out that operation] do not harm [touch] the penis, and they only interfere with the testicles (*illa annahum la yuhdithuna [fi] al-qadib hadathan wa-la yata'arraduna illa lil-unthayayn*). It is as if they only [want to] eliminate [the possibility] that their children would impregnate their women and their nuns and nothing else! (*ka-annahum innama karihu li-'ilajihim ihbala nisa'ihim wa-rawahibihim faqat*). As for sexual pleasure and lust satisfaction, they [the Rum] claim that they [the eunuchs] reach heights never reached by the unemasculated virile man (*fahl*). It is as if they claim that the eunuch draws out of the woman everything she has, because of his excessive ability to prolong the sexual act.'" (n. 25: Jahiz, Hayawan, I, 124, ll. 3-9. See also idem, al-Radd 'ala al-Nasara, cited in chapter 3, note 9.)

Ayalon's analysis (pp. 308-309): "Of special significance is the passage from al-Jahiz (passage III). There the author contrasts by implication the Byzantines' attitude to castration and the castrated with that of the Muslims. The Byzantines castrate their own children, which the Muslims do not do. The dedication of their own castrated children to their houses of worship is excluded in the case of the Muslims, because they do not have such children. It is the Byzantines who do not remove the penises of those children of theirs whom they castrate, a fact which provokes the obvious reaction of al-Jahiz: the only thing which those Christian Byzantines want to prevent is the pregnancy of their secular and monastic women, but they do not care a bit about these eunuchs having sexual intercourse with them. Such an attitude could not be more revolting and more irreligious to a Muslim. Finally, the prolongation of the sexual act and the pleasure accompanying it in a coitus with a eunuch whose phallus was not removed is something which al-Jahiz attributes to the Byzantine claim alone." And (p. 309): "Al-Jahiz's claim about the kind of castration which the Byzantines use when they emasculate their children, as well as about the purpose of that emasculation, are fully corroborated by al-Muqaddasi (although he says quite the contrary about their sexual lust after castration)."

### 2.4 al-Radd 'ala al-Nasara — Christians as "the castrators" (Ayalon p. 66, n. 9)

> "It is noteworthy that al-Jahiz, in his treatise against the Christians (*al-Radd 'ala al-Nasara*), does not blame the Jews of taking part in castration, in spite of the fact that in that treatise he does not spare his hard diatribe from them. He says: 'What proves their [the Christians'] lack of mercy and the pervertedness of their hearts, [is] that out of all the peoples, it is they who are the castrators; and castration is the severest mutilation and the greatest crime perpetrated by a human being. Furthermore, they do it to innocent and defenseless children. We do not know of other people doing this, except in the countries of Byzantium and Abyssinia (Bilad al-Rum wal-Habasha). Outside these two countries the castrators are few or even less than few, who learnt castration from the people of those two countries' (*Three Essays of al-Jahiz*, Cairo 1926, p. 2[?], ll. 10-17)."

### 2.6 Mufakharat al-Jawari wal-Ghilman — eunuchs "neither boys nor girls" (Ayalon pp. 255-261, Appendix A)

Ayalon (p. 257): "Al-Jahiz contrasts there the qualities of the boys and the girls (mainly the slave-boys and the slave-girls), shifting very often to men and women in general, and putting special emphasis on the sexual aspect (including adultery and pederasty). He does it by means of a debate or dispute between a protagonist of the girls (*sahib al-jawari*) and a protagonist of the boys (*sahib al-ghilman*). A repeated argument of this second protagonist is that the lovers of the females are primitive and rude, whereas the lovers of the males are refined and sophisticated. He includes the early Arabs (and particularly their poets) in the first category and the later ones in the second."

The key terminological fact (p. 257): "The last say in that epistle is given by al-Jahiz to the protagonist of the jawari, who speaks there about the khisyan, blaming the protagonist of the ghilman for forcing him to do so, because he (the protagonist of the ghilman) mentioned them, in spite of the fact that they are neither ghilman nor jawari (*wa-dhakarta al-khisyan ... wa-in lam yakun li-dhalika ma'nan fi kitabina idh kunna innama naqul fi al-jawari wal-ghilman*)" — "and you mentioned the eunuchs ... although that has no place in our book, since we are speaking only of the girls and the boys." (Jahiz, Jawari, ed. Harun, I, 123, ll. 4-6.) **This is al-Jahiz's own classification: in a debate structured as boys vs girls, the eunuchs fall outside both categories.**

The ghilman protagonist's praise of eunuchs (p. 258, Jawari ed. Harun I, 105, ll. 3-12): "Had Kuthayyir, Jamil and 'Urwa, as well as their likes whom you named, seen some of the khadam of the people of our time — I mean those who had been bought for huge sums of money — [and perceived] how good looking, clean coloured, and well balanced [they are] and how beautiful are their figures (*law nazara Kuthayyir wa-Jumayyil wa-'Urwa wa-man sammayta min nuzara'ihim ila ba'd al-khadam ahl 'asrina mimman qad ushturiya bil-mal al-'azim farahatan wa-shatatan wa-naqa' lawn wa-husn i'tidal wa-jawdat qadd wa-qawam la-nabadhu Buthayna wa-'Azza wa-'Afra' wa-tarakuha bi-mazjar al-kilab*), they would have cast off Buthayna, 'Azza and 'Afra' and discarded them as if they [i.e. the women] had been merely dogs. But your line of argument is to bring as an evidence against me the view of rude and uncivil Beduins (*wa-lakinna ihtajajta 'alayna bi-a'rab ajlaf jufat*), who were nurtured in misery and wretchedness and grew up in them. They know nothing about the luxuries of life and the worldly pleasures. They live in the deserts and shy away from [civilized] people like wild animals. They eat hedgehogs and lizards and cut open the colocynth in order to eat its kernel. The maximum that any one of them can reach is to cry over the remnants of the [Beduin] encampment, and liken the woman to a cow or a gazelle, when, in fact, the woman is more beautiful than both. Moreover, he [i.e. the Beduin poet] would liken her to a serpent and call her the disfigured and the scabby, alleging that he does it for fear of the evil eye."

Then (ed. Harun I, 105, ll. 14-15): "As for the cultured and learned persons (*al-udaba' wal-zurafa'*), they spoke well of the ghilman, and preferred them to the jawari, both when [speaking] earnestly or jokingly (*fi al-jidd minhum wal-hazl*)." The jawari protagonist on the beardless boy (ed. Harun I, 122, ll. 8-9 = ed. Pellat p. 52): "The maximum length of time that a ghulam preserves his radiant beauty and the cleanliness of his cheeks is ten years, that is until his beard becomes dense, which excludes him [i.e. the ghulam] from belonging to the category of beardlessness (*wal-ghulam akthar ma tabqa bahjatuhu wa-naqa' khaddayhi 'asharat a'wam ila an tattasil lihyatuhu wa-takhruj 'an hadd al-murd*)." Ayalon (p. 261): "It is the beardless, unemasculated boy that the ghilman protagonist praises throughout the epistle, and the term beardless (*amrad*, pl. *murd*) appears repeatedly in his statements, both in prose and in verse, and in very appreciative connotations."

### 2.7 Other al-Jahiz items

- Hayawan I, 293 and I, 117-118 (p. 265): the pigeon-fight anecdote — "A certain patron entered the private room (*maqsura*) of one of his slave-girls, where a pigeon-fight was going on, in which one of the pigeons injured the other. When he asked to whom that pigeon belonged, he was told: *li-fulan khadimika*" — and (p. 267) "al-Jahiz's *Khudayj al-khasi khadim fulan*" (cited by Ayalon from EI p. 74). Ayalon: "the only explanation to that phrase is that khadim and khasi are identical in meaning."
- Hayawan I, 124 cited (p. 231 n. 94) among the examples of *fahl* ("virile", "stallion") as the antonym of *khasi*.
- Kitab al-Bighal (p. 102 n.): al-Jahiz "speaks about the mule (*baghl*) and the barid".
- Mas'udi, Muruj VI, 227 (= Pellat V, 19) transmits al-Jahiz in the first person about "Fa'iq al-khadim, who was the mawla of Muhammad b. Humayd al-Tusi" (p. 255).
- Ayalon's judgment on the Byzantine Jahiz passage's reliability (p. 107-108): al-Muqaddasi, "in addition to his great reliability, was a contemporary of the situation he describes," is "another first ranking later authority" whose evidence "might supplement the above cited two passages from al-Jahiz."

### 2.8 Does al-Jahiz treat eunuchs as women, as a third kind, or as defective men? (synthesis of what Ayalon gives)

- **As women-like**: Hayawan I, 116/135 — castration gives the eunuch the women's aptitude for service, "and that is what comes to women."
- **As a third kind**: Jawari wal-Ghilman — the eunuchs are "neither ghilman nor jawari"; the ghilman-protagonist's passage on the beauty of eunuchs is "the only diversion which the ghilman protagonist makes" from his subject (p. 259).
- **As hyper-men in some respects**: Hayawan I, 124-125 and 135 — the eunuch is a better horseman and archer than Turk or Khariji, "belonging to the highest category of fighters"; and his lust for women is *excessive*, which "proves that the eunuchs know full well what they have been deprived of." The Byzantines allegedly claim that their testicle-only eunuchs out-perform the *fahl* sexually.
- **Ayalon's own gloss on the gender position** (Appendix F, pp. 316-317): "a man would love a eunuch because he resembled in some way a woman; and a woman, by contrast, would love him because of his resemblance to a man ... After all the eunuch, even the absolute eunuch, was more masculine than another woman. That kind of eunuch, deprived of all his genital organs, still possessed — as somebody had already put it so aptly — his eyes and his hands; and those were the hands and eyes of a man, or almost a man (and more often than not of a young one; and quite often a good looking one)." "In this 'battle of the sexes' the eunuchs occupied a middle place, which certainly did not diminish their attractivity to both men and women."

### 2.9 Other 9th-11th c. authors on eunuch gender quoted by Ayalon

**(a) al-Mas'udi (d. 345/956), Muruj al-Dhahab VIII, 147-150 (= Pellat V, 151-152), Ayalon pp. 209-211 (Appendix A) — the fullest statement in the book that contemporaries regarded eunuchs as women, and a refutation of it.** Context: the murder of Khumarawayh b. Ahmad b. Tulun in Damascus, Dhu al-Qa'da 282 / December 895, by "some of their khadam". Ayalon's translation:

> "In our book *Kitab al-Zaman* we wrote exhaustively about the khadam belonging to the races of al-Sudan, al-Saqaliba, al-Rum and al-Sin. In that book it was stated that the people of al-Sin castrate (*yakhsuna*) many of their own children as the Rum do to their own children. There were also mentioned the contradictory traits characterizing the eunuchs (*khisyan*), which result from the cutting of their genital organ (*qat' hadha al-'udw*), as well as the changes caused to them by nature, as an outcome of that castration. This is according to what people told about them and said about their attributes. Al-Mada'ini said: Mu'awiya b. Abi Sufyan entered once the living quarters of his wife Fakhita, who was a woman of brain and determination, together with a khasi, at a time when she was barefaced (literally: bareheaded). When she saw the khadim coming together with him she covered her face (literally: head). Mu'awiya said: 'But he is only a khasi.' She answered: 'O Commander of the Faithful! Do you think that his mutilation absolved him from what God had forbidden him?' Mu'awiya retreated, convinced that she was right. Thenceforward no khadim entered his women's quarters unless he was very old and worn out. People spoke about them [about the eunuchs] and mentioned the difference between those of them whose genital organs were completely cut off (*al-majbub*) and those who were only deprived of their reproductive ability (*al-maslub*). **They were also said to have been men among women and women among men. This is wrong and false talk. For they [the eunuchs] are men, and the absence of one member of their body does not justify their above mentioned classification. Neither does the absence of their beards transform them from what they were destined to be. Whoever claims that they are more similar to women, says in fact that the deed of the Exalted and Sublime Creator was changed, for He created them men and not women: males and not females. The crime against them does not alter their original substance (?) and does not eliminate God's creation of them.** We spoke also there [in that book] about the defect of the absence or bad odour emitted from the armpits of the khadam. In our previous books we mentioned what the philosophers said about this subject, for it is only rarely that the armpits of the khadam will give off bad odour, and this is one of the merits of the khadam. Abu al-Jaysh Khumarawayh was carried to Egypt in a coffin."
>
> Arabic (as transcribed by Ayalon): *... wa-qad takallama al-nas fihim wa-dhakaru al-farq bayna al-majbub wal-maslub wa-annahum rijal min al-nisa' wa-nisa' min al-rijal wa-laysa fi 'adam 'udw min a'da' al-jasad ma yujib ilhaqahum bi-ma dhakaru wa-la 'adam nabt al-lihya muhilan lahum 'amma wusifu [bihi] wa-man za'ama annahum bil-nisa' ashbah fa-qad akhbara 'an taghyir fi'l al-khaliq 'azza wa-jalla lahum ... wa-qad qulna fi 'illat 'adam natn arwah al-khadam ... fi-ma salafa min kutubina li-anna al-khadam qalla ma yujad li-abatihi ra'iha wa-hadha min fada'il al-khadam ...*

Ayalon (p. 212): "This is a passage of the highest significance." For the mod: al-Mas'udi records as current (in the 9th-10th c.) the formula *rijal min al-nisa' wa-nisa' min al-rijal* ("men among women and women among men") and the opinion that eunuchs are "more similar to women" (*bil-nisa' ashbah*), and then argues against it on creationist grounds: God created them male; losing a limb or a beard cannot change that. Note also the physiology: "contradictory traits ... as well as the changes caused to them by nature", beardlessness, and the absence of armpit odour counted as a "merit" of eunuchs.

**(b) Tha'alibi (d. 429/1038), Lata'if al-Ma'arif p. 79 and Tamthil p. 224 (Ayalon pp. 34-35, 324 n. 36):** the eunuchs "are described as being womanly and docile in bed at night and manly and warlike by day in a campaign and in similar circumstances (*hum nisa' li-mutma'inn muqim wa-rijal in kanat al-asfar: li-annahum bil-nahar fawaris wa-bil-layl 'ara'is*)" — "for by day they are cavaliers and by night they are brides." Ayalon translates the same line at p. 325: "The eunuchs [khisyan] are women to a man who stays at home and [fighting] men in [military] campaigns." Tamthil p. 224 also has the abuse-verse: "whoever has his male organ cut [is] deprived of his very essence. Is not any khasi [identical with an] adulterer, an unarmed attacker? (*man jubba zubbuhu dhahaba lubbuhu — alaysa zanin khasi ghazin bi-ghayr silah*)" (p. 311 n. 36); Ayalon: "Verses and statements of this kind, reflecting contempt and abuse for the eunuchs' impotence are not lacking at all in the sources ... This, however, had little effect on the de facto immense power which they possessed."

**(c) Bayhaqi (Ibrahim b. Muhammad, 4th/10th c.), al-Mahasin wal-Masawi II, 391, 393, 394 (Ayalon pp. 324-325):** "The eunuchs have a strong lust for and an amazing attraction to women (*'ala anna fi al-khisyan sharahan shadidan wa-maylan 'ajiban ila al-nisa'*)." "One of the merits of the eunuch is that the woman is attracted to him because he is safer and she covets him because he is forbidden to her and because she is secure from having a child [from him] (*wa-min fada'il al-khasi anna al-mar'a tamil ilayhi li-anna amrahu astar wa-'aqibatahu aslam wa-tahris 'alayhi li-annahu mamnu' 'anha wa-targhab fi al-salama min al-walad*)." Also Mahasin II, 390: the pious eunuch goes to fight at Tarsus (see 2.2 n. 9). Also the anecdote (p. 278-279) of al-Mansur and Ishaq b. Muslim al-'Uqayli: seeing a "bright faced" khadim pass, Ishaq asked whose son or brother he was; told "He is (fulan) al-khadim", he retorted "Are his smell and his embrace more desirable to her [your woman] than your smell and your embrace?" — al-Mansur "ordered to bar the khadam from entering the women's quarters" (Ayalon: "legendary").

**(d) al-Muqaddasi (d. c. 380/990), Ahsan al-Taqasim pp. 242-243 (Ayalon pp. 218-219):** three kinds of (black) khadam — the best brought to Egypt, the Barbar brought to Aden "the worst of all kinds", the third resembling the Habash; two kinds of white khadam — the Saqaliba "whose country is behind Khwarizm, but they are brought to al-Andalus, where they are castrated (*yukhsawna*) and then sent to Egypt", and the Rum "brought over to Syria and Northern Mesopotamia. With the destruction of the frontier fortresses their importation [to those regions] stopped." "I asked a group of them how they are castrated. I learnt that the Rum castrate (*yasulluna*) their own children and assign them to [service in] the churches (monasteries?) in order that they would not be occupied with women and harmed by lust (*inna al-Rum yasulluna awladahum wa-yuhrizunahum 'ala al-kana'is li-alla yushghalu bil-nisa' wa-tu'dhihim al-shahwa*). When the Muslims attacked the land of the Rum they raided their churches and released the [castrated] boys from them. As for the Saqaliba, they are taken to a town beyond Baljana (Pechina), whose people are Jews, and they [the Jews] castrate them (*yakhsunahum*). There is a difference of view about it [about how the castration is carried out]. Some say that the male organ and the scrotum (*mizwad*) are completely removed (*yumsah*) with one stroke. Others say that the scrotum is cut open and the testicles are taken out of it. Then a piece of wood is put under the male organ, which is cut to its very root. I asked 'Urayb al-khadim, who was a man of learning and rectitude, saying: 'O mu'allim! Tell me please about the khadam, and that is because the people of learning disagree about them. Abu Hanifa permits them to marry and recognizes their fatherhood of the children born by their wives. This matter can be learnt only from you eunuchs.' He ['Urayb] answered: 'Abu Hanifa, may God have mercy upon him, was right. And I am going to inform you about their [the eunuchs'] condition. You should know that during the castration (*al-ikhtisa'*) the scrotum is cut and the two testicles are taken out. Sometimes the castrated young boy takes fright, and one of the testicles goes up into his abdomen. It would be looked for but would not be found at the moment. Then it would descend after the fissure had healed up. If it was the left testicle which had been spared he will have sexual desire and ejaculate semen. If it was the right one he would grow a beard, as was the case with this or that person.' Abu Hanifa based his verdict on the saying of the Prophet, God bless him and grant him salvation, that the child belongs to the conjugal bed [*al-wuld lil-firash*]. He allowed that among the khadam there are those whose testicle remained. I mentioned what he ['Urayb] told me to Sa'id al-Juri in Nishapur and he said: 'This is quite possible, for one of my testicles is small', and, indeed, he had a thin haired beard. After the castration a rod made of lead is put in the urinary opening, which is removed only for the purpose of urinating, so that the opening would not cicatrize before being healed." Addition from another MS: "All that the Rum do [in their way of castration] is that they take out the testicles and leave the male organ. They do this to their own boys when they give them over to the churches. The Muslims, when they raided [the Rum territory], used to capture them from the churches." Earlier in the same work (Ahsan p. 31, l. 7; Ayalon p. 221) al-Muqaddasi lists five synonyms for eunuch: *mu'allim, khadim, ustadh, shaykh, khasi*.

**(e) al-Khafaji / popular etymology (Ayalon p. 40):** "The common people call the eunuch *ustadh*, because in most cases he educates the youngsters (*wal-'amma taquluhu bi-ma'na al-khasi li-annahu yu'addib al-sighar ghaliban*)." Ayalon on the eunuch as educator (p. 39): "He is said to have loved children because he himself was childless; and because, as a result of his emasculation, he acquired womanly traits, among them tenderness towards those youngsters" (citing Millant, Eunuques, pp. 128, 86).

**(f) Ibn al-'Arabi (Muhyi al-Din, 560-638/1165-1240), al-Wasaya p. 162 (Ayalon p. 279):** "Beware of letting the khadam enter your women's quarters, for they have sensual desire (*irba*). Bar your women from them exactly as you bar them [the women] from the 'stallion' males (*fuhul al-dhukran*), for they are men (*rijal*)."

**(g) Ibn Hanbal (Ayalon p. 278):** "'I asked Abu 'Abdallah [Ibn Hanbal]: Is the khasi permitted to look at the hair of his mistress?' He said: 'No!'" (Abu Talib al-Makki, Qut al-Qulub, IV, 206).

**(h) Abu Hanifa and the jurists (Ayalon pp. 218, 220, 324 n. 33):** Abu Hanifa "permits them to marry (*ja'ala lahum firashan*) and recognizes their fatherhood of the children born by their wives." Ayalon: "There was no indisputable prohibition in Islam for eunuchs to marry." al-Suyuti's treatise *Akam al-'Iqyan fi Ahkam al-Khisyan* (MS Chester Beatty, fols. 20a-20b) discusses "who amongst the eunuchs is allowed to look at a woman or marry her", contrasts "the semen of *al-rajul al-fahl* as distinguished from that of the khadim" ("Every semen is impure except the semen of the virile man, unlike [that of] the khadim"), and distinguishes *fahl* from *khasi, mamsuh, majbub, maslub*.

**(i) Abu Nuwas on al-Amin (Ayalon p. 129 n. 3, Tabari III, 965):** verses "where that poet claims that the whole population followed their ruler's example (*sayyara al-khisyana ... sayyara al-ta'nina dinan fa-iqtada al-nas jami'an bi-amir al-mu'minina*)" — "he made effeminacy/pederasty-with-eunuchs a religion (*din*), and all the people followed the Commander of the Faithful's example." Ayalon: "This is the nearest translation I can give."

**(j) Ghars al-Ni'ma (d. 480/1087), al-Hafawat al-Nadira (Ayalon pp. 232-233):** "'Is this youngster his child?' I said: 'Oh, sir, a khadim does not have a child!' (*Ya sayyidana khadim la yakun lahu walad*)." And Tha'alibi, Tamthil p. 223: "It was said to a eunuch: 'May God give you a son.' He retorted: 'Do not say something which can never happen!' (*qila li-khasi razaqaka Allah waladan fa-qala la taqul ma la yakun abadan*)."

**(k) Xenophon, Cyropaedia VII (Ayalon pp. 36-38),** quoted at length as "outstanding in its insightfulness": "men, too, in the same way, become gentler when deprived of this desire, but not less careful of that which is entrusted to them: they are not made any less efficient horsemen, or any less skilful lancers, or less ambitious men ... and if it is thought with some justice that they are inferior in bodily strength, yet on the field of battle steel makes the weak equal to the strong."

## 3. Terminology dossier

**Ayalon's thesis (Introduction pp. 7-9):** "Of all the euphemisms of *khasi* (pl. *khisyan*), 'the castrated one', by far the commonest is *khadim* (pl. *khadam*, and, at a later period, *khuddam*), which, as everybody knows, originally meant 'servant.' That the term had also the meaning of 'eunuch' was already well known for quite a long time. What I believe I succeeded in establishing beyond any reasonable doubt is the overwhelming predominance of that last meaning, as well as its early occurrence, at least in the historical and related sources, in the medieval period and well beyond it." "The whole study of that focal institution in Islam hinges on the correctness of the identification of *khadim* with *khasi*." "In order to visualize what the identity of our two terms implies one has to take, for example, the chronicles of al-Tabari and al-Ya'qubi; the works of al-Baladhuri and al-Mas'udi and those of the major geographers of the third and fourth/ninth and tenth centuries and replace there every *khadim* and *khadam* by *khasi* and *khisyan*. What a different, forceful, and, above all, truthful, picture would emerge!" (p. 9). Appendix A (pp. 207-284) is the "super-overkill"; its summary (p. 263): "the major elements on which the identification of our terms is based are: overwhelming evidence that the khadam are castrated; definitions which say explicitly that khadim is khasi; and innumerable individual instances where the identity of the two terms is beyond doubt."

### Definitions quoted (verbatim, with pages)

- **al-Sam'ani (506-562/1113-1167), Ansab V, 4, ll. 4-6 (Ayalon pp. 233-234):** "*Al-khadim. hadhihi al-lafza ishtahara biha al-khisyan alladhina yakununa fi dur al-muluk wa-'ala abwabihim wa-yakhtassuna bi-khidmat al-dar fa-yuqal li-kulli wahid minhum al-khadim.*" — "Al-khadim. This is a word (or designation, or name) by which are commonly known the khisyan who are in the abodes of the rulers and guard their doors. They specialize in performing the services of the abode and that is why each one of them is called al-khadim." Ayalon: "Thus khadim is the appellation of each single khasi!"
- **Ibn al-Athir (555-630/1160-1233), Lubab I, 409 (Ayalon p. 233):** "*Al-khadim. hadhihi al-lafza ishtahara biha al-khisyan*" — "Al-khadim. This is a word ... by which the khisyan are commonly known."
- **Ibn Khaldun, 'Ibar V, 262 (Ayalon p. 249, Example XVI):** "The *tawashi* in the language of the people of the Mashriq means [the khasi]."
- **al-Maqrizi, Khitat II, 380, ll. 11-12 (Ayalon p. 249, Example XV):** "The Royal khuddam ... [are known in the Turkish state (*al-Dawla al-Turkiyya*)] by the name of *tawashiya* ... the tawashi is the khasi." And elsewhere: "[From] Abyssinia are brought *al-khuddam al-khisyan*, who are known in Egypt by the name of *tawashiya*."
- **al-Qalqashandi, Subh V, 456, l. 6 (Ayalon p. 249, Example XV):** "*Al-khuddam al-khisyan*, who are now called *tawashiya*." Also Subh III, 477, ll. 4-5: "*al-ustadhun wa-hum al-ma'rufun al-ana bil-khuddam wa-bil-tawashiya*" (p. 51 n.).
- **al-Muqaddasi, Ahsan p. 31, l. 7 (Ayalon p. 221):** five synonyms for eunuch "in that order: *mu'allim, khadim, ustadh, shaykh, khasi*." Ayalon: "khadim is extremely frequent, and *mu'allim* and *shaykh* are, to use mild language, extremely rare."
- **al-Khafaji (Ayalon p. 40):** "*wal-'amma taquluhu bi-ma'na al-khasi li-annahu yu'addib al-sighar ghaliban*" — "The common people call the eunuch *ustadh*, because in most cases he educates the youngsters."

### Terms for the castration procedure and its degrees

- *khasa / khisa' / ikhtisa'* — to castrate / castration in general. al-Muqaddasi: "*wa-sa'altu kayfa yukhsawna*".
- *salla / sall* — "a less radical form of castration" (removal of the testicles only, as the Byzantines did: "*inna al-Rum yasulluna awladahum*"; "*fa-innahum yasulluna al-baydatayn wa-yada'una al-qadib*" = "they draw out the testicles and leave the penis") (pp. 219-220, 305). Zengi's eunuchs: "*istadama murudiyyatahu bil-khisa' wal-sall*" — "he perpetuated his beardlessness by castration and by drawing out [the testicles]" (p. 167).
- *masaha / yumsah / mamsuh* — "completely removed with one stroke" ("the male organ and the scrotum (*mizwad*) are completely removed (*yumsah*) with one stroke") (p. 219).
- *jabba / majbub / jabb* — cutting off the penis: al-Mansur's Arab eunuch "*fa-jabbani fa-isturqiqtu*" "he cut off my genital organ and enslaved me" (p. 75); Tha'alibi "*man jubba zubbuhu*" (p. 311 n.).
- *qat' hadha al-'udw* — "the cutting of that organ" (al-Mas'udi, pp. 209-210); *qat' al-nasl* — "cutting off of progeny" (Zengi, p. 167); *al-'udw al-maqtu'* (p. 277 n.).
- *imtilakh / salb* — other words for removing the testicles (p. 307).
- *yushaqq al-mizwad wa-tukhraj al-baydatan thumma tura' tahta al-qadib khashaba wa-yuqatt min aslihi* — "the scrotum is cut open and the testicles taken out; then a piece of wood is placed under the penis and it is cut to its root" (al-Muqaddasi, p. 219).
- the lead rod (*mirwad rasas*) in the urethra "removed only for the purpose of urinating, so that the opening would not cicatrize before being healed" (p. 219); 'Umari's Washalaw eunuchs "underwent a second operation, the purpose of which was to open the urethra, it generally having become clogged with pus" (p. 306).
- Clot Bey (19th c. Egypt): "they cut off with a razor all the exterior parts of generation ... then pour boiling oil on the wound ... and place a tube in the remaining portion of the urethra ... then sprinkle the wound with henna powder; finally they bury the patients up to above the belly and leave them in that state for twenty-four hours ... A quarter of the children who undergo this operation do not survive" (p. 307, in French).
- Ayalon's conclusion (p. 309-315): "Pellat's distinction between majabib and khisyan on etymological grounds is untenable"; "Khasi is a eunuch who was subjected to any kind of castration"; "the Muslim rulers found it much safer to employ total eunuchs in their harems and in the upbringing of their young Mamluk novices"; partial eunuchs "formed a marginal factor" within Islam; "the most dangerous castration must have been the total one."

## 4. Supply and castration dossier

### 4.1 The Islamic position and its evasion (Chapter 3, pp. 61-64)

> "The use of eunuchs in Islam encountered a quite feeble antagonism on the part of the 'ulama'. Since most of the castration was carried out outside the boundaries of Islam, and mainly by unbelievers, the Muslim religious scholars could comfortably condemn the operation, but react much more mildly to the purchase of the eunuchs and their employment, a reaction which was ineffective anyway." (p. 61)
>
> n. 2: "One finds in Muslim religious and related literature numerous denunciations of castration, including the statement that had the Muslims not been employing eunuchs, there would have been little incentive for carrying out that operation. Those denunciations are worthy of collection, analysis and publication (some of the sources belonging to this category are referred to in this study in various contexts; see e.g. the treatises of Mahasin and Masawi; al-Suyuti's pamphlet on the eunuchs; and, above all, the very early evidence of Muhammad b. al-Hasan al-Shaybani (died 189/805), the companion of Abu Hanifa, in his book Kitab al-Hujja 'ala Ahl al-Madina (Hyderabad, 1385/1965), the short chapter Bab Ibtiya' al-Khisyan, II, 373-376)."
>
> "The actual antagonism, which is the decisive one within the framework of the present study, seems to have been even feebler. For example: Sukayna, the daughter of 'Ali, and Fakhita, the wife of Mu'awiya I, are stated to have covered their faces when eunuchs entered their rooms, on the ground that in spite of their being castrated they still were males. In the case of Fakhita it is stated that 'thenceforward no eunuch entered his [Mu'awiya's] quarters unless he had been very old and worn out' (*fa-lam yudkhil ba'da dhalika khadiman illa kabiran faniyan*)." (pp. 61-62)
>
> "Al-Shafi'i had eunuchs and when one of these youngsters would reach the age of puberty, he would not let him go up to the women who lived on the upper floor and he would buy another one in his stead so that he would go up to them (*kana lil-Shafi'i khisyan fa-idha balagha al-ghulam minhum al-hulm lam yada'hu yas'ad ila al-nisa' fa-ishtara akhara makanahu fa-yas'ad ilayhinna*)." Ayalon: "Neither al-Shafi'i nor the womenfolk of the early Caliphs were concerned with the employment of eunuchs as such. What they wanted to prevent was that a male at the age of manhood, even if he were a eunuch, would be allowed to enter the harem ... [this] shows that even the most religious persons, at a very early date in Islamic history, had no qualms in employing eunuchs in their own homes. Up till now I did not find, either in earlier or later Islam, a single case in which a patron of means (i.e. one who could afford to buy slaves and maintain them) rejected the use of eunuchs of all ages, as a matter of principle." (pp. 62-63) The Zengid Sayf al-Din Ghazi b. Mawdud (d. 576/1180) likewise "permitted only under-age eunuchs (*al-khadam al-sighar*) to enter his harem, because he was a very jealous person (*kana ghayuran shadid al-ghira*)" (p. 63; Ibn al-Athir, Bahir, p. 180).
>
> "In evaluating the Muslims' attitude in real life to castration one cannot conclude that they were acquiescent, and not eager purchasers of the eunuchs imported from abroad. First of all, because castration was practiced, as already stated, within the boundaries of the lands of Islam as well, although on a much smaller scale. Secondly, and this is by far more important, because the share of the merchants who went deeply and regularly into the countries of the infidel has to be taken into full account ... it was much more worthwhile for the merchants to buy or kidnap the slaves before castration, and carry out the operation afterwards, in order to greatly increase their profits, because the price of a eunuch was considerably higher than that of an unemasculated slave ... There are no good merchants who ignore the decisiveness of the profit factor." (pp. 63-64)

### 4.2 Sources and routes (verbatim)

- **Byzantium (the main source):** al-Jahiz, "Every castration in the world has its origin in Byzantium" (p. 105); al-Muqaddasi: "The Rum eunuchs are brought over to Syria and Northern Mesopotamia (*wal-Rum yaqa'una ila al-Sham wa-Aqur*). With the destruction of the frontier fortresses their importation to these regions stopped (*wa-qad inqata'u bi-kharab al-thughur*)" (p. 107). Ayalon: "the two great capitals of the Muslim empire, Damascus the Umayyad and Bagdad the 'Abbasid, were situated quite near the Byzantine frontier, and their intake of Rumi eunuchs must also have been correspondingly great" (p. 108). Mechanism: "either by means of purchase, or by means of raids" (p. 107); the Muslims "raided their churches and released the [castrated] boys from them" (p. 219). Marwazi: the Byzantines castrated their children "to dedicate them to the church and to high offices (*bayt al-'ibada wal-kidkhuda'iyya wal-niyaba*)" (p. 108 n. 9). 'Abd al-Jabbar (writing 385/995): "They [the Byzantines] reject circumcision but castrate children (*wa-yabuna al-khitan wa-yakhsuna al-atfal*). When they capture Moslems they look for their children and castrate big droves (*al-qut'an al-kabira*) from among them. Then they discard them, and thus many of them die. And those are the people who pretend to be compassionate and merciful (*yadda'una al-ra'fa wal-rahma*)" (p. 347). Ayalon (p. 150-151): "The Muslims have certainly been greatly influenced by the Byzantine example when they introduced the employment of eunuchs in the early decades of Islam"; Byzantium also "brought over many eunuchs from abroad, especially from the area of the Caucasus" (p. 315). The Byzantine emperor's envoys included eunuchs (Basil al-khadim, 294/906; the eunuch Qunqula who killed 12,000 Muslim prisoners under Theodora, 241/855) (pp. 120-121).
- **The Slavs (Saqaliba) via Spain:** Ibn Hawqal (Ayalon pp. 230-231): "One of the famous items of their [the people of al-Andalus] merchandise is handsome jawari and ghilman captured in the land of the Franks and in Galicia, as well as Saqaliba khadam. All the Saqaliba khisyan on the surface of the earth are imported from al-Andalus, and that is because when they [i.e. the Saqaliba] are brought near that country they are castrated (*yukhsawna*). This is done to them by Jewish merchants. The Saqaliba are a people or a tribe descending from Japheth ... The Khurasani raiders ... get in contact with them from the direction of the land of the Bulgars. When they are captured and taken there [to Khurasan], they are left unemasculated as they had been before their captivity, and their bodies remain unimpaired (*turiku fuhulatan 'ala ahwalihim maqrurin 'ala sihhat ajsamihim*)." al-Muqaddasi: "the Saqaliba, they are taken to a town beyond Baljana (Pechina), whose people are Jews, and they [the Jews] castrate them" (p. 219). Ibn Khurdadhbih: "What comes from the Western seas is the khadam Saqaliba and Rumi and Frankish and Lombard boy-slaves and Rumi and Andalusian slave-girls" (p. 224); "Jazirat al-Dhahab [al-Rahib?]. The khadam used to be castrated there (*wa-biha kana yukhsa al-khadam*)" (p. 220). Ibn al-Faqih: "What comes from that direction [al-Andalus] is the khadam Saqaliba, the Rumi and Frankish slave-boys and the Andalusian slave-girls" (p. 224). al-Maqqari citing al-Qarawi: "Muslims learned that profession well, and practiced it on the same ethnic group" (p. 64 n.). Ayalon on Jewish castrators (p. 64): "Jewish merchants are said to have been castrators of the Saqaliba. However, they were not the only ones."
- **Africa — Nubia, Abyssinia, Takrur, the Sudan:** Hudud al-'Alam (372/982): "The merchants steal their children and bring them [with them]. Then they castrate (*khisi*) them, import them into Egypt, and sell them. Among themselves there are people who steal each other's children and sell them to the merchants when the latter arrive" (p. 64); and (p. 277) "the lands of the Sudan, from which eunuchs (khadiman) mostly come." Ibn Fadl Allah al-'Umari (d. 749/1349), Masalik (pp. 306-307): "Certain jurists (*fuqaha'*) informed our author that the ruler of Hadya was the strongest of all the seven Muslim rulers of Abyssinia, and that the eunuchs (*khuddam*) were brought to that town from the lands of the infidels (*bilad al-kuffar*). That author then adds that a certain merchant, named Hajj Faraj al-Fuwwi, told him that the Christian ruler of Amhara forbade the castration of black slaves (*'abid*) within [the boundaries of his realm] and strongly denounced that practice. Therefore, the [slave] thieves would go to a town named Washalaw, the people of which were savages, having no religion. They were the only ones in the country of Abyssinia who practice castration (*wa-ahluha hamaj la dina 'indahum wa-la yuqdim 'ala hadha fi jami' bilad al-Habasha siwahum*). Not only the thieves but also the slave merchants would call at Washalaw for the purpose of castrating their slaves, and thus raise their value (*li-ajl ziyadat al-thaman*). Each slave castrated there was carried (*yuhmal*) to Hadya, where he underwent a second operation, the purpose of which was to open the urethra, it generally having become clogged with pus. They were then treated at Hadya until cured, for the people of Washalaw had no knowledge of medical treatment. He added, however, that the number of those who died as a result of castration was larger than those who survived. The thing most harming them was their being moved from one place to the other without receiving any treatment [before their arrival at the second place]. Had they been treated on the spot, immediately after castration, they would have been much better off. On the other hand, had they not been moved at all to another place of treatment, none would have survived." al-Idrisi: "The city of Takrur. The people of the far Maghrib go to it bringing with them wool, copper and pearls, and go out of it with gold dust (*tibr*) and khadam" (p. 228). al-Zuhri: "al-Sus al-Aqsa ... from this region comes the desert import of khadam, 'abid and gold dust"; "the land of the Sudan, the Habasha, the Zanj and the Nuba ... from these lands comes the desert import of 'abid and khadam" (p. 228). Mamluk-era eunuchs "belonged to one of these four ethnic groups: Habash, Takrur, [Hind] and Rum" (p. 176).
- **China and India:** al-Mas'udi: "the people of al-Sin castrate many of their own children as the Rum do" (p. 210); "al-Sin employs al-khisyan min al-khadam" (p. 264); the kingdom of al-Mabudh in India "uses al-khadam al-khisyan" (p. 264). Indian eunuchs in Mamluk Egypt (p. 176).
- **Within Islam (the exception):** "castration within the lands of Islam was the exception and not the rule. Castration of Arabs was extremely rare. This is the only case I came across of an Arab eunuch serving in the Caliphal court" (p. 75, the Khawlani eunuch of al-Mansur: "An enemy of ours took me and then cut my genital organ and enslaved me (*fa-jabbani fa-isturqiqtu*)"). "There were also Muslims who were castrated within those boundaries, either as a punishment, or as a precaution, or for some other reason" (p. 295 n.). "Even Muslim born males were among the castrated. Castration on a relatively large scale was carried out by 'Imad al-Din Zengi" (p. 298). Castration also "at the court of the Ghaznavid ruler (Bosworth) and in the palace of the ruler of Darfur (Browne), both Muslims. Similar castration by order of 'Imad al-Din Zengi and of al-Hakam of Cordova are mentioned elsewhere in this work" (p. 315 n. 57). "Further systematic study should be devoted to the question whether converts to Islam were safe from enslavement (and castration) in border countries, especially primitive ones where Islam had started to take root" (p. 168 n.).

### 4.3 Who performed it

Christians (Byzantine clergy/families; Abyssinian pagans at Washalaw), Jews (Pechina/Baljana in Spain; Jazirat al-Dhahab), Muslim merchants who "learned that profession well" (p. 64), rulers for punishment or lust (Tughrul, Zengi, al-Hakam of Cordova), and in the 19th c. Coptic monks and Egyptian "bourreaux" (Clot Bey, p. 307). al-Jahiz exonerates the Jews (p. 66 n. 9); Ayalon (p. 64) does not.

### 4.4 Age

"Whereas the Mamluks were imported usually at, or around, the age of puberty, the eunuchs were ordinarily castrated and dispatched to their various destinations before or well before reaching that age" (p. 304). Clot Bey: between 6 and 9 (p. 303); von Maltzan: 7 to 12; Millant: "bien avant la puberté"; Guyot on antiquity: "carried out before the age of puberty. Only when the merchants wanted to sell their eunuchs as objects of lust (Lustknaben), they castrated them at that age, so that they would show sexual reaction" (p. 303). al-Shafi'i's eunuchs "started serving him well before the age of puberty" (p. 314 n.). Tughrul castrated Khumartakin as "the young boy" (p. 154) and al-Kunduri as an adult (he died at 44 in 456/1064; p. 160); Zengi castrated adolescents/young men he desired; Bihruz "after having been castrated (*khasahu*) as a punishment, in Duwin" (p. 278).

### 4.5 Mortality

"in the primitive conditions of those times a very high proportion of those castrated died or became invalids (or defective) as a result of the operation" (p. 15). Sources (pp. 314-315): 6th c. "only three survive out of ninety"; al-'Umari: dead exceeded survivors; Nachtigal: "out of 100 boys castrated only 30 survived, while another estimate put the percentage of the dead at 80"; Browne and von Maltzan: "a very small proportion"; Tylor: "the larger proportion of children die after operation ... owing to unskillful surgery, such as recover fetch at least three or four times the ordinary prices of slaves"; Clot Bey: 25% — "by far the most reliable estimate." "The most dangerous castration must have been the total one."

### 4.6 Prices

"Eunuchs were very expensive as a result of two combined reasons: a) their unique trustworthiness; b) the high death rate caused by that operation" (p. 300). Herodotus: "among the barbarians eunuchs fetch a much higher price than whole men, because they are trustworthy in every respect" (p. 14). Roman/Byzantine: "a eunuch cost four times as much as an ordinary slave" (p. 300); Justinian's Code: eunuch under 10 — 30 solidi, over 10 uneducated 50, educated 70, vs ordinary slave 10/20/30 (p. 301 n.). Leo Africanus (Fez, early 16th c.): men slaves 20 ducats, women 15, eunuchs 40 (p. 301). King Hajj Muhammad al-Amin of Bagirmi (1751-85) "purchased one eunuch in exchange of fifty slaves" (p. 301). Nachtigal, Kuka market 1870: adult man 12-14 Maria Theresa dollars, girl 10-13 yrs 20-25, "a eunuch — 50 to 80" (p. 302). Abir, Massawa: boy 20-50 thalers, wosif girl 50-60, "a eunuch fetched even 100 thalers" (p. 302). Burton, Mecca 1853: "A little black boy, perfect in all points, and tolerably intelligent, costs about 1,000 piastres; girls are dearer, and eunuchs fetch double that sum" (p. 302). Clot Bey: 1,500-3,000 piastres (325-750 francs) (p. 302). Millant (c. 1900): "the price of a totally castrated eunuch in the east was six times higher than that of a partially castrated one; ... in Turkey or Persia a eunuch of the first kind cost between 600 to 800 écu, as compared with 100 écu, the price of a eunuch of the second kind" (p. 302). Nur al-Din paid for a beautiful Mamluk "a price ten times higher than the ordinary one" (p. 320). Ayalon: "almost all the data collected here about the eunuchs' prices comes from outside the Muslim sources" (p. 303).

### 4.7 Numbers

Hilal al-Sabi: al-Muktafi's palace "20,000 Palace ghulams and 10,000 khadim, black and Saqlabi"; al-Muqtadir: "11,000 khadim of whom 7,000 were black (Sudan) and 4,000 white Saqlabi, and 4,000 women (imra'a), free born and slave girls" — "almost three eunuchs per one woman!" (pp. 16, 243). al-Khatib al-Baghdadi: 11,000 "*khadim khasiyy*", Saqlabi, Rumi and Black (p. 245). Harun al-Rashid and Zubayda each with c. 2,000 slave-girls (p. 18); Harun once accompanied by 100 white eunuchs (p. 95 n.). Fatimid palace at Saladin's takeover: 12,000 souls, "none of whom was a non-eunuch, with the exception of the Caliph, his kinfolk and his children" (p. 21). Nasr al-Dawla b. Marwan of Diyar Bakr: 500 concubines and 500 eunuchs (p. 18). Ziyadat Allah III (296/909) fled with "one thousand eunuchs (*alf khadim*) from amongst his eunuch Saqaliba slaves. To the waist of each one of them he fastened a belt containing one thousand dinars" (p. 203). The Delhi court: 2,000 eunuchs and 12,000 Mamluks (p. 18). Mecca 1990: 14 eunuchs at the sanctuary of Mecca and 17 at Medina (p. 23 n. 24).

### Caveats for the mod team
1. The exact "realm of male characteristics to that of females" wording is **not** Ayalon's; cite instead al-Jahiz Hayawan I, 116 via Ayalon pp. 268-269 and al-Mas'udi Muruj VIII, 147-150 via Ayalon pp. 209-211. Marmon (*Eunuchs and Sacred Boundaries*, 1995) and Pellat (EI2 "Khasi") should be checked for the original paraphrase.
2. Ayalon's book is about Islamic lands; its Byzantine material is second-hand (al-Jahiz, al-Muqaddasi, Marwazi, 'Abd al-Jabbar, Theophanes). For 867 Anatolia it gives: Byzantines castrate sons for church service with testicle-only cutting; the Cilician frontier (Tarsus, Adhana, the Lamis) is where castrates command, build and negotiate; Armenia (Duwin) practised penal castration; the Zengid Jazira castrated Armenian and Greek noble boys 1127-1146.
3. OCR limits: page numbers were read from running heads and should be verified against a clean copy before publication; Arabic transliterations are reconstructed and diacritics dropped.
