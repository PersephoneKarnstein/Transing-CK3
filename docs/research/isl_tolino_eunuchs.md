## 1. The argument in brief

### 1.1 Gender: read by context, not a separate category

- **The thesis.** Fatimid eunuchs "were not understood as a 'third gender' but were instead gendered according to the different contexts in which they acted or were represented" (T2018 p. 248).
  - Tolino rejects Ringrose's Byzantine "third gender category" as "too fixed" (p. 248, citing Ringrose p. 34).
  - She also rejects Marmon's "non-gendered individuals" as "problematic" (p. 248).
  - She keeps Marmon's link between eunuchs, sacredness and time (p. 248).
- **Gender depends on the kind of source** (T2018 pp. 249-252, 261-262):
  - **Adab: ambiguous.** Al-Jāḥiẓ (d. 868) is the one author who makes eunuchs explicitly ambiguous. The eunuch "becomes like the mule that is neither a donkey nor a horse"; his behaviour is "not that of a man or a woman, but mixed" (p. 249, *Ḥayawān* 1:108). Al-Jāḥiẓ gives them women's and children's traits: frivolity, greed for food, quick moods, being unable to keep secrets, and skill at housework (pp. 249-250). He also allows them masculine skills such as archery and cavalry (p. 249). Tolino calls his portrait "quite exceptional" and possibly coloured by Byzantine models. Al-Jāḥiẓ held Byzantium to be "the origin of every castration in the world" (p. 250, *Ḥayawān* 1:124-125).
  - **Chronicles and biographies: gender not mentioned.** "It is often very difficult to distinguish a eunuch from a non-castrated man" (p. 250). The usual word is *khādim*, "servant". Eunuchs also carried a *kunya*, which "may also be used in a hypothetical or metaphorical sense" (p. 251).
  - **Law: men.** Jurists "simply took for granted that eunuchs were men, even if they were 'mutilated' men" (p. 252). They never debated eunuchs' gender as they debated the *khunthā*'s (p. 251).
  - **Medicine: on a continuum.** Following Ze'evi and al-Rāzī ("there exist masculine women and feminine men"), the sexes form a "continuum of perfection". Eunuchs sit "closer to the masculine pole than the feminine one" (p. 252).
- **The functional proof.** "If eunuchs were considered 'complete' men, they could not have acted as harem guardians". Yet the same people served as "commanders, admirals, heads of police, provincial governors" (p. 250).
- **T2022 restates the point and sharpens it.** "Eunuchs were almost the only group of people at the Court who had the ability to move between the two genders." They were close to the master and in contact with the harem women, "who were very active agents in the struggle for power" (ms. p. 4). In conclusion: "Muslim jurists agreed on one point: eunuchs were men" (ms. p. 14). Even when they were seen as "mutilated" or "incomplete" men, "this did not change their status much" (ms. p. 14).
- **Höfert's framing** (introduction) pushes in the same direction (pp. 26-28):
  - she reports Cheikh-Moussa's reading of al-Jāḥiẓ: the norm is the adult, urban, uncastrated *faḥl*, and the eunuch's hybridity "is not a hybridity between two genders" (p. 26);
  - she proposes to "abandon the notion of eunuchs as either a third gender or as non-gendered individuals" in favour of "eunuchal masculinities" (p. 28);
  - she quotes the jurist al-Subkī (1284-1355): "when he mixes with women, he tells himself that he is a man", and among men "that he is a woman" (p. 25). This is late, Sunni and hostile.

### 1.2 Sunni law on castrated men (T2022; summarised in T2018 pp. 251-252 and T2023 p. 78)

- **Castration** is forbidden and subject to retaliation (ms. p. 2). The prohibition rests on Q 4:117-119, "change the creation of Allāh", and less often on Q 30:30 (ms. pp. 7-8).
- **Buying.** Abū Ḥanīfa (and Mālik, per al-Rāzī) disapproved of buying castrated men, "because the desire to possess them is an invitation to castrate them" (ms. p. 7, quoting al-Zamakhsharī). Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr (Mālikī, **d. 1070**, a contemporary of the 1066 start) reports that most Ḥijāz and Kūfa jurists disapproved of buying Ṣaqāliba, saying "if they were not bought, they would not be castrated" (ms. p. 9). A few jurists would limit a master to one eunuch (T2023 p. 74). Most "took eunuchs as a given fact" (ms. p. 9). The buyer was not held responsible for a castration done before the sale (T2023 p. 74).
- **Ritual.** A castrated man may give the call to prayer, "something a woman cannot do" (al-Shāfiʿī; ms. p. 9). He may lead prayer. Mālik is reported to bar him from being a mosque's permanent imam (ms. p. 9).
- **Marriage.** All jurists held it valid, which "confirms that they were perceived as men, as Muslim jurists did not conceive marriage between women" (ms. p. 10).
  - Most jurists gave the *khaṣī* (testicles removed) a one-year *ajal* to consummate, as for an impotent man. A *majbūb* (penis removed) got no *ajal*, and his marriage could be annulled at once (ms. pp. 10-11; T2023 p. 78).
  - An uninformed wife could choose to leave (ms. p. 11).
- **Divorce.** He has *ṭalāq*. That he might start a *khulʿ* "is not even mentioned" (ms. p. 12). The rules on *ʿidda*, the triple-divorce rule and the *īlāʾ* oath all turn on whether he can penetrate (ms. pp. 12-13).
- **Paternity.** A child may be attributed to him if he has testicles. It is mostly not attributed if he has only a penis, and not if he has neither (ms. p. 13; T2018 pp. 251-252). Some jurists, e.g. Ibn Qudāma, never attribute one after castration (ms. p. 13).
- **Looking at women: disputed.** Some jurists hold the eunuch to the same rule as other men, on ʿĀʾisha's saying that "castration is a mutilation, and does not make licit what was forbidden before it" (T2018 p. 252; ms. p. 13). Others count the *majbūb* or *khaṣī* like a *maḥram*, "together with ... old or impotent men, or the *muḫannaṯ*" (Ibn Qudāma, *Mughnī* IX:503; ms. p. 13). Tolino concludes that jurists did not agree "whether eunuchs could really act as harem guardians", though they plainly did (ms. p. 13).
- **Inheritance and testimony.** *Not discussed for eunuchs in any of the three works.* Inheritance comes up only as the *khunthā*'s problem: women inherit half a man's share (ms. p. 4). Any eunuch-specific rule on these is *unverified*.
- **No Ismaʿili or Fatimid comparison.** T2022 deliberately covers Sunni sources only. Including Shiʿi sources is planned "in the near future" (ms. p. 4). For the Fatimid legal position use al-Nuʿmān's *Daʿāʾim* (see `later_starts/mukhannathun_fatimid_cairo.md` §2), not Tolino.
- **Castration methods** (ms. pp. 5-7). Al-Jāḥiẓ names three: *khiṣāʾ*, *jibāb* and *wijāʾ*. In Islamic contexts the most common was probably *majbūb* (total) (ms. p. 7; T2023 p. 77). Al-Jāḥiẓ calls the man whose castration failed "neither woman nor man nor eunuch": bearded, barred from the harem and despised by both men and eunuchs (ms. p. 6).

### 1.3 Sacredness: closeness to the imam-caliph

- **Early phase: individual eunuchs** (T2018 pp. 255-258).
  - **Jaʿfar** was the Mahdī's milk-brother and was tortured at Sijilmāsa without talking. He became *ḥājib* in 909: "the 'door' to the imam-caliph" (p. 256). His biography, written by his eunuch secretary, never mentions his gender. Its one hint: on the Mahdī's wedding night Jaʿfar sat with the women outside the bedroom, put the thrown veil on his head and "danced with the women" (p. 256).
  - **Jawdhar** was a *ṣaqlabī* who served al-Qāʾim, al-Manṣūr and al-Muʿizz. He held the treasury and the textile store, governed Ifrīqiya, and was freed by al-Manṣūr (p. 257).
    - He paid 20 dinars to "buy" a disappointed *ṣaqlabī*'s *baraka*, and the Mahdī then blessed him (p. 256).
    - The dying al-Qāʾim said: "Protect him. May he not be humiliated after me." Al-Manṣūr answered: "Oh my Lord, is not Jawdhar one of us?" (p. 257).
    - His name went on the caliph's *ṭirāz*: "Manufactured through Jawdhar, Client of the Commander of the Faithful" (p. 257).
    - Halm suggests his secret designation of al-Manṣūr was an intrigue of Jawdhar, the prince's mother and his wet-nurse (p. 257, n. 67).
    - His biography too "makes no reference to his gender" (p. 258).
  - Höfert reads Jawdhar's role as the *ḥujja*, the keeper of the succession secret, as "the most sacred position, apart from the imam himself" (Höfert 2018 p. 22).
- **Barjawān** was regent for al-Ḥākim and was killed in 1000 (T2018 p. 258). The killing had the help of "another eunuch, the bringer of his parasol, Raydān" (T2023 p. 76 n. 24).
- **The 1060s and after: eunuchs as a group.** The famine and civil war of the 1060s brought in Badr al-Jamālī. After that, "Chronicles make no reference to any exceptionally powerful eunuchs during this phase" (T2018 p. 258). Instead "eunuchs as a group became instead the mark of the sacredness of the imam" (p. 258). Tolino argues the "holy eunuch" image was "already a recognized interpretation during the late phase of the Fatimid empire (from ca. 1060)" (p. 247).
- **The *muḥannakūn*.** Al-Qalqashandī (d. 1418) wrote from the lost chronicle of Ibn al-Ṭuwayr, a late-Fatimid and early-Ayyubid official. He describes eunuchs "who wrap their head turban to cover their mouths". "They are the closest to the caliph and his favourite and they are more than one thousand" (T2018 p. 258; *Ṣubḥ* 3:481; T2023 p. 80).
  - **Covering marks the sacred** (T2018 p. 259). At the sermon, twelve men climb the minbar and hold pieces of curtain over the imam so he is unseen while he speaks.
  - **Nine offices** were reserved to them (T2018 p. 259; T2023 p. 80; *Ṣubḥ* 3:485-486):
    - *shadd al-tāj*: winds the caliph's crown "in a specific way that no one except for him knew" (T2023 p. 80);
    - *ṣāḥib al-majlis*: chamberlain;
    - *ṣāḥib al-risāla*: carries the caliph's messages;
    - *zimām al-quṣūr*: major-domo of the palaces;
    - *ṣāḥib bayt al-māl*: treasury;
    - *ṣāḥib al-daftar*: records what happens at audiences;
    - *ṣāḥib al-dawāh*: keeper of the inkwell, an insignia;
    - *zamm al-aqārib*: in charge of the caliph's family;
    - *zamm al-rijāl*: food and banquets.
  - **The Ramadan procession** (T2018 pp. 259-261). The caliph mounts from a curtained chair. The *ṣāḥib al-majlis*, a eunuch, raises the curtain, "revealing the 'sacred' to the public" (p. 260). The *muḥannakūn* ride closest. One eunuch holds the parasol and two carry fly-whisks. At the palace door the *muḥannakūn* close round him again (p. 260).
    - The parasol-bearer ranked fourth in the army and was "often a eunuch, like the *ṣāḥib al-bāb*" (p. 267 n. 79).
  - **At the Fast-Breaking sermon** most of the twelve curtain-holders were eunuchs (p. 261).
- **Conclusion.** Eunuchs were "guardians of the sacred" (p. 262). As the imam grew holier he grew unreachable: "A defensive 'wall' of anonymous eunuchs would stand between him and his people" (p. 263).
- **Source warning.** All the ceremonial comes through Mamluk authors writing after 1171 (p. 259, citing Sanders). The *muḥannak* corps of "more than one thousand" is a **late-Fatimid (12th-century)** picture. *Its size and offices in 1066 are unverified.* Tolino dates the group sacredness only "from ca. 1060" (p. 247).

### 1.4 Names and titles (T2023)

- **Not personal names.** T2023 studies *terms and titles*: lexica, *fiqh* and Fatimid chronicles. It gives **no personal names** of Fatimid eunuchs: no stones, scents or flowers. The only personal names in it are Barjawān, Raydān and the Prophet's eunuch Mābūr (pp. 74 n. 15, 75-76).
- **Each kind of source names by a different thing.** "A eunuch was addressed with the general word servant (*khādim*) in chronicles, but was defined according to the grade of castration" in *fiqh* (p. 71). Chroniclers name by "function ... as well as his rank". Jurists name by "the kind of castration" (p. 81).
- **Legal terms** (pp. 76-78):
  - *khaṣī*: testicles cut;
  - *majbūb*: penis cut too, "probably ... the majority";
  - *mamsūḥ*: "cancelled", the same as *majbūb*.
  The lexica blur these terms; Ibn ʿArafa's legal lexicon keeps them apart. Their opposites are *rajul* and *faḥl* (p. 79).
- **Fatimid chronicle titles** (pp. 79-80):
  - *khādim* and *khaṣī* are the general terms.
  - Much more often the chronicles use **ustādh** (the most frequent), **muʿallim** ("teacher") and **shaykh**. "These three terms seem to refer mostly to the teaching function that eunuchs had" (p. 79).
  - **ḥurāmī** (harem-connected) is rarer.
  - **fatā** was not found; it is a Western (Andalusi and Maghribi) usage.
  - **ṭawāshī** "is not yet in use". Al-Maqrīzī calls it a Turkish term the Mamluks introduced (p. 79).
  - No ranking among *ustādh*, *muʿallim* and *shaykh* could be established (p. 80).
- **No slave words.** "None of the terms found in chronicles on the Fatimid Empire directly refer to them as being slaves". The exception is *khādim*, a word of service, not possession. *ʿAbd* and *milk al-yamīn* do not appear (p. 81).
- **Tutors.** Eunuchs "often also acted as teachers and guardians to the caliph's children". Securing their pupil's accession bound their loyalty "to him and by extension to his mother" (p. 75).
- **No adoption.** Eunuchs could found no new family. Unlike Chinese eunuchs, they could not adopt: adoption "was prohibited by *sharīʿa*" (p. 75 n. 22).
- **Size of the palace staff.** Al-Maqrīzī counts 12,000 dwellers in the palace when Saladin took it, all eunuchs except the caliph's family (T2023 p. 73; ms. p. 3). Tolino calls the figure "clearly an exaggeration" (T2023 p. 73).

### 1.5 *Mukhannathūn* and effeminacy

There is no study of *mukhannathūn* in any of the three works. The only traces:
- **al-Rāzī** (9th-10th c.): "there exist masculine women and feminine men" (T2018 p. 252).
- **Ibn Masʿūd, via al-Zamakhsharī:** the change to God's creation "also includes *al-taḫannuṯ*" (ms. p. 8). Al-Bayḍāwī adds *liwāṭ* and *saḥq* (ms. p. 8 n. 44).
- **Ibn Qudāma's list** puts the *muḫannaṯ* with eunuchs, the old and the impotent, as men before whom women need not veil (ms. p. 13).
- **Höfert:** uncastrated men "could also be accused of being effeminate" (Höfert 2018 p. 28).

None of this is Egyptian or Fatimid.
