**Period warning.** Almost every full description in the book comes from two late Fatimid writers, transmitted by Mamluk compilers (al-Maqrīzī, al-Qalqashandī, Ibn Taghrībirdī):
- Ibn al-Maʾmūn al-Baṭāʾiḥī (d. 1192), for 1121–25;
- Ibn al-Ṭuwayr (d. 1220), for c. 1130–71.

Sanders says so herself: these texts "decontextualize and depoliticize the ceremonies" (p. 10). For the reign of al-Mustanṣir (1036–94) she has very little. - the 1024–25 snapshot from al-Musabbiḥī;
- Nāṣir-i Khusraw's canal procession of 1047;
- al-Mustanṣir's own chancery letters.

## 1. The argument in brief

**Thesis.** Fatimid ceremonial was urban and processional, unlike the static palace ritual of the ʿAbbasids (p. 8). Through processions the Fatimids built Cairo as an Ismaʿili "ritual city" (ch. 3). Under al-Ḥākim it took in Fusṭāṭ: he led Friday prayer at the Mosque of ʿAmr in 403/1012 (pp. 60–61). In the 12th century the wazir al-Maʾmūn rebuilt it as a "ritual lingua franca" that was broadly Islamic, not Ismaʿili, for a mostly Sunni army and city (pp. 74–82). Rituals "have a multiplicity of meanings and functions that may conflict" and change over time (p. 6).

### 1.1 The caliph, the curtain and the eunuchs

- **Hierarchy by proximity.** Rank was shown by nearness to the caliph, by salute (kissing the ground, the stirrup, the hand) and by who might stay mounted (pp. 13–23).
- **The audience.** In the late Fatimid audience the door was curtained:
  - the majordomo (*zimām al-qaṣr*) and the director of the treasury (*zimām bayt al-māl*), "both eunuchs", stood either side of the door;
  - the eunuch master of the audience hall (*ṣāḥib al-majlis*, also "master of the curtain") set the inkstand and signalled;
  - the two *zimām*s raised the curtain (p. 33).
  This is Ibn al-Ṭuwayr's description, so 12th-century.
- **Eunuchs speaking for the caliph.** "His eunuchs were so closely associated with him that, in his presence, they could act for him" (p. 35). On the nights of lights, the caliph showed only his face at a window of the belvedere. A eunuch put his head out of another window and said: "The Commander of the Faithful returns to you the greeting" (p. 35; *Khiṭaṭ* I:432–433, late Fatimid).
- **Eunuchs keeping the ceremonies alive.** When al-Afḍal abolished the *mawlid* festivals, "the eunuchs began to talk about them again" to al-Amīr, as a way of opposing the wazir (p. 35, quoting al-Maqrīzī; *Khiṭaṭ* I:432–433). Eunuchs and the wazir al-Maʾmūn together restored the festivals in 1121 (pp. 68, 95).
- **The *muḥannak*.**
  - The highest eunuchs were *muḥannak*, "so-called because of the fashion in which they wound their turbans under their chins" (p. 177 n. 33).
  - The tail-under-chin style (*taḥnīk*) "had been adopted by the caliph al-ʿAzīz" (975–96) (p. 178 n. 36), and the caliph and the wazir wore it in the New Year procession (pp. 89–90). So the mark was not eunuch-only.
  - The *muḥannak* offices: turban-winder (*shadd al-tāj*), master of the audience hall, messenger, majordomo, director of the treasury, keeper of the register, bearer of the inkstand, "and the officials responsible for the caliph's relatives and for their board" (p. 177 n. 33).
  - The turban-winder was "always held by a eunuch" (p. 25).
  - A *muḥannak* could also be an amir: "Iftikhār al-Dawla, one of the highest ranking *muḥannak* eunuchs", superintendent of the royal wardrobe (p. 107).
- **Inside and outside the gate.** Inside the palace the *muḥannak* eunuchs stood closest to the caliph. When he left the gate and the parasol opened, they gave up their places to the mounted escort (*ṣibyān al-rikāb*). They took them back only when he re-entered (pp. 90, 92, 94–95; p. 178 n. 41). Proximity to the person was a palace privilege.
- **Earlier eunuchs (1024–25, al-Musabbiḥī).**
  - The Ṣaqlabī eunuch Muẓaffar carried the caliph's parasol (pp. 64–65).
  - Eunuchs "were now among the most privileged soldiers in the Fatimid army" and bore arms in the Fast-Breaking procession (p. 64).
  - All al-Ẓāhir's eunuch commanders gave banquets at their own houses (p. 65).
  - The eunuch Miʿḍād took over the Office of the Kutāma (p. 66).
- **The nearest image to 1066.** At the canal in 1047 (Nāṣir-i Khusraw): "To the right and left, eunuchs carried incense burners with ambergris and aloe" (p. 104).
- **Limits on eunuchs.** Al-Afḍal's new Ḥujariyya regiment (c. 1106) seems to have been used "to limit the broad powers of the eunuchs" (p. 98, after Hamblin).

### 1.2 Women in the ceremonial record (all late Fatimid unless dated)

Sanders has no chapter on the palace women, and she never names Rasad. What there is:
- **Rasad's boat.** A silver Nile boat, *al-fiḍḍī*, may be the one "made for the mother of the caliph al-Mustanṣir in 436/1044–45 by Abū Saʿīd Sahl al-Tustarī". It took 130,000 dirhams of silver and 2,400 dinars for the work, besides its curtains. In the same year 400,000 dinars were spent on thirty-six Nile boats (pp. 190–191 n. 79; Ibn al-Zubayr, *K. al-Dhakhāʾir wa'l-tuḥaf*, via *Khiṭaṭ* I:479). By the 12th century the royal boats were used "only for the personal entourage of the caliph at the time of the inundation" (p. 109).
- **Women in the palace's gift lists.**
  - New coins were distributed to the caliph's "brothers and wives, relatives, female servants" and to eunuchs of high and low rank (p. 84, 1120s).
  - Costumes for the Nilometer night went to the caliph, his brothers and "four of his female servants", among others (p. 113, 1123).
- **The Festival of Gala Costumes.** Under al-Amīr the Fast-Breaking was called *ʿīd al-ḥulal*. *Ḥulla* in Fatimid texts designates female clothing. Sanders suggests the name came from the many costumes given to "the women of the court" (p. 79; p. 172 n. 218, her conjecture).
- **The *daʿwa* for women.** The chief missionary lectured to the men in the Iwan and to the women in his own hall, *al-muḥawwal*, next to the Gold Hall (p. 43, late Fatimid). Lectures at the Azhar were also given to women (p. 156 n. 16, *Khiṭaṭ* I:391).
- **Ghadīr as a day of marriages.** On Ghadīr 516/1122 "the poor and wretched", with the eunuchs of first and lesser rank (*al-ʿawālī*, *al-adwān*), went to the wazir's gate "seeking marriages for widows" (p. 127). The *Daʿāʾim* encourages women to seek husbands on the Sacrificial Festival (p. 198 n. 26).
- **A mother's refusal.** In 1121 al-Afḍal's widow refused to send her son to the caliph's banquet, "fearing for his life", until he was brought (p. 69).

### 1.3 Shared festivals and the city

- **Shared feasts.** "The Christian festivals of Epiphany, the Festival of the Cross, and Nawruz were celebrated by the entire population" of Fusṭāṭ (p. 81). Sanders gives **no source and no date** for this sentence.
- **Nawrūz bans.**
  - The Mosque of ʿAmr was "the terminal point for parades on Nawruz" (p. 44). Her source is al-Kindī, *Wulāt* p. 269 (p. 156 n. 24), the same **913** episode Shoshan cites. It is pre-Fatimid.
  - In 363/973–74 al-Muʿizz prohibited public festivities on Nawrūz. "His prohibition was disregarded the following year", and three days of festivities "provoked the caliph to arrest and publicly humiliate the offending celebrants" (p. 83; *Ittiʿāẓ* I:214, 224; *Khiṭaṭ* II:31; p. 173 n. 2).
  - Nawrūz was celebrated by Muslims and Christians alike, "but it was at the sufferance of the ruler" (p. 83).
- **Easter at al-Maqs, 1024.** Al-Musabbiḥī reports a gathering of Christians and Muslims at the Church of al-Maqs, with tents by the bridge for eating, drinking and merriment, until al-Ẓāhir sailed there by boat (p. 103). He remarks on "the drunken behavior of the women, who mingled with the men". *Ittiʿāẓ* II:132 identifies the occasion as **Easter** (p. 186 n. 34).
- **Canal and Cross.** Under the Ayyubids (590/1193–94) the canal was cut seven days after the Festival of the Cross. Al-Qalqashandī says the cutting was once held on the Cross (17 Thout) and later moved to Nawrūz (1 Thout) "on account of the desires of the people". That shift is undated (p. 184 n. 17).
- **Ghadīr in the early period.** Ghadīr began as a popular festival. In 362/973 people of Fusṭāṭ and the Maghribī troops gathered to proclaim ʿAlī's designation (p. 124). Around 1000 and in 415/1025, *munshidūn* (singers of religious praise) went from the Azhar to the palace with poems and invocations (pp. 125–126; p. 197 n. 19).
- **Dress was not legislated.** In the Fatimid state "the hierarchy of costuming that reflected rank was not legislated". This was "quite unlike" Byzantium, where the court monopolised silk, purple and gold embroidery (p. 31). The Geniza shows it was "difficult to tell Jews, Christians, and Muslims apart in the streets" (p. 31). The wide *durrāʿa* robe the caliph wore to the canal in 1047 "is worn by both men and women" (p. 186 n. 38).

**The 1047 procession** (Nāṣir-i Khusraw):
- ten thousand led horses in Rūmī brocade;
- the young caliph, "a beautiful young man with a shaved head", on a mule with a plain saddle, in a white *dabīqī* robe;
- the parasol beside him, and eunuchs with censers;
- people prostrating as he passed. This is "the only instance … that describes the actions of the onlookers" (p. 186 n. 39);
- the caliph throws a spear at the dam and workmen breach it (pp. 103–104).

**The drought.** "The long drought of 457–64/1065–72" lingered in memory, and a flood came in 481/1088 (p. 116).

### 1.5 Al-Mustanṣir's reign (1036–94) in the book

- **Famine and civil war.** Low Niles brought "intermittent famine for nearly twenty years", and the caliph appealed "to the Byzantine emperor for grain". Turkish and Sudanese slave soldiers fought openly, and he sold his treasuries to placate them (p. 3). The war broke out in 454/1062 (p. 97).
- **The wardrobe sold.** The palace storehouses, including the wardrobe, "were opened and their contents sold" in the drought and famine (p. 30). Even then the gold coinage kept its fineness (p. 85).
- **Festival processions continued.** They are mentioned in his letters to the Ṣulayḥids:
  - *al-Sijillāt al-Mustanṣiriyya* no. 13 (445/1053) and no. 1 (451/1059), for ʿĪd al-Fiṭr;
  - no. 64 (476/1083), for ʿĪd al-Naḥr (p. 73; pp. 168–169 n. 179).
- **The sons by rank.** At "a perilous juncture" he stationed his sons by rank: the higher the son, the nearer the capital. Al-Mustaʿlī was kept in the palace (p. 21, Ibn Muyassar; undated).
- **Badr al-Jamālī** arrived in 466/1074. After him the wazirs were "the real rulers of the state" (p. 3).
