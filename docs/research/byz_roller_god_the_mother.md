## 1. The argument

**Phrygia and the goddess.**
- Her Phrygian name was *Matar*, "Mother". "Kybele" is an epithet from the Phrygian word for mountain (pp. 244, 328–29).
- She is "normally represented alone"; "the Graeco-Roman god Attis, however, had no counterpart in Phrygia" (pp. 113–14).
- She was not a fertility goddess. Roller calls her "a figure of power and protection, able to ... mediate between the boundaries of the known and the unknown" (p. 114). The Roman stress on fertility is "uniquely Roman" (p. 318).
- **Dorylaion lies in the cult's heartland.** The twenty-three rock-cut Matar facades lie "within a roughly triangular area defined by the modern Turkish cities of Eskisehir, Kutahya, and Afyon" (p. 84). Eskişehir is Dorylaion. Midas City, with its Hellenistic hilltop shrine, and the Angdistis altars of the 2nd–3rd centuries CE both lie inside that triangle (pp. 151–54, 336).

**Pessinus.**
- Pessinus is "renowned in Roman history and legend as the oldest and most venerable sanctuary" but "has produced surprisingly little material" from the cult (p. 192). No written source before the 2nd century BCE mentions it (p. 192 n. 10).
- The site lies "in a deep valley that is regularly subject to alternate bouts of extreme desiccation and flooding" (p. 193). It was a Hellenistic temple-state "ruled by priestly dynasts". The Attalids built it a marble temple (p. 193).
- The dynasts "all took the title of Attis". By the 2nd century BCE one Attis had a brother with the Celtic name Aiorix, so the sanctuary was "under the control of the Galatians" (pp. 193–94). Polybios names the two chief priests as **Attis and Battakes** (p. 193 n. 19).
- Its heyday was brief. By Strabo's day the dynasts had little power (p. 194).
- In the Roman period "archaeological investigations at Pessinous have failed to uncover the Meter sanctuary". The big temple is the **Imperial cult** temple, "erroneously identified as the temple of Cybele in many plans and guidebooks" (p. 341 and n. 77).
- An otherwise unknown group, the *Attabokaoi*, conducted the mysteries (p. 341). The chief priesthood was a civic honour for rich Galatian-Roman families. Ti. Claudius Heras held it nine times and shared the title Attis with his son (p. 342). "The evidence for eunuch priests at Pessinous during this period is very slim" (p. 342 n. 81).
- Rome's black stone was "a small, dark sacred stone ... that had fallen to the shrine of Pessinous from the sky" (p. 264). Roller follows Gruen in doubting that it really came from Pessinus rather than from Pergamon or Ida (p. 193 n. 16).

**The galli: what we actually know.**
- **Origin of the word.** "Gallos" first appears in Hellenistic epigrams of the late 3rd and early 2nd century BCE, probably from *Galatai*, Galatian (p. 229). "Since the term never appears in any text describing religious activity", Roller reads it as a slur that made the priests out to be barbarian Gauls (p. 231). The ancients' own etymology derives it from the river Gallos at Pessinus (p. 229 n. 173).
- **Gallai is attested.** A Hellenistic galliambic couplet quoted by Hephaestion has "the roaming thyrsus-loving Gallai of the Mountain Mother" (p. 230). Roller adds: "the feminine form Gallai anticipates Catullus 63" (p. 231). In ch. 10 she says "the Romans, too, saw the Galli as women, and used the feminine pronoun", citing Catullus 63 and Hephaestion (p. 323 n. 130). **In both places the feminine is an outsider's, poetic usage.** No gallus calls herself *galla* in any surviving inscription, and Soterides of Kyzikos calls himself *Gallos* (p. 333).
- **Women who "gallize".** A 2nd–1st-century BCE sacred law from Eresos on Lesbos bars from a precinct women recently delivered, "Galli, and women who γαλλάζειν—that is, hold Gallic rites to Meter" (pp. 231–32). This is the one ancient trace of *women* performing galli rites.
- **Castration in Anatolia.** It was "sparse and equivocal; the practice was apparently limited to an elite class within the priesthood and may have originated in a form of ritual chastity" (p. 318). The best homeland image is a silver statuette of a beardless priest in a long gown from a tomb at Bayındır near Elmalı. It shows "a mature figure of dignity" (p. 105).
- **Chastity, not madness.** Roller links the cut to the Philadelphia cult rule, which forbade fornication and adultery. The cut would be "an affirmative action, publicly declaring a man's fidelity to the goddess" (p. 253). She compares Origen (p. 254 n. 56). Phrygians were known as straitlaced (p. 253).
- **The method.** The galli "removed the testicles only" (p. 324). The ancient evidence suggests the Mother's priests chose "the more dramatic way of cutting off their testicles to create a public show of the finality of the act" (p. 254 n. 58). The cut came after apprenticeship, not in frenzy: the ancient sources "give no indication that these individuals were mad" (pp. 324–25).
- **Rome magnified it.** Self-castration is "attested far more frequently and vividly in Rome than in any other part of the ancient Mediterranean world" (p. 318). The protected sacred status "proved a magnet for transsexuals, transvestites, and others who found themselves on the margins of society" (p. 319). Galli "could not be Roman citizens" (p. 301). Under Claudius, Attis entered the Roman pantheon with a March festival, and citizens could hold his priesthood (pp. 315–16).
- **Why Rome hated them.** Not because they were castrated, since eunuch slaves were everywhere, but because "this particular group of eunuchs enjoyed a sacred status ... affording the Galli a position of inviolability and social standing denied to slaves" (p. 319).

**The myth was made late and from outside.**
- Attis in Phrygia was "the most frequently attested personal name", scratched on household pots. It was also the royal and priestly title (pp. 244–45).
- Agdistis (*Angdistis*) was a local name of the Mother, centred on **Dokimeion and Midas City**. "Nowhere in Anatolia is there any hint of Angdistis's dual sexuality" (p. 246). The androgynous monster comes from Greek and Christian tellings.
- The castration episode "seems like a late addendum" joining two Phrygian facts: the annual mourning for a dead priest-king Attis, and the priests' self-castration (p. 254). "The Mother's priests did not castrate themselves in imitation of Attis"; rather, the myth was made to explain them (p. 256).
- The violent Pessinuntine version comes mainly from Arnobius, who "had a personal stake in emphasizing the elements of incest, rape, and deviant sexuality" (p. 258). Placing the myth at Pessinus reflects Hellenistic prominence, since "Pessinous had not been a particularly prominent cult center before that time" (p. 257).

**Roman Asia Minor, 1st–2nd centuries CE (ch. 11).**
- Every village had its own Meter: "the Mother who listens", "the Mother of the cave", "the Mother of four faces". People prayed to her for health, family and stolen gold (pp. 328–29). Her cult was "most at home in her mountain and rural shrines" (p. 343).
- **Women's houses.** At Apollonia Salbake the mountain police paid for a *Parthenon*, a house for "the virgin priestesses of the cult". There were virgin attendants of Meter at Kyzikos too (p. 335). This is the closest ancient analogue to the Sisters' Houses.
- **Kyzikos into Constantinople.** "Constantine admired its cult statue so much that he had it brought to Constantinople" (p. 334; Zosimos 2.31.2). The Mother's image from inside the mod's homeland stood in the capital.
- At Aizanoi, near Kotyaion, Meter Steuene's cave cult moved in the 2nd century into a vaulted crypt beneath the temple of Zeus (pp. 336–41). This is a real underground Mother's shrine in Phrygia.
- At Dokimeion the red veins in the marble were said to be "the blood of Attis washing through the stones" (p. 342).

## Iconography, for the Sisters' arms (read 2026-10-23)

Read in part: ch. 3 on Hittite antecedents (p. 41 ff.); ch. 4 on the attributes of Phrygian Matar (pp. 97, 108–9).

- **The bird of prey comes first.** "In central Phrygia, her most frequent animal attribute, the hawk or falcon, is a predator of practical value" (p. 108). It hunts, and it can be trained to hunt for people. A Boğazköy seal impression already shows a seated goddess with a bird of prey and a bowl, "the same attributes as the Phrygian Mother Goddess" (ch. 3).
- **The lion is secondary in Phrygia.** "The lion is not the constant and ubiquitous companion of Matar in Phrygia, as it is in the images of the Greek Meter" (p. 108). The lion monuments are Kalehisar, the Etlik relief and Arslankaya (pp. 108–9). There the lions both guard her and are mastered by her.
- **The doorway.** The Phrygians inherited "the presentation of the divinity standing in the sacred doorway" (p. 97). The rock-cut facade is the cult's own monument.
- **Not Phrygian.** The drinking bowl is an old Anatolian convention that marks nothing in particular (p. 108). The Boğazköy musicians are "unique in the Phrygian material" (p. 109). The tympanum, the mural crown and the lion chariot belong to the Greek and Roman Meter.
