**Chapters, one line each.**
- Intro: Wu built a pantheon of female "political ancestors" from all three teachings, beside the male sage-king lineage (Wechsler's "empire open to all").
- 1 Nüwa: the creator goddess revived as Wu's mythic template.
- 2 Luo River goddess: the 688 "Sage Mother" stone from the Luo; Wu takes "Sage Mother, Divine Sovereign".
- 3 Leizu: the First Sericulturist rites (656–675) as Wu's "first lady" theatre.
- 4 Mother of Qi: Mount Song made a female sacred landscape; 696 titles for its goddesses.
- 5 Jiang Yuan: the Zhou ur-mother.
- 6 Mother Wen: a mother-administrator; Wu grafts her into her own genealogy.
- 7 Four exemplary mothers in Wu's *Regulations for Ministers*.
- 8 Queen Mother of the West (Xiwangmu): jade, phoenix, sun-crow, longevity; the Crane Institute theatre of 697–705.
- 9 Mother Li (Laozi's mother): "Grand Dowager of the Anterior Heaven" as a mirror for Wu the dowager-regent; dropped once she is emperor.
- 10 Wei Huacun: a Daoist goddess Wu *declined* to use; Huang Lingwei's relics confiscated.
- 11 Māyā: Wu as "Buddha mother".
- 12 Jingguang: the *Great Cloud* Commentary, the *Baoyu jing*, Maitreya, Vairocana.
- Conclusions: motherhood, weaving, and stage-by-stage choice of patrons.

## 1. The argument, with what matters for the Way

### 1.1 A pantheon chosen by stage of career
- **Thesis.** Wu "constructed a parallel pantheon of female divinities and paragons drawn from every ideological persuasion" (p. 2). The patrons changed with her station: empress, grand dowager-regent (684–690), emperor (690–705). "Exemplary wives and ideal mothers" for the empress, "strong-minded women with administrative and political experience" for the regent, deities for the emperor (p. 229).
- **The Daoist patrons were dowager patrons.** Mother Li was named "Grand Dowager of the Anterior Heaven" in 666, and the title was reconferred in 684, "mere months" into Wu's regency (pp. 171, 174–175). Rothschild's reading: the title "reaffirmed that Wu Zhao, like Laozi's mother, was a grand dowager" (p. 175). Once she was emperor in 690 she took a title that "effectively muted her gender", and Mother Li "vanished from rhetoric and rites" (pp. 176, 178).
- **The Way's own theology appears here.** Laozi "had no father, only a demiurgic mother. With Gaozong gone, the empire had no father" (p. 175). Quoting Schipper, Mother Li "was Laozi before Laozi"; the Dao "has taken form in her" (p. 178). Du Guangting (850–933, inside the game window) records her four stages: born of primordial qi, gives birth to Laozi from her left side, teaches him, and ascends as Grand Dowager (p. 168).
- **The patriarchal retrofit.** In 743 Xuanzong gave Laozi a *father* with a matching title. Rothschild calls this an effort "to retrofit this divinity into a patriarchal ancestral framework"; under Wu, "Laozi's father was never mentioned" (p. 177).

### 1.2 The Queen Mother of the West (ch. 8)
- By the Tang she was the highest goddess of the Daoist pantheon and "a powerful patron deity of women" (p. 151, after Cahill). Tang poets "compare especially lofty or talented priestesses and adepts to the Queen Mother" (p. 165, quoting Cahill).
- Wu's links to her: two jade rings "of the Queen Mother" presented in 676 (p. 152); the phoenix finials on both Bright Halls (p. 153); the *sheng* headdress, read as a loom part, worn in the sericulture rites (pp. 153–154); her new characters for sun and moon (pp. 155–156).
- **Mount Song becomes her local seat** (p. 158). Li Bo on the Daoist ordination of Princess Yuzhen, Wu's granddaughter: "Whenever she enters the Lesser Room, / The Queen Mother will certainly be there to meet her" (p. 158).
- **The late theatre (697–705).** The Crane Institute, the Zhang brothers, Zhang Changzong as the immortal Prince Jin, and Wu as the implied Queen Mother. Rothschild judges it "a playful diversion" that "did little to rekindle the fading power" of the Zhou (p. 166). Confucian critics read it as cover for "lascivious goings-on in the inner palace" (p. 163). The material rests partly on a Sui sex manual (p. 161). The design should not use it.
- **Elixirs and longevity.** Wu announced in 692 that her lost teeth had regrown; in 700 the Hongzhou adept Hu Chao's potion, three years in the making, "wasting millions of cash", was followed by a new era name (p. 157).

### 1.3 Wei Huacun and Huang Lingwei (ch. 10)
- Wei Huacun (252–334), the Lady of the Southern Marchmount, was a Celestial Master libationer and the "first matriarch" of Shangqing (p. 180, after Kirkland and Kohn).
- **Female Daoist community.** A Runzhou stele of 677 "lists 134 Daoist nuns and female adepts among the donors" (p. 184).
- **Confiscation.** In 693 the nüguan Huang Lingwei (640–721) found Wei Huacun's lost altar and its relics. Yan Zhenqing's inscription: "When the Celestial Empress heard this, she gathered the relics into her inner quarters" (p. 186). Wu gave her no honour and no money. Rothschild's reason is timing: the find came a month after Wu took the Buddhist "Golden Wheel" title (p. 187). After 705 the shrine was reconsecrated by a *male* court Daoist, Ye Fashan; Huang "never received her due recognition in her mortal body" (p. 188). (Huang is covered in `cn_kirkland_huang_lingwei.md`; this adds the court side.)
- **Wu co-opted the people around the cult.** She hired Hu Chao, the master who led Huang to the altar, to brew her elixir, and she hired the local literatus who wrote a Wei Huacun shrine inscription (pp. 184, 187–189). Faced with Luo Binwang's polemic, she said: "How is it that a man this talented has been cast out and isn't serving in office?" (p. 189).
- **Why Wei was left out:** bad timing, the wrong geography (Huaizhou's Daoist circles backed the Li clan), and Wei "was not a creatress … She was not a mother goddess" (p. 190).

### 1.4 The Daoist establishment against the female usurper
- In 684, when Wu wanted to make her Wu kinsmen princes, Du Guangting records that Laozi appeared and sent word: "It is inappropriate for you to seize the throne" (p. 174). In 685, 250 members of the Li clan commissioned a statue of Laozi (p. 174). Daoism was the Tang house's ancestor cult, so a female usurper threatened it.
- Under the Zhou, Wu raised Buddhism over Daoism (691) and dropped the *Daodejing* from the examinations (693) (p. 187). The Commentary glossed "the violent, forcible tonsure of Daoists in 685" as a fulfilled prophecy (p. 212).
- **The opposite case: palace women as Daoist nuns.** "In the second month of 684, Wu Zhao, as grand dowager, established a Daoist convent at Goldtower Pavilion, ordaining former concubines and palace girls from Gaozong's harem as Daoist nuns" (p. 91). The Two Sages also "ordained imperial family members" (p. 180).

### 1.5 The *Great Cloud* material (beyond Chen)
- The sutra's line: Jingguang will "be a bodhisattva who will show and receive a female body in order to convert beings" (p. 20). The Commentary's gloss: "the form that a bodhisattva assumes to benefit beings is not fixed … This is the reason why she now has a female body" (p. 213).
- **Two lineages.** The 690 Commentary gave a purely female line: Queen Hufa, then Devi Jingguang, then Wu (pp. 209–210). The 693 *Baoyu jing* instead made Wu the rebirth of a *male* devaputra who would "manifest a female body", with two of the Five Impediments waived. Rothschild suggests this was "a bit less offensive to the Confucian establishment" (pp. 220–221). His conclusion: Jingguang was Wu's "most unapologetic … strategy to validate female sovereignty on its own merit, without elaborate gender-shifting" (p. 226).
- **Body-change imagery.** A Zhang Yizhi poem alludes to the Vimalakīrti goddess who turns Śāriputra into a woman: "The form and shape of my female body does not exist … yet does not not exist" (p. 221).
- **Rival claimants.** A nun from Henei called herself "the Future Pure Radiance". When she failed to foresee the Bright Hall fire of 694, Wu sent her home; later Wu "made her a palace slave" (p. 219).
- **Other institutions.** A "Convent of the Devi" in Luoyang (685–688) (p. 211); the 694 Maitreya title, dropped after the Bright Hall burned (pp. 223–224).

### 1.6 Hostile voices (all in Rothschild's introduction)
- 676 remonstrance: "The relationship of the empress to emperor is like that of the moon to the sun—each has its place and role" (p. 2).
- 684 rebels' oath to "expunge the she-demon scourge" (*qing yao nie* 清妖孽) (p. 2). The *yao* 妖 is the same portent word as in *renyao* 人妖.
- Luo Binwang's polemic: "her vulpine glamour beguiled the ruler … Her heart is half viper and half chameleon" (p. 3).
- An omen reading: "your majesty is a female ruler situated in the yang role … the earth sundered" (p. 3). Hens were said to turn into roosters, "chicken abominations" (*ji huo* 雞禍), read as signs of her rule (p. 3).
- *Book of Songs*: "disorder does not come from Heaven; it is wrought by women" (p. 234).

### 1.7 Dunhuang
- **No link between Wu and Cave 331 anywhere in the book.** This confirms the Chen note's correction.
- **Cave 96:** "the 105-foot giant statue of Maitreya in cave 96 at Dunhuang … a powerful testament to local support for Wu Zhao as emperor", built by "local elite working in conjunction with the community of sangha" (p. 224). It was local work, not paid for by Wu.
- **S.6502**, the Commentary itself, is a Dunhuang manuscript (Forte's source; notes to ch. 12). So the cave library kept Wu's prophecy text.
- **Lead to check:** n. 65 to ch. 4 cites Zhao Xiaoxing, "Mogao ku di 9 ku 'Songshan shen song mingtangdian ying tu' kao", *Dunhuang yanjiu* 2011.3, on a **Cave 9** mural of the god of Mount Song sending a pillar for Wu's Bright Hall. Rothschild cites it only for "Mount Song became a Daoist paradise" (p. 91). Cave 9 is usually dated late Tang. Not verified here.
- Cave 158 (mid-Tang) is used only as an illustrative apsara (fig. 12.1).
- **Low relevance otherwise.** The book is about Luoyang and Mount Song, not the northwest.

**Contradictions flagged.** (1) The Cave 331 + Wu claim (again). (2) The idea that Wu was a Daoist patron throughout is false. She patronised Daoism as empress (to 683), was ambivalent as regent, put Buddhism first as emperor, and turned to Daoist longevity theatre only after about 697 (pp. 157, 187).
**Usable primary cases.** The 684 ordination of Gaozong's concubines; Huang Lingwei's relics, 693; Laozi's warning, 684; the Luo River "Sage Mother" stone, 688 (p. 50); the yang-role omen; Wu hiring Luo Binwang's talent; the "Future Pure Radiance" nun; the Mogao Cave 96 Maitreya.
