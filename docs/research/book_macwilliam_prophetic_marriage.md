## The argument in brief

- **Scope.** The "marriage metaphor" is shorthand for the sexual imagery of Yhwh and Israel/Judah in Jer. 2–3, Hos. 1–3 and Ezek. 16 and 23 (p. 1). Macwilliam keeps the phrase while accepting Moughtin-Mumby's warning that it implies one story behind very different texts.
- **The addressees are men (ch. 4).** Across about fifty passages, when the Hebrew Bible speaks to or about "the Israelites", it is usually the men who are meant: "it is the men to whom the speaker speaks, or it is the men who do the speaking" (p. 71, restated p. 81). Inclusive passages are few and often bring women in only as especially prone to finery or idolatry (pp. 72–74). He leans on feminist predecessors: Plaskow ("the Jewish textual tradition treats men as normative Jews", p. 70; women part of the community "in a submerged and non-normative way", p. 70), Leith ("So Hosea, on one level, is calling the Israelites 'women'", p. 71), Bird ("Hosea says, 'You (male Israel) are that woman!'", pp. 37, 82 n. 1). His working premise: "male Israelites are the brides of Yhwh" (p. 70).
- **The metaphor undoes itself.** The metaphor ought to teach patriarchal, heterosexual marriage by divine example. Because its bride is the male citizenry, "the marriage metaphor is compromised within the biblical text itself" (p. 5). In Jeremiah the text slips between feminine and masculine address in ways commentators smooth over (ch. 5).
- **The prophets unmanned (pp. 111–114, 212–213).** Jeremiah is forbidden to marry (Jer. 16:2) where "celibacy was virtually unknown in Israel" (Holladay, quoted p. 112). Hosea is ordered to marry a woman of "harlotry" and cannot be sure his children are his. "In obeying this command, he becomes less than a man, just as Jeremiah is less than a man in not marrying at all" (p. 113). Jeremiah complains that Yhwh enticed him (Jer. 20:7): "a demanding and ineluctable seducer" (p. 96).
- **The third movement (pp. 123–125, 213).** The metaphor has three movements: a first love, the wife's betrayal (the violent part feminist critics have rightly attacked), and reconciliation. Feminist critics mostly dismiss the third. Macwilliam argues that in Hosea it is "unconditional forgiveness, of fulfilled sexual intimacy" (p. 213), and asks, "Whose heart is Yhwh about to win over? Whose body is he about to enjoy?" His answer: "it is the male Israelites who are the recipients of this sexual fervour" (p. 213).
- **Other readings he weighs.** Eilberg-Schwartz (*God's Phallus*, 1994): the female images of Israel "were addressed primarily to men and conceptualized the male relationship to God"; his ch. 7 shows that "the logical implications have been noticed by male readers in an early period", in rabbinic literature (p. 43). Jennings (2005) calls it a "deliberate transgendering of a standardly male subject" and the relationship one between "a male and his transvestite beloved" (pp. 47–48). Macwilliam rejects this as fixing the Israelite as "a male drag artist" (p. 48) and wants something more fluid. Schüngel-Straumann (1986) reads Hos. 11:3–4 as a nursing mother-God (pp. 100–102).
- **The modern stake.** A conservative Anglican's objection (2006) that a "mother Jesus" with a bride makes "a lesbian relationship" (pp. 4–5, 215): the Bride of Christ figure descends from the prophets' metaphor, which never was straightforwardly heterosexual.

## Relation to existing notes

- **Ha-Nehpakhot.** None of the `jud_*` notes uses the prophets' bride. The sect's scripture so far is Dinah (Berakhot 60a), Esther 9:1 (*ve-nahafokh hu*), Isaiah 56 and Kalonymus. The marriage metaphor is a further, older precedent for **men of Israel addressed as a woman before God**, inside the Hebrew Bible, with no Christian intermediary.

Eilberg-Schwartz ch. 7 is the lead for rabbinic readings (see Open, below).

Hardy's finding that commentators turned the poems' girl into a soul (`ind_hardy_viraha_bhakti.md`) has an exact parallel in Macwilliam's point that commentators naturalise the prophets' metaphor so no one notices its male bride (p. 211). The comparison is for the design notes only; no cross-faith borrowing.
- **Adelphai.** The Church as Bride of Christ is the Christian descendant of this metaphor (p. 215). `book_hornsby_stone_bible_trouble.md` proposes the Fathers' Bride-voice from the Song of Songs (Origen) for the Adelphai. That use must not pass through Hosea's Israel (see Cautions).

## Cautions

- **Modern reading.** Macwilliam's "queer" bride is a modern literary reading of the grammar. The narrator must not say the prophets meant men to be women, nor that ancient Israel recognised a man as a bride. In the faith's voice it is the daughter's argument, and the elders answer it.
- **Violence.** The second movement (Hos. 2:4–15; Ezek. 16 and 23) is sexual violence and public exposure of a woman. Not for loc, not even hostile. Chs. 8–9 of the book pair Ezek. 23 with explicit gay material; nothing from them.
- **Supersessionism.** The "Bride of Christ" inherits Israel's title in Christian teaching (p. 215). See proposal 4. The mod's Christian faiths may use the Song's Bride but never claim Israel's.
- **No moneylending, no betrayal, no badges** arise from this book. Nothing here touches the Ha-Nehpakhot hard limits except the anti-Jewish "adulterous synagogue" trope, which is excluded.
- **The Garland.** Do not import Hebrew-Bible imagery into Tamil text. The comparison is for designers only.
- **Plaskow's sentence** ("the Jewish textual tradition treats men as normative Jews") is a modern feminist critique. Do not put it in a medieval Jewish voice, and do not use it to make the sect's elders look worse than their sources.
