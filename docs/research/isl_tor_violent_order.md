# Tor, *Violent Order* (2007): design reading for bands, brotherhoods and the Islamic neighbours

Source: D. G. (Deborah G.) Tor, *Violent Order: Religious Warfare, Chivalry, and the ʿAyyār Phenomenon in the Medieval Islamic World*. Istanbuler Texte und Studien 11. Würzburg: Ergon Verlag in Kommission (for the Orient-Institut Istanbul), 2007. 314 pp. (main text pp. 11–297, bibliography pp. 299 ff.). ISBN 978-3-89913-553-4. **Verified** from the title and copyright pages of the text extraction (`sources_pending/bands_trade/tor_violent_order.txt`, gitignored).

**Page numbers.** The book's own, read from the running heads (even pages "nn D. G. TOR", odd pages "VIOLENT ORDER nn"; each page in the extraction begins with a form feed and its head). Footnotes are cited as "p. n n. x".

**What was read.**
- **In full:** ch. 1 (pp. 11–38, with the lexicon survey); ch. 2 (pp. 39–84); ch. 3 (pp. 85–116); ch. 7 (pp. 231–251); ch. 8 (pp. 253–287); Conclusions (pp. 289–297). Footnotes included.
- **In part:** ch. 4 (pp. 117–122, 128–129, 149–151); ch. 5 (pp. 159–183, read at pp. 167–168, 172, 177–183); ch. 6 (pp. 185–229, read at pp. 185–186, 195–201, 213–217, 223–229).
- **Only searched (grep for women, dress, oath, *mukhannath*, eunuch, disguise, Jew):** the rest of chs. 4–6, which follow Yaʿqūb's and ʿAmr's campaigns against the Ṭāhirids, the Zaydīs and the caliph. Not read: the bibliography.

**Scope of the book.** The old Iranian world from Iraq to the Indian border, 800–1055 (p. 36). Nothing on the Maghreb, al-Andalus (except one battle, p. 75), Egypt or the Sahel. Anything below about those regions is marked as general knowledge.

**Tor's thesis, and how far to trust it.** Tor argues against nearly every earlier scholar (Nöldeke, Bosworth, Cahen, Lapidus, Lambton, Sabari, Paul: pp. 11–21). They read the *ʿayyārūn* as brigands, mobs or urban poor. She reads them as:
1. in the 9th c., sworn bands of Sunni volunteer warriors (*mutaṭawwiʿa*);
2. from the 9th–10th c., men of a chivalric code (*futuwwa*, Persian *javānmardī*) tied to Sufis;
3. a well-connected armed elite, not an underclass (pp. 34–35, 289–297).

Her evidence is good on the Ṣaffārid sources and the Persian courtly texts. Her equation of *ʿayyār* with *mutaṭawwiʿ* is her own case, made against the consensus she reports. Use it as one strong reading, not settled fact. Her tone is partisan in places:
- she calls al-Mutawakkil's persecution of non-Muslims "commendable intolerance" (p. 94 n. 43);
- her last footnote draws a line from medieval volunteers to a 1993 al-Qāʿida journal (pp. 294–95 n. 12).

None of that framing belongs in the mod.

## 2. Finding: volunteer warriors (*mutaṭawwiʿa*) as a private, self-financed movement

- **Origin.** After the failure before Constantinople in 717 and the collapse of the 740s, the caliphs let the frontier slide. Into the gap stepped volunteers, "fiercely religious Sunnis (or proto-Sunnis)", mostly from eastern Iran, who went to the Byzantine frontier (p. 42). Founders: al-Awzāʿī, ʿAbdallāh b. al-Mubārak, Ibrāhīm b. Adham, Abū Isḥāq al-Fazārī (pp. 44–60).
- **They paid their own way.** In 159/775–76 al-Mahdī sent an expedition to India. A thousand Basran volunteers "went out with him on their own money", and al-Mahdī's commander could still name an officer over them (p. 69).
- **They stood apart from the state.** Their hallmark was fighting "irrespective of the directives of the caliph or the government" (p. 45). Al-Fazārī threw anyone who dealt with the government out of his circle (p. 52). Ibn al-Mubārak, asked who the kings are: "The ascetics" (p. 56).
- **A life in three rotations.** Ibn al-Mubārak was said to spend one year on the Hajj, one on a raid and one in trade, sharing his profit among his companions (p. 55). He kept up a cloth trade so that he could visit his teachers (p. 54 n. 85). This is a model for a devout trader-warrior with no landed tie.
- **Organisation by the late 9th c.**
  - Companies marched with their own *faqīh*. A Nishapur "army of the *mutaṭawwiʿa*" bound for Tarsus had an *amīr* (ʿAbdallāh b. al-Ashkam al-Khwārizmī) and a jurist-preacher (p. 67).
  - Bukhara had a *raʾīs al-mutaṭawwiʿa* by the mid-9th c. (p. 67).
  - Tarsus, rebuilt in 181/797, had a hostel (*ribāṭ*) for the volunteers of every great city from Sistan to the Maghrib (pp. 62–63).
- **A band takes a town.** Aḥmad b. Tawba, a student of Ibn al-Mubārak, "conquered Isfījāb with 40 men". Their descendants were pointed out there as "the children of The Forty" (p. 64).
- **Commoners with horses.** At the siege of Heraclea (190/806), twenty volunteers asked Hārūn to let one of them answer a Byzantine champion: "We are commoners", so a loss would not shame the army (pp. 73–74). Their champion Ibn al-Khazarī won by a feigned flight (a *ḥīla*, the trick later typical of *ʿayyārs*). Tor's point (p. 74): these "commoners" owned war-horses, so they were not poor.
- **Rulers feared them.**
  - In 205/820–21 ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Mutaṭawwiʿī raised troops at Nishapur against the Khārijites "without the command of the governor". Al-Maʾmūn's court rushed to appoint Ṭāhir b. al-Ḥusayn over the East lest the volunteer leader be given the vicegerency (pp. 75–76).
  - In 231/845–46 the caliph al-Wāthiq killed Aḥmad b. Naṣr with his own hand. Aḥmad's volunteers had grown so strong that "the ʿAbbāsid state feared" a schism (p. 76).
- **Vigilantes when the state failed (201/816–17).** With Baghdad left to ruffians who "seized women and youths openly" (p. 78), Khālid al-Daryūsh and then Sahl b. Salāma raised their quarters to "command the good". Sahl hung a Qurʾān round his neck, and crowds pledged him allegiance (pp. 78–79). The usurper Ibrāhīm b. al-Mahdī jailed Sahl and announced his death "from fear of the people" (p. 80). Al-Maʾmūn later rewarded him and told him to stay home (p. 80).
- **A mother's leave to go.** A Khurāsānī asked Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal: "My mother has permitted me to go on a *ghazw*". He meant to go to Tarsus, and Ibn Ḥanbal told him to raid the Turks, the nearer infidels (pp. 61–62).

**Serves:** the Islamic neighbours (a volunteer frontier culture that a ruler can neither command nor ignore); Badenya (the mother's leave; general shape of a Muslim frontier society to the north).

## 3. Finding: the first *ʿayyārs*, and how governors bought, fought or fed them (Sistan, 807–861)

- **First appearance.** Not Baghdad in 811–13, as usually said, but Sistan in 191–92/807. A governor shut out of Zarang by Khārijites came back with Abū al-ʿUryān, "an *ʿayyār* from Sistan, one of the troop commanders, and the commonalty were his friends" (p. 82, *Tārīkh-i Sīstān*).
- **Bought with banquets.** Ibn Tarassul, governor from 200/815, made peace with the Khārijites and spent the province's whole revenue inviting the *ʿayyārs* to banquets (p. 83).
- **Revolt and soft handling.** In 211/827 an *ʿayyār* led a revolt in Bust, joined by the common people. A governor put it down; no cause is known (p. 83).
- **Revolts against appeasement, 230–31/844–46.** The governor Ibrāhīm al-Qūṣī "never warred upon the Khārijites". Men revolted at Bust, and "the *ʿayyārs* and heroes [*mardān-i mard*]" gathered to them (p. 100). His son was ejected, and a milder sub-governor, Yaḥyā b. ʿAmr, kept Bust quiet by "treating the people kindly" (p. 100).
- **A band's power base.** By c. 845, "all of [Ṣāliḥ b. Naṣr's] military strength derived from Yaʿqūb b. al-Layth and the *ʿayyārs* of Sistan" (p. 100).
- **Banners and the townspeople, 234/854.** Ṣāliḥ entered Zarang by night with Yaʿqūb, his brothers and "the *ʿayyārs* of Sistan". To the elders the governor sent, he said: "I have come to fight the Khārijites ... there is no war between Ibrāhīm al-Qūṣī and me" (p. 107). The governor ambushed him, then fled to the Khārijite leader. Yaʿqūb fought under the black ʿAbbāsid banners against the governor's white ones. The townspeople, "on account of the Khārijites", sided with the *ʿayyārs* (pp. 108–109). Ṣāliḥ also went to consult the leading jurist of Sistan (p. 108).

**Tor's summary of the band (pp. 101, 185):** "Sunni *mutaṭawwiʿ* who fought in brotherhoods or bands". She suggests the *ʿayyārs* began "possibly as sworn brotherhoods of *mutaṭawwiʿa*" (p. 84). "Possibly" is hers: no oath text survives from the 9th c.

## 4. Finding: the Ṣaffārid path from band to dynasty (the band-to-landholder case)

The book's centre (chs. 3–6). In order:

1. **Craftsman to band.** Yaʿqūb and his brother ʿAmr were coppersmiths (*ṣaffār*), Tor doubts whether this is literal (p. 102 n. 87). They "manifested asceticism" and joined Ṣāliḥ b. al-Naḍr (or Naṣr) al-Mutaṭawwiʿī of Bust, who fought the Khārijites. Ṣāliḥ made Yaʿqūb his deputy (pp. 102–103, Ibn al-Athīr and Ibn Khallikān).
   - The hostile Gardīzī gives a ladder: Yaʿqūb was a hired labourer at 15 dirhams a month, then an *ʿayyār*, then a robber, then a *sarhang*, then *amīr*. Gardīzī lists *ʿayyārī* and robbery as separate stages (p. 107).
2. **Band politics.** Ṣāliḥ's Bust men and Yaʿqūb's Sistan men quarrelled. Dirham b. Naṣr, "also from among the *mutaṭawwiʿa*", took over, and "the army of Sistan ... swore allegiance" to him. Yaʿqūb and Ḥāmid became his commanders (p. 109).
3. **The companions choose.** One version: Dirham plotted against Yaʿqūb, who struck first (p. 109). Another (Ibn Hawqal, Ibn al-Athīr): seeing Dirham's weakness, his companions "transferred the rule over them" to Yaʿqūb for his organising skill and his care for their affairs. Dirham "did not contend ... but rather surrendered it" and stayed in Yaʿqūb's band (pp. 110–111). A third: the Ṭāhirids lured Dirham to Baghdad and jailed him. Freed, he served the caliph, went on the Hajj and lived piously at home, later returning as the caliph's envoy (pp. 103, 110–111). **Master of Sistan, 247/861** (p. 109).
4. **Amnesty for rebels.** Yaʿqūb wrote to the Khārijite ʿAmmār telling him to drop his claim to be Commander of the Faithful and "make one cause with us" (p. 112). Thousands of Khārijites defected once they saw they would get amnesty and a place in his army (p. 113). The holdouts he destroyed in 251/865 (p. 114).
5. **Frontier raids.** He raided Zābulistān and Kābulistān every year, killing the Zunbil (pp. 114–115). In 249/863 he refused to take the enemy's war-elephants as booty, citing the Qurʾān's "people of the elephant" (p. 115). He minted no coins and built nothing grand (pp. 115–116).
6. **Patent and robe.** In 253/867 he took Herat, then left when asked. The Ṭāhirid ruler sent "the patent for Sistan, Kabul, Kirman and Fars and a robe of honour", and Yaʿqūb "was patient and returned" (p. 122). Notables of Fars wrote praising his "volunteer fighting for religion" but asked him not to shed blood without a caliphal letter (p. 129).
7. **The sword as diploma (Nishapur).** When notables murmured that he had no caliphal patent, he had a sword brought: "This sword has placed me, too, in this position", for justice and against "the people of deviation" (p. 150, *Tārīkh-i Sīstān*). In Gardīzī he pulls the sword from under his prayer mat: "This is my *ʿahd* and standard" (p. 150 n. 164). Tor reads it as a *ghāzī* claim, not "might makes right" (pp. 150–151).
8. **Overreach and refusal.** Lured to Iraq, his army was flooded out at Dayr al-ʿĀqūl (262/876) (p. 167). He then refused an alliance against the caliph with the rebel Zanj, answering with Sūra 109, "I worship not that which you worship" (p. 172). He died in 265/879 (p. 177).
9. **The *ʿayyār* army's loyalty was ideological.**
   - Masʿūdī describes communal living, provisioning and obedience "whose like has never been heard of" (p. 180).
   - His tent held only weapons and sackcloth: "nothing but lightness is fitting for us" (p. 181).
   - The *Tārīkh-i Sīstān* says he gave converts back their property and children with a robe of honour, and took no land-tax from anyone with under 500 dirhams (p. 180).
10. **Fall under ʿAmr (287/900).** ʿAmr built (a palace, treasury and market at Nishapur, p. 223 n. 222). Before Balkh he boasted he could dam the Oxus with money (p. 214). The Sāmānid Ismāʿīl wrote that he was "a border man ... my clothing is coarse, my men are rabble without pay" (p. 214). He then won over ʿAmr's commanders: "We are *ghāzīs* and do not possess wealth; while this man continually seeks this world" (p. 215). ʿAmr's army melted away, he was captured, and his heirs would not ransom him (p. 215). The caliph is said to have praised him as a frontier guardian before he was killed in prison (p. 217).
11. **Collapse under an heir who was never an *ʿayyār*.**
    - The grandson Ṭāhir gave himself to drink, hunting, mules and pigeons and would not receive commanders (p. 224).
    - The Arab chief Iyās b. ʿAbdallāh resigned: "This reign was established by the sword, and you want to keep it by amusing yourself" (p. 225, *Tārīkh-i Sīstān*).
    - The officers trussed Ṭāhir up and sent him to Baghdad in 296/908–09 (p. 226).
12. **Afterlife of the band.** When a Sāmānid governor raised taxes and billeted troops in Zarang, a former Ṣaffārid client led "the *ʿayyārs*" in revolt in the name of ʿAmr's ten-year-old great-grandson (p. 228). The governor could not find him because he had gone "among the *ʿayyārs*" (p. 228). Tor reads this as a hint that they already lived together. The Sāmānids retook Sistan in 300/913 (p. 228).

**Tor's verdict (p. 227):** "the only known *ʿayyār* state". Had Yaʿqūb not had brothers, Sistan "might have witnessed ... an *ʿayyār* state on the Mamluk model", with the band electing each new leader.

**Serves:** any faith's landless band. The steps map onto vanilla Roads to Power (see §12).

## 5. Finding: other *ʿayyār* routes to rule or office

- **The Sāmānid founder.** Sāmān "became occupied with *ʿayyārī*. After a short time he became ruler over the town of Ashnās". The source is a pro-Sāmānid, anti-Ṣaffārid Persian history (pp. 106, 260).
- **A Sāmānid cousin.** Abū ʿAlī Ilyās "in the beginning engaged in *ʿayyārī*", took Kirman and held it 37 years (p. 261). He fed the Daylamites besieging him by night: "During the day, you are my enemies ... but at night ... you are my guests" (p. 261).
- **Princes recommend it.** The *Qābūs-nāma* (11th c., by a Ziyārid prince for his son; the date 1082 is general knowledge) treats *ʿayyārī* as a noble calling (pp. 248–250).
- **The police.** In 327/938–39 Ibn Yazdād, made police chief of Baghdad, "appointed a group of the *ʿayyārīn*", gave them oaths of safety (*amān*), then whipped them when they stole garments (p. 268). A century later the authorities were still trying to draft them into the police (p. 268 n. 62).
- **A share of the tolls (1032–33).** The official of the upper Baghdad tollhouse gave the *ʿayyār* al-Burjumī a share of the tolls and two large boats in return for keeping order (p. 273). In 425/1033–34 the *ʿayyārs* levied market taxes and took the police chief's dues, "addressed as 'commanders'" (p. 273).
- **Caravan escort.** In 265/878–79 the Arab tribes near Dimimmā murdered "Jaʿlān the *ʿayyār*" because "he used to go out to protect the caravans". The government sent troops after the killers (p. 260).
- **Town defence.** At Ḥarrān (352/963), after the Ḥamdānid's slave fined the town a million dirhams and left it with no governor, "the *ʿayyārīn* ruled over its people" (p. 260).
- **Parade troops.** For a Chinese embassy to Bukhara, the Sāmānid Naṣr b. Aḥmad put out 1,700 banners, "between two hundred and a thousand *ʿayyārs*" with each (p. 261). They were mounted and on foot, in the *ghāzī* host (p. 262).
- **Armed retainers.** In Mosul (392/1001–02) a secretary kept "a group of infantry who bore weapons and followed the path of *ʿiyāra*" (p. 270). In Damascus (368/978–79) the *ʿayyārs* of the notable Qassām drove out the governor (p. 270).
- **Pardon for service.** In 380/990, after a sectarian riot, *ʿayyārs* were hunted down. One leader, "known as Ibn Javānmard", was amnestied by Bahāʾ al-Dawla because "he had shown pity ... and guarded the markets" (p. 278).

## 6. Finding: *futuwwa* — the code, initiation, dress and oath

- **Initiation and dress (the only explicit account).** From Ibn al-Jawzī, *Talbīs Iblīs* (12th c.; his death in 1201 is general knowledge), a hostile cleric (p. 248). The *ʿayyārs* call themselves *fityān* and their order *futuwwa*. "Sometimes one of them swears by the truth of the *futuwwa*, and abstains from food and drink" (p. 248). And: "They dress trousers (*sarāwīl*) upon the initiate into their rite, as the Sufis clothe the initiate in a patched garment" (p. 248). They boast of enduring pain. Their stated rule: a *fatā* "does not commit fornication and does not lie, and preserves the sacredness of women" (pp. 247–248).
- **Patched robe.** Nūḥ, head of all the *ʿayyārs* of Nishapur, defined his *javānmardī* to a Sufi as putting on the *muraqqaʿa* so that shame before people keeps him from sin (pp. 233–234). A Sufi on why he did not wear it: "It is hypocrisy to wear the clothing of the *fityān*" without bearing their burdens (p. 197). Dress could be borrowed both ways.
- **Leaders and structure.** "All the *ʿayyārs* of Nishapur were under [Nūḥ's] command" (pp. 198, 233). Aḥmad b. Khiḍrawayh (d. 855) wanted to invite "a cunning *ʿayyār* who was the leader of the *fityān* in their city" to dinner (p. 235). Junayd called ʿAbdallāh al-Ribāṭī "head of the *fityān* of Khurāsān" (p. 242).
- **The code (definitions gathered pp. 243–250).** Its parts:
  - **Justice:** Abū Ḥafṣ al-Ḥaddād: give justice, never demand it for oneself (pp. 196, 244).
  - **Equal treatment:** treat resident and stranger alike, and make no distinction at one's table between a saint and an infidel (p. 244).
  - **Loyalty:** keeping promises and loyalty (p. 245), and forgiving the brothers their faults (p. 245).
  - **Generosity:** keep the house door, the table and the purse open to friends (p. 250).
  - **The stranger under protection:** if a man, even one's worst enemy, "has surrendered to you ... and entrusted himself to your chivalry", protect him at the risk of your life (p. 250, *Qābūs-nāma*).
- **The riddle of the road.** The *Qābūs-nāma* tells of mountain *ʿayyārs* challenged by the town *ʿayyārs* (pp. 249–250). A killer asks an *ʿayyār* at the roadside which way his quarry went. Pointing him on is betrayal and lying is forbidden. So the *ʿayyār* moves one step and says: "While I have been sitting here nobody has passed" (p. 249).
- **Soldier and *ʿayyār* differ.** In the *Qābūs-nāma*, "a cavilling tongue, regard for self, obedience and submission to command are merits in a soldier, they are faults in an *ʿayyār*" (p. 250).
- **Truthful speech.** "We do everything that we say", an *ʿayyār* tells a Sufi (p. 235).
- **Sufi side.** Tor traces *ʿayyār*–Sufi ties to the 9th c. Abū Ḥafṣ of Nishapur, a Ṣaffārid supporter, taught *futuwwa* and offered to teach Junayd "generosity and chivalry" (pp. 195–196). The Malāmatiyya, who hid their virtue under blame, are treated as near kin of the *ʿayyārs* (pp. 232–234, 237–238). A 15th-c. manual tells the Sufi novice to be "an *ʿayyār* in nature" (p. 236; outside 1453).
- **Women in the code: protection, not membership.** A highwayman before his conversion, Fuḍayl b. ʿIyāḍ "would not attack" a party of travellers with a woman in it, and always left the poor something (p. 257, Hujwīrī). Al-Burjumī (420s/1030s) "would not stand in the way of a woman, nor of one who had submitted to him" (p. 248). Aswad al-Zabad (360s/970s) bought a slave girl for 1,000 dinars. She said she disliked him and asked to be sold; instead he freed her before the qadi and gave her 1,000 dinars (p. 248).

**Serves:** the Islamic neighbours above all (a code a Muslim lord or town can live by, which gives a protected stranger a claim on him). Sister-Making already exists; *futuwwa* is its Islamic neighbour, not a source for it.

## 8. Finding: violence, sectarianism and the clerics

- **Who wrote the bad press.** Arabic clerical and bureaucratic chroniclers (Ibn al-Jawzī, Ibn al-Athīr, Miskawayh, Tanūkhī) condemn the *ʿayyārs*. Persian courtly works (*Qābūs-nāma*, *Samak-i ʿayyār*, the *Tārīkh-i Sīstān*) praise them (pp. 263–265, 291–293). Tor compares Peter of Blois on knights (p. 292).
- **Protection rackets and market taxes** were what governors and Turkish officers also did: "as the ruler would do with those whom he mulcted" (p. 271, 417/1026).
- **Sectarian targets.** In Buyid Baghdad, almost every *ʿayyār* outbreak Tor traces fell on Shiʿi quarters (al-Karkh, Bāb al-Ṭāq) or Shiʿi targets (pp. 268–286). This includes the killing of a Fāṭimid missionary in 391/1000–01, whose slave woman lost her hand shielding him (p. 279). It also includes rival pilgrimages by "the *ʿayyārs* of the people of the Sunna" and "the *ʿayyārs* of al-Karkh" in 425/1033–34 (p. 284).
- **Merchants.** In 362/972–73 the *ʿayyārūn* "deprived [the merchants] of their wealth, their goods, their wives, and their houses", and sold "protection" to whichever side paid (p. 278, Miskawayh). Al-Karkh is called "the place of the merchants and the Shiʿa" (p. 277).
- **Jihad money.** In 355/966 Khurāsānī volunteers bound for the Byzantine front demanded that the Buyid governor of Rayy hand them the land-tax, since it was meant for jihad, and fought the Daylamites when refused (p. 243 n. 52; p. 264).

**Design consequence:** none of this sectarian violence belongs in the mod. It is listed so that no one imports it by accident (see §11).

## 9. Women, *mukhannathūn*, eunuchs, gender crossing, disguise: findings and absences

- **No woman *ʿayyār*, *fatā* or *mutaṭawwiʿa*** anywhere in the book. No woman leads, joins or is initiated into a band. Tor never raises the question.
- **Women appear as:**
  - **mothers:**
    - a son needs his mother's leave to go raiding (p. 61);
    - Dīnār the *ʿayyār*'s mother admonishes him, then nurses his penance (pp. 239–240, Ibn Qudāma, *Kitāb al-Tawwābīn*, late 12th c.);
    - al-Khayzurān, mother of two caliphs, watches the crowd leave Hārūn for Ibn al-Mubārak (p. 58);
  - **a wife who mocks the *fityān*:** Umm ʿAlī tells Aḥmad b. Khiḍrawayh to kill sheep, cattle and donkeys along the way if he invites one, so that "at least ... the dogs of the quarter" profit (p. 235);
  - **protected women** in the code (§6);
  - **victims and captives:** Kurds in Kirman "deflowered two hundred virgins" and carried off 2,000 women, a charge Yaʿqūb made against a rival governor (p. 129). Ruffians "seized women and youths openly" in 816 (p. 78). Merchants' wives were seized in 972 (p. 278). These are not usable as events;
  - **a slave woman** who shields her master and loses her hand (p. 279);
  - **a penitent** woman at the Kaʿba, in the same repentance book (p. 240);
  - **Yaʿqūb's possible wife:** one late source (Ibn Khallikān) says he married an unnamed Arab woman of Sistan, whom ʿAmr later married. Tor rejects it. The *Tārīkh-i Sīstān* says he "never gazed with a shameless glance upon anyone, neither woman nor young boy" (p. 182). She ties his celibacy to the volunteers' ideal of shedding family ties (p. 182).
- ***Mukhannathūn*: one passing use.** In a hostile anecdote about Junayd, Abū Ḥafṣ says of him: "He made my friends *mukhannathūn*", meaning Junayd imposed on them, which *futuwwa* forbids (p. 196). Tor's note (p. 196 n. 71) cites Rowson for a "possible homosexual meaning". But since the passage is about Junayd giving the friends clothes, "it may simply be referring to dress here, or even to powerlessness". So: an insult meaning "made soft or dependent", in a *futuwwa* quarrel. It is not evidence of *mukhannathūn* in the bands.
- **Effeminacy as a sin to repent.** The same late-12th-c. *Book of Penitents* that holds Dīnār the *ʿayyār* also has "the repentance of a youth for effeminacy and displaying effeminate behaviour" (p. 240). Tor's paraphrase; she does not quote it. This is hostile-world material.
- **Gender crossing / disguise: one case, a test of chastity.** Al-Qushayrī's *Risāla* (11th c.): someone sold Nūḥ the *ʿayyār* of Nishapur a beautiful slave girl "in the clothing of a slave boy". She lived with him for months, and he never touched her and believed she was a boy (p. 234). The disguise is someone else's trick; the point is Nūḥ's purity. The same Nūḥ took 1,000 lashes rather than hand a boy servant over to the ruler, whom Tor assumes wanted him for sex (p. 234).
- **Eunuchs:** none in the bands. The only eunuch-adjacent figure is the caliph's *mawlā* Badr, said to have urged ʿAmr's killing (p. 217); the book does not call him a eunuch.
- **Cross-reference, not from Tor:** Hamadan 455/1063, *ʿayyārūn* hire *mukhannathūn* with drums and pipes to feast over their dead enemies (`later_starts/mukhannathun_baghdad_1066.md`, Ibn al-Jawzī 16:82).
- **Sexual violence:** Tor cites al-Tīfāshī for a gang rape of a boy by *ʿayyārs* and doubts it (p. 266 n. 55). Not usable.
- **Design consequence.** As with Sophoulis: a band of trans women among Muslim neighbours rests on the mod's invention. What Tor offers is the *code* (protecting women and anyone who has surrendered to one's chivalry), the *dress* (borrowed robes and trousers) and *a story of a girl in a boy's clothes treated with honour*. These belong in the Islamic neighbours' voice, never as claims that such bands admitted women.

## 11. Risks and cautions

- **Sectarian violence.** Tor's *ʿayyārs* in Baghdad mostly attack Shiʿis. No mechanic or event should reward a band for harming another Muslim sect, or any community. Do not model protection rackets on a quarter.
- **Bandit-people labelling.** The sources and some modern scholars cast Sistanis, Kurds (p. 129), Arab tribes (p. 260), Daylamites and Turks as plunderers. Do not gate brigand or *ʿayyār* mechanics on any culture. *ʿAyyār* is a calling, not an ethnicity.
- **Khārijites.** The book frames them as heretics to be fought. If Khārijite characters appear, do not make fighting them a holy deed in the narrator's voice.
- **Jihad framing.** The volunteers' purpose was holy war. The mod can use their *organisation* (self-financed bands, a code, a rotation of trade, pilgrimage and service) without endorsing the war. Tor's modern-jihadist footnote (pp. 294–95 n. 12) and her partisan asides (p. 94 n. 43) are not to be echoed.
- **Ha-Nehpakhot guardrails.**
  - The book's one Jew is a convert who died raiding Byzantium (p. 63). Do not use him.
  - The book has merchants robbed and "protected" for money (p. 278); keep money out of any Jewish content.
  - The *futuwwa* protection proposals below give protection *for service and honour, not gold*. A Jewish character is never the one who sells out or harasses.
- **Geography.** Everything here is Iran and Iraq. Ha-Nehpakhot's Muslim lords are in the Maghreb and Sahel, and Badenya's Muslim neighbours in the Sahel. That *futuwwa* or *ribāṭ* culture reached them is general knowledge: the Almoravids grew from a *ribāṭ* in the 11th c., and *futuwwa* orders spread west under the caliph al-Nāṣir after 1182. **Not from this book.** Check before any loc places a *fatā* in Fez, Qayrawan or Walata.
- **Gender.** No source here shows a woman or a gender-crossing person in a band. The slave girl in boy's clothes (p. 234) is a trick played on Nūḥ, not her choice. Use it in an Islamic courtier's voice as a story about honour, never as a precedent for crossing. Do not cite p. 196 as evidence of *mukhannathūn* among the *fityān*.
- **No sexual violence content** (pp. 129, 266 n. 55, 278).
- **The salt story** (pp. 105–106) is from a 14th-c. history Tor calls riddled with errors and apocryphal. Use as legend in a character's mouth.

## 13. What is still missing

- **The Maghreb and the Sahel.** Nothing on *ribāṭs*, *futuwwa* or volunteer bands west of Iraq except Spain in 806. A source on the Almoravid *ribāṭ* and on Maghrebi *fityān* or urban militias would ground proposals 3 and 8 for Ha-Nehpakhot. Something on Sahelian Muslim courts would do the same for Badenya.
- **Post-1055 *futuwwa*.** Tor stops at the Saljuqs. The caliph al-Nāṣir's reorganised *futuwwa* (after 1182) and the Anatolian *akhī* brotherhoods (13th–14th c., near Dorylaion) are general knowledge, not in this book. A source on the *akhī* would serve Adelphai Dorleyaiou and the Islamic neighbours in Anatolia.
- **The oath text.** No 9th–11th-c. oath or initiation text survives here; the only account is Ibn al-Jawzī's, hostile and 12th-c.
- **Women in *futuwwa*.** Tor offers protection norms only. Whether any women's *futuwwa* or Sufi women's associations existed needs another source.
