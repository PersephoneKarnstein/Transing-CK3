# *Analects* 13.3 and the rectification of names (正名), design reading for the Rectified Names tenet

**Citation.** James Legge (trans.), *The Chinese Classics*, vol. 1: *Confucian Analects, The Great Learning, The Doctrine of the Mean*, Project Gutenberg eBook #4094 (2003, updated 2020).
- **The edition.** The reading list gives Oxford: Clarendon Press, 2nd ed., 1893. The etext itself carries no imprint, year or pagination. It reproduces Legge's Chinese text and translation but **not his commentary notes**. So passages are cited by book and chapter, and the 1893 imprint is unverified from this copy.
- **What was read:** 13.3 in full, and the other core *zhengming* passages: 12.11, 12.17 and 6.23.
- **Not read:** Xunzi ch. 22, "Zhengming"; scholarly accounts of *ming/shi* 名實 (reading list §6).

## 1. The passages

**13.3, the locus classicus.**
- Zilu asks what the Master would do first if the ruler of Wei gave him the government. The answer is 「必也、正名乎」, "What is necessary is to rectify names."
- Zilu calls this "wide of the mark" (迂). Confucius replies with a chain of consequences:
  - 「名不正、則言不順」, "If names be not correct, language is not in accordance with the truth of things";
  - then affairs fail, rites and music decay, and 「刑罰不中」, "punishments will not be properly awarded";
  - and at last "the people do not know how to move hand or foot."
- He closes: a superior man must be able to *say* the names he uses, and *do* what he says.
- **Legge's gloss.** 「言不順」 means literally "speech does not flow / is not consonant". Legge's "not in accordance with the truth of things" is already an interpretation in the *ming/shi* direction, with names matching realities.
- **The political context, from tradition and not from this etext.** The ruler of Wei then held the throne against his own father. Classical commentary reads the "name" at stake as *father* and *son*.

**12.11.** Duke Jing of Qi asks about government: 「君君、臣臣、父父、子子」, "when the prince is prince, and the minister is minister; when the father is father, and the son is son." The duke answers that if the son is "not son", he could not eat his grain even if he had it.

**12.17.** 「政者正也」, "To govern means to rectify."

**6.23.** 「觚不觚、觚哉觚哉」, "A cornered vessel without corners. A strange cornered vessel!" This is the classic image of a thing that no longer matches its name.

## 2. Is the tenet's reading fair?

The tenet says: "the palace calls us eunuchs so it need not call us women". The loc says: "Confucius taught that when names do not match things, the state falls into disorder. The Way agrees, and draws the other conclusion…"

**Where it is fair.**
- 13.3 does say that wrong names corrupt speech, and that the chain ends in **punishments wrongly awarded**. A palace register that files a woman as a eunuch, followed by a censor acting on that register (the month-3 event), is close to the exact chain Confucius describes.
- The loc's summary, "when names do not match things, the state falls into disorder", is a fair paraphrase of 13.3 as Legge reads it.
- 6.23 gives the faith a classical image for the palace's error: it calls her a vessel she is not.

**Where it is strained, and should be written as a turn rather than a gloss.**
1. **Direction.** In the *Analects*, rectifying means making persons conform to their role-names. The prince must *act as* prince, and the son *as* son (12.11). The names are fixed and inherited, and the people must fit them. The Way runs it the other way: the name follows what she has become. That is closer to the later *ming/shi* debates, and to Xunzi's claim that names are fixed by convention and decree. (Xunzi was not read in this pass; check before citing.) The *Analects* itself does not say a person may choose her name.
2. **Who rectifies.** In 13.3 it is the ruler's minister, and 12.17 makes rectifying the work of governing. *Zhengming* is a tool of the state. The faith takes up the state's own tool, which is a strong move in its own voice, but it is an appropriation.
3. **The tradition used it against gender-crossers.** Sommer (2024, p. 168) cites Zeitlin calling the castration ending of a sex-change tale "a perverse Confucian 'rectification of names'": the body was cut to fit the name (see `china_reading_list.md` §5). The Han omen reading 「男化為女，宮刑濫也」 treats a man becoming a woman as a sign of misgoverned punishment (reading list, finding 3). Lo Tung-hwa shows scholar-officials denying eunuchs' sons by naming: 「謁者監何由有兒」, "how does a Palace Steward come to have a boy?" (`cn_lo_tunghwa_tang_adoption.md`, p. 40). In period, rectification of names was mostly done *to* people like the Way's daughters.
4. **12.11 is the hostile reply already in the text.** "Let the son be son." A palace scholar would quote it against her.

**Verdict.** It is a fair *polemical* reading in the faith's own voice, and the tenet script comment ("zhengming turned on its makers") already frames it correctly. It is not a fair *exegetical* reading. Narrator text must not say Confucius supports it. The current tenet description says "Confucius taught … The Way agrees, and draws the other conclusion". That keeps the line between them, so it can stand.

## 4. In-world loc lines (attribute in-world)

- "The Master said: if names are not correct, punishments miss their mark. The register calls her a eunuch, and the censor punishes the name."
- "A cornered vessel without corners: so the palace keeps a eunuch who is a woman."
- Scholar (hostile): "Let the son be son. That is the whole of government."
- Answer (Way): "Then let the daughter be daughter. We ask nothing more of your Master."
