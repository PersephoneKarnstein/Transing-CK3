## 2. Key theses of the most relevant chapters

**Stephen Whittle — Foreword (L244–L497).** Trans identity became a mass phenomenon through 1990s home computing and online community; "trans" as a stand-alone word was "coined by a parliamentary discussion group in London in 1998" (L276–278). He frames the core theoretical fight as essentialism vs constructionism and says trans people themselves increasingly doubt pure nurture explanations. Quote: "Homophobia and sexism are not based on your genitals or with whom you sleep, but on how you perform the self in ways that are contraindicative to the heteronormative framework" (L351–353).

**Susan Stryker — (De)Subjugated Knowledges (L501–L1324).** Transgender studies is defined as a Foucauldian "insurrection of subjugated knowledges": buried historical content (ethnographies, case law, asylum files) plus the disqualified experiential knowledge of trans people themselves (L1061–L1105). Western preoccupation with gender variance is traced "since Greek and Roman antiquity" (L1110) and through "a centuries-old ethnography" of colonial encounter that produced "a parade of gender exotics" (L1137–L1151). Quotes: "'Transgender' moved from the clinics to the streets over the course of that decade, and from representation to reality" (L581–582); "'Transgender' is, without a doubt, a category of First World origin that is currently being exported for Third World consumption" (L1156–1157). Also useful: the "homonormativity" critique of queer studies (L826–828) and the parallel drawn between trans inclusion fights and earlier feminist fights over working-class women, women of color, lesbians, SM practitioners (L807–817).

**Magnus Hirschfeld — The Transvestites (L1651–L2236).** Hirschfeld, "a gay, Jewish, Socialist" (L1661), places cross-dressers among innumerable "sexual intermediaries" on a male–female spectrum and argues the drive is neither homosexuality, fetishism nor masochism. He coins "transvestism" from Latin "used also by the Roman classical writers" (L2156–2157) and judges that "from a purely medical standpoint, nothing can be said against the actual putting on of the clothing of the opposite sex" (L2195–2197), while advising that spouses be told everything (L2210).

**Harry Benjamin — 1954 paper (L2481–L2867).** Benjamin (ex-Hirschfeld and Steinach collaborator) argues psychotherapy cannot cure the desire to change sex and that aetiology mixes "constitutional, psychological, and hormonal influences" (L2506–2508). He records that Havelock Ellis "proposed the term 'eonism,' naming it after its prototype, the Chevalier D'Eon" (L2523) and notes "castration produces a eunuch and not a woman" (L2822).

**Donna Haraway — A Cyborg Manifesto (L5303–L6115).** The essay that Sandy Stone built on. Its political-economy core: "Marxian socialism is rooted in an analysis of wage labour which reveals class structure... Labour is the pre-eminently privileged category enabling the Marxist to overcome illusion" (L5635–5639); socialist-feminism "expand[ed] the category of labour to accommodate what (some) women did" (L5643–5644). She faults both Marxist and MacKinnon-style radical feminism for erasing difference, and famously "would rather be a cyborg than a goddess" (L5969).

**Janice Raymond — Sappho by Surgery (L6591–L7246).** The text everything else reacts to. Trans women who are lesbian-feminists are "boundary violators" who "rape women's bodies by reducing the real female form to an artifact" (L6746–6747). She likens them to historical eunuchs used as "keepers of women" and asks "Will every lesbian-feminist space become a harem?" (L6768–6787). Her self-definition thesis: "we know who we are... Transsexuals have not had this same history" (L7006–7009). She also uses the Dionysus/Zeus "single parenthood by the father" myth (L6800–6827).

**Carol Riddell — Divided Sisterhood (L7251–L8002).** The first trans-feminist rebuttal (1980). Riddell offers a materialist account of patriarchy — "the sexual division of labour which centres women's primary existence around the bringing up of children and the servicing of people, and men's about the production of things" (L7528–7530) — and compares Raymond to "some Marxists, who, accepting hetero-sexuality as 'given', define homo-sexuality as an aberration of class society" (L7536–7537). She frames anti-trans feminism as scapegoating in the lineage of witch-hunts: "Witches were not responsible for the social evils of the mediaeval form of patriarchy; they attempted to alleviate them" (L7885–7886). Her utopian aside: in a post-patriarchal society "there will still be trans-sexuals... all they will need is an operation" (L7806–7809).

**Lou Sullivan — A Transvestite Answers a Feminist (L8004–L8299).** Pre-transition Sullivan, a "female transvestite" at home among drag queens rather than lesbians, defends gendered self-image against a feminist co-worker's demand to "junk any kind of image" (headnote L8025). Diary line: "I want them to think, 'There's one of those people with their own interpretation of happiness'" (L8030–8031).

**Judith Butler — Doing Justice to Someone (L9127–L9664).** Reads the David Reimer case through "a certain regulatory regime, one that informs the law, and one that also exceeds the law" (L9152–9153). Editors flag the trans objection: "if gender is not real, how real can its oppression be?" (L9150).

**Stephen Whittle — Where Did We Go Wrong? (L9666–L10098).** A former "radical separatist" in Manchester's Radical Lesbian Collective (1974) who came out as a man and was given "shirts and ties out of the back of 'formerly identified as butch' women's wardrobes" (L9738). He contrasts Liberal, Marxist and Radical Separatist feminism (L9716–9731) and argues Raymond's discourse "promotes radical separatism as the only viable alternative to the patriarchal hegemony" while "liberal feminism and Marxist feminism will always open the gate to the horse" (L9808–9810). Closing: "Feminism is about a better set of values in which gender loses some of its power of oppression" (L10088–10089).

**Leslie Feinberg — Transgender Liberation (L10105–L10872).** The one explicitly Marxist text. Thesis (editors): "transgender people in pre-capitalist tribal and agrarian societies were revered and honored, while the widespread contemporary oppression of gender-variant people is an effect of the capitalist mode of production" (L10123–10125). Feinberg's own words: "Transgender is a very ancient form of human expression that pre-dates oppression. It was once regarded with honor" (L10232–10233); "It is passing that's historically new" (L10223); "We are the class that does the work of the world, and can revolutionize it" (L10863–10864). The pamphlet is a world-historical catalogue (see §5) culminating in "From Joan of Arc to Stonewall".

**Sandy Stone — Posttranssexual Manifesto (L10876–L11620).** Against both clinical and Raymondite erasure, Stone calls on trans people to stop passing and to "read oneself aloud" (L11454–11455): "The essence of transsexualism is the act of passing" (L11452); "I suggest constituting transsexuals not as a class or problematic 'third gender,' but rather as a genre—a set of embodied texts" (L11382–11383). She turns Raymond's "transsexuals divide women" into a call "to multiplicatively divide the old binary discourses of gender" (L11379–11380). Historical capsule: Sardanapalus, Philo, d'Eon, Lord Cornbury (L10957–10964).

**Kate Bornstein — Gender Terror, Gender Rage (L11623–L12004).** Redefines "gender terrorists" as the "Gender Defenders" (L11659–11661). Cites the Navajo nadle as "historical, cross-cultural precedent" for the fear that MTFs act as "strike-breakers or scabs" against women (L11755–11766) but says the real problem "is male privilege" (L11774). Refuses to call separatist exclusion "oppression" (L11936–11939) and insists "anger and activism mix about as well as drinking and driving" (L11950).

**Susan Stryker — My Words to Victor Frankenstein (L12010–L12636).** Claims the monster's position: "I find no more reason to mourn my opposition to them—or to the order they claim to represent—than Frankenstein's monster felt" (L12108–12110). Documents lesbian/bi exclusion driving Filisa Vistima to suicide (L12114–12126) and names Daly and Raymond's construction of "the transsexual as their own particular golem" (L12084).

**Jacob Hale — Are Lesbians Women? (L13804–L14770).** Tests Wittig's "lesbians are not women" against a 13-point reconstruction of the US concept "woman" (none necessary or sufficient; L14272–14484). Verdict: "Some are, some are not, and in many cases there is no fact of the matter" (L14567). Argues gender "should be consensual" (L13893) and that "gender proliferation and feminist redefinition and revaluation of womanhood" should run "at the same time" (L13895–13899). Nuns and lesbians both "escape" the category of sex in Wittig (L14002–14020).

**Dean Spade — Mutilating Gender (L15537–L16493).** Foucauldian critique of gatekeeping: the GID diagnosis "produces a fiction of natural gender" (L15843) and requires a "passing" imperative; trans people lie strategically ("I'll lie my ass off to get what I have to," L15801). Program: "to promote sex reassignment, gender alteration, temporary gender adventure, and the mutilation of gender categories, via surgery, hormones, clothing, political lobbying, civil disobedience, or any other means available" (L15871–15873). Notes success stories are "mediat[ed]... through capitalist values" (L16398).

**Nan Alamilla Boyd — Bodies in Motion (L20808–L21505).** Reads 1990s controversies (Michigan, Powersurge leatherdyke conference) through "lesbian nation" vs state: "the body politic (the nation) exists for intelligible bodies" (L21352). Outlaw bodies sharpen a boundary "between abject and intelligible" (L21357–21358).

**Patrick Califia — Manliness (L21509–L21739).** Post-transition reflection by a lesbian-feminist elder: "if I am no longer a dyke, no longer an Amazon, what/who am I?" (L21599–21600); "Being a fag or a third-gender person is a way for me to try to salvage the good that I saw in my father" (L21711). Keeps "altars to goddesses all over my house" plus Shiva, Cernunnos, Pan, Sulis (L21723–21725) and dreams that "cocksucker" will one day "designate a holy person, a state of priesthood, a healer, a hero" (L21739).

**Zachary Nataf — Lesbians Talk Transgender (L21744–L22229).** Montage of lesbian and trans voices on MTF/FTM inclusion; Nataf's own thesis: "Separatists have re-entrenched behind the view of gender as bi-polar, policing the borders... separatism seems an obsolete tool" (L21929–21931) and transgender is "the heir to the 'anatomy is not destiny' legacy" (L22213–22214). Bornstein quoted: "this transgender movement is simply the next logical phase of feminism" (L22125).

**Gayle Rubin — Of Catamites and Kings (L23208–L23751).** "Butch is also the indigenous lesbian category for women who are gender 'dysphoric'" (L23280). Lesbian communities "harbor... a great deal of gender dysphoria" yet have "a deep streak of xenophobia"; "FTMs are another witch-hunt waiting to happen" (L23483–23491). Closing creed: "let us strive to maintain a community that understands diversity as a gift, sees anomalies as precious" (L23611–23612).

**Viviane Namaste — Genderbashing (L28651–L29549).** "a perceived transgression of normative sex/gender relations motivates much of the violence against sexual minorities" and such violence "is fundamentally concerned with policing gender presentation through public and private space" (L28694–28696); the most exposed are MTF sex workers of color.

**Marjorie Garber — The Chic of Araby (L30946–L32015).** The Orient is gendered feminine; Western men in Arab robes are already cross-dressed. Case studies: Isabelle Eberhardt (Russian-Swiss woman living as "Mahmoud Saadi", Qadiriyya initiate) and the Omani xanith controversy in *Man* (1977–80), where critics argued "Poverty, not the demands of the male role, might be the cause of the xanith's lifestyle" (L31652–31653). US cross-dressers read the xanith as "role models" in an "ethnocentric pluralism" (L31709–31721).

**Katrina Roen — The Risk of Racial Marginalization (L32018–L32497).** Transgender theory is largely white/Western; medicalised "transsexualism" can "efface other cultural responses to gender liminality in colonized geographical locations" (L32031–32032), drawing on Pacific Islander interviews in New Zealand.

**Towle & Morgan — Romancing the Transgender Native (L32499–L33448).** Five flaws in trans use of "third gender": primordial location, reductionism, typological error, inconsistent culture concept, West-vs-rest (L32794–32806). Against Feinberg: "In several cases, this means upholding a rigid gender system by formalizing variations" (L33141–33142); "Transgender and transsexual activists need not invoke mythical gender warriors... (If warriors are sought, they are here.)" (L33262–33264).

**Emi Koyama — Whose Feminism Is It Anyway? (L34098–L34488).** Both the "womyn-born-womyn" camp and the white post-op trans women proposing a "no-penis" policy reproduce racism/classism. "it is never feminist when some women are silenced and sacrificed to make room for the more privileged women" (L34375–34376); "white skin is just as much a reminder of violence as a penis" (L34338–34339); trans women's "borderland" positionality is "an asset" (L34412–34424).

**Richard Juang — Transgendering the Politics of Recognition (L34497–L35178).** Recognition (value, dignity, self-expression) is the basis of rights; racism and transphobia are "mutually constitutive" (Tyra Hunter, Vincent Chin). Economic justice is necessary but not sufficient (L34856–34877). Sets ethics for cross-cultural comparison: historical context, defined purpose, "reciprocity rather than parasitism" (L35081–35082).

## 5. Historical, premodern and non-Western precedents (exhaustive from the file)

Each entry: what the text claims, exact quote, chapter and location. Caveat from the source itself: Towle & Morgan (ch. 47) and Juang (ch. 50) warn that Feinberg's catalogue is "generally ignored as a work of history" (L32931–32932) and that such examples are "romantic", "primordialist" and often appropriative — useful as *flavour* for a medieval faith, not as scholarship.

### A. Ancient Near East, Egypt, Mesopotamia
1. **Assyrian king Sardanapalus** — "the Assyrian king Sardanapalus, who was reported to have dressed in women's clothing and spun with his wives" (Stone, L10958–10959).
2. **Queen Hatshepsut as bearded pharaoh (1485 BC)** — "An Egyptian sculpture of a bearded Queen Hat-shepsut dressed in the garb of a pharaoh... shows the persistence of popular folklore about the bearded woman as a sacred symbol of power and wisdom" (Feinberg, L10338–10340).
3. **Eunuchs as palace officers, warriors, scholars; eunuch priests of goddess temples** — "In Mesopotamia, many eunuchs became royal officers and managers of palaces... Some were famous warriors and statesmen, as well as scholars. One finds eunuchs associated with temples dedicated to the goddesses from at least 2000 B.C. until well into the Roman period" (Raymond, L6776–6779); footnote: "some royal eunuchs also wore women's clothing... as represented on Assyrian monuments... Eunuch priests of goddess temples were said to wear women's garb and perform women's tasks" (L7209–7211).
4. **Minoan Crete** — "the earliest mention of eunuchs is in connection with the Minoan civilization of Crete, which was a transitional period from an earlier gynocentric society" (Raymond, L6779–6781).
5. **Neolithic goddess worship (25,000 BC; pre-4500 BC)** — "artifacts... dating back to 25,000 B.C. that prove these societies worshipped goddesses, not gods. Some of the deities were transgendered, as were many of their shamans" (Feinberg, L10277–10279); "prior to 4500 B.C. goddesses, not gods, were worshipped throughout Europe and Western Asia" (L10321–10322, citing Gimbutas/Lerner).
6. **Mosaic law** — "An early prohibition against transgender was codified in the Mosaic Law of the Hebrews... (Deuteronomy, 22:5)" (Feinberg, L10363–10366) — a ready-made in-game *hostile* doctrine for Abrahamic neighbours.
7. **Islamic harem eunuchs** — "Eunuchs were supervisors of the harem in Islam and wardens of women's apartments in many royal households... the word eunuch, from the Greek eunouchos, literally means 'keeper of the bed'" (Raymond, L6770–6772).
8. **Egyptian eunuchs (19th–20th c.)** — Voronoff: "From the period of my observations among the eunuchs in Egypt" (H. Rubin, L23937); Lane's khawals: "females impersonators called khawals, 'dancers'... who wore their hair long and braided, plucked out the hair of their beards, applied kohl and henna" and Egyptian boys "dressed in girls' clothing" before circumcision and "to avoid the evil eye" (Garber n.44, L31882–31889).
9. **Omani xanith** — Wikan's 1977 account: "effeminate males who wore pastel-colored dishdashas... who functioned as house servants and/or homosexual prostitutes, and who associated... not with the men in this rigidly segregated Muslim society, but with the women" (Garber, L31600–31604); they are "juridically... men", are "punished if they attempt to wear women's clothes", may marry and "cease to be xanith and become men" by consummation, and may revert when widowed (L31628–31640). Critics attributed the role to poverty (L31649–31653).
10. **Isabelle Eberhardt (1877–1904)** — European woman living as "Mahmoud Saadi", "an initiate of the Kadriya brotherhood", "dressed as an Arab, sometimes as a man, and at other times as a woman" (Garber, L31380–31382); "Her head was shaved completely, in the Muslim style, and she wore a tasseled fez" (L31299).

### B. Greece and Rome
11. **Achilles at Scyros; Hercules and Omphale** — "The myth of Achilles notes that he lived and dressed as a woman at the court of Lycomedes in Scyros" (Feinberg, L10346–10347); "Hercules himself spent three years dressed as a woman at the court of Omphale, Queen of Lydia" (L10355–10356).
12. **Cypriot cults: Bearded Aphrodite, Ariadne's mock-birth** — "male priests dressed as women in honor of the Bearded Aphrodite of Cyprus; on the same island, the cult of Ariadne... a boy was dressed in female clothes and proceeded to enact all the symptoms of labor and birth" (Feinberg, L10348–10351, citing Macrobius).
13. **Scythian shamans; priests of Artemis at Ephesus** — "Herodotus noted that Scythian religious shamans spoke and dressed as women and were highly revered. The priests of Artemis at Ephesus were reported to have worn 'women's clothing'" (Feinberg, L10352–10354).
14. **Athenian Oschophoria, Argive Hybristika, Hera at Samos, rites of Hercules at Rome** — "At the vine growers' festival, the Athenian Oschophoria, two boys dressed in women's clothes... At the Argive festival of Hybristika, the men adopted female clothing. At the feast of Hera at Samos, the men wore long, white robes and placed their hair in golden nets" (Feinberg, L10355–10359).
15. **Dionysos as feminine/cross-dressed "make love, not war" god** — "Greek painters and writers portrayed Dionysos as feminine or dressed in women's apparel. Transvestism also persisted in the rituals of Dionysos, which endured even after Christianity became a state religion" (Feinberg, L10376–10378); Raymond's rival reading: Dionysus as "mad god who breaks down boundaries" born of Zeus's thigh (L6819–6827, L6860).
16. **Amazons** — "depictions in artwork of battles against Amazon warriors symbolized the overthrow of matrilineal communal societies" (Feinberg, L10372–10373); Haraway: "Amazons of ancient Greece established the limits of the centred polis of the Greek male human" (L5913).
17. **Philo of Judea; Roman Caesars; Caligula** — "instances of something very like transsexualism were reported by Philo of Judea, during the Roman Empire" (Stone, L10960–10961); "The Roman Caesars were reported to show a fondness for wearing women's clothes and Caligula, according to Seutonius, often adopted female clothing" (Feinberg, L10396–10397).
18. **Elagabalus (r. 218–222)** — "he often appeared in public in drag, practiced ritual sex with members of both sexes, and publicly declared one of his male lovers to be his husband... He was assassinated by an indignant Praetorian Guard in 222 A.D." (Feinberg quoting Arthur Evans, L10399–10404).
19. **Clement of Alexandria (3rd c.)** — "demanded pagan Greeks recognize the error of their beliefs... 'he will at once identify your gods from their disgraceful depictions, Dionysos from his dress'" (Feinberg, L10385–10388).
20. **Baptismal "putting on" (endeuein)** — Stone glosses the Koine verb for baptism as "to put on, to insinuate oneself into, like a glove", with Sahagún's Aztec priest donning "the flayed skin of a young woman" (L11554–11559) — a theological hook for a transition rite framed as baptism.

23. **Christianity's class trajectory** — "Christianity as a mass religion really began in the cities of the Roman empire among the poor... transformed from a revolutionary movement of the urban poor into a powerful state religion that served the wealthy elite" (Feinberg, L10417–10420).
24. **Pagani = peasants; rural transvestite shamans under feudalism** — "The word pagan derives from the Latin paganus, which meant rural dweller or peasant... Many shamans were still transvestites. 30. **Shrovetide cross-dressing (Naogeorgus, 1570)** — "Both men and women chaunge their weede, the men in maydes aray, And wanton wenches drest as men" (Feinberg, L10565–10567).
