### Findings that change the design

1. **At Arles the women's synagogue was downstairs, not a gallery.**
   - A sale of 31 January 1452 conveys three seats "infra scolam Judeorum de Arelate". Two are "in scola hominum desuper" and one is "desubtus in scola Judearum". Hildenfinger's summary reads "deux dans la synagogue supérieure des hommes et une dans la synagogue inférieure des femmes" (REJ 47, p. 233; text REJ 48, pp. 75–76).
   - The sellers are two women, Astruge (daughter of the physician-surgeon Cressent Tefillos) and her daughter Blanquette, so women owned and sold synagogue seats.
   - The same deed calls the men's preaching-place *scacalh*. Hildenfinger guesses it is *heikhal*, the ark (p. 76 n.).
   - **Fix:** in tm_hipukh.0010, change "women's gallery" to the women's synagogue, *la scola de las juzievas*, below the men's. Baumgarten 2016 shows that Ashkenazi women's synagogues (Worms 1212/13, Speyer, Cologne) were separate buildings or annexes. "Gallery" is a modern default.
2. **A ḥevrah is attested at Arles: the *holim* confraternity of 1401.**
   - On 22 April 1401 the *baylons* Crescas Salamias and Vital Calhi asked the royal court to re-found "quamdam elemosinam sive confratriam, ebrayce dictam holim". Its purpose was to feed the poor, clothe the naked and bury poor Jews. Leave was granted for twenty years, and the statutes followed on 2 May.
   - It had 18 members, two yearly administrators, and dues of 3 gros a year. Every poor person got 2 gros a week, member or not. Bread went out after Passover. Members took turns to sit up with the gravely ill, and could pay a substitute. They attended every burial (REJ 41, pp. 69–70; doc. II, from p. 78).
   - On 1407 Hildenfinger writes "les chefs de famille, hommes et femmes, se réunir pour accepter une donation" (REJ 41, p. 63). Women heads of household voted in the *kahal*.
   - **No medieval women's ḥevrah has been found.** Women's sororities with their own bylaws and record books are early modern (Kaplan and Carlebach 2022, Amsterdam to Moravia).
   - **Recommendation:** model the sect as a circle inside a mixed *holim* confraternity. Its sick-watch duty is the cover under which the women meet. Do not present it as a recognised women's ḥevrah. If the mod keeps a "women's ḥevrah", the narrator should call it the sect's own claim.
3. **"Who made me a woman" is a real Provençal women's blessing.**
   - The Judeo-Provençal women's siddur (Leeds, Brotherton Library, MS Roth 32, 14th–15th c.) reads "Bendig tu … qui fis mi fenna". Its first two blessings are "qui non fis mi serventa" and "qui non fis mi goyah" (Baricci 2014, p. 236).
   - Two Hebrew manuscripts copied by Abraham Farissol, a cantor of Avignonese origin, read שעשיתני אשה ולא איש, "who made me a woman and not a man": JTSA 8255 (Ferrara, 1478) and Jerusalem 8° 5492 (Mantua, 1480) (Baricci, p. 236 nn. 48–49, following Kahn 2011).
   - The usual women's form, "who made me according to His will", is first attested in the mid-14th century (*Arba'ah Turim*) as a "custom of women". Kahn and Baricci suggest women may have created it.
   - Baricci links Kalonymus's lament to the same Provençal play on fortune and sex (p. 235 n. 43; p. 236).
   - **Use:** this is the strongest period hook the sect has. It is a blessing women spoke aloud, in Provence, thanking God for making them women. The narrator may cite it as fact. That the sect adopted it is the sect's own act.
   - The title-page dedication "my sister, be the mother of thousands of ten thousands" (Gen 24:60) suggests the book was a wedding gift (Baricci, pp. 232–233).
4. **Catalan reaction is harsher than Provençal.**
   - In the Crown of Aragon, men accused of sodomy were tried. They were often pardoned for a payment (Lleida 1263; Alfandech 1274; Alagón 1328). In 1374 a Jew was condemned to burning for homosexual relations (Assis 2001, p. 44, ACA Reial Patrimoni reg. 1682 f. 83v).
   - Use this for Catalonia: royal courts fining, and once burning. Do not show *beit din* executions.
5. **There is a danger exception to *lo yilbash*.**
   - *Sefer Ḥasidim* (Parma §206, §261) lets women travellers dress as men, or as nuns, to escape rape.
   - §207 lets "young boys who have not yet grown a beard" dress as women or as Christians "to be saved" (Roos 2017, pp. 4–5).
   - Roos reads these permissions as reinforcing the boundary, not crossing it (p. 16).
   - **Reaction data only.** The women-as-men passages are FtM-adjacent. A rabbi could cite §207 to say "the law allows a boy to pass as a girl only to save his life", which concedes nothing.
6. **The Purim masquerade custom is datable.**
   - Davidson (1907, p. xiv) puts Purim masquerading among European Jews "as early as the sixteenth century". *Even Boḥan* (Provence, c. 1322, p. 29) is two centuries earlier and is the stronger witness.
   - Davidson dates *Massekhet Purim* to 1319–1332, places it with the first Hebrew parodies of the Talmud (pp. 19–23), and gives it a whole chapter (Part II, from p. 115).

### Findings that affect the design now

1. **The h-p-k root has one more Talmudic anchor, on the same page as Sarah the *aylonit*.**
   - In bYevamot 64a, R. Yitzḥak says the prayer of the righteous is like a pitchfork: "just as this pitchfork turns over [מהפך] the produce", so prayer "turns over [מהפכת] the attributes of the Holy One" from rage to mercy.
   - The same passage goes on to say that "Abraham and Sarah were originally *tumtumin*" (R. Ami, 64a) and that "Sarah was an *aylonit*" with "not even a place [a womb] for a child" (Rav Naḥman for Rabba bar Avuh, 64b).
   - Rashi on 64b glosses Isaiah 51:1 ("the rock whence you were hewn ... the hole of the pit whence you were dug") as "a male organ was made for him" and "a female organ was made for her" (נעשה לה נקבות).
   - **Use:** in the faith's own voice only. "Our mother Sarah was shaped by God into a woman, and prayer turns." It is aggadah about a congenital condition healed for fertility. It is not a male-to-female crossing. The narrator rule from finding 2 of the comparanda list still holds.
2. **Gen. Rab. 60:16, Sarah's lamp, checked.** "All the days that Sarah was alive, there was a lamp kindled from Shabbat night until Shabbat night. When Sarah died, that lamp ceased. When Rebecca came, it returned." The same passage pairs the lamp with the cloud over her tent, open doors and blessed dough, and with *ḥallah* separated "in purity". Sefaria Midrash Rabbah 2022, CC BY.
3. **Gilgul into a woman is 13th-century, not only 16th-century.** Sachs-Shmueli (2023, abstract) credits Joseph of Hamadan, in 13th-century Castile, with "an unprecedented articulation" of gilgul in which "Jewish men could be reborn as gentiles, women, or even animals". The EJ "Gilgul" article (already listed) puts Hamadan in the 14th century and says that "transmigration into the bodies of women and of gentiles was held possible by several kabbalists". So the README's "Lurianic, 16th c." needs a correction.
   - Earliest firm attestation: Castile, late 13th or 14th century.
   - The framing is punitive: rebirth as a woman is a demotion.
   - Still nothing comparable found for Provence or the Bahir circle.
   - **Use:** reaction only, a hostile kabbalist's reading ("a man's soul sent down into a woman for his sins"). It must not become the faith's own doctrine.
4. **Massekhet Purim was written in Rome, 1319–22. Purim masquerade is otherwise dated to the 16th century, so Kalonymus's description of c. 1322 is early.**
   - Davidson (1907, p. 133), following Zunz, puts Massekhet Purim in Rome "sometime between 1319 and 1322". It was first printed at Pesaro, then at Venice in 1552.
   - Davidson (p. xiv) dates "the custom of masquerading on Purim" among European Jews only "as early as the sixteenth century". The *Even Boḥan* passage (Lemberg p. 29; `jud_kalonymus_even_bohan.md`) is therefore about two centuries earlier than the standard dating. This supports keeping the youths-in-women's-dress event, but flag it as an early, single witness.
5. **Kalonymus's career, from the Jewish Encyclopedia.** These points fill in Chotzner's account:
   - born at Arles 1286;
   - at Avignon from about 1314, "associated with Robert of Anjou, who sent him ... on a scientific mission to Rome";
   - in Catalonia in 1322;
   - at Arles again in 1328.
   - *Even Boḥan* laments the persecutions of 1319–22: the Shepherds, the lepers, and the burning of the Talmud at Toulouse.
   - The article says he "studied ... at Salonica". That is very likely a slip for Salon(-de-Provence) (*unverified*). Do not use Salonica.
6. **Deut. 22:5 in the medieval commentators is about sexual access, not identity.** Rashi gives two readings: dressing as a man "to consort with men", and dressing as a woman "to go and stay unnoticed amongst women". Sefer ha-Ḥinukh 542–543 gives a "distance from promiscuity" rationale and an idolatry rationale, both taken from Maimonides. Roos (2017) shows that Sefer Ḥasidim (13th c., Ashkenaz) **permits** a woman travelling alone to disguise herself as a man to avoid rape. That is the only explicit permission she found before it. Aquinas allows the same "in necessity" (*ST* 2-2.169).
   - **Use:** none of this is a gate. Keep the narrator rule from `jud_fonrobert_sexed_body.md`: a worthy may cite the verse but should not voice the "slip among women" rationale. Rashi's wording is exactly that rationale.
7. **The "Jewish male menses" myth is in the game's period. Scholars disagree on whether it feminised Jews.**
   - Jacques de Vitry (*Historia Orientalis*, begun 1219) says Jews have "become unwarlike and weak as women, and it is said that they have a flux of blood every month" (quoted in Roguin Maor et al. 2021, p. 2).
   - Johnson (1998, abstract) argues the gendered reading rests on "numerous misreadings". He says a belief that Jewish men menstruate "did appear around 1500", and that earlier references to a "bloody flux" "were not gendered".
   - Resnick (2000) argues for medieval roots.
   - **Use:** hard limit. No text in any faith may give Jews, or Jewish daughters of the Turning, a bloody flux, "weak as women", or a melancholic complexion. This extends DeVun row 4 in `cmp_devun_shape_of_sex.md`. The Nehpakhot make this trope especially dangerous: a Christian line about "Jewish men who become women" would land on it directly.

### 1. Kalonymus: editions, career, *Massekhet Purim*

1. **Qalonymos ben Qalonymos.** *Even Boḥan*. Ed. A. M. Habermann. Tel Aviv: Maḥbarot le-Sifrut (with Mossad ha-Rav Kook), 5716 [1956]. Vocalised by Y. Toporovsky. 192 pp.
   - Use: gap 1. The critical edition, "from manuscript and first prints, with notes and afterword" (title page). Rosen quotes it, and puts the lament at pp. 17–21 and the "not cut of one cloth" phrase at p. 177. Lets the loc be checked against a critical text instead of the Lemberg 1865 print. 2. **Davidson, Israel.** 1907. *Parody in Jewish Literature*. Columbia University Oriental Studies 2. New York: Columbia University Press. xxii + 292 pp.
   - Use: gap 1. The standard account of *Massekhet Purim*:

### 3. Cross-dressing law (Deut. 22:5)

7. **Roos, Lena.** 2017. "Cross-dressing among Medieval Ashkenazi Jews." *Nordisk Judaistik / Scandinavian Jewish Studies* 28.2: 4–22. DOI 10.30752/nj.67749. CC BY-NC-ND 4.0.
   - Use: gap 3, with the Christian parallels.
     - Sefer Ḥasidim permits women travellers to disguise themselves as men, or as Christians or even nuns, "to avoid being sexually assaulted". Roos found no earlier Jewish permission (p. 9).
     - The Talmud's two readings of *keli gever* (garment or weapon), and Rashi and Rashbam on illicit mixing (pp. 8–9).
     - The Vulgate's flattening of the verse, and canon-law repetition: Burchard, Gratian (p. 9, n. 8).
     - Christian tolerance of women dressed as men versus suspicion of men dressed as women, except for "comical functions during festivals or carnivals" (p. 11, after Bullough 2000). This is the Christian twin of the Purim licence.
     - Aquinas's necessity clause (p. 11).
     - Her conclusion: the permissions "strengthen" group boundaries; they do not transcend them.
   - FtM content. Use it only as reaction data and for the law. The "festival licence" parallel is the part that matters for the mod.
   - P1 · OA at https://journal.fi/nj/article/view/67749 (bot-gated; fetched via a Wayback copy) · **Downloaded** as `roos_2017_cross_dressing_ashkenazi.pdf`.
   - Leads from Roos, not read: Vedeler 2008 (JBL 127.3: 459–476, DOI 10.2307/25610133), on the ancient Near Eastern meaning of the verse; Hotchkiss 1996, *Clothes Make the Man* (Garland), on female cross-dressing, reaction only; Bullough 2000 in Bullough and Brundage (eds.), *Handbook of Medieval Sexuality*, *unverified* pages.
8. **Walfish, Barry Dov.** 1993. *Esther in Medieval Garb: Jewish Interpretation of the Book of Esther in the Middle Ages*. Albany: SUNY Press. ISBN 0-7914-1039-0.
   - Use: gaps 3 and 6. How medieval Rabbanite and Karaite exegetes read Esther. It is the base for "it was turned around" (9:1), for Esther's concealed identity, and for what each community thought Purim licensed. It is the nearest scholarly frame for a Purim-centred sect.
   - P2 · Library · Not downloaded.

### 5. Christian polemic about Jewish bodies (reaction data the mod must avoid)

11. **Johnson, Willis.** 1998. "The Myth of Jewish Male Menses." *Journal of Medieval History* 24.3: 273–295. DOI 10.1016/S0304-4181(98)00009-8.
    - Use: gap 5 (finding 7). Holds that a belief in Jewish male menstruation appears only around 1500, and that earlier "bloody flux" texts were not gendered. It is the check on any claim that medieval Christians "feminised" Jewish men. That matters because the Nehpakhot invite exactly that frame.
    - P1 · Library · Not downloaded.
12. **Resnick, Irven M.** 2000. "Medieval Roots of the Myth of Jewish Male Menses." *Harvard Theological Review* 93.3: 241–263. DOI 10.1017/S0017816000025323.
    - Use: gap 5. The case for 13th-century roots: Jacques de Vitry, Caesarius of Heisterbach, Thomas of Cantimpré, and the humoral and hemorrhoidal explanations. Read it with Johnson, and use the pair to set the hard limit.
    - P1 · Library · Not downloaded.
    - Companion: **Resnick, Irven M.** 2012. *Marks of Distinction: Christian Perceptions of Jews in the High Middle Ages*. Washington, DC: Catholic University of America Press. DOI 10.2307/j.ctt2851pt. The book-length catalogue of bodily marks (smell, complexion, flux, the badge). P2 · Library · Not downloaded.

### 6. Karaites

13. **Rustow, Marina.** 2008. *Heresy and the Politics of Community: The Jews of the Fatimid Caliphate*. Ithaca: Cornell University Press. DOI 10.7591/9780801455308 (2014 e-book).
    - Use: gap 6. Karaites and Rabbanites in Egypt and Syria shared courts, marriages and patrons. A Karaite seeker joining a Rabbanite-born sect is plausible in Fustat but not in Provence, where there were no Karaites. Read it before writing any Karaite reaction.
    - P2 · Library · Not downloaded.
14. **Ankori, Zvi.** 1959. *Karaites in Byzantium: The Formative Years, 970–1100*. New York: Columbia University Press. DOI 10.7312/anko91724.
    - Use: gap 6. The Byzantine Karaite community: its calendar disputes with Rabbanites, its law codes and its location (Constantinople, Thessaly). It gives the frame for a Karaite reaction in the Byzantine theatre, where the mod already has eunuch content.
    - P2 · Preview (archive.org lending: `karaitesinbyzant0000anro`). The other archive.org copies are user uploads, not used. Not downloaded.
    - What is known so far without these books: Karaites kept Purim as a festival on **both 14 and 15 Adar**, in Adar I of a leap year (a month before the Rabbanites), without the Fast of Esther. Aaron ben Elijah (*Gan Eden*, 1354) lists feasting, joy, gifts to friends and gifts to the poor (Lichaa 2015; popular source, primary citations *unverified*). Karaites reject the Mishnah's categories as binding. Nothing was found on Karaite law for *tumtum*, *androginos* or castration (Open).
