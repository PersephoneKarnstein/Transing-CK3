# Guilland, "Les eunuques dans l'Empire byzantin" (1943): design reading

**Citation (checked against the PDF).** Rodolphe Guilland, "Les eunuques dans l'Empire byzantin. Étude de titulature et de prosopographie byzantines", *Études byzantines* 1 (1943): 197–238. DOI 10.3406/rebyz.1943.907 (Persée PDF, generated 2018). Reprinted in Guilland, *Recherches sur les institutions byzantines* I (Berliner byzantinische Arbeiten 35), Berlin–Amsterdam 1967, pp. 165–197. **What was read:** the whole article, plus the three indexes (names, dignities, places). Quotes are my English translations, with the French for key terms in brackets. Page numbers are the journal's.

**Read with care.** This is 1943 scholarship. Its tone is often contemptuous ("adroit and insinuating people", p. 200), and it explains imperial favour by "the influence of the Orient". Use it for facts and citations: offices, laws, names. Do not use it for interpretation.

**Relevance:** high for laws, the trade, office-holders and era decay. Moderate for church and monastic settings.

## 1. The argument in brief

### Numbers and the trade
- In the palace eunuchs "swarmed like flies in a stable in summer" (Constantine VII, p. 197). Fulcher of Chartres put them at 20,000 c. 1100 (p. 232).
- **They were the gift an emperor valued most.** The widow Danielis gave Basil I (867–86) 300 young slaves, a hundred of them eunuchs, "for she knew there is always room for eunuchs in the imperial palace" (p. 197). Tedbald of Spoleto castrated captured Greek soldiers and sent them as a gift: "knowing that to your holy emperor nothing is more precious than eunuchs" (p. 197, Liudprand).
- **Prices** (Justinian, Cod. Just. 7.7.1.5): 30 solidi for a eunuch under ten, 50 if older, 60 if trained in a craft [artiste] (p. 198).
- **Law against making eunuchs on Roman soil, not against buying them.** Domitian and Nerva banned castration. Hadrian applied the *lex Cornelia de sicariis* (the murder law) to surgeons and to anyone who allowed the operation (p. 198). Leo I (457–74) "forbade the sale of eunuchs of Roman nationality" but "had to authorise the trade in eunuchs of barbarian nationality", so foreign eunuchs flooded in (p. 199). Most palace eunuchs were Abasgian. Justinian sent the Abasgian palace eunuch Euphratas home, and he persuaded the princes to stop the practice (p. 199).
- **Justinian, Novel 142.** Castration persisted. Of 90 operated, "3 at most survived". Operators and accomplices suffered retaliation in kind [la peine du talion], then the mines and confiscation; women who ordered it were exiled. Every operated slave became free, "even if the operation had been ordered as treatment for an illness". Free men operated on for illness were exempt. "This Novel had the effect of freeing all the eunuchs of the empire" (p. 199).

### Leo VI's legislation (the key passage, pp. 199–201)
- **Novel 60 is a softening, not a ban.** It "marks a regression in the repression of eunuch-making". Leo abolished retaliation in kind as "too draconian". The person who ordered a castration was fined 10 pounds of gold and exiled for 10 years, and a palace official lost his post. The operator was flogged, shaved, had his goods confiscated and was exiled for 10 years. The operated slave was freed. **"But Leo VI tolerated the voluntary operation on a free man, on a physician's decision"** [sur décision d'un médecin]. **"The law did not fix the age from which voluntary consent could be given, and parents could always claim they had consented to the operation on their children"** (p. 200).
- **Novel 26 (adoption).** Castrates [ektomiai] could not marry, adopt or name a posthumous heir. Leo let them adopt, on the ground that "it is not nature but the malice of men that deprived [them] of the joys of fatherhood", so the law owes them "compensations", just as the mute are allowed gestures and writing (p. 201). A case under Nikephoros III: the eunuch protovestiarios John promised in battle to adopt George Palaiologos and make him his heir, then broke his word (pp. 201, 213).
- **Two legal kinds.** *Ektomiai* (castrati): fathering children is certainly impossible. *Spadones / thladiai* (congenital or illness): it is only improbable. Hence *spadones* could marry and *ektomiai* could not (p. 201).

### Why emperors wanted them; the ceremony
- Families saw that "the quality of eunuch led to fortune, power and honours", so "parents, even of the highest classes, consented to the mutilation of one of their children" (p. 200).
- **The angel staging (Guilland's own reading, pp. 200–201).** "Like the angels, they surrounded the basileus, God's representative here below. Like the seraphim, the eunuchs approached the basileus veiling their faces with their white sleeves." They held visitors by the shoulders "as if the angels supported them with their wings". They took the armour off a returning emperor in a chapel of the Virgin, turning "warrior prince into Christian prince". They escorted him "like a living mantle of angels" to the sacred bath at Blachernai, "spring of the Mother of God, where he plunged as if to be reborn". "At Byzantium the eunuchs represented on earth a heavenly power" [une puissance céleste]. This passage gives no source for the angel comparisons. The ceremonial acts come from *De cerimoniis*; the angel reading is Guilland's.

### The order and its offices
- "An order of eunuchs" [ή τῶν εὐνούχων τάξις] (De Cer. II 52 = Philotheos 899), "as numerous as it was powerful and hierarchically organised. Special titles of nobility were created for them, and certain offices were specially reserved for them". Eunuchs were nonetheless fit for "all public functions, with rare exceptions" (p. 198).
- **Precedence: eunuchs ranked above the bearded.** "At equal title, eunuch dignitaries had precedence over bearded dignitaries". A plain eunuch patrikios outranked a bearded *anthypatos* patrikios "even when the latter held the highest military and civil offices" (p. 198).
- **Offices reserved for eunuchs or usually held by them (from the text and the dignity index):** *praipositos* (head of the Sacred Chamber; "had the absolute trust of the basileus and often ran the state in his master's name", p. 215); *parakoimomenos* ("always reserved for eunuchs", given exceptionally to the future Basil I, p. 220); *protovestiarios* (in the 10th c. this and the parakoimomenos were "reserved for eunuchs", p. 208); *ho epi tes trapezes* (master of the table, p. 208); *papias* (palace keeper); *koitonites* (bedchamber); *sakellarios*; *primikerios*; *cubicularius* / *spatharokoubikoularios*; "first eunuch" [premier eunuque] and "great eunuch" (pp. 215, 219). Eunuch titles: *patrikios* (the "eunuch patrikios" with its own precedence), *proedros* (a new dignity created for Basil the Parakoimomenos, above *magistros*, p. 224), *nobilissimos* (first given to a eunuch under Michael V, p. 228). The mixed and governing posts they reached: *paradynasteuon* (chief minister), *logothetes tou dromou*, *orphanotrophos*, *droungarios* of the Watch and of the fleet, *domestikos ton scholon*, *megas hetaireiarches*, duke of Antioch and of Iberia, *strategos*.
- **The political logic.** Emperors "felt secure beside eunuchs, who, even at the summit of honours and power, could not dream of the purple". A eunuch who had stopped pleasing "could easily be broken without causing trouble" (pp. 214–215). The same logic put eunuchs at the head of armies: "a victorious eunuch was not to be feared, whereas a victorious general could always become a dangerous pretender" (p. 205). Basil the Parakoimomenos "took care to place only eunuchs at the head of the imperial armies" (p. 209).

### Church and monasteries
- **Nicaea canon 1 and Apostolic Canons 21–22.** The man made a eunuch "by surgeons through illness or by barbarians" stays in the clergy. The man who "being healthy, mutilated himself" is barred. The Apostolic Canons also admit those castrated "by the violence of men" or in persecution (p. 202).
- **Eunuch patriarchs.** Germanos I was castrated as an adult for his father's part in a regicide. Niketas I was "of Slavic and servile origin". Methodios was a *spado*: accused of seducing a woman, he undressed before the judges and said his state was a miracle "that preserved him from the desires of the flesh". Ignatios (847–58, 867–77), son of Michael I, was castrated and made a monk when his father fell. Stephen II. Theophylaktos, Romanos I's youngest son, was castrated young and made patriarch at 16. Polyeuktos was "made a eunuch by his own parents". Then probably Constantine III Leichoudes, and Eustratios Garidas (pp. 202–203). Pope Leo IX and Liudprand protested the number of eunuch bishops (p. 203).
- **Monastic settings.** Disgraced eunuchs were tonsured as punishment (Antiochos, Samonas). Narses (under Justin II) founded the Katharoi monastery "reserved for eunuchs", plus hospices (p. 204). Leo VI's St Lazaros was "reserved for eunuchs" (p. 204). Stoudios was open to eunuchs. **"In convents of women, the posts of steward and vice-steward [économe et vice-économe] were always held by eunuchs (Irene Komnene, *Typikon*, XIV)"** (p. 205). **Alexios I banned eunuchs from the Athos monasteries in 1083** (p. 232).

### Family
- Michael IV's three brothers were eunuchs; "the imperial family was a family of eunuchs" (p. 228). Michael V "had made eunuchs of almost all his kin, even those who were already fathers of families" (p. 229, after Psellos). The bearded general Leo Phokas "did not think it beneath him" to marry the sister of the eunuch Constantine the Parakoimomenos (p. 222). Basil the Parakoimomenos was Romanos I's son by a Slav slave, "a eunuch from his tender childhood" (p. 224).

### Decline (era decay)
- **1057 is the first break.** From Isaac I Komnenos (1057–59), emperors "do not seem to have undergone the domination of eunuchs ... they no longer officially exercised power; they contented themselves with intriguing" (p. 230). Eunuchs revived briefly under Michael VII (Nikephoritzes, John of Side). Under the Komnenoi "the eunuchs make little noise", still numerous but not in front-rank posts (p. 232).
- **12th–14th c.: fewer eunuch generals.** Secure dynasties had less reason to fear generals. By the 14th century historians seem to avoid mentioning that someone was a eunuch (p. 214).
- **The Palaiologan turn.** Elite society adopted "Western prejudices": the eunuch was now "regarded as an inferior being, and his physical inferiority became a blemish [tare] rather than an advantage" (p. 234).
- **Popular hostility throughout.** The proverb "If you have a eunuch, kill him; if you have none, buy one to kill" (Cedrenus). Crowds jeered "klou, klou" at eunuchs. Manasses wrote of "eunuchs with effeminate souls" (pp. 234–235).

## 4. Flags

- **Contradicts current design:** the "Leo VI loophole withdrawn after 1066" premise (faith_design.md / README "decaying after 1066"). No repeal is attested here; the decline dates to 1057–1083 and is social and political.
- **Nuances an existing note:** the Kecharitomene typikon (see row 8).
- **Primary-source cases usable as events or characters:** Danielis's gift of 100 eunuchs to Basil I in 867–86, which falls inside the start window; Methodios's naked defence before the judges; Polyeuktos, castrated by his own parents; Michael V castrating fathers of families; John the protovestiarios's broken adoption vow (1078–81); Basil the Parakoimomenos (illegitimate Lekapenid, chief minister under four emperors, exiled by Basil II, pp. 224–226); the 1083 Athos ban.
