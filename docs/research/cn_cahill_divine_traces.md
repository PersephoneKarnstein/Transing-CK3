## 1. The argument and the text

### 1a. Cahill's introduction

**Aims.**
- She wants "to put women back into the historical picture". She reads hagiography as evidence: it claims truth as *zhen* 真, "genuine ... embodiments of the doctrines", rather than as fact (p. 2).
- She refuses "victim feminism" and "the orientalist image of unchanging China" (p. vii). She also keeps the "darker side" in view: women "can be agents of their own self-destruction" as well as of liberation (p. vii).

**Context: Du's career.**
- Du was born in Chuzhou (Zhejiang), failed the classics exam, and trained on Mount Tiantai. He rose to become the leading Shangqing master of his generation.
- He followed Xizong to Chengdu in **881**, returned in 885, and then served Wang Jian's Former Shu.
- Wang Jian titled him "Prior-Born of Broad Completion" in **913**. Du signs the preface with that title, so the book was finished shortly after 913 (pp. 12, 30).
- He ended his life on **Mount Qingcheng** and "probably lived there while compiling his text" (pp. 5, 13). This confirms the reading list's guess.
- The last date in the text is 886 (p. 12). He presented the book to the Former Shu throne (pp. 27, 30).
- His hidden agendas (p. 13):
  - Daoism over Buddhism, which he treats as the real rival;
  - Shangqing at the head of a unified church;
  - bringing local cults into the canon;
  - Former Shu as the Tang's legitimate heir;
  - allying the church with "Confucian values such as loyalty and filial piety".

**Tang gender norms (pp. 8–9).**
- The norms were *neiwai* 內外 (inside and outside), the "three follows" *sancong* 三從, and *nannü zhi bie* 男女之別 (the distinction between men and women).
- Tang women could inherit and widows could remarry. Even so, the period was "not exactly a golden age for the liberation of Chinese women" (p. 9).

**Women and Daoism (pp. 10–11).**
- The church gave women education, leadership, and "a social and economic safety net for women in need".
- Through the end of the Tang, "Daoist women and men engaged in roughly the same practices and rituals". Separate women's regimes come after the Tang.
- Du's decision to "sequester" women's lives in a book of their own "prefigures the segregation" that followed.

**The text (pp. 14–16).**
- Song bibliographies list 10 scrolls and 109 lives; 79 survive in all.
- Cahill reads the YJQQ recension as a **lineage register**. The *lu* 錄 of the title is also the word for a Daoist register.
- The book runs from the Queen Mother down to Du's own day. Du "gathered this flock of explanations and collected them as the records of a single household" (*jia* 家, p. 16).
- *Wangmu* 王母 is glossed "ancestress" in Han dictionaries. "Du provides his subjects, even the unmarried and childless ones, with an ancestral lineage to care for them in the next world" (p. 16).

**The life cycle of a female saint (pp. 17–20).**
1. Family and place.
2. Childhood vocation.
3. **"the marriage crisis"** at puberty. Celibacy means "refusal to reproduce; asceticism denies offspring to her husband's ancestral line". Some saints mutilate themselves or kill themselves to avoid marriage, which we will not use (p. 18).
4. Choosing a way of life: hermit, wanderer, or convent. Women entered convents to keep a vow, to escape poverty, to avoid "marriage and childbirth", to retire from the harem, or for "freedom and literacy" (p. 18).
5. Mature practice: hidden good works (*yinde* 陰德 / *yin'gong* 陰功), restoring shrines, opposing Buddhists, weaving, ritual, and asceticism leading to meditation and "inner alchemy" (p. 19).
6. Fruits of practice: flight, youthfulness, long life.
7. Departure, in one of two forms:
   - ascension in broad daylight before witnesses, the rarest and most prestigious;
   - *shijie* 尸解, deliverance by means of the corpse: an "empty husk, light as a beetle's carapace" is left behind. The body does not decay and smells fragrant (pp. 19–20).
8. A post in the celestial bureaucracy.

**Saints, compared with the Catholic church (p. 7).**
- Daoist saints are verified by the body: "when they do not die and decompose".
- They are legitimised "by their ties to ... lineage and bureaucracy".

**Law, the most useful find (p. 18 n. 18).** Tang Code, General Principles art. 57:
- "The relationship of Daoist and Buddhist priests and nuns to their masters is the same as for paternal uncles and aunts."
- A master's disciples stand to her "as nephews and nieces in the male line".
- "When a woman enters a convent, the convent then becomes her family, according to Tang law as well as to her own self-concept."

**Terms (pp. 21–23).** Cahill renders *guan* 觀 as "belvedere" (temple or convent). She also defines *sanshi* 三尸 (the three corpses), *shijie*, *xian* 仙, *yinde*, *zhenren*, and *zhuan* 傳 (transmission).
- *Nüguan* 女官 means "female official", often written with its homophone 女冠, "female cap" (p. 107 n. 2).
- Du calls Hua Gu *nü daoshi* 女道士, "female Daoist master" (p. 123 n. 1).

**Cahill on Du's gender politics (pp. 28–30).**
- Du says marriage, "the Way of one yin and one yang", is a vocation. Its patrons are the Queen Mother and the Lord King of the East. "And yet Du's subjects repeatedly refuse marriage" (p. 29).
- "In Du's scheme of things, the standard adept was male. He valued the female practitioner but probably regarded her as a somewhat deficient male" (p. 30).
- He proves Daoism's power "by demonstrating that even women can attain it" (p. 30).

**Conclusions (pp. 195–198).**
- Daoism did not make men and women equal, but it "provided opportunities for some women that were quite extraordinary".
- "they made their worlds, but they did not make them just as they pleased."
- The body is "both instrument and obstacle".

### 1b. Du Guangting's preface (YJQQ 114.1a–4b; pp. 34–40)

**Many paths.**
- His opening describes the saints as having "transformed shapes made of bubbles and foam into ones as solid as metal and rock" (p. 34).
- Against the popular view that a transcendent must leave the body behind, or that "bones and flesh must ascend and soar": that belief is "an extreme case of ignorant blindness". Some saints are hidden and some are seen. "the Ways of the transcendents number in the hundreds" (p. 36).
- He lists the paths:
  - friendship with deities;
  - receiving texts and registers ("their numina and flesh having been born repeatedly");
  - firm resolve through tests;
  - elixirs, including "the mottled talisman of sperm in the womb". Cahill notes Du knew both outer and inner alchemy, "while at times claiming that inner alchemy is superior" (p. 37 n. 18);
  - astral elixirs.
- "My fate rests with me" (cf. 我命在我) (p. 38).

**Ranks of departure.** Flying up in body and spirit is best. Next comes transformation hidden in a valley. Next comes shedding the form "as snakes shed their skins or cicadas their shells". "deliverance by means of the corpse is the inferior method" (p. 38).

**Ghost transcendents (p. 39).**
- Some adepts are sincere but die short of the goal. They "refine their practice alone within their own bodies".
- They become "good and happy ghosts" whom no ghost register inscribes, and "after a sufficient number of years" they are "ghost transcendents" who ascend.
- "doing good deeds is never wasted effort." "Transcendence may be learned" (p. 39).

**Marriage.** "The Way of one yin and one yang ... giving birth to the flock of forms." "the Wood Lord presides over the east, while the Metal Mother is revered in the west" (pp. 39–40).

**Gender and the register (p. 40).**
- "The positions governed by male realized ones and female transcendents are very distinct". The texts, however, mix them together, so Du separates them out.
- "Women transcendents take Metal Mother as most honored, and Metal Mother takes the Fortified Walled City as her base for governing" (女仙以金母為尊，金母以墉城為治, YJQQ, check).
- "the ultimate position for a man who obtains the Way is Realized Lord, and the ultimate position for a woman who obtains the Way is Primal Ruler" (真君 / 元君, YJQQ, check).
- "This transmission takes Metal Mother as head."

### 1c. The Queen Mother of the West (#1, pp. 43–69)

**Names.** She is the "Metal Mother of the Nine Numina, the Grand Marvel, and Tortoise Mountain", "The Primordial Ruler, Metal Mother" (p. 51).

**Origin.**
- The Way produced the Wood Lord first, then the Metal Mother.
- She "was born at the Yi River [near Luoyang]". "Her surname is Gou. As soon as she was born, she soared up in flight" (p. 51).
- She is made of the "unmixed seminal breaths of the great Way ... bound together to make her shape" (p. 51).

**Office.**
- "all women who ascend to transcendence and attain the Way are her dependents" (p. 52).
- She presides at celestial audiences. Whatever is "bestowed at ordination ... [is] either obstructed or given by her" (p. 53). She "decides who can receive ordination" (p. 46).
- Every ascending transcendent must "salute the Wood Lord, and afterwards pay a ceremonial visit to the Metal Mother" (p. 58).
- Du's closing words: she "holds central control of registration in both the hidden and revealed" (p. 69).

**How Du cleans her up.** Du turns the tiger-toothed, wild-haired Queen Mother of the *Shanhai jing* into her envoy, the white tiger of the west (p. 53). Cahill: she "was the patron of women in Daoism, especially women outside the family such as prostitutes and nuns" (p. 43).

**Her teaching to Han Wudi.**
- She teaches that the body is the field: "If you want prolonged life, you must first obtain it within your body" (p. 61).
- "Once you lose your present body, for a myriad kalpas it will not return. Don't you think you should treasure it?" (p. 64).
- Her drug lists run from cinnabar elixirs down to pine resin (pp. 62–63).

**Her lineage.**
- She gives Lady **Wei Huacun** to Mao Ying as a divine wife (p. 50).
- Transcendent Maiden Gou's blue bird tells her "The Queen Mother of the West has the surname Gou. She is in fact the Maiden's incomparable ancestor" (p. 133). The bird also names Mount Goushi (Yanshi, Henan) as the place "where the Queen Mother cultivated the Way" (p. 134).

**Crossing gender.** Wang Zideng 王子登, the Queen Mother's musician and messenger, "can appear as female or male, depending on the needs of the situation" (p. 56 n. 17). This is a note by Cahill, not something Du says.

### 1d. Hagiographies read in full, most relevant first

**#27 Xue Xuantong 薛玄同 (c. 828–882), alive at the 867 start (pp. 186–193).**
- She was wife of Feng Hui, Lesser Governor of Hezhong.
- "Twenty years after being sent in marriage ... she told him that she wanted to live chastely and used illness as her pretext for dwelling alone" (p. 189). She chanted the *Huangting jing* daily.
- **874.** Lady Wei Huacun came down with 27 attendants, taught her visualisation, and gave her one grain of elixir: "Swallow it after eight years" (p. 190).
- Her husband "harbored ignorant and jealous feelings. He regularly slandered and ridiculed her" (p. 190).
- **881**, amid the Huang Chao war, a divine escort met her boat. "Not yet! I will only depart next spring from my private residence. Don't rush me!" (p. 191).
- **882.**
  - She washed her own hair and face, the rite that female kin perform on a corpse. Then she swallowed the elixir and died overnight.
  - 36 cranes came. "a spot of white radiance" in her forehead turned to purple vapour, and her hair grew "several feet". Her coffin flew open: "Her corpse had disappeared, leaving behind just her empty burial garments and quilt" (pp. 191–192).
- **883.** Governor Zhou Bao memorialised her as an omen of the dynasty. The rescript from Chengdu orders "history officials" to record her and officials "to provide money for ... repairing Daoist altars ... where she dwelt" (p. 193).
- Cahill names the emperor as **Yizong** (p. 188). In 883 it was **Xizong**, as her own p. 12 shows. This is an error in Cahill.

**#26 Wang Fengxian 王奉仙 (c. 838–886), alive at the 867 start (pp. 176–185).**
- Born a peasant weavers' daughter of Dangtu county, Xuan prefecture.
- At 13 or 14, "ten or more young girls" came to play with her and came back at night to feast. Her parents suspected "bewitching goblins". She learned to fly from the bamboo tops (pp. 180–181).
- Divine girls cut her hair to the neck, and it never grew back. Cahill calls this "mini-tonsure" a sign of vocation (pp. 177, 181).
- People called her **Guanyin**. To a suspicious official: "What I have encountered is the Way. What I have gained is transcendence. Babbling common folk just flatter me with the name of Guanyin" (p. 182).
- **The palace levy.** Common-lord Du Shenquan (governor, 860s) meant "to offer her as tribute for the consorts' apartments". "She tried to avoid this, using the excuse of having short hair". When refused leave to go home, she hid in a Buddhist temple for years (p. 182). Du's comment: "there is no obstacle between names and the Way". Then a Zhuangzi line not found in any current *Zhuangzi*: "If people consider me an ox, then I will act like an ox; if they consider me a horse, then I'll act like a horse" (pp. 182–183).
- From 860 to 888 she preached loyalty, filial piety and self-cultivation through Huai and Zhe. Warlords who came "to coerce [her] with white swords" knelt as disciples instead (pp. 184–185). Qin Yan, a Huang Chao leader, took her as his master (p. 182).
- At Mount Dongting (Jiangsu) she "formally entered the Way in the company of two women disciples".
- In 885 she moved to Mount Qianqing (Yuhang). She was "transformed without ever getting sick" in 886, at 48 (p. 185).
- Du uses her to state the three teachings: the Heavenly Honored Ones are the father, Confucius the elder brother, "Shakyamuni ... like a mother"; "A baby in this world recognizes only its mother" (pp. 183–184).

**#22 Bian Dongxuan 邊洞玄 (628–711) (pp. 149–160).**
- Born in Fanyang (Youzhou).
- At 15 she asked to enter the Way, and her parents refused. "When she reached marriageable age, [Bian] vowed not to marry, but to serve and nourish her parents" until they died. Only then, with no close kin left, did she join a convent (p. 155).
- The convent lived by weaving silk: "The whole household of nuns in this belvedere wove silk as a service" (p. 156). Nuns kept part of their earnings (p. 152). She spent hers on grain for birds and rats.
- An old medicine seller counted her forty years of feeding animals as the price of the elixir. "If you ingest this cinnabar, it will change your intestines and transform your blood" (p. 158).
- She ascended in broad daylight at the Central Prime festival (7/15) of 711. To Xuanzong she said: "Your handmaiden is the Woman Daoist Master Bian Dongxuan from Youzhou" (p. 160).
- The emperor named the convent the Belvedere of Climbing to Transcendence. The same year Princess Yuzhen entered the Way "and donated her enfeoffed land" (p. 160).
- Cahill reads her fasting and elixir-taking alongside modern anorexia and self-harm (p. 149). Those links are hers, and not something to model.

**#17 Hua Gu 花姑 = Huang Lingwei (640–721) (pp. 119–126).** Du's account, checked against Kirkland:
- **693:** the theurgist Hu Chao pointed her to a headless stone tortoise, beside which lay the altar. She dug up images and lamps and restored it (p. 124).
- **711:** Ruizong sent Ye Fashan, who "ordained seven female Daoist Masters as presiding servers" (p. 124).
- **721:** "do not nail my coffin shut. Just cover the coffin with crimson silk gauze". After thunder came "a hole about as big as a hen's egg" in the gauze, "and in the coffin were only her shroud and some wooden slips" (p. 125).
- **740:** a white deer appeared during the *jiao*.
- **750:** two women were ordained to tend the incense.
- **768:** Yan Zhenqing installed "twenty-seven men" in the monastery and "seven female Daoist Masters" in the convent (p. 126).
- **Differences from Kirkland.**
  - Du's account **omits** Empress Wu's confiscation; Cahill puts it in Yan's version (p. 124 n. 3).
  - Cahill writes **Linquan** 臨泉 throughout. Kirkland's Linchuan 臨川 in Fuzhou is right, and Cahill herself cites Yan's stele as *Fuzhou ... Linquanxian* (p. 123 n. 1).
  - Du names no disciple Li Qiongxian.

**#15 Wang Fajin 王法進 (d. 752) (pp. 103–111).**
- From Jian province, Sichuan. "At just over ten years old", her parents gave her to a passing nüguan, who ordained her with a Rectified Unity register and gave her a religious name (p. 107).
- **The male role.** In a famine, the Supreme Thearch summoned her: "acting as one of my unsurpassable attendant lads". Cahill: "She is commanded to function as a male rather than a female deity" (p. 109 and n. 8). She brought back a Lingbao fast of repentance, held in spring and autumn.
- Cahill reads this as Du viewing "gender as a performance rather than as essential identity" (p. 103). Again: "She becomes the part she plays, at least while she performs it" (p. 105).
- She ascended in broad daylight in 752.

**#16 Ms. Wang 王氏 (8th c.) (pp. 112–118).**
- She married Xie Liangbi and was an invalid for ten years. Wu Yun cured her with a talisman, and she took his registers.
- On her deathbed: "because of the sickness inherent in ordinary behaviors of everyday life, I have inclined towards cherishing jealousy and envy ... After twenty years [of practice] I will certainly be able [to transform] like a cicada" (p. 117).
- She asked not to be coffined but laid in the wilds. Twenty years later "her body was as light as an empty husk ... on the right side of her rib cage a scar more than a foot long had split open" (p. 118).
- Her practice went on after death. Cahill says this is the first such case she has met (p. 112).

**#18 Xu Xiangu 徐仙姑 (Sui–Tang, alive 859) (pp. 127–130).**
- She was daughter and heir of the wonder-worker Xu Zhicai, and wandered alone.
- To monks who drew knives: "I am a woman and yet I have been able to reject the household life and travel amidst clouds and rivers. I shun neither serpents and dragons nor tigers and wolves, so why should I be afraid of you rats?!" (p. 129).
- Cahill: Du "demonstrates an understanding that the patrilineal system could be good for Daoist men, but was full of conflicts for women" (p. 128).

**#19 Gou Xiangu 緱仙姑 (c. 790–870) (pp. 131–134).**
- She kept Lady Wei's altar beneath **Mount Heng (the Southern Marchmount)** alone for over ten years, among tigers.
- Lady Wei's blue bird served her and told her that the Queen Mother was her ancestor.
- Monks tried to topple the altar. It moved under one hand and stood "like a mountain peak" against many; tigers ate nine monks (p. 134).
- Around 870 the demoted minister Zheng Tian became her disciple. "After the present time, all within the four seas will encounter many difficulties". She then withdrew to the Jiuyi mountains (p. 134).

**#25 Shen Gu 神姑 = Lu Meiniang 盧眉娘 (792–c. 820) (pp. 170–175).**
- "In the primal year of the Eternal Probity reign period [805], the Grand Protector of the South Seas submitted her as tribute to the capital". She was fourteen (pp. 174–175).
- Her silk canopies held whole paradises. Around 813, Xianzong, since "she had been unwilling to dwell in the palace annex ... had her ordained as a female Daoist Master and released her to return home", naming her Xiaoyao 逍遙 (p. 175).
- Shijie at Mount Luofu: "nothing was there but her old shoes" (p. 175).
- One of her three poems in the *Quan Tangshi* speaks in a male voice. Cahill calls this "the literary equivalent of cross-dressing" (p. 171).

### 1e. Gender-crossing: the direct answer

No one in the collection is male-bodied and becomes a woman, and no one changes sex. The crossings run the other way, or are only role or voice:
- Wang Zideng appears as female or male (Cahill's note, p. 56).
- Wang Fajin serves heaven as an "attendant lad" (p. 109).
- Lu Meiniang writes in a male poet's voice (p. 171).
- Yangping Zhi and her women friends drink in the market like male eccentrics (p. 166).

Cahill's reading of this as "gender as a performance" (p. 103) is exactly the framing Serano warns against. The strongest usable line is Du's own, on women in grotto office (#9): they rise "because of excellence and talents, unrestricted by distinctions between male and female" (p. 97). The **body** changes through practice in many entries: it grows youthful, light and fragrant, and the old one is shed as a husk. On how the women transcend, Du's answer is: by **hidden good works, fasting, celibacy, visualisation and elixirs**, ending in ascension or shijie. There is no women's inner alchemy and no embryo. Cahill places separate women's practice after the Tang (pp. 10, 112).
