## 0. Framing warning

The jōgappas are a **living, stigmatised community** in North Karnataka and neighbouring states, studied here through fieldwork with named and unnamed individuals in 2010–2015. Nothing in this note may be used to present the jōgappas, Ellamma's devotees, or hijras as the faith, or as medieval. Merkle herself refuses to settle whether jōgappas are "transgenders, MSM or gay" (p. 379) and warns that stressing their empowerment "tends to romanticise" regional traditions (p. 371). Use the article for **structure and contrast**, never for borrowed rites or names.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **Evidence base.** "Due to a lack of sources I largely draw on my own field research" (p. 368 n. 3). There is no historical attestation in the article; the oldest cited works are Bradford 1983 and Kittel's 1894 dictionary.
- **Who.** Jōgappas are "male-born dedicated devotees" of Reṇukā-Ellamma, whose main temple is at Saundatti (pp. 367, 369). Once initiated they "adopt feminine characteristics and the social role of women" (p. 370). Most dedicated devotees at the temple are female-born (jōgatis, jōgammas, basavis, dēvadāsis); jōgappas are a minority within the field (p. 369 n. 5).
- **The rite.** Initiation is *muttu kaṭṭuvudu*, "tying the dēvi's chain", a bead thread with the goddess's symbols (pp. 369–370). It "can be equated with the maṅgaḷasūtra", and the rite "shares patterns of a regular marriage ceremony" (p. 385). A jōgappa is "a consort of the dēvi" and a *sadā muttayde*, "an always married woman" (p. 370).
- **Duties.** Door-to-door alms on Tuesdays and Fridays carrying the goddess's mobile shrine; possession and mediumship; auspicious presence at weddings and births; keeping shrines (p. 370).
- **The calling.** Illness, misfortune, matted hair (*jaḍe*), and sometimes dreams precede initiation; priests or senior jōgappas name the goddess as the cause, and the person and goddess "negotiate" how to live (pp. 370–371).
- **Chosen, not choosing.** Jōgappas hold that femininity comes from the goddess's presence: "Only if the goddess herself comes and resides in us, can we become Jogappas" (Aneka 2014a, quoted p. 384). Those who "simply choose" are considered invalid (p. 384). One jōgappa had no prior feminine identity, became one in penance for disrespecting the goddess, and "grew into the role of a woman" (p. 385).
- **Body.** Jōgappas reject "the operation" as the hijra marker; the goddess "accepts only an intact body as her medium" (p. 380). Younger jōgappas are drawn to hijra households for freedom and income (p. 381); one jōgappa wavered over surgery, wanting both to be "accepted as a woman" and to remain the goddess's medium (p. 382).
- **The goddess changes sex.** Ellamma is "well known for her unique and extraordinary ability to change males into females and females into males" (p. 384, citing Bradford 1983: 310).
- **Comparanda.** Telangana devotees take the jōgappa vow "only for a few years" (p. 384); Tirupati men take *strī vēṣam* (female guise) for a week of Gangamma's festival, when "the world ... becomes female" (pp. 384–385, Flueckiger 2013). The Karnataka case is "extraordinary because of the permanent and irrevocable consequences" (p. 385).
- **Festival cycle.** At *raṇḍe huṇṇime* devotees follow the goddess into widowhood and break their bangles; a month later, at *muttayde huṇṇime*, they regain married status (p. 375 and n. 12), after the myth of Jamadagni's death and revival.
- **The opposition is modern.** A 2007 reformers' leaflet calls the jōgappas' dress "mental weakness or ... physical defects" or pretence, and asserts "only two genders" (p. 373). Merkle traces this to colonial classifications and Sanskritising reform (pp. 373–374). Dedications have "declined significantly since the 1990s" (p. 374).
- **Bhakti reading.** The devotee "adopts a subordinate, serving and devotional attitude" while the deity "takes the protecting, guiding and blessing role of a husband or master" (pp. 385–386, after Brückner 1996).

## 2. What matters for the design

1. **No medieval evidence.** Merkle confirms the reading list: the jōgappas are 20th–21st-century evidence. Nothing here can be dated before 1453.
2. **Wedding to the deity as the rite of change** is the article's structural core (p. 385). It supports, by analogy only, the Garland's fourth gate step (the dreamed wedding). The Garland's wedding is to Viṣṇu and dreamt; the jōgappa's is to a goddess and tied. Keep them apart.
3. **"Always married" status** (*sadā muttayde*, p. 370; Ramberg's *nityasumaṅgalī*, p. 370 n. 6) is an attractive idea for a bride of God who cannot be widowed. But it is a term of the living devadāsī and jōgamma field. Do not use the words.
4. **Chosen vs. choosing** is a real doctrinal tension. The jōgappas insist the goddess chooses (p. 384); Bhaṅgāśvana chooses for herself (`ind_mahabharata_bhangasvana_ila.md`). The Garland can hold both: she chooses to keep the vow, and the Lord answers. Its dissenters can push either way.
5. **Temporary guise vs. permanent life.** Merkle's contrast (Tirupati's one-week guise vs. the irrevocable vow, pp. 384–385) maps onto the faith's line against Arjuna's one-year disguise and Pārvatī's alternation: the faith's womanhood is permanent.
6. **The hierarchy risk is real.** Merkle's bhakti reading (pp. 385–386) shows how bridal devotion re-inscribes the wife as "subordinate" and the god as "master". This is the critique the reading list asks hostile and dissenting voices to make.
7. **Medicalising and "only two genders" rhetoric** is modern (p. 373). Hostile medieval voices must not use the leaflet's language (doctors, mental weakness, nature's rule of two genders).

## 3. Corrections to the candidate

- None to the facts in the reading list. One sharpening: the Ellamma sex-change power is Bradford's 1983 ethnographic report (p. 384), not an old text. Check Bradford for any source before using it even as a character's claim.
- The reading list does not note the **body rule**: jōgappas reject surgery and value the "intact body" (p. 380). If the Garland ever refers to bodily change, it must not echo either this rule or hijra practice; it should keep to its own miraculous, dream-given change.

## 4. Design implications

- **Gate:** keep the dreamed wedding (step 4) and the companions (step 1). Do not add a tied chain, alms basket, possession, matted hair or Tuesday/Friday rounds; those are the jōgappas'.
- **Doctrine screen, a dispute:** "Does the Lord choose the bride, or does the bride choose the Lord?" Orthodox Garland answer: both. A dissenting tenet or heresy event can take either extreme.
- **Reaction lines:** a hostile Śaiva or Vaiṣṇava ascetic can call the vow "a dancer's guise" (from the epic, not the leaflet). Do not use modern medical language.
- **Dissent event:** a daughter of the Garland complains that the Lord is called "husband and master" and asks why the brides serve. The options record the tension; none resolves it.
- **Narrator note:** loc and docs should not mention jōgappas, Ellamma, Saundatti, hijras or Bahuchara. If a design doc needs the comparison, it cites this note as modern ethnography.
