# Arab queer and trans feminisms, progressive Muslim readings, and a Cairo ethnography: guardrails and answers for the Adelphai's Cairo storyline

Written 2026-10-26 for the v2 Adelphai Dorleyaiou storyline at al-Mustanṣir's court in 1066 (`later_starts/mukhannathun_fatimid_cairo.md`). These are **modern works**. Nothing in them is evidence for 1066. They are used here for three things: guardrails on language and framing, counter-moves against the hostile voices the storyline already sources, and a check on the few pre-modern claims they make.

## 1. The arguments in brief

### 1.1 Abdelmoez 2021: translation as politics

**Positionality as stated.** He/him. An independent researcher on "Arab media, gender politics and feminist activism", focused on Egypt and Saudi Arabia. A former lecturer in Middle Eastern Languages at Stockholm and founding chairman of the Swedish MENA association. MPhil in Gender Studies, Cambridge (p. xi). He does not state an Arab, queer or trans identity in the chapter, and writes as a scholar addressing scholars: "As scholars, we need to be attentive" (p. 299). His evidence is mostly the published words of Arab organisations: Meem, Aswat, Helem, Al-Qaws, PQBDS, IraQueer, *Kohl* and *my.Kali* (pp. 286–288). **Those organisations are the insiders here**, and their statements outrank his framing.

**Argument.**
- **Two themes.** Since 2000 Arab queer and trans organising has become more visible. At the same time, people argue over whether LGBTQ+ identities "belong" in the Arab world or are Western imports (pp. 283–284).
- **Trans is not a subset of queer.** "I do not treat queer theory and queer feminisms as necessarily encompassing trans theory and feminisms" (p. 284). Arab LGBTQ+ work is centred on cisgender gays and lesbians, and trans lives are "often overlooked" (pp. 284, 287). Movements led by gay men risk "universalizing their experience at the expense of others" (p. 287). Trans writing is mostly in online journals rather than academia (p. 288). Meem has run trans-only support groups since 2007 (p. 288).
- **The state performs too.** Raids and televised arrests show "authorities make performances out of the policing of queer communities" to appear as "defenders of public morality" (p. 285). Visibility is "at the heart of the controversy" both ways (p. 285).
- **Naming is a political act.** Organisations want to avoid both derogatory Arabic words and English loans (p. 291). Each choice of term "may open up or foreclose upon a certain type of politics" (p. 289). The term survey is in §2.2 below (pp. 290–291).
- **Against Massad, with qualifications.** Massad's "Gay International" thesis says Western NGOs create the gay subjects they claim to protect (p. 292). Abdelmoez follows Ze'evi, Georgis, Gagné and Hasso: Arab writing knew same-sex desire before any Western influence, and "where Massad sees queerness in the Arab world, he sees Westernization" (pp. 292–293).
- **Against homonationalism.** Following Puar, he describes pinkwashing as the use of gay rights to paint neighbours as "backwards, repressive and intolerant" (pp. 293–294, quoting Pinkwatching Israel). Helem and the Palestinian groups refuse "being used as a tool to discredit the Palestinian cause" (p. 294).
- **Context changes the act.** The same act differs by place. Nude protest in Cairo is not nude protest in Stockholm (pp. 296–297). Huda Shaarawi's unveiling is offered as a *local* precedent for a provocative act, so that the act does not read as imitation of Europe (p. 298). Waving a rainbow flag in Greenwich Village differs from waving one in Cairo (p. 299).
- **Closing rule.** Scholars should "pay attention to who is made invisible when giving preference to certain translations". They should be "careful to look for subjects who might not want to be seen in the light you see them" (p. 299).

**Verdict: reliable as a map of modern terminology and activist positions.** It is thin on trans lives, as he says himself (pp. 284, 288). His term glosses lean on one article (Amer 2012). His few historical remarks are secondhand (§5).

### 1.2 Zaharin and Pallotta-Chiarolli 2020: progressive Muslim readings of the fatwas

**Positionality as stated.** Zaharin is at the School of Social Science, University of Queensland. Pallotta-Chiarolli is in Gender and Sexuality Studies at Deakin University (p. 240). They write "from the progressive Muslim standpoint" and aim at "reclaiming transgender Muslim identity" and "stronger voices and empowerment within transgender Muslim communities" (p. 235). Neither author's own gender or religion is stated in the text. Treat the piece as an advocate's guest editorial, not peer-reviewed research.

**Argument.**
- **The fatwas.** Tantawi (Sunni, Egypt, June 1988) and Khomeini (Shiʿi, Iran, given as 1986) allowed genital surgery. The authors read both as proof of "trans-inclusiveness in Islam" (p. 235).
- **Qur'an 4:119.** The conservative argument is that "changing the creation of God" bans bodily change. The authors answer:
  - classical exegetes (al-Ṭabarī, Ṭabāṭabāʾī) read the verse as about polytheism. Its context is the pagan slitting of camels' ears for idols (p. 236);
  - other scholars read "change" as changing "religion ... and God's commands" (p. 236);
  - if every change is forbidden, then curing a cleft palate, or even cooking, would be forbidden too (p. 236).
- **Qur'an 42:49–50** ("He grants females to whomever He pleases and males to whomever He pleases") is read as recognising people "neither male nor female, or are in between" (pp. 235, 237). Hence "God does not make mistakes" (p. 235).
- **Method.**
  - Tantawi's fatwa is said to meet the Shāfiʿī conditions for *ijtihād* (p. 237).
  - Khomeini is said to use the maxims of permissibility (*aṣālat al-ibāḥa*) and lawfulness (p. 238).
  - Both are said to use "necessity overrides prohibition" (p. 238).
  - Fiqh may change with "time or place" (p. 237).
- **What the fatwas actually say, as the authors quote them.** These quotes undercut the editorial's own thesis:
  - Tantawi (via Alipour): the natural *mukhannath* "is not to be blamed but, as s/he has a disease, s/he must be cured" (p. 237).
  - Surgery is "not permissible ... at the mere wish to change sex" (p. 238).
  - Khomeini requires "reliable medical doctors" to recommend it (p. 238).

  The authors admit Alipour and Tolino call the fatwas binary and patriarchal (p. 239).
- **The hadith.**
  - The hostile hadith are "quoted out of context". Many scholars read them as about "cross-dressers who want to deceitfully gain access to women's spaces" (p. 238).
  - Hīt was barred because "he had breached the trust of the Prophet, but not because of her effeminate identity" (p. 238).
  - The Prophet spared a *mukhannath*'s life (Abū Dāwūd) (p. 238).
  - *Mukhannathūn* served in households before unveiled women (Q 24:31) (p. 239).
  - Ibn Ḥajar is cited for confining the curse to "those who want to dress as females to deceive for immoral gain" (p. 239).
- **Framing.** "Islam has always taken sides with the oppressed" (p. 235). Esack's "prophetic solidarity" with the *mustaḍʿafūn*, "the weak", is cited too (p. 235).

**Verdict: useful as a catalogue of counter-moves; unreliable as history.**
- Several claims are wrong or garbled (§5).
- The authors use "third gender" in their own voice (p. 239).
- Their science (brain structure, receptor genes, p. 237) is beside the point for the mod.
- The moves themselves are real and old: the deceit reading of the Hīt hadith, the hadith of sparing "those who pray", the necessity maxim and the permissibility presumption. Several have medieval roots (Rowson, in the repo). That makes them translatable into 1066 speech. The fatwas themselves are not.

### 1.3 Revelo La Rotta 2013: an outsider's ethnography of queer men in Cairo

**Positionality as stated.**
- A Duke undergraduate on study abroad in Cairo and Qatar in summer 2012. He later stayed on, teaching at an NGO (pp. 27–28).
- He names his "privileged position as a Western academic" (p. 30), and calls himself a "diasporic and racialized subject" (p. 33 n. 68).
- His Egyptian Arabic was imperfect. Friends translated for him, or he used Google Translate (pp. 26, 33).
- He was a sexual participant in the group he studied, and wrote about it on purpose as "queer ethnography" (pp. 23–24, 32–33).

He is **not an insider**, and he studies men, not trans women.

**Argument.**
- **Against the saviour.** Western LGBT outrage misreads Middle Eastern cases. His examples are the "Gay Girl in Damascus" blog, invented by an American student (pp. 4–7), and Iranian executions recast as "gay" executions (pp. 5–8). He follows Puar and Massad. The Gay International falls "into the trap of not critically assessing their savior complex" (p. 35).
- **Strategic relativism.** He adopts relativism with limits, following Abu-Lughod (pp. 34–36).
- **Doorkeepers.** In Cairo, informal gatekeepers police morality: the building doorman, the family's "familial ethos" (Singerman). Both can be bought or worked around (pp. 37–40).
- **Roles over orientation.** Same-sex practice among the men he met was organised by active and passive roles, not gay or straight. The passive partner carried the stigma (pp. 41–45). He describes three stances towards Western categories: refusing the "gay" label, taking it up, and "disidentification" (Muñoz), which he favours (pp. 50–56).
- **Language.** Members of the group avoided Arabic for sexual topics. Arabic terms such as *mithliyya* are scholarly coinages, and *al-shudhūdh al-jinsī* ("sexual deviance") is the usual media word (pp. 48–49).
- **The danger of foreign categories.** He cites a published Human Rights Watch testimony from the Queen Boat arrests (2001): police asked the English question "Are you gay?". Knowing how to pronounce the word was taken as guilt (pp. 75–77).

**How it treats trans women.** The trans women at the parties he attended are called "transvestites" and "true ladyboys". He uses "he" for one and stresses another's "male biology". They appear only as objects of the men's desire or derision (pp. 45–47). Sex-work profiles are described the same way (pp. 70–71). He borrows Kulick's "third gender" for Brazilian *travestis* (p. 42). **This thesis is an example of the erasure Abdelmoez warns against** (p. 287): trans women filed under "men who have sex with men".

**Verdict: low reliability.** It is useful for one thing only: the Massad and Puar critique and the risk of being *named*, as an outsider saw them in practice. It is a model of what the storyline must not do: outsider exoticism, eroticising East and West (pp. 74–75), and misgendering. Its one historical claim (*khawal*) is unsourced and wrong (§5).

## 2. Guardrails for the Cairo storyline

### 2.1 Rules

1. **No modern Arabic identity words in loc, in any mouth.** This covers *mithlī/mithliyya*, *junūsiyya mithliyya*, *mughayyir* ("transgender"), *aḥrār al-jins*, *mutaḥawwil(a)* and *ʿābir/ʿābira*. *Mithliyya* and its kin are mid-20th-century coinages (Abdelmoez p. 291; Revelo pp. 48–49). The last two do not appear in any of the three works; treat them as modern and unsourced here. Nor should Arabic loc use *jins* for "sex/gender": that sense is recent (Revelo p. 48, citing Massad).
2. **No modern slurs anywhere:** *khawal*, *biyinik*, *manyūk*, *sālib* ("negative", for the passive partner), and the English "ladyboy", "shemale" and "tranny". These are live slurs (Abdelmoez pp. 285, 290; Revelo pp. 45, 68–69). *Khawal* is also anachronistic (§5).
3. **Medieval insults belong to hostile or legal mouths only.** These are *mukhannath*, *lūṭī*, *maʾbūn*/*ubna*, and the *saḥq* words. The narrator uses *mukhannath* only as the sources' term. Modern Arab activists are split on reclaiming insults: some reclaim *shādh* as "queer", but they are "in the minority" (Abdelmoez p. 290). **So show both stances among the Sisters**: one who takes the word and one who refuses it. Neither is the faith's doctrine.
4. **Never render "the Sisters" as *ukht al-rijāl*, "sister of men".** Today that phrase is Lebanese usage for *lesbian* women, sometimes as a compliment (Abdelmoez pp. 290–291). It is not a trans women's word, and borrowing it would confuse two communities. If the faith needs an Arabic name, build it from *akhawāt* ("sisters") with no "of men".
5. **A daughter is not a *mukhannath*, not a *khunthā*, and not a passive partner.** Trans lives get erased into gay men's when the categories merge (Abdelmoez pp. 284, 287). Revelo's thesis shows the erasure in practice (pp. 45–47). Zaharin's claim that *mukhannith* is "the Arabic term for a trans woman" (p. 236) is wrong (§5) and must not be used. Medieval law also distinguishes manner from act (Cairo note §2.1, §1599). Let the Sisters insist on that distinction.
6. **Pronouns.** The narrator never uses "he" for a daughter. Hostile speakers may, and the text should register it as an injury, not as neutral description (compare Revelo pp. 46–47).
7. **Islam is not uniquely hostile.**
   - Every Islamic hostile instrument should sit beside an Islamic leniency: the Prophet who would not kill "those who pray"; the Mālikī rule on softness "by nature"; al-Sarakhsī's licence for the natural *mukhannath* (Zaharin pp. 238–239; Rowson in `hist_rowson_mukhannathun.md`).
   - It should also sit beside Byzantine hostility (`isl_el_azhari_queens_eunuchs.md` row 1).
   - Arab feminists reject the "backwards, repressive" portrait (Abdelmoez p. 294; Revelo pp. 8–10).
   - These leniencies are **Islam's own**. Never write them as lessons the Sisters taught Cairo.
8. **No Byzantine saviours.** The Sisters do not come to free Cairo's *mukhannathūn*. They do not file them as lost daughters, and no conversion is framed as rescue. This is the in-world twin of the "Gay International" (Abdelmoez p. 292; Revelo pp. 34–35).
9. **Let locals refuse the Sisters' category.** A Fusṭāṭ *mukhannath*, a eunuch *ustādh* or a singing-girl may say "I am not one of you", and the narrative accepts it. Look "for subjects who might not want to be seen in the light you see them" (Abdelmoez p. 299).
10. **Pinkwashing has a medieval shape.** A Byzantine envoy or chronicler can use the Sisters to paint Cairo as barbarous. The Sisters refuse to be "a tool to discredit" their hosts. This follows the Helem and Palestinian groups' statement (Abdelmoez p. 294). It is one of the strongest in-world stances available.
11. **Visibility is risk, and so is naming.** The state stages its policing (Abdelmoez p. 285). A foreign *word* can convict: in the Queen Boat interrogations, knowing English "gay" was taken as guilt (Revelo p. 77). **Mechanic:** a *shurṭa* interrogator who asks for the Greek name. Answering in Greek marks a daughter as one of the Adelphai, so she risks a foreigner's exposure, not only a *mukhannath*'s.
12. **The same act means different things in different places** (Abdelmoez pp. 296–299). The Sisters' public acts in Cairo should be grounded in Cairene precedent, not imported as neutral Constantinopolitan custom: the Nawrūz crowds, Nasab the drummer-woman, Zaknāda's mosque (Cairo note §§1.4, 3.1–3.2).
13. **No fatwas, no medicine, no surgery in 1066.** Translate the move, not the content. The fatwas themselves call the natural *mukhannath* diseased and in need of cure, and they require doctors' approval (Zaharin pp. 237–238). The Sisters take the half that says "not to be blamed", and **refuse the cure** (compare `isl_mukhannath_recent_studies.md` row 4).
14. **The Sisters are not Muslim jurists.** Arguments from the Qur'an and hadith go to Muslim characters: a Mālikī qāḍī, an Ismaʿili *dāʿī*, a pious woman of Rasad's household. Or they are framed as a guest citing "your own Book" in a court debate. The narrator never rules on Islamic law.
15. **No living-community models.** Zaharin's Mughal and hijra material (p. 239, via Hinchy), Revelo's interlocutors and Kulick's *travestis* (Revelo p. 42) are all out of bounds. This is the standing project rule.
16. **No "third gender" in the narrator's voice.** Zaharin (p. 239) and Revelo (p. 42) both use it.
17. **No ventriloquism.** The "Gay Girl in Damascus" was a native voice invented for a Western audience (Revelo pp. 4–7). Cairene characters should speak for their own ends, never as witnesses staged for the player's sympathy. Do not eroticise the Byzantine–Cairene gradient either, as Revelo openly does (pp. 74–75).
18. **Choose Arabic renderings once and keep them.** A translation "will likely follow" a group "for a long time" and shapes its alliances (Abdelmoez p. 289). Settle the Arabic forms of the faith's name and titles in one loc glossary before writing events.

## 3. Answers to hostile voices

Speakers in brackets. "Sister" means a Dorleyaian daughter. Arguments from scripture go to Muslim allies, or are framed as a guest's appeal to "your Book" (rule 14). The modern counter-moves come from these three works. The 1066 answers keep the move and drop the anachronism.

| Hostile voice (sourced in the Cairo note) | Modern counter-move (source) | Medieval-idiom answer |
|---|---|---|
| **"Put them out of your houses"** (*Daʿāʾim* §1597; the Hīt hadith) | The curse and the exclusion answer conduct, namely deceit and carrying women's secrets to men, not who the person is (Zaharin p. 238). Medieval root: al-Muhallab's reading in Rowson p. 676 | [Sister] "Hīt was put out for describing the women to men. Ask your women whether I have ever carried a word out of this house." |
| **"Changing God's creation"** (Q 4:119; a preacher, sharpest against the knife rite) | The verse concerns idolatry, the slit ears of camels vowed to idols, or "religion", not the body; and if every change were forbidden, so would cooking be (Zaharin p. 236) | [Ismaʿili *dāʿī* or qāḍī] "The verse speaks of camels marked for idols. Does your baker change God's creation when she makes bread?" Check §5 item 9 before use |
| **"Rebellion against the order He made"** (a preacher on the curse of imitation) | Q 42:49–50: God gives as He wills, and "God does not make mistakes" (Zaharin pp. 235, 237) | [Sister, to "your Book"] "He gives daughters to whom He wills. You say He erred in me. I say He gave me late." |

**Humour** is the one strategy these works barely touch. Revelo's question games (p. 50) are internal policing, not resistance. For humour, use the Medinan *mukhannathūn*'s own wit (repo: `hist_rowson_mukhannathun.md` §2.7), which is native to the Islamic record.

## 5. Historical claims to check

None of the three authors works from medieval primary texts. Every pre-modern claim below is secondhand at best.
