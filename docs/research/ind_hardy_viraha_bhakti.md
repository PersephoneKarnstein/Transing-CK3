**Relation to existing notes.** Venkatesan (`ind_venkatesan_secret_garland.md`) already gives the *pāvai nōṉpu*, its rules (TP 2), the waking verses (TP 6–15), the refused drum (TP 29), the dream (NT 6), Āṇṭāḷ's 9th-c. date and Periyavāccāṉ Piḷḷai's "guise" line. Narayanan covers the Mārkaḻi Festival of Recitation and the *araiyar*; Ramanujan covers the Kannada and Ācāryahṛdayam theology. This note records only what Hardy adds: a **dated sequence of the Āḻvārs**, the **sand image (*pāvai*)** and the **separate Tai bath**, the **two wakings**, the **meaning of the drum**, the **mother/friend/gossip structure** of the girl poems, the **13th-c. *Īṭu* line on the devotee wearing a woman's dress**, and Hardy's argument **against reading the girl as "the soul"**.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **The subject.** "Emotional Kṛṣṇa bhakti" is devotion that cultivates *viraha*, separation, on three planes at once: the devotee's own life, love poetry (the girl), and myth (the gopīs) (pp. 6–10). Hardy's preferred name is *viraha-bhakti* (p. 9).
- **It is southern and Tamil.** It first appears complete in the Āḻvārs, built on the Caṅkam love-poetry convention (*akattiṇai*). The Sanskrit *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* (BhP), composed in the Tamil South in the 9th or early 10th century, carried it north (pp. 11, 488, 511–512).
- **Before the commentaries.** The Āḻvārs' girl is a figure in a poem, not an allegory. The Śrīvaiṣṇava commentaries (12th–16th c.) and their "abstruse allegorical interpretation", fashionable "from the thirteenth century onward", read her as the soul. Hardy argues this misreads the poems (pp. 244–245, 270 n. 91, 364, 368).
- **From girl to self.** Nammāḻvār keeps the girl at a poetic distance. Tirumaṅkai lets her stand for the poet's own ecstasy. In Āṇṭāḷ, "herself a girl", poet, girl and gopī meet: "the girl is now intrinsically her own I" (pp. 414, 427).
- **Separation, not union.** The poets end in the painful knowledge that God is present only in symbols (temple image, myth, poem, nature). What the bhakta finds is separateness, not merger (pp. 365–366, 581).
- **Social history.** The early Āḻvārs were an elite (a landlord, chieftains). Popular genres such as festival, game and lullaby songs come late (after the 8th c.) and are by Brahmins (Periyāḻvār, Āṇṭāḷ, Toṇṭaraṭippoṭi). Later Śrīvaiṣṇavism kept the Tamil Veda but let the emotionalism fade (pp. 473–480).

## 2. What matters for the design

**For the mod (start 867).** Nammāḻvār is old, about a century or more before the first bookmark. Āṇṭāḷ and Periyāḻvār are near-contemporaries, "recent saints". Tirumaṅkai's Kanchi (the seat) is the only Āḻvār world with a datable king.

### 2.2 The Mārkaḻi vow (pp. 414–418, 474, 476, 512–515)

- **The *pāvai* is a sand image.** Hardy renders the title as "[Song for] the [sand-image of] the Goddess" (p. 414 n. 1). In its oldest layer the rite "presumably centred on the *pāvai*, the sand-figure of a 'lady' or 'mother goddess'", which the BhP reads as Kātyāyanī (p. 416). Akanāṉūṟu 269 has "dolls made of sand" (*vaṇṭal pāvai*), with girls playing with them in Tai (p. 647). At Akan. 269 the girls make sand dolls on the beach before the *kuravai* dance (p. 620). The BhP's gopīs make "a figure of sand of the Devī" (X.22; p. 513).
- **An ancient girls' fertility rite, given a Kṛṣṇa meaning.** TP 3 asks rain and fertility for the country; TP 4–5 make Kṛṣṇa the rain-cloud and ask him to remove sin (pp. 415–416). TP 26 calls the bath "used by the ancient ones" (p. 416). The "we" of the girls includes Āṇṭāḷ, "who may well have participated in such an observance as a young girl" (p. 416). Genre: songs for festivals and folk rites (p. 474).
- **Two baths, not one.** The Mārkaḻi bath (*mārkaḻi nīrāṭal*, the *Tiruppāvai*) and the Tai bath (*tai nīrāṭal*, NT 1) are **different rites**, since Āṇṭāḷ treats them separately. Only the BhP merges them "for literary purposes" (p. 512).
  - The Tai bath is "an archaic custom known from caṅkam literature" and "connected with young girls' hopes of obtaining husbands" (p. 418; Hardy cites Gros ad Paripāṭal XI.85f). Gros, cited by Hardy: in the Tai bath girls ask for "rain and a husband" (p. 512).
  - NT 1 runs through Tai and Māci, addressed to Kāma ("who is after all Kṛṣṇa's son Pradyumna"). Its observances are drawing patterns on the street (*kōlam*), bathing before sunrise and eating once a day (p. 418 and n. 18). Another custom is set in Paṅkuṉi, "when Kāma comes" (n. 19).
  - This corrects the Venkatesan note's "bath likely adapted from" the Tai bath: Hardy keeps the Mārkaḻi and Tai rites distinct.
- **The two wakings.**
  1. TP 6–15: the girls wake friends still asleep (p. 416). The BhP keeps it: the gopīs, "getting up at dawn and calling each other by their names" (X.22.6; p. 513). So the waking crossed into Sanskrit by the 9th–10th century.
  2. TP 16–22: at Kṛṣṇa's house the girls wake the gatekeeper, Nanda, Yaśodā and Piṉṉai, then Kṛṣṇa himself: "a miniature *palliyeḻucci*", the song that wakes the god (p. 416). The genre is in temple use in period: Toṇṭaraṭippoṭi's *Tiruppaḷḷiyeḻucci* (9th or early 10th c.) is "clearly designed to be sung every morning before the idol to wake up the god" at Srirangam (pp. 268, 436). The village temple (conch, bell-hung gates, brass lamps) is merged with Nanda's house (p. 417).
- **The drum (*paṟai*).** Against the commentators' vague "desired object", it "cannot mean anything but 'drum'" (p. 476). Its Tamil connotations are "vulgar, low" (*paṟaiyaṉ*, "pariah"), which Hardy thinks is why the commentators kept silent (p. 478). Asking to be his servants instead of taking the drum, "seen in the light of the *mārkaḻi* ritual, thus sublimates the desire of girls for husbands" (p. 476).
- **Wearing what the god has worn is approved practice.** Periyāḻvār (9th c.) has the god's servants "wear the yellow garment worn by you" and adorn themselves "with the tulsi that has adorned you" (PāḷTM I.1.9; p. 461). This is ordinary temple *prasāda*. Āṇṭāḷ's legend reverses the order: she wore it *before* the god did (Venkatesan).
- **Hagiography on Mārkaḻi.** The 3,000 *Guruparamparāprabhāvam* credits Tirumaṅkai with instituting the *Tiruvāymoḻi* recitation at Srirangam "for the ten days of the month Mārkaḻi", called "the Festival of Sacred Recitation". Hardy: "Naturally it is not possible to decide the historical value of this" (p. 267 n. 88).

### 2.3 The girl, her mother, her friend and the gossips (pp. 331–353)

Hardy counts 26 love songs in the *Tiruvāymoḻi*: 23 girl poems and 3 gopī poems (pp. 331, 353). The structure is fixed (Tables XIX–XX, p. 332):
- **The girl** speaks to herself, to her mother, to her friend, or to nature (birds, clouds, the night). She never speaks to Kṛṣṇa directly; only in myth can she (p. 355 n. 175).
- **The mother** worries about "the slander of society", scolds Kṛṣṇa, and is the only one who talks to him (p. 333). She counts what the girl has lost to Kṛṣṇa: bracelets, complexion, honour, decorum (TVM VI.6; p. 344). In TVM V.5 her refrain is that the girl "will always be a shame to our family" (p. 335).
- **The friend** stands between girl and mother, unlike in Sanskrit or Caṅkam poetry (p. 333). She tries to "re-establish the girl's links with society" (p. 349). In TVM VI.5 she tells the mother: you yourself took her to the temple and showed her the god (p. 350). The girl's own plea is "use my own heart to see" (TVM V.5.2; pp. 335, 350).
- **The neighbourhood women** gossip; the mother fears them as "women with mouths like crickets" (TVM VI.7.4; p. 344).
- **The diviner and the possession dance.** The worried mother calls a female soothsayer (*kaṭṭuvicci*) and has the *veṟiyāṭṭu* dance for the young god Murukaṉ. The friend says this is useless. The girl's state "is a very exalted deity", and the only remedy is the god's names, "conch" and "discus" (TVM IV.6; pp. 351–352). Tirumaṅkai's *Tirumaṭal* describes the diviner's trance in full and has her name Viṣṇu as the cause (pp. 394–395).
- **The girl goes out.** In TVM VII.3 she breaks the rule that "nice girls" wait: "I have no shame left!" She will go to the god's town (p. 336). The genre for this "excessive love" that breaks decorum is *peruntiṇai* (p. 336 and n. 107).
- **The *maṭal*.** The extreme public act is to "ride the *maṭal*", a horse made of palmyra stems, to shame the beloved before the village (TVM V.3.9f; pp. 337–338). In Tirumaṅkai's *Periya Tirumaṭal* (8th c.) the girl cites "a rule which forbids girls" to ride it, prefers "the Northern attitude", and lists northern women who went after their lovers: Sītā, Uṣā, Umā, Vāsavadattā (p. 393). She vows to proclaim the god's misdeeds "so that the world comes to know" (p. 394). Hardy: "an act which society refuses her" (p. 394).
- **The community answers.** Āṇṭāḷ's last song (NT 14) is antiphonal. The girl asks "have you seen him?" and a chorus replies "we have seen him in Vṛndāvana". "The 'we' who have 'seen' provide assurance for the girl who has not yet seen" (p. 425; see also p. 428 on the community of bhaktas as a "tension-relieving" body).
- **Āṇṭāḷ's arc.** Hardy reads the *Tiruppāvai* and NT as a girl's development "from childhood to womanhood": collective innocence (TP, NT 1–4), the solitary adolescent who speaks to nature and dreams (NT 5–10), then the woman who argues with her mother and the neighbours and refuses "an ordinary marriage" (NT 11–13), and the chorus (NT 14) (pp. 425–426).

### 2.4 A man in a woman's voice: the theology (pp. 270, 325, 364, 368, 559–566)

- **Hardy's main point: the girl is a body, not "the soul".**
  - Das Gupta's "to regard all beings as but women dependent on Him" and Zvelebil's "the human soul is female, God male" "confuse an emotional-poetic convention with an ontological-theological statement" (p. 270 n. 91).
  - The "she" stands for "a whole human being, physical and emotional—swooning, sweating, weeping, and dancing". "The spiritualization implied in the allegorical interpretation murders the most fundamental characteristic of the Āḻvār's religious awareness" (p. 368).
  - No girl poem suggests the body "can be or ought to be abandoned" (p. 353).
  - The allegory is not even fixed. The Śaiva *Tirukkōvaiyār*'s commentators read the soul as the *man* and Śiva as the girl (p. 325).
- **Why the question did not arise in the South.** "How can a man 'turn into a woman'?" is a problem the BhP created when it took the songs to readers who did not know the *akattiṇai*. In Tamil love poetry "a man participates in a woman's emotions" as a matter of course (p. 559). Popular performance (dance, mime, theatrical roles "playing the part of the gopīs") also avoided it (p. 559).
- **The in-period commentary that says it outright (13th c.).** The *Īṭu* on TVM 1.4: when the Lord is met face to face, his maleness is so overwhelming that the bhakta "loses his own male nature and will wear the dress of a woman" (p. 563, n. 24). A verse quoted in the *Arumpatavurai* on the *Īṭu*: he alone "can be called male; the remaining universe ... is like a woman" (p. 564). Hardy gives no date for the *Arumpatavurai* or for the verse's source.
  - **The catch.** The *Īṭu* defines "woman" as total dependence: someone with no other purpose than to serve him and no other refuge, who cannot bear his absence (p. 564).
- **Gopīs as the highest bhakti.** The *Nārada Bhakti Sūtra* ("eleventh century?", post-BhP) makes the women of Vraja the model: "the feeling of greatest agitation when He is not thought of" (p. 563). Their being women "is by no means accidental", because their sacrifice was greater than any man's (p. 563).
- **Later answers (out of period or out of region; do not use as period practice).**
  - Late Padma Purāṇa: the sages of the Daṇḍaka forest became women and were born as gopīs (p. 564 n. 28). Hardy counts its "many late portions" among post-BhP works (p. 557).
  - Rūpa Gosvāmī's *rāgānugā bhakti*, 16th c. (p. 566).
  - The Bengal hagiography of Caitanya as Rādhā incarnate, 16th c. on (p. 565).
  - A Telugu farce in which Kṛṣṇa makes an old celibate a girl, 1875–1914 (p. 565).
  - The **Sakhībhāvakas**, who "attempted quite literally 'to turn into women' by wearing women's clothes", appeared in Calcutta c. 1850 (p. 565 and n. 32). **Post-period**: never a model for the faith.
- **Āṇṭāḷ as Bhūmi is late.** Seeing her as the goddess's *avatāra* "is something belonging to a much later period" (p. 351 n. 162). The 6,000/3,000 *Guruparamparāprabhāvam* has her imitate the gopīs (p. 565 n. 30).
- **The conclusion's frame, not for the mod.** In §6.4 Hardy sets the bhaktas' "turning into a woman" beside Schreber's paranoia, only to distinguish them. Schreber turned woman to dominate God; the bhakta imitates the gopīs to accept the given order (pp. 576–577, 580). Hardy's verdict is positive, but the comparison is clinical and includes self-harm material. Do not borrow it.

### 2.5 Temple girls (pp. 474–478, 560 n. 9)

- In the hagiography two "temple-girls" compete before a king. Nāthamuni vindicates the one who sings in the *devagāna* style (p. 475). Hardy coins "temple-girls" so that the later terms (*vārastrī*, *dāsī*, *vārāṅganā*, all meaning "prostitute" in the hagiography) are not projected back (p. 475 n. 189, p. 478).
- He posits a conjectural genre VI, songs "designed for girls, 'Kṛṣṇa's slaves', attached to a temple" (p. 477). He refuses to equate them with "the notorious devdasis": "silence on this topic is preferable to wild speculation" (p. 477 n. 199).
- Later he suggests an early temple *dāsī* institution as "another form of living out bhakti, in the image of the gopīs", marked "if this is correct" (p. 560 n. 9).
- **Grade:** conjecture. It is compatible with the Orr note (temple women were donors, not brides), but adds no evidence.

## 3. Corrections to the candidate and earlier notes

1. **Nammāḻvār's date.** The reading list gives "9th c." Hardy argues for the 7th or early 8th century, before Tirumaṅkai (later 8th). Āṇṭāḷ stays 9th. Since every date but Tirumaṅkai's is soft, use "before 900" for Nammāḻvār in loc and docs.
2. **"Before God all men are women" needs dating and a caution.** It is not in the poems. It is the 13th-c. commentarial reading (*Īṭu* on TVM 1.4, p. 563), and Hardy argues it misreads them (pp. 270 n. 91, 368). The faith may cite the *Īṭu* line in its own voice, with the date. It must reject the *Īṭu*'s definition of woman as dependence (p. 564). The narrator says "medieval commentators read the poems so", never "the Āḻvārs taught".
3. **Two baths.** Mārkaḻi and Tai are separate rites (p. 512). The gate's bath is the Mārkaḻi dawn bath. The Tai bath ("for a husband", Caṅkam) belongs to the optional solitary second stage, as in Venkatesan's Mārkaḻi→Tai order.
4. **The *pāvai* is a sand image of a goddess**, not "Fortune" (Filliozat) and not a person (pp. 414 n. 1, 415 n. 4, 416). The vow's name can stay "Keep the *Pāvai* Vow". Its object is a figure the girls make with their own hands.
5. **"Whose Mārkaḻi?"** Hardy thinks the *Tiruvempāvai* came after the *Tiruppāvai* and possibly used it as a model (p. 417 n. 14). He gives no date for Māṇikkavācakar. Keep the choice, but do not call the Śaiva song the older one. The in-period evidence for *women singing* it remains Orr's.
6. **The waking has two parts**: friends, then the god (pp. 416, 436). The god-waking is in daily temple use at Srirangam by the 9th–10th c.
7. **The drum is a low thing.** The refused reward is the *paṟai*, a drum with "vulgar, low" connotations (pp. 476, 478). "Not for the drum" remains the hidden best choice. The drum can also be the temptation that an orthodox observer mocks.
8. **Āṇṭāḷ as goddess and the Sakhībhāvakas** are out of period (p. 351 n. 162; p. 565 n. 32).
9. **Hardy does not use "third sex" language.** He puts "turn into a woman" in scare quotes. Keep his structural point (a man in Tamil love poetry takes a woman's emotions without a "problem") and leave his Schreber comparison alone.

## 4. Design implications

**Gate, "Keep the *Pāvai* Vow" (additions to the §15 steps).**
1. **The waking, in two parts.** (a) She wakes the girls of the street (companions at court), calling each by name (BhP X.22.6; p. 513). (b) On the last dawn the vow-keepers wake the god: a *palliyeḻucci* at the temple gate (pp. 416, 436). Success at (b) needs at least one companion still in the vow.
2. **Shape the *pāvai*.** At the river on the first dawn she and her companions make the sand image of the goddess (pp. 416, 513, 647). Flavour: the river takes it back each night and it is made again each dawn. A Śaiva-leaning keeper calls it Kātyāyanī (the BhP's reading, p. 513); a Vaiṣṇava-leaning one calls it simply "our *pāvai*".
3. **The dawn bath, kept for the country.** The *Tiruppāvai*'s rewards are rain and harvest for all (pp. 415–416). Keeping the vow in a drought year gives a county modifier ("The Girls Bathed for Rain"), so the vow is a public good as well as a private crossing.
4. **The drum offered and refused** (unchanged; pp. 476, 478).
5. **Optional Tai stage, alone.** The Tai bath and NT 1's rites (*kōlam* on the street, a sunrise bath, one meal a day; p. 418), addressed to Kāma. It is solitary, harder, and the dream belongs to it. It mirrors Hardy's arc from the collective girl to the woman alone (pp. 425–426).

**Events (family and village reaction, from the girl-poem cast).**
- *"A Shame to Our Family"*: the mother's lament (TVM V.5, p. 335; VI.6, p. 344). The mother is the character most likely to oppose; the friend is the counterweight.
- *"You Took Her to the Temple Yourself"*: a friend (or sister) argues with the hostile mother (TVM VI.5, p. 350). If the friend succeeds, the mother's opinion improves; the option text uses "use my own heart to see" (p. 335).
- *"The Diviner Is Called"*: the family hires a *kaṭṭuvicci* or holds a Murukaṉ possession dance to "cure" her (TVM IV.6; CTMaṭal; pp. 351–352, 394–395). Options: submit (stress); let the friend speak (the god's names, not the dance); or the diviner herself names the god (as in the *Tirumaṭal*, p. 395), which ends the cure. **Caution:** the sources call her state a "disease", even a "good disease". The faith's voice must never call her womanhood an illness. Use "they thought her possessed"; the faith answers "it is not a demon, it is the god" (p. 351).
- *"Mouths Like Crickets"*: neighbourhood gossip (p. 344). It lowers opinion of her and is answered by the chorus decision below.
- *"Ride the *Maṭal*"* (optional, high-risk decision): a public declaration in the street, defying the "Southern rule" (p. 393). It gives a large faith prestige and county-opinion swing, both ways. Hardy dates the poem to the later 8th c. (Tirumaṅkai). Use it as a daughter's public coming-out, framed as defiance of gossip, never as a man's act.
- *"We Have Seen Him"*: a decision for an established daughter to answer a struggling one with the NT 14 chorus. It gives stress relief to the target and piety to the speaker (p. 425).

**Doctrine / tenet text.**
- In the faith's voice: "The poets' girl was never a soul. She sweated, wept and danced; she was a body, and the body was hers." (Hardy, p. 368, rendered as doctrine.)
- In the faith's voice, with the date: "A teacher of Srirangam wrote that before the Lord a man lays down his manhood and puts on a woman's dress. We say: she did not lay it down for him. It was never hers." (*Īṭu* on TVM 1.4, 13th c., p. 563; the second half is the faith's answer to its dependence clause, p. 564.)
- Narrator, safe: "Tamil love poetry let a man speak a girl's longing; medieval commentators made the girl a soul" (pp. 368, 559).

**Holy-site text.**
- **Kanchipuram (seat):** "Here a poet praised a temple of the Pallava king Nandivarman, the Vaikuṇṭha Perumāḷ; in the same poet's songs a girl rides out to shame the god who left her." (Tirumaṅkai, later 8th c.; Parameśvara-Viṇṇagaram built by Nandivarman II or Parameśvaravarman, p. 264.)
- **Srirangam:** "Two poets sang of no other temple. Each morning a Brahmin's song wakes the god." (Toṇṭaraṭippoṭi and Tiruppāṇ, 9th–10th c., pp. 260, 268, 436.)
