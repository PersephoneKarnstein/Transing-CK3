# Paul of Aegina, *Epitome* VI.68 "On Castration" (with VI.46, VI.69), design reading

Source: *The Seven Books of Paulus Ægineta*, translated from the Greek with a commentary by Francis Adams, vol. II (London: Sydenham Society, 1846; title page "MDCCCXLVI"; Project Gutenberg eBook #70532, whose metadata wrongly gives 1844, the date of vol. I). Book VI (surgery), Sect. 68 "On Castration" (pp. 379–381 per the table of contents, Adams's commentary included). Also read: VI.46 "On Male Breasts Resembling the Female", VI.69 "On Hermaphrodites", VI.70, and the elephantiasis commentary to IV.1 (only other "eunuch" hit in the volume). Whole volume searched for *castrat-*, *eunuch*, *effemin-*, *hermaphrod-*. Paul wrote in Alexandria in the 7th century (before 642); his surgery book was the standard Byzantine and, via Albucasis and Haly Abbas, Arabic manual. Quotes are Adams's English.

## 1. What the text says

- **Castration is anti-medicine, done on orders from above.** "The object of our art being to restore those parts which are in a preternatural state to their natural, the operation of castration professes just the reverse." He describes it only "since we are sometimes compelled against our will by persons of high rank" (VI.68). The physician frames himself as a reluctant instrument of the powerful.
- **Two methods, both on children for one of them.** *Compression*: "children, still of a tender age, are placed in a vessel of hot water", and the testicles are "squeezed with the fingers until they disappear". *Excision*: the patient on a bench, two straight cuts in the scrotum, the testicles "dissected around and cut out".
- **Why excision is preferred: desire.** "those who have had them squeezed sometimes have venereal desires, a certain part ... of the testicles having escaped the compression." The test of a successful castration is the absence of desire, not any change of sex. No word on voice, beard, fat, womanliness or a third state.
- **No reason "why it was done" beyond the client's rank.** Paul gives no medical indication at all. Adams's commentary supplies the context: copied by Albucasis (*Chirurg.* ii.69) and Haly Abbas; surgeons in Juvenal's Rome "often called upon"; castration "an important part of the surgical practice in Bagdad" (Abulpharagius); "the Emperor Justinian condemned the operation as being dangerous and often fatal."
- **The only medical motive recorded is prophylaxis, and it is self-castration.** Aëtius (after Archigenes, in Adams's commentary on elephantiasis, IV.1): "Some ... having remarked that eunuchs escaped taking this complaint, have castrated themselves as a preventive."
- **VI.46, male breasts.** Gynaecomastia is operated on because "this deformity has the reproach of effeminacy". Surgery here removes womanlike flesh from men for social reasons, the mirror image of VI.68.
- **VI.69, hermaphrodites.** Four kinds (after Leonides), three in men, one in women; the condition "occasions great deformity to both sexes"; three of four "may be cured by removing the supernumerary bodies". The surgical aim is to restore one of two sexes. There is no third-sex category in Paul.

Short version: Paul is a practitioner, not a theorist. He says nothing about what the cut body *is*. His frame is that castration reverses the physician's purpose and is done at the command of "persons of high rank", often to young children.

## 3. In-world loc lines

- "Our art restores what is out of nature. This cut does the reverse, and I make it because the great command it." (Paul, VI.68, adapted, for a court surgeon)
- "The court's own surgeon writes that he cuts boys in a bath against his will. We cut no boys, and no one against hers." (Rite of the Knife polemic)
- "The squeezed ones still burn, he says; the cut ones do not. That is all their medicine asks of a eunuch: that she want nothing." (Dorleyite preacher)
