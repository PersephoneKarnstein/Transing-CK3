## 1. The work and its frame

- **Genre.** *Even Boḥan* ("The Touchstone") is a rhymed-prose moral satire. Chotzner says it is a "mosaic" of biblical and Talmudic phrases, used "in a sense that is different from its original meaning" (pp. 130, 141–142). The same author wrote *Massekhet Purim*, a mock Talmudic tractate for Purim (Chotzner pp. 137–138).
- **Date.**
  - The Open Siddur gives 1322.
  - Chotzner reports that most bibliographers put it "about the year 1324" (p. 130).
  - The colophon gives the author's age as 83, which Chotzner emends to 38 (pp. 136–137).
  - Use **c. 1322–24**.
- **Place of writing.** Catalonia, not Provence (Rosen 2000/2003; `jud_rosen_circumcised_cinderella.md`). Arles is his birthplace only.
- **Author.** Born at Arles "in the year 1287" (Chotzner p. 128), son of Kalonymos ben Meir, who was *Nasi* of the Arles community. In Chotzner's account the archbishop had confined the Jews of Arles to "a single street" (p. 128). Kalonymus worked as a translator for Robert of Naples, went to Rome, and carried a petition from the Avignon community to the Pope (pp. 128–129).
- **The persecutions behind the book.** Chotzner notes that *Even Boḥan* mourns the Shepherds' and Lepers' persecutions of 1320–21 and the burning of the Talmud at Toulouse in 1319 (p. 135). Any Provençal start date for the sect sits right after these. That should colour the mood (fear, a low voice), never the mechanics (the Jews must not be cast as villains).
- **Chotzner's title** says "Thirteenth-Century", but his own dates put the work in the fourteenth.

## 2. The lament and prayer (pp. 15–19)

The section is marked in the margin of p. 16 as **טבע הזכרים** ("the nature of males"). It runs as one arc.

**The turnings list** (p. 17), every item built on the root ה-פ-ך:
1. Abraham turned from the furnace of Ur.
2. Dinah turned in the womb.
3. The staff turned to a serpent.
4. The hand turned white.
5. The Red Sea turned to dry land.
6. "The rock into a pool" (Ps. 114:8), continued on p. 18.

The editor's apparatus (*Avnei Milu'im*, n. 86) appears to cite Berakhot 60a "regarding Dinah". The OCR of the note is garbled, so this is *unverified*.

**The blessing and its grounding.** The prayer ends in the morning blessing *shelo asani ishah*. The text justifies saying it from the rule "that one blesses over the good and over the bad" (שמברכין על הטובה ועל הרעה, p. 18). That is the Mishnah discussed on the same Talmud page as the Dinah story (Berakhot 60a; see the rabbinic note §2). Chotzner glosses the blessing as the well-known one in "the Daily Morning Prayer" (p. 133 n. 1).

**The women's commandments.** Kalonymus lists three duties he would keep in exchange for the husband's three (food, clothing, conjugal rights):
- **niddah**, the separation in menstruation (the Hebrew reads "dam", blood);
- **ḥallah**, separating a portion of dough;
- **lighting the Sabbath lamp**.

The Open Siddur notes this cites Mishnah Shabbat 2:6. The passage closes on the Talmud's question, "נשי במאי זכיין" ("by what do women gain merit?", p. 17).

**Translation crux: "a fixed blemish".**
- The Lemberg text reads "אי אפשי להסירו מעלי" (p. 18).
- In Mishnaic Hebrew, אי אפשי means "I do not wish". The Open Siddur translates "then I do not wish to remove it".
- The next clause speaks of grief over "what is impossible" (במה שאי אפשר). Most paraphrases, including the reading list, take the line as "cannot be removed".
- **The design should not lean on either reading.** If a faith voice quotes it, use "a fixed blemish" (מום קבוע), which is unambiguous.

**Avoid one line.** After the resignation the speaker says he will bear it "until I expire" (עד אגוע, p. 18). Chotzner's verse turns this into "grim death, a timely friend" and "the wished-for end" (p. 133). Under the no-suicide rule, **neither line may be used**, in loc or in a faith's voice.

## 3. The Open Siddur translation

- **Licence and credit.** CC BY-SA 4.0 is a share-alike licence. Any loc line that reproduces the Open Siddur English verbatim makes that string, and arguably the loc file, subject to share-alike, and requires credit to Greenberg, Rosen and the Open Siddur contributors. **Recommendation:** write the mod's own short renderings from the Hebrew, which is public domain (1322; 1865 edition), and credit the Open Siddur in the README bibliography as a reading aid only.
- **Framing.** The page frames the text as a "Prayer for Transformation" for trans observances. Its provenance note quotes Chotzner's "most humorous" verdict. Varady's note on "encamped in my house against armies" (Zech. 9:8) rejects the satirical gloss ("exempted from military service") as "mistaken". That is a modern sincere reading, set against the satirical one. **Narrator rule:** say the text is a satire whose sincerity is debated, and let the sect hold the sincere reading.
- **Accuracy.** The translation matches the Lemberg text in every line checked. It includes the Dinah line, which Chotzner's verse drops.

## 4. Chotzner 1900

- His verse (pp. 131–133) is a **free, bowdlerised paraphrase**. It drops the whole turnings list, Dinah included, and the petition "turn me from male to female". In their place he writes "If thou hadst but created me / A girl". He adds a parenthesis of his own, "(With silent protest and dismay)". It is useful only as a witness to Victorian reception ("one of the most humorous parts", p. 131). **Do not quote his verse in loc.**
- **Purim material** (pp. 137–138), all from *Massekhet Purim*, not *Even Boḥan*:
  - It is a parody of Talmudic debate over Purim usages.
  - Some rabbis "took it au serieux" and called it heretical (p. 137).
  - It proposes reading the Megillah in the vernacular, written in Hebrew letters (p. 138).
  - It records that the rabbis allowed dancing "provided that the dancers chose their partners from their own sex" (p. 138).

  For the sect, this means a woman dances among the women on Purim. *Massekhet Purim* itself was not read; Chotzner is the only witness. Correction (Rosen): *Massekhet Purim* was written in Rome, so the custom is attested for Rome, not Provence.

## 5. Purim in *Even Boḥan* (pp. 28–30)

This is the most useful find of the round. The festival-cycle satire, which begins "I will tell their custom in their festivals" (p. ~24), has a Purim passage on p. 29. It attests **men in women's dress on Purim in Kalonymus's own milieu**, nearly two centuries before Judah Minz (d. 1508), whose ruling Isserles codified.

- On the fourteenth of Adar the young men of Israel "go mad and run riot" ("ישתגעו ... יתהוללו", p. 29).
- They "wrap themselves in the glory of anklets" ("יעטו כמעיל תפארת העכסים", p. 29). *Akhasim* are women's anklets (Isa. 3:18).
- One sets a basket on his head (n. 154, a Talmudic idiom).
- **"זה ילבש שמלת אשה ולגרגרותיו ענקים"**: "this one puts on a woman's dress, and necklaces on his throat" (p. 29). The phrase שמלת אשה is Deut. 22:5's own. Isserles uses the same words for the custom he permits (rabbinic note §8).
- Another plays the fool with drum and dance, "these with those, men" (אלו עם אלו אנשים, p. 29). This shows the revel is all-male.
- Then come the evening feast with game birds (p. 29), shrinking gifts to the poor (p. 29), and wine "until he cannot tell between cursed Haman and blessed Mordecai" (p. 30, after Megillah 7b). The day may be extended "tomorrow too" (גם מחר, Esther 9:13; p. 30).
- The neighbouring festival satires all have the same shape: show and quarrel replace devotion. On Yom Kippur the community splits over who leads prayer (pp. 26–27). On Sukkot it is decorated booths, dice and cards (pp. 27–28). On Hanukkah it is pancakes (p. 28). On the eve of Passover it is painted matzot (p. 30). Chotzner (p. 134) says the same.

**Reading.** Kalonymus mocks the Purim cross-dressing as foolishness. He does not condemn it as a lashable sin, and he lists it beside the anklets and the basket-hat. That supports the reading-list premise that Purim was the licensed day, and it moves the evidence back to **Provence in the 1320s**. The sect can claim, in its own voice: "In the Touchstone, the young men put on the woman's dress on the fourteenth of Adar. We put it on and do not take it off."

**Incidental.** נהפוך הוא appears once in the book (p. ~50, *unverified* page), in the satire on astrologers whose forecasts come out the other way. It is not used of Purim.

## 6. What the sect may quote in its own voice

All of these are short, and all are from the public-domain Hebrew, in the mod's own rendering:

- "מי יתן ותהפכני מזכר לנקבה" ("would that you turn me from male to female", p. 18). This is the sect's motto.
- "והפכת דינה במעי אמה" ("you turned Dinah in her mother's womb", p. 17). This is its precedent.
- "אלו בראני אומן שעשאני אשה הגונה" ("had the craftsman made me a worthy woman", p. 16).
- "גברת הבית הייתי" ("I would have been lady of the house", p. 18).
- "בקול נמוך בשפה חלושה" ("in a low voice, a weak lip", p. 18). The rite reverses this.
- "זה ילבש שמלת אשה" ("this one puts on a woman's dress", p. 29). This is Purim precedent.
- The spinning circle by moonlight, "מספרות זו עם זו" ("telling stories to one another", p. 16). This is the image of women's company the sect offers.

**Narrator voice.**
- The narrator says "Kalonymus of Arles wrote, perhaps in jest". It never says "Kalonymus was a trans woman".
- The narrator never uses Chotzner's or the Open Siddur's English verbatim.
- The narrator never quotes the "until I expire" line.

## 7. Corrections to the current proposal (`comparanda_reading_list.md`, "Medieval Judaism")

1. **Pages.** The lament runs pp. 15–19. The prayer is p. 17 (turnings) into p. 18 (petition and blessing). The reading list's "about pp. 17–19 by the OCR page markers" should read: **p. 17 (Dinah), p. 18 (petition, blessing); lament pp. 15–19**.
2. **The phrase "אילו עשני אשה"** in the brief does not occur. The text has "אלו בראני אומן שעשאני אשה הגונה" (p. 16).
3. **"A permanent blemish that cannot be removed".** This is one reading of a crux. אי אפשי can mean "I do not wish" (§2).
4. **Purim cross-dressing is attested c. 1322 in *Even Boḥan* itself** (p. 29), not only by Minz and Isserles in the 16th century. Finding 4 and the "flag as late" caveat can be softened. The licence is a later codification of a custom Kalonymus already describes. He describes it, though; he does not rule on it.
5. **Date.** Use "c. 1322–24", not a bare 1322 (Chotzner p. 130).
6. **Chotzner's verse omits the Dinah line and the petition.** Anyone reading only Chotzner would miss the turning. Cite the Hebrew.
7. **The Lemberg title page** names Cremona 1558 as its base. Chotzner names Venice 1558 (p. 130). This is minor; cite "Lemberg 1865" only.

## 8. Design implications

- **Gate verb.** Use ה-פ-ך throughout. The rite is *ha-Hipukh* or *ha-Hafikhah*, "the Turning"; the members are *ha-Nehpakhot*. Kalonymus's six turnings make a ready-made liturgy: one loc line per turning, read aloud at the rite.
- **The three women's commandments** (niddah, ḥallah, the Sabbath lamp), from p. 17 via Mishnah Shabbat 2:6. These are what she *does* after the Turning:
  - a modifier `tm_three_lamps` (working title);
  - a yearly event where she lights the lamp;
  - opinion from other members.

  This grounds her womanhood in practice, not in the body.
- **Adornment.** The text's verb is עדה, "to adorn" ("יעדה עדי זהב", p. 17). It is the same verb Maimonides uses for the lashable offence (עדי אשה, Avodah Zarah 12:10). A gold ornament (bracelet, necklace, anklet) is the visible sign. *Item idea:* "the adornment" as an artifact or court-amenity flavour.
- **Women's company.** The moonlit spinning circle (p. 16) can be a recurring sisterhood event. It is the sect's equivalent of the völva's foster-daughter chain: older members teach spinning and weaving.
- **Reaction lines** for Rabbinic characters:
  - "One does not cite miracles" (Berakhot 60a).
  - "Kalonymus blessed *shelo asani ishah*, and so must you."
  - "It was a jest; even Chotzner laughed." (This is a scholar's line, so avoid it in character voice; use "the learned call it a jest". Correction (Rosen): no source ties the lament to Purim.)
- **Era.** Spawn no earlier than c. 1322. Arles fits: Kalonymus's birthplace, with its single Jewish street under the archbishop (Chotzner p. 128).

## 9. Proposed outline: the Turning, a Purim event chain

This is modelled on `tm_seidr` (0010 feast → 0011 warding songs → 0001 platform → 0002 naming → 0003 the thing). It is grounded only in the texts above and in `jud_rabbinic_primary_texts.md`. Proposed namespace `tm_hipukh`. Root = the seeker. Scopes: `scope:tm_elder` (a member who has already turned, a woman of the *ḥevrah*), `scope:tm_kahal_head` (the community's leading man; *tovei ha-ir*, per Isserles).

**Trigger.** Yearly, on Purim. CK3 has no Hebrew calendar, so use a fixed in-game month (Feb/Mar), a yearly on_action pulse with a random chance, or a decision "Prepare for the fourteenth of Adar". Requirements:
- male, adult;
- Rabbinic faith, or the sect if it is a separate faith;
- living in a county with the sect present (Arles first);
- game date ≥ 1322.

**Hard limits for this chain.**
- No knife and no potion.
- Never "her courage was manly".
- No suicide line (do not use "until I expire").
- Gentile characters are never shown as tormentors of the *kahal* inside the rite.
- No Jewish character is a slave or eunuch trader (see `jud_phelan_slave_trade_motif.md`).
- The narrator never says the Talmud or Kalonymus "recognised trans women".
