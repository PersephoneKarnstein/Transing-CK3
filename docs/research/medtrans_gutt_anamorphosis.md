Source: Blake Gutt, "Medieval Trans Lives in Anamorphosis: Looking Back and Seeing Differently (Pregnant Men and Backward Birth)," *Medieval Feminist Forum: A Journal of Gender and Sexuality* 55, no. 1 (2019), special issue *Medieval Trans Feminism*, ed. M. W. Bychowski and Dorothy Kim: 174-206. DOI: [10.17077/1536-8742.2150](https://doi.org/10.17077/1536-8742.2150).

Citation verified against the article's running footers ("mff, vol. 55 no. 1, 2019: 174-206") and the PDF metadata. The PDF metadata gives no author; the author is named on the first page. The DOI is not printed in the PDF; it was taken from the issue's table of contents on ScholarWorks. Read in full, notes and figure captions included. Quotes are verbatim, with the journal's page numbers.

**Scope flag:** the article's case studies are **pregnant men**, read as trans men. The mod excludes trans men (faith_design.md, open question 2). Its direct case material is therefore out of scope, and this note uses it only for method and for a few side-arguments that bear on trans women. Relevance is **low to moderate**.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **Anamorphosis and "backward birth".** Lacan's reading of Holbein's *Ambassadors* (the skull seen only from the doorway) is combined with Stockton's "backward birth": the queer child is named only in retrospect. "The phrase 'gay child' is a gravestone marker for where or when one's straight life died" (p. 175, quoting Stockton). Trans adults, most of whom did not transition as children, know the same look back (pp. 175-176).
- **Does it "take one to read one"?** Not necessarily, "trans voices and perspectives are essential in establishing trans readings" (p. 181). Mills reaches trans readings "sideways" through gay history, and that yields a view "both secondhand and incomplete" (p. 178).
- **The persecutor's eye.** Peter Damian's *Liber gomorrhianus* (1049) imagines sodomites recognising each other, and Damian claims the same sight. "The paradox of seeing queerness irrevocably binds the non-straight and the most fervently straight together" (p. 182).
- **Falsigraphy.** In Alain de Lille's *De planctu Naturae* (c. 1170), Venus writes each gender with pen on parchment according to Nature's grammar. Deviation is *falsigraphia*, which "could refer equally to homosexual or to transgender behavior or identities" (p. 183).
- **Torelore.** In *Aucassin et Nicolette*, the King "gissoit d'enfent", the phrase for a woman lying in, and says "When my month is over and I am fully healed" (p. 185). Generations of critics called it couvade, "unwilling to take the text at its word" (p. 185). Aucassin beats him into swearing no man will lie in again. Gutt reads this as "a clear case of queer-bashing" (p. 187). The Toreloreans then expel Aucassin for killing in a food fight: "it is certainly not our custom to kill one another" (p. 187).
- **Saint Fanuel.** In a 13th-century apocryphal romance, Fanuel conceives Saint Anne in his thigh from apple juice, is "greatly ashamed", and orders the baby killed. She survives in a bird's nest and later says: "I have never seen my father, but I see my mother coming now. Tell him to come to me" (p. 194).
- **Against the anachronism objection.** "The line of argument that maintains that non-cis gender identities are anachronistic to medieval thought and culture is itself both anachronistic and ill-informed" (p. 189). Recognition "does not equate to a declaration that such characters were intended by their authors to represent transgender individuals" (p. 188).
- **Male pregnancy as orthodoxy.** The image of Eve drawn from Adam's side spread from the late 11th century. It is "the very emblem of social and religious orthodoxy" (p. 200, quoting Gilbert): the father as originator and owner of his offspring.
- **Raymond's use of the myth.** Raymond says trans women act out "single parenthood by the father". They are children of "the medical fathers", "renouncing [their] mothered birth" (p. 203). Gutt answers in two ways. First, the clinics were opposed by the medical establishment (Riddell): "the impetus for trans women's medical transition clearly originates with the women themselves" (p. 204). Second, only ignorance of "the long history of non-cis identities" lets Raymond present trans people as a modern artefact (p. 204).
- **The payoff.** Looking back through trans anamorphosis shows "an affirmation of lives and of identities" (p. 205), and "communities across time ... even if the mirror can never be crossed" (p. 206).

### Primary-source cases flagged

- *Aucassin et Nicolette* (BnF fr. 2168) and *Le Roman de Saint Fanuel* (Fitzwilliam MS 20): **out of scope** (trans-masculine readings). Do not use.
- **Peter Damian, *Liber gomorrhianus* (1049)**: a period source for hostile Latin text and for the "persecutor's eye" event (row 4). Damian is a historical cardinal (d. 1072). He could appear as a reaction voice in an event for Latin-Christian Italian courts c. 1050-1072. Check whether vanilla CK3 already has him as a character before scripting.
- **Alain de Lille, *De planctu Naturae* (c. 1170)**: source for *falsigraphia* (row 5) and a possible book artifact for late-12th-century Latin courts.

### Methodological cautions

- **Anachronism cuts both ways.** Gutt calls the anachronism objection itself anachronistic. The mod should neither apologise for trans presence in the past nor put modern vocabulary into medieval mouths. Recognition goes in-world, in the faith's voice.
- **Male pregnancy is not a trans-women's motif.** Its medieval charge was patriarchal (Eve from Adam), and Raymond used it against trans women. Any gestation imagery in the mod (the Jade Embryo) must be framed through mothers.
