## 1. The argument in brief

- **Rabbinic generation is messier than Genesis.** The Priestly writers bind human reproduction to the image of God ("in his likeness, as his image", Gen 5:3). The Tannaim (1st–early 3rd c., Roman Palestine) instead live in "a world of reproductive uncertainty" (Intro, opening). Cows deliver camel-like calves; women deliver things "like a kind of" bird or beast. The rabbis treat these as facts to be classified, not as portents or monsters (ch. 1, "Generation and Variation in Late Antiquity").
- **Kind follows origin, not look (the "generation principle").** mBekhorot 1:2: a cow that delivers something like a donkey has delivered a cow; "that which emerges from the impure is impure, and that which emerges from the pure is pure". The Tosefta (tBekhorot 1:9) extends it: no small beast from a large one, "and not a human from any of them, nor any of them from a human" (ch. 1, "The Sameness of Difference").
- **But there are dissenters.** Rabbi Simon holds that a camel born of a cow is "as if it were born of a camel", reading Scripture's doubled "camel" (tBekhorot 1:9). The sages answer him with the generation principle. For human births the sages demand "something of human form" before a delivery counts as offspring; Rabbi Meir does not (mNiddah 3:2). Rabbi Hanina son of Gamliel splits the difference: animal eyes "resemble human eyeballs", so only bird-like births fail (tNiddah 4:5). The Sidonian woman's raven fails (tNiddah 4:6).
- **Multiform is not hybrid.** Neis insists the *koy* is not *kilayim* (ch. 4, "The Ab Initio Multiform Creature"). A hybrid is the offspring of two kinds. The *koy*, like the androginos, is "a creature unto itself" (*biryah le-atsmo*) that the sages "could not compel" into wild or domestic (tBikkurim 2). She reads "decide" (*le-hakhria*) as also "compel" or "force" (ch. 5, "Rabbinic Nonbinarity").
- **The Tannaim do not moralise hybrids; later rabbis do.** The Tosefta puts fire and *kilayim* among things "considered" part of creation. The Sifre puts the female mule among things made at twilight on the first Sabbath eve. The Yerushalmi (yBerakhot 8:5) recasts the mule as a "harmful" human invention by Anah, punished measure for measure (ch. 4, "Etiologies" and "Kilayim after the Tannaim").
- **The human is a "trihybrid".** yKilayim 8:4 (31c): bone and sinew from the man, skin and blood from the woman, "breath and soul and spirit" from God, "all three of them are partners" (ch. 4).
- **Ch. 5 reads two Babylonian cases without cis defaults.** Rabbi Yoḥanan, beardless, who "looked like a woman" in MS Florence of bBava Metsia 84a; and Lilith in the incantation bowls, who appears "to men in the likeness of women and to women in the likeness of men".

## 2. How the rabbis thought about one kind turning into another

This is the question the sect needs answered. Neis's material sorts into five distinct cases. Only two are true transformation of a living being, and both are God's act.

| Case | Text (via Neis) | Who changes it | Status for the rabbis |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Born looking like another kind | mBekhorot 1:2; tBekhorot 1:6–13; mNiddah 3:2 | No one; "spontaneous" | Kind follows parentage (majority); look wins (Rabbi Simon, minority) |
| B. Hybrid | Lev 19:19; tKilayim 5:3 | Humans (forbidden) | Tannaim neutral; Yerushalmi: a harmful invention, punished |
| C. Creature of its own | tBikkurim 2 (androginos, *koy*) | Created so | Neither/both; "the sages could not decide" |
| D. Living creature changed by God | yShabbat 1:3, 3b: God "changes the world every seven years", including turning the hyena "from male to female" and other species changes (ch. 3, n. 78; Intro n. 40 on cows into bees) | God | Neutral, cosmological. *Neis cites it in passing; read the Yerushalmi itself before using it* |
| E. A body's order changed by miracle | bShabbat 53b: a poor widower "developed breasts like the two breasts of a woman and nursed his child"; the Amoraim dispute whether this shows his greatness or his disgrace, "because the orders of creation were transformed for him" (*shenishtanu lo sidrei bereishit*) (ch. 5, n. 107) | God | Disputed: merit or dishonour |
| (F. Devolution as punishment) | GenRab 23:6, faces turn "monkeylike"; bSanhedrin 109a, Babel's builders become "monkeys, spirits, demons and liliths" (ch. 5, n. 20) | God, punitively | Negative; later sources |

Three things follow.

1. **The rabbinic default is the counter-claim.** The generation principle says kind is fixed by origin: "that which emerges from the pure is pure". Applied (by the sect's opponents) to sex, it becomes "what came forth a male is male". This is stronger than "one does not cite miracles" (Berakhot 60a), because it is a rule of classification, not a caution about piety.
2. **Every sanctioned change of kind is God's act, never a human craft.** In D and E God changes the body. In Berakhot 60a Leah prays and the fetus is turned. When humans make a new kind (B), the later rabbis call it harm and punish it. This backs the reading list's finding 1 (no knife, no potion) from a second direction: in rabbinic terms a human-made change is *kilayim*-like, while a prayed-for change is a miracle.
3. **Change of kind is never framed as gender in these texts.** Neis says plainly that even the Yerushalmi's mule-and-human passage does not make male and female "different species" (ch. 4). Mapping "turned from male to female" onto species-turning is the sect's own exegetical move. Only case E (bShabbat 53b) concerns a body crossing a sexed function, and it is about nursing, not about becoming a woman.

## 3. Is this a usable frame for a sect whose daughters are "turned"?

**Yes, in the faith's own voice, with three exclusions.**

What works:
- **Passive grammar.** All the good precedents are passive and divine: "the fetus was turned into a daughter" (Berakhot 60a), "the orders of creation were changed for him" (bShabbat 53b), God "changes the world" (yShabbat 1:3). This fits Kalonymus's "would that You turn me" and a Purim rite of prayer. The daughter does not turn herself; she asks to be turned.
- **The rabbinic habit of the minority opinion.** Rabbinic argument keeps losing opinions on record. A Provençal sect would argue the way the Talmud argues: by claiming Rabbi Simon ("the camel is a camel"), Rabbi Meir ("it is offspring"), and the Amora who called the nursing widower great. This makes the sect recognisably rabbinic rather than an outside import.
- **The soul as the third partner.** yKilayim 8:4: "breath and soul and spirit" come from God, not from the father. In the faith's voice: the father gives bone; the Holy One gives the soul, and the soul was a daughter's. This is a medieval-sounding argument that needs no modern vocabulary. (Medieval Provençal access to the Yerushalmi is *unverified*; the "three partners" also appears in bNiddah 31a, which they certainly had.)

What to exclude:
- **No animals as figures for the daughters.** The hyena that changes sex was a Christian weapon against both gender-variant Christians and Jews. The Physiologus likens it to men who enter church as men and leave "in the habits of women"; the Latin Physiologus B says Jews are "like it" (ch. 3; Ep. Barnabas 10.6–7; Chrysostom compared Jews to hyenas, n. 128). Neis calls this "a transgender animal" used "to castigate genderqueer Christians while simultaneously standing for false-dealing Jews". A Jewish trans-feminine sect with hyena imagery would reproduce that slur exactly. Keep the hyena, the raven birth and the camel calf out of loc as images of the daughters. Rabbi Simon's camel may be cited only as a rule of argument, in a learned character's mouth.
- **No "creature of her own".** The androginos and *koy* frame is a third-thing frame. It clashes with "daughters" and with the mod's rule against romanticised third-gender framing (Bhatt). **Design use:** a moderate rabbi *offers* the sect the androginos status as a compromise ("let her be a creature of her own, and the sages could not decide"). The sect *refuses* it: "We are not a creature of our own. We were turned, as Dinah was." That dramatises the rule rather than breaking it.
- **No demons.** Lilith appears in the bowls as a being of shifting sex who seduces women. Neis reads her generously, but tying trans women to demons in a Jewish setting would be harmful whichever way it is read. Keep Lilith out.

## 4. How Neis positions her work against trans and queer readings

- **She uses trans studies as a lens, not a verdict.** Trans theory helps "suspend assumptions about how bodily configurations inevitably map onto particular classifications" (Intro, "Multiplicity"; citing Stryker and Enke). She coins "sexgender" to avoid treating sex as prior to culture (ch. 1, n. 6; ch. 5).
- **She refuses analogy and advocacy.** "I do not suggest an analogy between these contemporary stakes and ancient concerns. Nor do I advocate ancient Jewish answers" (Intro, end). She also attacks Haraway's "Judeo-Christian" shorthand as a Christian supersession of Judaism (Intro, "Likeness"). **Lesson for the mod:** the sect is not "the Jewish version" of a universal trans religion; it must be built from Jewish argument.
- **She calls Rabbi Yoḥanan nonbinary, carefully.** "So, am I arguing that Yoḥanan is nonbinary? Naturally, my answer is yes and no" (ch. 5). It is "not a biographical claim". The point is that storytellers shaped a figure "who doesn't quite fall on either side of a sexgender binary". She criticises her own earlier queer reading for keeping Yoḥanan safely cis.
- **She concedes the hierarchy.** She admits a binary "plays a large part" in rabbinic obligation and is "deeply hierarchical" (ch. 5). She cites Fonrobert's "functional gender duality" and Strassfeld on the costs of illegibility. She warns against valorising the androginos's marginality.
- **On the *saris*.** Via Sarra Lev: the rabbis "treat him as a man, but see her as a woman" (ch. 5). tYevamot 10:6 lists a *saris*'s signs: no beard, soft flesh, and a voice "not distinguishable as a man's or woman's", which Lev and Neis read as applying to the whole person (n. 85).
- **Disagreement with Strassfeld.** Neis "differs with Strassfeld who sees both koy and androginus people as exemplary hybrids" (ch. 5, n. 98). She did not engage his 2022 book, which she calls *Transgender Talmud* (its title is *Trans Talmud*).

- The proposal should add **bShabbat 53b** (the nursing widower) to Berakhot 60a and Kalonymus as a precedent the sect would cite. It is the only Talmudic case in this material of a male body given a woman's bodily function by divine act, and the Amoraim argue openly whether it honours him.

## 6. Design implications

**Reaction (Rabbinic and Karaite toward Ha-Nehpakhot):**
- Opinion modifier, Rabbinic courts: *"What came forth male is male"* (generation principle; the majority view). Larger penalty than toward Bædling, smaller than toward a knife-faith, since no forbidden act was done.
- Karaite variant: Karaites reject the Oral Torah, so neither Rabbi Simon nor the generation principle binds them. They would argue from Gen 1:27 alone. *Inference; no Karaite source.*

**Event chain: "The Sages of Arles Dispute the Turning"** (*kahal* event, after 1322, fires once a Turning is known):
- Setup loc (narrator): "The *kahal* has heard that a son of the community now prays among the women and answers to a daughter's name. The learned gather."
- Option A, orthodox: "That which emerges from the male is male. The law follows the sages." → opinion penalty with the sect; the daughter may leave the *kahal*.
- Option B, the sect's voice: "Rabbi Simon taught that the camel is a camel, whatever bore it. And for the widower the orders of creation were changed." → the sect gains piety/standing; rabbinic opinion penalty.
- Option C, moderate: "Let her be a creature of her own, as Rabbi Yose said of the androginos." → follow-up where the daughter refuses: "I am not a creature of my own. I was turned, as Dinah was." Choosing to honour the refusal is the sect's path.

**Tenet or doctrine loc (faith voice only):**
- *The Third Partner*: "Three make every child: the father, the mother and the Holy One. Bone from the father, blood from the mother, and from the Holy One the breath and the soul. The soul was a daughter's from the first."
- *The Changed Order*: "For the widower who had no bread for a nurse, the orders of creation were changed, and he nursed his child. What was done for him may be done for her."

**Narrator rules:**
- Never: "the Talmud recognised that people could change sex", or "the rabbis were nonbinary". Say instead that the sect *reads* these passages so, and that the rabbis' majority held the opposite.
- No hyena, raven, camel or mule as an image of a daughter. No Lilith.
- No "creature of her own" as the sect's own self-description; only as an outside offer that she refuses.

**Open questions:**
- Did fourteenth-century Provençal Jews read the Yerushalmi (yShabbat 1:3, yKilayim 8:4)? If not, use bNiddah 31a for the three partners and drop yShabbat.
- Check bShabbat 53b's wording and attributions directly (Neis gives only a footnote summary).
