## 0. Framing warning (Bhatt)

- **Cabezón** translates *paṇḍaka* as "queer". He argues that Buddhist authors "implicitly accepted the existence of a third gender, the queer gender" (ch. 6, "Four (and More) Possibilities"; nn. 873–875). Both are his modern choices.
- **Gyatso** ends with a speculative "law of the non-excluded middle" (pp. 103–104, 114–115). It glosses the paṇḍaka as "the compassionate bodhisattva". She says this reading was "never actually made in historical Buddhist writings at all" (p. 104).
- **Both authors also give the correction.**
  - Gyatso: the positive Tibetan pictures of the third sex "were not really about actual third sex people" (pp. 104–105).
  - Cabezón: the typologies were made "not to celebrate human sexual diversity but to exclude" (ch. 6, end of "Four (and More) Possibilities"). The tantric "neuter" is metaphor and never became "social tolerance" (ch. 7, "The Neuter, Mahāyāna Antinomianism, and Tantra").
- **Rule for the mod.** A Buddhist court never calls a daughter a sacred third thing. When Buddhist law calls her "neither man nor woman", it does so in order to exclude her. That is Bhatt's dumping-ground, seen from inside the Vinaya.

## 1. The argument in brief

### Gyatso

- **Women are defined against a third excluded class.** The Vinaya distinguishes women from monks, and also from "persons who cannot receive ordination under any circumstances" on grounds of sex (p. 92).
- **The terms.**
  - The early lists bar the hermaphrodite (*ubhatovyañjanaka*), the *paṇḍaka* and sometimes the *ṣaṇḍha* (p. 94).
  - There are male and female paṇḍakas (p. 94, n. 11). The Āyurvedic texts already count three sexes at birth (pp. 93–95).
- **The fivefold list** (p. 96, n. 16):
  1. the congenital;
  2. the half-monthly changer;
  3. "one who comes out when embraced", or the fellator;
  4. the voyeur ("jealous");
  5. the one made a paṇḍaka "by virtue of adventitious conditions such as illness or surgery". This is the Pāli *opakkamika* and Yaśomitra's *lūna*, "mutilated".
  - The term paṇḍaka is not a code word for homosexuality (p. 97).
- **Exclusion goes beyond ordination** (p. 98). A paṇḍaka:
  - may not act as preceptor;
  - may not give alms to monks;
  - may not be taught by a bodhisattva (Lotus Sūtra);
  - "cannot understand the Dharma" (*Milindapañha*).
- **A contradiction at the root** (p. 99). Abhidharma says the paṇḍaka has too little will to sin to take a vow, and also too much unstable desire.
- **Tibetan revaluation, all in metaphor** (pp. 100–103):
  - the *ma ning* as the Middle Way;
  - the "bodhisattva pulse" in the *rGyud bzhi* (probably codified in the 12th century);
  - the deity mGon-po Ma-ning.
  - The pulse doctrine allows that "women can have male pulses, men can have female pulses" (p. 105).
  - The 16th-century Zur-mkhar-ba separates "mind-continuum", which can change, from sex, which cannot (p. 106). This is **later evidence**.
- **The paṇḍaka is "the category of the uncategorizable"** (p. 107). It is an abomination to legalists "because the very project of the Vinaya depends on exact definition" (p. 108).
- **Spontaneous sex change is legally routine** (pp. 110–111):
  - A monk wakes to find "his beard and male shape gone" (*Samantapāsādikā* 1:273).
  - A nun walking alone turns male and so is no longer in breach.
  - Only a third change forfeits monastic status (Guṇaprabha, *Vinaya-sūtra* 617). So one or two changes are "a relatively innocuous event" (p. 111).
- **Buddhaghosa's asymmetry** (p. 111, n. 67):
  - Male genitals are "superior" and more stable.
  - It takes strong bad karma for a man's organs to disappear, and only weak good karma for female organs to appear.
  - For both sexes, losing one's original genitals is the fruit of bad deeds, and growing the other set is the fruit of merit.
- **The female paṇḍaka list doubles as men's insults to women** (pp. 112–113). Women are refused ordination if they:
  - are without organs or have defective ones;
  - do not bleed, or bleed constantly;
  - are "manlike";
  - and so on (Cullavagga 10.17.1).
  - The Pāli Vinaya repeats the same list as abuse men heap on any woman (Suttavibhaṅga Saṅghādisesa 3.3.1).
- **Thesis.** Tibetan *za ma* means both eunuch and woman (pp. 113–114). The third sex is "a second second sex", a scapegoat that let "normal" women into the order (p. 114).

### Cabezón

- **Control is the purpose.** The lists decide who may be ordained, who counts as a lay Buddhist and whom monks may associate with. Their "goal is social marginalization and exclusion" (ch. 7, "Control").
- **Lay status is denied too.** The *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī* says paṇḍakas and ṣaṇḍhas "should not be called upāsakas" even if they keep the precepts (ch. 7, "Control"; n. 958).
- **The Tibetan *Verse Summary of the Vinaya*** bars from ordination (ch. 7, "Control"):
  - male and female paṇḍakas;
  - "someone who has changed sexes three [or more] times";
  - the ṣaṇḍha (eunuch);
  - magically created beings, nāgas and spirits.
- **A pan-Indian contempt** (ch. 7, "Sexual Deviance and Social Marginalization"):
  - "Paṇḍakas are the lowest of men" (*Vinayavastu*, n. 969).
  - A paṇḍaka's birth "signals the death of the king" (*Dvadaśalocana Sūtra*, n. 976).
  - A Jain heckler in the *Divyāvadāna*: "neither a man nor a woman … neither a beard nor breasts" (n. 977).
  - Rebirth as a eunuch or a paṇḍaka is a karmic punishment, for instance for having castrated another man (n. 980, Beyer's translation of an image-making text).
- **Buddhaghosa's fivefold list.** The two with anatomical lack are never ordained (ch. 7, "The Pāli Buddhist Lists"):
  - the congenital neuter;
  - the *opakkamika*, "a eunuch by castration". This means testicles removed. A man whose penis alone is cut off does not fall in the category.
  - Some commentators allow a castrate to become a novice only "so long as 'the nature of a eunuch' does not arise". Others hold that all eunuchs eventually "transform" and must be refused.
- **The Mūlasarvāstivāda rule for a monk castrated after ordination** (ch. 7 opening, and "The Sanskrit Paṇḍaka Lists"; nn. 942, 1083–1084):
  - He keeps his status "so long as he shows no sign of conversion".
  - "Conversion" means coming to desire to be penetrated. He is then expelled.
  - Kalyāṇamitra records a dissent that any castrate is already "the equivalent of laypersons".
- **Some later Indian commentators treat the heterosexual eunuch as "less abnormal".** Cabezón asks whether "eunuchs served an important function in the royal court" (ch. 7, "Ṣaṇḍhas and Their Relationship to Paṇḍakas"; n. 1053).
- **Court eunuchs before Islam.** Cabezón collects Nāṭyaśāstra 34 (the *varṣadharas*), the Pāli Jātaka (4: 502, "guardians of the harem") and the *Dharmaskandha Sūtra*. They show the court eunuch "pre-dates the arrival of Islam by centuries" (n. 858). The *Dharmaskandha* passage makes ṣaṇḍhas and paṇḍakas "protect the ladies' harem" and says most "are reborn in hell" (ch. 7, n. 1042).
- **The five female paṇḍakas** (Mūlasarvāstivāda) are:
  1. one who menstruates constantly;
  2. **one who does not menstruate**;
  3. one with conjoined anus and vagina;
  4. one with a "mere organ";
  5. one with a diseased organ (in some lists the hermaphrodite).
  - Cabezón's conclusion: "to be a candidate for ordination as a nun, a woman must be fertile" (ch. 7, "The Five Female Paṇḍakas"; n. 1112).
  - The Pāli tradition has the same items, but does not group them under "female paṇḍaka".
- **Sex change in the commentaries.**
  - Saṅghabhadra's Chinese *Samantapāsādikā*: a man asleep loses "beard and the jaw". He keeps ordination, seniority and spiritual guide, "although she must be escorted to a nunnery" (n. 703).
  - The Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinaya has "many cases" of monks and nuns changing sex, and they keep ordination and seniority. Many of these cases come up in the middle of an offence (n. 703).
  - Cabezón: "I have not found any cases in which the Vinaya's monks or nuns spontaneously turn into persons of the third sex" (n. 703).
- **The causes given for male-to-female change** are all moral:
  - "strong non-virtuous karma" (Buddhaghosa, *Atthasālinī* 322; "the male marks are superior, and the female inferior", ch. 6 "Male and Female Faculties in the Pāli Tradition", n. 917);
  - a monk who called the saṅgha "a pack of women" and was instantly made a woman (*Abhidharmakośabhāṣya* 4.55, n. 878);
  - Soreyya, who desired the monk Mahā Kaccāyana and became a woman, bore two children, and changed back when forgiven (*Dhammapada Commentary*, ch. 5, n. 704);
  - rebirth "as a woman for five hundred lives" for sexual misconduct (*Pañcaśikṣānuśaṃsa Sūtra*, ch. 1, n. 138).
  - In the *Mahānāradakassapa Jātaka*, an adulterer is reborn as castrated animals and paṇḍakas, then as a woman (ch. 1, n. 138).
  - In the same Abhidharmakośa passage, a paṇḍaka who **saved bulls from castration** "regained his manhood" (n. 878).
- **Sex faculties are karmic matter.** Vasubandhu and Buddhaghosa make the *strī-* and *puruṣa-indriya* subtle matter, laid down by karma at conception, and the cause of genitals, gait, voice and desire (ch. 6, "Ābhidharmika Constructions"). Neither posits a third faculty. That was "not an option for Buddhist Abhidharma theorists", unlike the Jains (same section).
- **Brahmanical comparanda Cabezón supplies.**
  - Manu forbids a king to strike a queer man. A brahman's penance for killing one is that for killing an animal (ch. 7, "Sexual Deviance"; n. 968).
  - The play *Ubhayābhisārikā* has a third-sex courtesan whose sight is "inauspicious" (n. 967).
  - Nārada's fourteen impotent husbands include men impotent "owing to the guru's curse" and "the wrath of a god" (ch. 7, "Queerness in the Nārada Smṛti").
  - In Mahābhārata 13.12, King Bhaṅgāśvana is turned into a woman and then refuses to change back (n. 892).
- **Modern survivals are later evidence.**
  - Ordination still asks every candidate whether they are a paṇḍaka (ch. 7, "Control").
  - The modern categories kathoey, nachchi, achauk and ma-ning are "genealogically related" to paṇḍaka, but not in a one-to-one way (Epilogue).

## 2. What matters for the design

1. **Buddhist law has two different readings of a daughter, and the gate decides which applies.**
   - (a) **A spontaneous change of sex** is an administrative transfer to the women's side. It is caused by karma and is a fall, but it does not make her a third thing. This applies until a third change (Gyatso pp. 110–111; Cabezón n. 703).
   - (b) **A man made sexless by the knife** is the *opakkamika / āpat / lūna* paṇḍaka. He is unordainable, possibly not even a lay Buddhist, and sits on the same list as "the lowest of men" (Gyatso p. 96 n. 16; Cabezón ch. 7).
   - The Dorleyite daughter of the knife falls under (b), not (a).
2. **Infertility is a bar of its own.**
   - Every daughter is `can_have_children = no` (H40).
   - Under the Mūlasarvāstivāda list, a woman who does not menstruate is a female paṇḍaka. The Pāli Vinaya also refuses her ordination.
   - So even where a Buddhist court accepts a daughter as a woman under (a), she cannot become a nun.
   - This is Bhatt's point about barren women exactly ("Girls who do not menstruate may be considered hijra"), and Gyatso's "second second sex" (p. 114).
3. **Karmic causes for man-to-woman are standard in South Asian sources, not only in Chinese.** These are Buddhaghosa, Vasubandhu, the *Dhammapada Commentary* and the *Pañcaśikṣānuśaṃsa*. The vanilla-faith reading behind `tm_eastern_daughter_opinion` is therefore well grounded, but only for Buddhists and only for gate (a).
4. **In-life change and rebirth are different things.**
   - Vin III.35, Soreyya and Abhidharmakośa 4.55 are changes **within one life**: the monk "becomes" a woman.
   - The *Pañcaśikṣānuśaṃsa* and the Jātaka are about **rebirth** as a woman.
   - The current loc name says "reborn". For a living daughter, a Buddhist would more precisely say she has *become* a woman.
5. **The knife carries its own karma.** Castrating another is a cause of rebirth as a eunuch (n. 980). Saving bulls from castration restores manhood (n. 878). A Buddhist observer therefore condemns the Dorleyite rite twice: the daughter is a made paṇḍaka, and the officiant has made karma.
6. **Court eunuchs are period-true in Indic courts before 1206** (Cabezón n. 858). Hindu and Buddhist kings in 867 can have harem guards, though not a Byzantine-style eunuch officialdom.
7. **For the Tibetan (Vajrayāna) courts:**
   - The 12th-century *rGyud bzhi* "bodhisattva pulse" and the *ma ning* as the Middle Way are in the game window, but they are medical and tantric metaphor (Gyatso pp. 100–105).
   - Tantric texts still deny empowerment "to … paṇḍakas" (Cabezón ch. 7, *Vajraguhya*, n. 1133).
   - Usable only as a learned monk's backhanded line, never as acceptance.
8. **The Theravāda splinter in the deferred Southeast Asian expansion** (`comparanda_reading_list.md` §2) gets firmer law from these sources:
   - the transfer keeps "the same teacher, the same ordination, the same rainy seasons";
   - the change is "relatively innocuous" until the third time;
   - the Vinaya never turns a changed monk into a paṇḍaka (Cabezón n. 703).
   - **Doctrinal hinge.** The infertility bar (the female paṇḍaka who does not menstruate) is the rule such a splinter would have to break. That is a better schism point than "a rite of asking".

## 4. Answer: does `tm_eastern_daughter_opinion` fit South Asian Buddhist law?

- **Partly.** "A man become a woman as karmic fruit" is the correct Buddhist reading for the daughters of seiðr, of the embryo and of the turning, if a Buddhist court reads them as spontaneous changes.
- **It is the wrong reading for the daughter of the knife**, whom every Indic law (Buddhist, Jain and Brahmanical) reads as a castrated man.
- **The single −10 also blurs three different religions** (see `sea_zwilling_sweet_third_sex.md`):
  - Buddhists: karmic fall, and unordainable because she is infertile;
  - Brahmanical: *klība*, disinherited and inauspicious;
  - Jains: a desire-category (*veda*), with a Śvetāmbara door left open.
- **Recommendation: split by gate, then by religion.**
  1. Knife (all eastern_syncretic_recipient): new `tm_eastern_unmanned_opinion` (−15), loc "Unmanned by the Knife". Buddhist *opakkamika*, Jain *kṛtrimaklība*, Brahmanical *ṣaṇḍha*: all three agree.
  2. Other gates: keep `tm_eastern_daughter_opinion`, but lower it to −5 and rename the loc "A Man Become a Woman". The Vinaya treats a single change as "relatively innocuous" (Gyatso p. 111).
  3. Religion-specific weight goes in events, not in the triggered opinion. The parameter `eastern_syncretic_recipient` covers `buddhism_religion`, `hinduism_religion` and `jainism_religion` alike (vanilla `special_doctrine_is_eastern_faith`, checked in 00_buddhism/00_hinduism/00_jainism). Events can check `religion = religion:hinduism_religion` and so on. Use this for the Hindu disinheritance and the Jain ordination door in the Zwilling and Sweet note.

## 5. Design implications

- **Event "The Questions at the Ordination"** (Buddhist court, a daughter of any gate asks to enter a nunnery). The preceptor asks the ritual questions "Are you a paṇḍaka?" and "Do you bleed?"
  - (a) She is refused. The court may support her as an unordained devotee (piety and opinion trade-off).
  - (b) A patron forces the issue: −prestige with the saṅgha, +opinion from the daughter.
  - (c) For a faith with Ha-Nehpakhot or Yutai neighbours, a cross-faith line in her own voice: "Your Vinaya keeps the monk who woke a woman. Keep me."
  - Narrator never mocks the questions. Gyatso (pp. 112–113) shows the same list served as men's abuse of women.
- **Reaction loc lines** (attribute to in-world speakers; ≤ one sentence each):
  - Theravāda elder: "The male mark is the higher and the female the lower; a strong fault has brought you down." (after *Atthasālinī* 322)
  - Mūlasarvāstivāda abbot, to a daughter of the knife: "The Vinaya does not ordain the unmanned, nor keep them once they turn."
  - Tibetan physician (backhanded): "Your pulse is the steady one we call the bodhisattva's; that is a metaphor, not a welcome." (after *rGyud bzhi*, Gyatso p. 101)
  - Hostile courtier: "Neither beard nor breasts." (*Divyāvadāna*, Cabezón n. 977)
- **Karma of the officiant.** A Buddhist ruler who learns of a Dorleyite cutting adds a hidden "made a eunuch" sin flag to the officiant (rebirth-as-eunuch karma, Cabezón n. 980). A Buddhist daughter-sympathiser's counter-option cites the paṇḍaka who saved bulls from the knife (n. 878), but the mod should not write it as an endorsement of the gate.
- **Three-changes rule.** Turning or seiðr daughters whom a Buddhist court reads as changed keep any monastic seniority they had. A modifier `tm_vinaya_transfer_modifier` ("Same Teacher, Same Ordination") applies only if she held Buddhist clergy rank before. This is rare, but it is the cleanest case of law treating her as a woman.
- **Deferred Southeast Asian Theravāda splinter.** Possible tenet "The Transfer Is Not a Fall": it refuses Buddhaghosa's karmic gloss and the menstrual bar. Seat Anurādhapura (b_anuradhapura, before 1017) or Pagan (c_pagan). Doctrinal citations in the faith's own voice: Vin III.35, Saṅghabhadra's sleeping monk, the three-change limit. It has no historical community and must be marked as reconstruction.
- **Do not** build any Tibetan "ma ning deity" or "bodhisattva pulse" content as positive recognition of daughters. Gyatso pp. 104–105 and Bhatt both rule it out.
