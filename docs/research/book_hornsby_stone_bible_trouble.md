## The argument in brief

- **The volume.** Queer reading as a practice that "troubles" both gender categories and the boundaries of biblical scholarship. Most essays read the Bible against modern theory or modern lives (film, prison, migration, the DSM). Only Burke and parts of Townsley and Twomey work with ancient or medieval material that the mod can use directly.
- **Burke.** For Greek-speaking readers of Acts, *eunouchos* meant a castrated man: Burke found no Greek text from the 5th c. BCE to the 2nd c. CE where it clearly meant an uncastrated official (p. 178). The audience knew two kinds of eunuch: the court eunuch, castrated as a boy and a slave, and the *galli* of Cybele, whose "voluntary self-castration was described as producing a slave of Cybele" (p. 180). Eunuchs were "variously gendered in ancient discourses as 'effeminate males,' 'half-males,' 'girls,' hybrids of male and female, and neither male nor female" (p. 181). Those castrated after puberty raised "the troubling possibility that one could be simultaneously identified as a man and as a eunuch" (p. 181). In Acts 8 the eunuch's baptism is the hinge of the book: once he is baptised, no one can be refused. Burke notes that the Greek of 8:38 lets a reader imagine "a reversal of subject and object", so that "the baptism represents Philip's conversion as much as it does the eunuch's" (p. 184; OCR drops the apostrophes). Burke explicitly does not call eunuchs ancient trans or gay people (p. 182).
- **Villalobos.** A Mexican gay theologian reads Acts 8 with Anzaldúa's borderlands and his own life. Useful facts are second-hand: Deut. 23:2 as the "clobber text" against eunuchs (p. 205); the Talmud's description of eunuchs as beardless and smooth-skinned (b. Yevamot 80b, p. 200); Lucian's satire *The Eunuch*, quoted in his note 10 (p. 204). The chapter's frame of "Israel's aggression" toward the eunuch's body (p. 200) and of Jerusalem and the Temple having "failed" (p. 201) is a Christian supersessionist reading. It also contains a first-hand account of an attack on a gay man (p. 210). Neither is usable.
- **Marchal.** He reads Antoinette Clark Wire's reconstruction of the Corinthian women prophets beside trans activism (Bornstein, Butler, Stone). Paul tells the community that belonging means voluntary suffering and descent. The women, low in status before they joined, experienced joining as a rise: they felt "called out of lowness, not into it" (Wire, quoted p. 229). The medical gatekeepers of Marchal's day likewise demanded proof of suffering before granting the status. Marchal's conclusion: "there is no necessary connection between attaining the status of their identity and experiencing specific forms of suffering" (p. 239), and "One is not moving down in order to go up; one is moving into the identity and the community" (p. 240). He also notes that Paul's baptism is a change of clothes (Gal. 3:27, "put on Christ"; p. 241).
- **Townsley.** In 1 Cor. 11:2–16 Paul objects to the hair and head-covering of the men as well as the women who pray and prophesy. Townsley reads the men as "gender-scrambling" and connects them to Greek festival cross-dressing for Heracles and Dionysus, where worshippers would "show themselves off as ambisexed beings, striving to transcend gender categories" (Frontisi-Ducroux and Lissarrague, quoted p. 257). The dominant voice is the Stoic Musonius Rufus, for whom a man must avoid being seen "to appear as women, and to be seen as womanish" (p. 257). Her summary: men "made not-men and free through the taking-on of the female" (p. 258).
- **Twomey.** Patriarchal readers of the Pastorals keep putting men in women's places. Augustine on Ps. 132: "the whole Church therefore is one widow, whether in men or in women" (p. 288). Chrysostom on 2 Tim. 3:6 makes "silly woman" a status anyone can fall into: "He who is easily deceived is a silly woman, and far removed from being a man" (p. 289). Anselm's prayer makes Paul a mother in labour (p. 290). Estius, citing Prudentius, applies the rule on women's jewellery (1 Tim. 2:9) to "men who have a fondness for bodily ornament" (p. 292).
- **Epstein, citing Moore (2000).** Celibate Church Fathers who wrote on the Song of Songs (Origen, Bernard of Clairvaux and others) each "internalizes a feminine persona so completely that he speaks fluently in her voice"; Moore calls them "Christian cross-dressers" (p. 99).
- **Stone.** The "houses" of the New York balls, each ruled by a "mother", as chosen kin for people cut off from birth families (p. 77). A modern community, used here only as a comparison.

## Cautions

- **No Esther, Purim or Deut. 22:5 chapter.** Do not cite this book for Ha-Nehpakhot's Purim or for cross-dressing law.
- **Supersessionism.** Villalobos (pp. 199–201) and, more mildly, Burke's Acts frame (Jew/Gentile boundaries "converted" by the Church) read Judaism as the thing Christianity outgrew. Never use either frame in any narrator voice, and never in Ha-Nehpakhot text. In the Adelphai's own voice, Acts 8 may stand as their scripture without any claim about Jews.
- **Modern lives.** Villalobos's autobiographical section includes violence against a gay man in his village, with self-harm imagery (pp. 209–211). Marchal discusses modern violence and suicide among queer and trans youth (pp. 234–235). Nothing from these passages goes into the mod.
- **Trans men out of scope.** Guest's Jael chapter and its butch/FTM framing are not used.
- **"Third gender."** Burke's "neither male nor female" is an ancient *label*, not a category the faiths accept. Use it only in hostile mouths.
- **Modern houses.** Stone's ball "houses" and their "mothers" are a living community. Do not present them as a faith or model a faith on them by name. Badenya's mothers already rest on West African sources.
- **Dates.** Bernard of Clairvaux, Anselm, Estius and Doddridge are all after 867; only Origen, Chrysostom, Augustine, Lucian, Xenophon and Musonius are early enough. Augustine is a Latin author and little read in Byzantium; keep him for Latin settings.
