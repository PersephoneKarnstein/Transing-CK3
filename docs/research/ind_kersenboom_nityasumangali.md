# Kersenboom-Story, *Nityasumaṅgalī: Devadasi Tradition in South India* (1987): design reading

Source: Saskia C. Kersenboom-Story, *Nityasumaṅgalī: Devadasi Tradition in South India* (Delhi: Motilal Banarsidass, 1987), xxi + 226 pp. Revised from the author's Utrecht Ph.D. thesis (1984).
Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo (OCR of a scan; some lines are garbled). Page numbers are the book's.

**What was read.** In full: the Preface and Introduction with its notes (pp. xv–xxi); ch. I part A, "Diachronic modifications" (pp. 1–38: Caṅkam, Pallava–Pāṇḍya, Chola and Vijayanagara periods); ch. I's conclusion to part A (pp. 47–49); ch. III, "Rites of Passage of the Devadasis of Tamilnadu", with all its notes (pp. 179–202); the Conclusion (pp. 203–207). In part: ch. I notes 78–92 (pp. 75–76) on Āṇṭāḷ's date and the Mārkaḻi songs; ch. II on the daily rising song and the Kanchipuram festival (pp. 61, 117, 131–32); the bibliography's entry for the Kāmikāgama commentary (p. 211). Skipped: ch. I part A.4 (Tanjore courts, pp. 38–47) and part B (village, Āgamic and court "foci", pp. 49–68); almost all of ch. II (temple ritual, pp. 85–178).

**Relation to existing notes.** `ind_orr_temple_women.md` (inscriptions, 850–1300) and `ind_goodall_rudraganikas.md` (Śaiva Sanskrit prescriptive texts) are the period evidence. `ind_merkle_tying_knot.md` already warned off the modern "always married" vocabulary. Kersenboom is the source of that vocabulary: she built the "ever-auspicious woman" thesis from fieldwork in 1977–83. This note records where her claims come from and what dates they can carry. It does not repeat Orr's terms or Goodall's staff rite except where Kersenboom adds to them.

## 0. Framing warning

- **The book's evidence for the rites is twentieth-century.** Fieldwork was 1977–78, 1980–81 and 1982–83 (p. xv). Ch. III "is based mainly on the accounts of devadasis who were attached to different temples in Tamilnadu until 1947" (p. xix). The main informant was dedicated at Tiruttani in 1934 (p. 65).
- **The title thesis began as a remark.** It came from one answer to a question asked in 1977 (p. xv and n. 2, p. xx): a devadāsī may wear toe-rings because she is "married to an immortal, divine husband".
- **Method.** Kersenboom says her work is "not the study of the fact of the devadasi tradition, but of its meaning" (p. xv). She uses semiotics and Huizinga's play theory, and her chapter on history is "neither historical nor sociological" by her own description (p. 2).
- **Living people.** The informants and the families named in ch. III are a living, stigmatised community (Isai Vellalar and related). Use the book for structure only. Never name them in loc and never present their rites as the faith.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **The thesis.** A devadāsī is a *nitya-sumaṅgalī*, an "ever-auspicious woman": her auspiciousness never lapses, because her husband is a god who cannot die (Conclusion, p. 204). She is the human form of the goddess's power, ritually merged with it (pp. 197, 204).
- **Her job is protection, not sex.** Her main task is waving the pot-lamp (*kumbhadīpa*) to remove the evil eye from the god after processions (pp. xix, 205). Kersenboom calls the labels "sacred prostitutes" and "goddesses of fertility" "vastly exaggerated" (p. 205).
- **A long ancestry.** She traces the role to the female bards of Caṅkam poetry (the *viṟali* and *pāṭiṉi*, pp. 11, 15–16), through the dancer Mātavi of the *Cilappatikāram* (c. 450), to the temple women of the Chola inscriptions and the dancers of the Tanjore courts (pp. 16–38).
- **Not a caste.** Devadāsī status was a "structural function" a girl entered by initiation, not birth (p. 184).

## 2. What matters for the design

### 2.1 The historical chapters: what is dated, and what is not (pp. 3–38)

| Period | What Kersenboom actually cites | Page |
|---|---|---|
| Caṅkam (c. 100 BCE–300 CE) | Female bards (*viṟali*, *pāṭiṉi*) sing the king's fame and are paid in gold lotuses and garlands. Captive women (*koṇṭimakaḷir*) wash the shrine post and light its lamps. | 11–12 |
| Late classical (c. 450) | Mātavi's debut: seven years' training; the king gives her gold and a garland. | 16, 181 |
| Pallava–Pāṇḍya (550–850) | The female saints Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār (6th c.) and Āṇṭāḷ (dated "8th century" here, p. 22, but 9th century in n. 80, p. 75, quoting Zvelebil). Māṇikkavācakar (9th c.) describes girls of twelve or thirteen serving in a temple. | 22–23 |
| Chola (850–1279) | Hereditary temple posts; land grants for dancers (*nṛttabhōgam*, *patiyilār kāṇi*); the court favourite Paravai Naṅkaiyār, Rājendra I's *aṉukki*, gilds the Tiruvārūr shrine; a ranking of temple women; the title *talaikkōli*. | 26–29 |
| Vijayanagara (1336–1565) | Domingo Paes (c. 1510): every girl born to the temple women "belong to the temple" and they "live in the best streets". | 36 |

- **No marriage to the god in the period chapters.** The Chola section has no wedding, tāli or pottu. The nearest statement is that Āṇṭāḷ "'marries' lord Vishnu of Srirangam", with the scare quotes Kersenboom's own (p. 22).
- **Hereditary posts.** The descendants of actors, dancing girls and musicians "had the right to hold the same post" if they proved their skill (p. 26). Some people gave themselves or their relatives to a temple to repay debts, or "even as an act of devotion" (p. 26). This is the bonded side that Goodall stresses, and the faith must not build on it.
- **Ranks.** One inscription ranks the temple women as "those of the temple of the bull", "slaves of god" and *patiyilār* (p. 28). A twentieth-century informant called the *patiyilār* the highest class at Tiruvārūr (pp. 28, 183).
- **The staff title.** *Talaikkōli*, "she who holds the *talaikkōl* rod", "occurs rather frequently" in the inscriptions of the Tiruvārūr temple. It connects the dancers there with Mātavi, "whose initiation ceremony was performed by worship of the talaikkol" (p. 28).
- **The lamp-waver.** An order of Kulottuṅga I (1070–1120) makes worshippers stand behind the *māṇikkam*, the woman waving the sacred light (p. 28).
- **The Mārkaḻi song.** Inscriptions endow the singing of the *Tiruvempāvai* (p. 29). The waking song *Tiruppaḷḷiyeḻucci* "is now sung in the month of Markali" by male singers (*ōtuvārs*). Devadāsīs sang it until 1947 (p. 23; witnessed by the author on 29 December 1982, n. 87, p. 76).

### 2.2 Dedication: what the rites were, and when (pp. 179–192)

**A. The Āgamic marriage** (pp. 186–87, 196–97). The source is a commentary on the *Kāmikāgama* by Sadyojātaśivācārya. Kersenboom calls him "medieval" (p. 61) but gives no date. The bibliography lists the text as "read out by Sri Muttaiya Bhattar, Tiruccentur, 1978" (p. 211). It is an oral rendering of an unedited commentary, and some passages were "not quite clear" to her (n. 5, p. 198). What it prescribes:

- **Who.** Three classes: *rudragaṇikā*, *rudrakaṉṉikai* and *rudradāsī*. All are said to arise from aspects of the goddess (p. 180).
- **The rite.** Śiva-initiation (*dīkṣā*) in the shrine. The teacher gives the five-syllable mantra and ties the golden *liṅga* sign onto the tāli. Facing north in the hall, the woman has the worshipped *pottu* tied round her neck (p. 186).
- **The teacher is not the husband.** She "should not consider the acarya who has tied the bottu in this way as her own husband" (p. 186).
- **Ages.** Before eight for the *rudrakaṉṉikai*; at nine for the others, and never after (pp. 186–87).
- **Leaving the god.** If a *rudrakaṉṉikai* wants a human lover she removes Śiva's tāli as a widow would. She keeps her auspiciousness (pp. 187, 197).
- **Too many is dangerous.** Tying the pottu "because of lust", or tying too many, brings "destruction of the king, famine and death" (pp. 186–87, 204).

**B. The twentieth-century informant's rite** (Tiruttani, early 1930s; pp. 187–89):

- **Application.** At sixteen she applies to the local king, countersigned by ten priests and ten devadāsīs. The wait is six months.
- **The bells.** A worship of the ankle-bells (*gajjai pūjā*) ends her training.
- **The wedding.** Elderly women bring the god's sword-trident (*kaṭṭāri*) from the temple. A full wedding is held at home, and an elderly devadāsī (her grandmother's sister) ties the tāli.
- **At the temple.** She dances; she is branded with the trident; the priest gives her the pot-lamp; she receives a silk head-cloth and is taken home with the temple band.
- **After a month** she chooses a patron or a steady "husband" (p. 189).

**C. A dissent from inside.** A second informant, born 1896 and dedicated in Mysore State, said "no one in her tradition ever received a tali or toe-rings". In her words: "Our status is our gajjai" (n. 50, p. 201). Even in the twentieth century the "marriage" was not universal.

**D. Was there a staff, and an initiation?** Yes, and Kersenboom's evidence supports Goodall's step 5 from a different direction.

- **Mātavi's debut** (*Cilappatikāram*, c. 450; table, pp. 181–82):
  - the *talaikkōl* staff, representing Indra's son, is washed with holy water and the dancer garlands it;
  - it rides on the state elephant, and the king and his five councils walk around it;
  - she dances before the king, who gives her 1,008 gold coins and "a garland of green leaves".
  - There is no wedding in this account.
- **Her reading of the objects.** Kersenboom reads the staff, the sword and the trident as "synonyms of the goddess". She concludes that every *nityasumaṅgalī* is "married" first to the goddess, then to the god (pp. 196–97). That is her interpretation, not something a source says.

**E. Her dating and Orr's.** Orr dates "married to the god" to the 17th century (Orr ch. 6 n. 11; see `ind_orr_temple_women.md` §2.7). Kersenboom never dates it. Her evidence is:

1. the undated commentary, read aloud in 1978;
2. informants dedicated c. 1910–47;
3. colonial ethnography of the 19th–20th centuries (the Basavi petition, p. 194);
4. a 1975 magazine interview with a Yellamma *jogti* (p. 195);
5. a structural parallel to Mātavi, whose rite has no wedding.

- **The disagreement is one of method, not evidence.** Kersenboom reads the meaning backwards from living practice; Orr dates the words.
- **Nothing in Kersenboom places the marriage in 867–1453.** Goodall puts the first textual marriage in a 16th-century witness (`ind_goodall_rudraganikas.md` §2.6).
- **The tāli itself is late.** Dehejia (pp. 144–45) reports that the tāli rite enters South Indian weddings only from the 11th century (`ind_dehejia_venkatesan_antal.md`).
- **Ruling:** keep Orr's rule. The narrator never calls the faith's daughters "brides of the god" or "ever-auspicious" as a period title.

### 2.3 Inheritance and adoption (pp. 26, 179, 184, 194–95, 200–01)

- **A hereditary right, not a caste.** Informants spoke of a *devadāsī murai*, a "hereditary professional right", and not of a devadāsī caste (p. 179). Whether a girl used the right depended on her parents, her looks and skill, her conduct, and the authorities' consent (p. 179).
- **Outsiders admitted, but late.** As the temples declined, "children of non-devadasi parentage were even accepted" to keep the posts in use (p. 184). This sits in the Tanjore section and is undated, probably 18th–19th century.
- **One adoption, twentieth century.** A Mysore informant was the "adopted daughter" of a palace dancer, herself the daughter of a wrestler (n. 50, p. 200).
- **No adoption before 1453 in Kersenboom.** She adds nothing to Orr's guess (Orr ch. 3 n. 28) that 13th-century temple women sometimes adopted.
- **Later ethnography** (Chatterjee 1945, from 19th–20th-c. reports; a 1975 magazine). Use as reaction data at most:
  - A Basavi, dedicated when a family has no son, "shares in the family property as though she were a son". If she has only a daughter, that daughter becomes a Basavi in turn (p. 194).
  - A Yellamma *jogti* "can be regarded as a boy" for inheritance and for performing her parents' funeral rites (p. 195).
  - These are daughters made heirs. They are not a gender crossing and are not to be read as one.

### 2.4 Funeral honours (p. 192): later, but usable as marked reconstruction

Two twentieth-century informants describe the funeral:

- The god observes pollution, and worship stops when she dies.
- Her pyre is lit with fire from the temple kitchen.
- The bier is set down for a moment at the temple gate.
- A garland taken from the god is laid on her body, with a new sari, sandal and consecrated food.
- She always gets the funeral of a woman whose husband is living.

### 2.5 Men dressing as women: twentieth-century ethnography only (pp. 193, 202)

- **The typology.** The most dangerous goddesses are handled "by a man dressed as a woman" or by a man "impersonating the goddess Kali" (p. 193).
- **Its sources.** Elmore's *Dravidian Gods* (1915/1925): a storyteller dresses as a woman so that the other gods will not recognise him later (n. 55, p. 202). And a 1975 magazine on Yellamma: "The boys are dressed like women, and treated as such" (n. 66, p. 202).
- **Ruling.** This is the *jōgappa* and related living practice (see `ind_merkle_tying_knot.md`). Do not use it. It is later evidence, it concerns living communities, and it frames the man as a tool for handling danger. That is the instrumental frame the faith refuses.

### 2.6 Kanchipuram in the book (pp. 131–32, 188)

- **The spring festival.** At Kanchipuram the Paṅkuni Uttiram festival recalls the burning of Kāma, while at Madurai the same festival is Mīnākṣī's wedding (pp. 131–32; modern ritual calendar).
- **A refused girl.** In the twentieth century a girl refused at Tiruttani because her mother had "gone astray" was accepted at "a temple in Kanchipuram; there the rules of purity were not so strictly observed" (p. 188). This is a modern anecdote. It could inspire a fictional line that Kanchi takes in those other temples turn away, but only if the narrator does not present it as medieval.

## 3. Corrections to the candidate

1. **"Ever-auspicious" and "bride of the god" have no in-period source in Kersenboom.** Both come from 1977–83 fieldwork and an undated commentary read aloud in 1978. Orr's later dating stands. Record the disagreement as above: Kersenboom gives no date; Orr dates the words to the 17th century; Goodall finds the first textual marriage in a 16th-century witness.
2. **Gate step 5 (staff and name) is strengthened.** Kersenboom confirms *talaikkōli* in the Chola inscriptions of Tiruvārūr and its link to Mātavi's staff rite (p. 28). The *Cilappatikāram* gives a **court** form of the staff rite, with the ruler honouring the staff and paying the dancer (pp. 181–82). Goodall gives the **temple** form (*Uttara-Kāmika* 73, 12th c.).
3. **Mārkaḻi singing is confirmed.** Chola inscriptions endow the *Tiruvempāvai* (p. 29). The waking song in Mārkaḻi was the temple women's until 1947 and passed to men afterwards (p. 23). The faith's dawn song is a temple women's duty that men later took over.
4. **Adoption.** Kersenboom has no medieval adoption. *Take as Daughter* stays a marked reconstruction (Orr's guess).
5. **Āṇṭāḷ's date.** Kersenboom's "8th century" (p. 22) is an outlier; her own note follows Zvelebil's 9th century. Use 9th century (c. 850 per Dehejia).

## 4. Design implications

- **Gate step 5 has two settings.** The narrator presents both as reconstruction.
  - *At the temple:* a teacher touches her head with the staff and gives her a name, for a fee (Goodall).
  - *At court:* the ruler has the staff washed and garlanded and walks around it. She dances before him, and he gives gold and "a garland of green leaves" (Mātavi, *Cilappatikāram*).
  - Mechanically, the court version costs the ruler gold and gives her prestige and an opinion bonus with him. The temple version costs her a gift and gives her piety.
- **No tāli in the faith's rite.** If a tie is wanted, use the yellow thread on the wrist from Āṇṭāḷ's dream wedding (NT 6.4; Dehejia pp. 95–96), which is in period. Do not use the pottu or tāli.
- **The pot-lamp as a court duty.** A daughter holding the court position can "wave the lamp" after a procession or a returning army: a small stress or dread reduction for the ruler. This draws on the *māṇikkam* order of Kulottuṅga I (p. 28) and Paes's Vijayanagara palace women with lamps (p. 36, c. 1510, post-period; mark it).
- **Funeral event, marked as later.** "The god keeps mourning for her." When a daughter of the faith dies holding a temple share, the temple stops worship for an hour and sends a garland to her bier. The text frames this as the faith's custom, without naming any community. Source: twentieth-century memory (p. 192).
- **Reaction lines.**
  - An orthodox priest: "No pottu is tied for lust, or the king burns." The line echoes the commentary's warning (pp. 186–87) and, unlike the commentary itself, is not tied to a date.
  - A Jain or Buddhist voice can quote Paes's "all girls born of these women belong to the temple" as the danger of temple service. This is post-period and for flavour only.
- **Never.**
  - "Nityasumaṅgalī" as a title or trait name.
  - The Basavi or *jogti* "regarded as a boy", the Yellamma cross-dressing, or the twentieth-century initiation of named informants.
  - Brands, police permits and patron selection (*prayōjanam*), which are 20th century.
