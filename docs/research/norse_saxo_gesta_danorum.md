## 1. Relevant passages

### Book 6 (p. 135): Starkaðr at Uppsala. **The exact text**
- Starkaðr goes to the Swedes, where "he lived at leisure for seven years' space with the sons of Frey". Then he leaves for Hakon of Denmark, "because when stationed at Upsala, at the time of the sacrifices, he was disgusted by the effeminate gestures and the clapping of the mimes on the stage, and by the unmanly clatter of the bells."
- Saxo's moral follows: "Thus does virtue withstand wantonness."
- **What Saxo does not say:**
  - He names **no priests**.
  - He does not say who rang the bells.
  - He does not tie the bells to the mimes, and he says nothing about dress.
  - Freyr appears only in "the sons of Frey", Starkaðr's hosts. They are the Swedish royal kin, not cult staff; Book 8 has Swedes who "held the god Frey to be the founder of their race".
- **What he does say:**
  - The effeminacy is in the **gestures of stage mimes**, with their clapping, at the sacrificial season.
  - The bells are **unmanly** in sound.
- **The current comment in `tm_faith_types.txt`, "Saxo's bell-ringing priests of Freyr", overstates the text.** This confirms `norse_reading_list.md` finding 1.
- Saxo's contempt for mimes runs through the book. On the same page, King Hugleik's "hordes of mimes" flee the battle in panic.
- The Latin from memory, unverified here: "effeminatos corporum motus scenicosque mimorum plausus ac mollia nolarum crepitacula". Check it against Friis-Jensen.

### Other Uppsala and Freyr passages (relevant to the holy site)
- **Book 1, p. 54.** Óðinn "used more continually to sojourn at Upsala". The kings of the North send his golden image to Byzantium.
- **Book 1, p. 57.** Hadding atones for a crime by sacrificing "dusky victims to the god Frey". He makes it an annual feast, "Froblod".
- **Book 3, p. 78.** "Frey, the regent of the gods" settles near Uppsala and replaces the old sacrifices with "a ghastly and infamous sin-offering". He begins human sacrifice.
- **Book 7, p. 166.** King Halfdan's wife Gurid is barren. He goes "to Upsala in order to procure fruitfulness for her", and the oracle tells him to atone to his brother's shade. **Uppsala is where a barren woman's case is taken.**
- **Book 6, p. 133.** Fridleif consults the Fates in "the house of the gods" and finds "three maidens, sitting on three seats". Two give the boy gifts; the third spoils them. The place is not named. **Three seated women decide fate from their seats.**

### Book 3 (pp. 80-82): Óðinn, Rinda and the exile
- The Finn Rostioph foretells that Óðinn will father an avenger for Baldr on Rinda, daughter of the king of the Ruthenians. Óðinn courts her in three disguises: soldier, smith, horseman. Each time she cuffs him or pushes him away. He answers by touching her with bark carved with spells, which drives her into a frenzy.
- **The fourth disguise.** He comes "assuming the garb of a maiden" and calls himself **Wecha**, a woman healer. "Most people believed him to be a woman, as he was dressed almost in female attire."
  - He serves the princess as a waiting-woman and washes her feet.
  - When she falls ill, he prescribes a drug so harsh that she must be **bound**. Her father ties her to the bed, "tricked by the sight of the female dress".
  - Óðinn rapes her: "the seeming remedy became an opportunity of outrage". In Saxo's second version the father allows it. The child is Bous (Elton "Boe"), Baldr's avenger.
- **The exile.**
  - The gods "had him not only ousted from the headship, but outlawed and stripped of all worship and honour". A substitute, Oller (Ullr), rules for "nearly ten years" under Óðinn's name.
  - Óðinn returns. Some still judge him unworthy, "because by his stage-tricks and his assumption of a woman's work he had brought the foulest scandal on the name of the gods". Others say he bought his godhead back with bribes.
- **This is the central design finding of the note.** In Saxo, Óðinn's woman's dress is **a disguise used to get a woman alone and rape her**. He is outlawed for "a woman's work" and for "stage-tricks". This is the "man in a dress to get at women" story, and it recurs:
  - **Book 7, p. 155.** A giant may have "disguised himself as a woman" to lure Sigrid from her house and carry her off.
  - **Book 9, p. 198.** Ragnar "went in female attire" among a farm's women at the spinning, to reach and impregnate a guarded girl.
  - **Óðinn as Wecha cannot be a positive model for the faith.** What the faith can take is the outlawry for "a woman's work", which mirrors its own era arc.

### Book 7 (pp. 157-158): Alfhild (Alvild) and the shield-maiden digression
- **The tower.** Alfhild, daughter of King Siward of the Goths, keeps her face veiled. Her father shuts her in a chamber guarded by a viper and a snake. Alf kills both, but Siward lets her choose.
- **Her choice.** Her mother rebukes her for liking his looks. Alfhild then "exchanged woman's for man's attire" and became a rover.
- **Her crew.** She enlists "many maidens who were of the same mind". She also meets "a band of rovers" mourning a dead captain, and "they made her their rover captain". Saxo never says those rovers were women.
- **The defeat.** Alf boards her ship. Borgar knocks off her helmet and sees "the smoothness of her chin". Alf "made her change her man's apparel for a woman's" and fathers a daughter, Gurid, on her.
- **Groa.** Borgar marries "the attendant of Alfhild, Groa". In Saxo, Groa is a shield-maiden's companion, not a seeress.
- **The digression (p. 158).** "There were once women among the Danes who dressed themselves to look like men." They gave their lives to war, put away "the softness and lightmindedness of women", and trained so hard "that they might have been thought to have unsexed themselves". They "devoted those hands to the lance which they should rather have applied to the loom." **Saxo frames them as women crossing toward manhood, and he disapproves.**

### Book 8 (pp. 172-175): Brávellir
- **The muster.** From Sle (Schleswig) come "the captains Hetha (Heid) and Wisna". Saxo says "On these captains, who had the bodies of women, nature bestowed the souls of men". "Webiorg was also inspired with the same spirit." Wisna, "a woman, filled with sternness", is guarded by a band of Slavs. "The maidens I have named ... led their land-forces to the battle-field."
- **The order of battle (p. 174).** Hetha holds the right flank, and **Wisna is standard-bearer**.
- **The fighting.** Starkaðr "cut off the right hand of Wisna". "The maiden Weghbiorg (Webiorg)" kills the champion Soth and is shot through by an archer from Telemark.
- **After the battle (p. 175).** The victor Ring sets Hetha over Zealand. The Zealanders think it "shameful to obey the rule of a woman". Ole forces her out of everything but Jutland, which he makes tributary, "so as not to allow a woman the free control of a kingdom".
- **Swedish side (p. 173).** "Berg the Seer" is a male seer, mentioned without stigma. Swedes who are "kindred of the god Frey".

### Other passages: gender-crossing, women warriors, sorcery
- **Book 1, pp. 52-53, Hardgrep.** A giantess, Hadding's **foster-mother** and nurse, who changes her size at will. She becomes his lover and follows him "in man's attire". She raises a dead man by carving spells on wood and having Hadding put them under the corpse's tongue. She is torn apart for it. This is the closest thing to necromantic seiðr in Saxo, and it is done by a **fostering** woman.
- **Book 3, p. 76, the wood-maidens.** Hother meets "wood-maidens" who say "it was their guidance and government that mainly determined the fortunes of war"; they fight invisibly for their friends. Later they give him "a girdle which assured victory" (p. 80). Saxo dismisses them as "an unreal trick of the arts of magic". **This is Saxo's valkyrie passage, and he does not link them to Óðinn.**
- **Book 3, p. 85.** Koller's sister Sela, "a skilled warrior and experienced in roving", is hunted down and killed.
- **Book 4, p. 101.** "the amazon Rusila", defeated by Huyrwil.
- **Book 5, p. 116.** Kraka, a woman "cunning in witchcraft", disguises herself as Gunwar's half-sister to help a wedding trick.
- **Book 5, p. 123.** The maiden Stikla leaves her country "to save her chastity", choosing war over marriage.
- **Book 6, pp. 143-145, Starkaðr's song to Ingeld.** "the locks of a man ought not to wear a woman's head-band". "Such attire is right for the throngs of the soft and effeminate." Foreign ("Teutonland") food and "a more dissolute dress" are called "unmanly wantonness". This is the period voice of hostility to effeminacy.
- **Book 7, p. 152.** Frode consults "a woman skilled in divination" whose spells can bring hidden things to light. She is bribed with gold, then "feigned death, and fell like one lifeless". A trance-like collapse.
- **Book 7, p. 167.** Harald fights "the maidens Stikla and Rusila", angry "at this arrogance on the part of women".
- **Book 8, p. 177.** "the amazon Rusla" fights her brother Thrond for Norway, beats both the Danes and him, and is driven out by her own commons.
- **Book 9, pp. 195-196, Ladgerda (Lathgertha).**
  - **Origin.** "Fro (Frey?)", King of Sweden, puts the women of Siward's kin in a brothel. Matrons who fear outrage join Ragnar "in male attire". Ladgerda, "a skilled amazon", fights at the front "with her hair loose over her shoulders".
  - **Marriage and divorce.** Ragnar marries her and later divorces her.
  - **Rescue.** She still brings 120 ships to save him, and her flanking charge wins the battle.
  - **Rule.** At home she kills her husband "with a spear-head, which she had hid in her gown" and rules alone.
  - **Why it matters.** Women in men's dress fleeing a brothel run by a king named after Freyr: in Saxo, Freyr's name stands on the side of the oppressor.
