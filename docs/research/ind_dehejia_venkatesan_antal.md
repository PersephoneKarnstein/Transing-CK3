## 1. The argument in brief

- **Dehejia.** An art historian's translation of both poems, with a dating essay and the medieval record of Āṇṭāḷ's fame. She reads the *Tiruppāvai* as the earlier, communal work and the *Nācciyār Tirumoḻi* as the later, solitary one (pp. 33–34). In bhakti poetry, "the speaker ... is the poet himself" (p. 28).
- **Venkatesan 2007.** The Tamil love-poem heroine (*talaivi*) is not one generic figure. A woman poet speaking as a woman (Āṇṭāḷ) and a man taking a female persona (Tirumaṅkai, Nammāḻvār) build her differently.
  - The men surround her with mothers, friends and fortune-tellers, "to attest to the authenticity" of her experience.
  - Āṇṭāḷ's heroine is alone, or speaks in a collective "we" (pp. 19–22).

- **Āṇṭāḷ at Srirangam, in stone.** The 1126 inscription is the earliest **inscriptional** tie between Āṇṭāḷ and Srirangam (p. 13). It supports Srirangam as a holy site of the faith.
- **Correction to the existing note.** `ind_venkatesan_secret_garland.md` §2.2 calls the Śrīvilliputtūr Āṇṭāḷ temple "probably built around 1571" and post-period.
  - Dehejia's 1453/54 inscription stands "on the south wall of the mandapa in front of the Antal temple" (p. 161), and the bronze is c. 970 (p. 10). So an Āṇṭāḷ **shrine** existed there by the very end of the period, and her image by the 10th century.
  - Dehejia also calls the Āṇṭāḷ shrine "clearly a later addition" to the reclining-Viṣṇu temple (p. 6).
  - Reconcile the two: the c. 1571 date is for the present structure or its status, not for the first shrine. Śrīvilliputtūr is still a poor in-period holy site (it is not on the map; it is near b_tirunelveli), but "out of period" is too strong.

### 2.2 The garland legend and its sources (Dehejia pp. 5–9)

- **The legend** comes from the 14th-c. *Guruparamparāprabhāvam* and the 15th-c. *Divyasūricarita* (p. 7). In it:
  - Kōtai secretly dresses as a bride and winds the god's garland in her hair;
  - she looks "into a mirror to see if she looked a bride fit for the lord";
  - her father finds out and does not offer the garland that evening;
  - Viṣṇu tells him in a dream that the worn garland "had an added fragrance" (pp. 7–8).
- **The name.** Her father calls her *cūṭi-koṭutta-nācciyār*, "lady who gave what she had worn" (p. 8). The 1126 inscription and the 10th-c. *taṉiyaṉ* show the epithet and the garland were known long before these texts.
- **No hair in the garland.** Like Venkatesan 2010, Dehejia's version has no "hair in the garland". Drop that detail (see `ind_venkatesan_secret_garland.md` §2.2).
- **Her refusal and end.** She "totally rejected the idea of marriage with a mere mortal". At Srirangam she climbs onto the god's couch and "vanish[es] mysteriously" (pp. 8–9). The *Divyasūricarita* adds a formal wedding at Śrīvilliputtūr in Paṅkuni (p. 9).
- **Her parentage in the poems.** Every signature verse names her "Kōtai of Viṣṇucittaṉ". That she was his **adopted** daughter is presumed; "she does not specify this" (p. 9). The foundling story is hagiography. For the faith, a daughter named by her father's name in every signature verse, whose origin is a found child, is a strong in-period model for *Take as Daughter* (§4).
- **The daily rite at Śrīvilliputtūr.** Āṇṭāḷ's worn garland is moved to the reclining Viṣṇu each dawn (pp. 5–6). This is **modern observation**. Do not present it as medieval.

### 2.3 The *Tiruppāvai* vow (Dehejia pp. 16–21, 33, 43–44)

- **The vow.** Unmarried girls bathe at dawn through the month, "to secure the blessing of a happy married life" (p. 16).
- **The month moved.** Earlier literature put the vow in **Tai**. The *Paripāṭal* (6th–7th c.) has girls bathing in the Vaikai at dawn, drying their clothes at Brahmins' fires and praying for "a fine lover and many children" (p. 17). Cutler explains the move to Mārkaḻi as a lunar-to-solar calendar change (p. 17).
- **It crossed religions.** The vow had Vaiṣṇava, Śaiva and **Jain** forms. A single Jain *pāvai* verse to Arivaṉ is quoted in a 9th-c. Jain work on metre and "perhaps predates" Āṇṭāḷ (p. 17; n. 19, p. 38). A later Jain *pāvai* of 20 verses is attributed to the 14th c. (n. 19).
- **The rules of the vow** (TP 2, p. 43). Dehejia's translation lists them:
  - "We eat no ghee, drink no milk, / wear no flowers in our hair, / no kohl in our eyes";
  - "we do no wrong, speak no evil";
  - they give alms.
- **The bath is figurative.** Āṇṭāḷ's girls "never go to a pond or lake". They ask Kṛṣṇa to "bathe us now in the waters", meaning his grace (p. 18). Bathing (*nīrāṭal*) is also a euphemism for union (p. 19).
- **The drum.** The *paṟai* the girls ask for is the sign that the vow is fulfilled (p. 19).
- **Two works, two months.** Hudson notes the *Tiruppāvai* begins with the word "Mārkaḻi" and the NT with "Tai" (p. 33).
- **A second vow.** NT 1 is a vow to **Kāma** kept through Tai and Māci into Paṅkuni, "for forty or forty-eight days". The girl sweeps the threshold and draws mandalas of fine sand (notes, pp. 131–32).
- **The communal versus the solitary.** The communal *Tiruppāvai* entered temple ritual. The NT, "a 'solitary unitive experience'" (Hudson), did not (p. 5).
- **Modern use.** The *Tiruppāvai* is sung by unmarried girls in Mārkaḻi and broadcast on the radio. NT 6 is recited at weddings, where brides are dressed as Āṇṭāḷ (pp. 4–5). This is living community practice; do not quote it as liturgy.

### 2.4 The dreamed wedding, NT 6 (Dehejia pp. 29, 95–97, 144–45)

- **What happens.** Each verse ends "I dreamt this dream, my friend":
  - Durgā drapes the bridal garb and garland on her (6.3);
  - "the sacred yellow thread" is tied round their wrists (6.4);
  - he takes her hand and they walk round the fire (6.6–7);
  - her foot is set on the grinding stone (6.8);
  - her brothers place her hand in his and heap rice into the flames (6.9);
  - they ride an elephant through the streets (6.10).
- **No tāli.** Dehejia: the tāli rite "became a part of South Indian marriages only from the eleventh century", first in the *Kantapurāṇam* and Kampaṉ (pp. 144–45, after Jayadev 1959–60).
  - This undercuts any medieval "tāli-tying to the god" for temple women before the 11th century (see `ind_kersenboom_nityasumangali.md` §2.2E).
- **The dream is not the end.** NT 7 follows with longing. Venkatesan 2007: she "awakens to find that her grand wedding ... was but a cruel deception" (p. 21).

### 2.5 Male poets in a woman's voice (Venkatesan 2007; Dehejia p. 34)

- **How much there is.**
  - "two hundred and seventy" verses of the *Tiruvāymoḻi* "dramatize the longing of the talaivi";
  - all 100 verses of Nammāḻvār's *Tiruviruttam* do;
  - Tirumaṅkai's two *maṭal* poems and the *Tiruneṭuntāṇṭakam* are given wholly to the heroine (p. 19).
- **Witnesses.** The male poets use mother, friend and fortune-teller "to attest to the authenticity and believability of the heroine's experience". As a woman, Āṇṭāḷ needs no third-party verification (p. 22). Her heroine has "no mothers, no friends", only silent birds and clouds (p. 19).
- **The commentators merge poet and persona.** From the 11th century on they "dissolve the fragile barrier between poet and persona" (p. 18).
  - The same page has the uncited claim: "all souls are ultimately female with the only male" (p. 18, n. 9).
  - **Unverified** in a dated text; see `ind_carman_narayanan_tamil_veda.md` §2.2.
- **A woman on the palmyra horse.** The grammars hold that "only a man could undertake the madal". Tirumaṅkai writes "two madal poems in which it is the rejected heroine who threatens" to ride (n. 11, p. 23).
- **Uncertain voice in the *Tiruppāvai*.** Periyavāccāṉ Piḷḷai (13th c.) could not settle whether Āṇṭāḷ kept the vow herself, pretended to be a cowherd girl, or imagined one. He "happily resolves not to resolve the question" (p. 19).
- **The caution.** Venkatesan cites Selby: a male poet's female voice gives "representations of male attempts to aesthetically capture and portray female sexuality", not female desire (p. 22).
  - **For the design:** the faith's daughters do not inherit the male poets' female voice as costume.
  - The faith takes Āṇṭāḷ's model: a woman's voice that needs no witness. The witnesses (mother, friend) belong to the *crossing*, not to her womanhood afterwards.
- **Dehejia agrees.** The "feminine approach ... is so compact of the element of total surrender that several men saints" put themselves in the place of love-sick cowherd girls (p. 34).

### 2.6 The 1453/54 inscription (Dehejia Appendix One, pp. 161–65)

- **What it records.** On the south wall of the hall before the Āṇṭāḷ temple, a Bāṇa chieftain, Uraṅkavillitāsaṉ (Mahābali Vāṇātarāyar), gives a village. The preamble is a **love-letter** (*praṇayapatrikā*) from Raṅganātha of Srirangam to Kōtai.
- **What the letter says.**
  - It quotes her verses back to her, including the NT 13 threat.
  - It declares "I desire none but her".
  - It orders that she be given "a palanquin, parasol, garlands, jewels, silks" and a temple kitchen "run in the same grand manner as mine" (pp. 162–63).
- **Why it matters.** It is the period's own picture of the faith's "gifts in stone" economy: a chieftain's endowment written as the god's devotion to a woman.

## 3. Corrections to the candidate

1. **Āṇṭāḷ's date: c. 850** (Dehejia). Kersenboom's "8th c." is an outlier.
2. **Garland legend.**
   - Earliest witness: the 10th-c. *taṉiyaṉ*.
   - Inscriptional tie to Srirangam: 1126.
   - First full narrative: the 14th-c. *Guruparamparāprabhāvam*.
   - No "hair in the garland".
3. **Śrīvilliputtūr.** An Āṇṭāḷ shrine is attested by 1453/54 and her bronze by c. 970. "Out of period" in `faith_design.md` §15 should read: "present temple c. 1571; first shrine by 1453/54".
4. **The vow.**
   - In Tai in older sources and in Mārkaḻi in Āṇṭāḷ's (9th c.).
   - Shared with Śaiva and Jain *pāvai* poems.
   - TP 2 gives in-period abstentions usable as gate costs.
5. **The wedding dream has no tāli**, and the tāli rite is 11th century. Use the yellow wrist-thread (NT 6.4) if the rite needs a token.
6. **"God alone is male" is uncited** (Venkatesan 2007 n. 9). Keep it out of loc.

## 4. Design implications

- **Gate step 2 (dawn bath and song) gets TP 2's abstentions.**
  - For the 30 days: no ghee or milk (health or stress cost), no flowers or kohl (prestige or attraction cost), no harsh words (a scheme or insult lock), and alms (gold).
  - **Breaking the vow** (feasting, adorning, insulting) is a setback.
  - **Jain courts** see the vow as kin: Jain characters get a small positive reaction line, "Even the Jina's girls keep a pāvai".
- **The drum (*paṟai*) as the success token.** On success she "receives the drum". This can be the in-period sign the vow is fulfilled, used in place of or beside the yellow thread. A small artifact or modifier is possible: "the Drum of the Vow".
- **A second chance in Tai: the Kāma vow (NT 1).** A seeker who fails Mārkaḻi can keep a 40-day vow to Kāma in Tai–Māci. She sweeps the threshold and draws sand mandalas. Kept at Kanchi, the shrine where Kāma is revived (`ind_shulman_temple_myths.md` §2.2), it raises the next Mārkaḻi's odds. This gives the faith a two-month calendar and makes Kanchi matter.
- **Step 4 (the dream) stays a sign, not the success.**
  - Option text echoes "I dreamt this dream, my friend". The dream is told *to a friend*, so the friend gains a memory.
  - The aftermath echoes NT 7: "she woke, and it was still to come." A sustained vow, not the dream, completes the gate.
- ***Take as Daughter*.** Āṇṭāḷ is "Kōtai of Viṣṇucittaṉ" in every signature verse, a found child raised as a daughter (pp. 7, 9). The legend is 14th c.; the name is 9th c.
  - The faith's adoption interaction can grant the name "of [adopter]" in the daughter's epithet.
  - Flavour line: "She signed every song with her father's name, and he had found her in the basil."
- **Endowment event: "A Letter from the Lord".** A ruler of any Hindu faith may endow a daughter's shrine-share by inscribing a "love-letter of the god" on the temple wall (after the 1453/54 record).
  - Costs gold; gives piety and the faith's opinion.
  - The daughter gains a modifier: "Honours like the god's: palanquin and parasol".
  - The narrator marks it: "as the last record of the age would do".
- **Holy-site support.** Srirangam: "where a garden bore her name in 1126". Possible loc: `holy_site_tm_srirangam_effects:0 "The island temple of the reclining Lord, where the poet who gave the garland she had worn is said to have gone to him, and where a garden was named for her by 1126."`
- **Reaction lines.**
  - An orthodox Vaiṣṇava teacher: "Our Lady was Earth herself, born in a basil bed; you were born a man in a man's house." This uses the 12th–13th-c. Bhūdevī identification.
  - A daughter's reply, after Venkatesan's point: "She needed no witness. Neither do I."
- **Never.**
  - Modern wedding use of NT 6 or Śrīvilliputtūr's daily garland rite as medieval.
  - NT 1.4–1.5 "I cannot live", NT 13's breast-plucking threat, or other death-wish lines.
  - Āṇṭāḷ as a trans figure or founder. She is venerated; the faith claims her vow, not her person.
