# Research report: Snorton, *Black on Both Sides*, as a guardrail source for Badenya

Source read in full:

- C. Riley Snorton, *Black on Both Sides: A Racial History of Trans Identity* (University of Minnesota Press, 2017). `scratchpad/books/snorton.txt` (pdftotext -layout), 10,590 lines: front matter and contents 1–97, preface 98–402, introduction 403–948, chapters 949–7912, acknowledgments 7913–8103, endnotes 8105–9918, index 9920–10580. Cited below as **S:line**.

Line numbers refer to the plain-text file. Quotes are verbatim, 25 words or fewer; everything else is paraphrase. Where pdftotext split a word at a line end ("trans-/posed"), the quote joins it.

**What this note is for.** The book is about Black people in the United States and the Atlantic world, from 1836 to 2017: enslaved women used for surgery, fugitives, the Black press of the 1940s and 1950s, the murders of 1993 and 2015. It is **not evidence about the medieval Sahel**, and nothing in it may be cited for Badenya's history. It is read here the way the project read scholarship on antisemitism for Ha-Nehpakhot (`docs/silk_and_silver.md`, "Guardrails against antisemitic stereotypes": no Creditor purpose, no betrayal of a patron, protection from service and not gold, no supersession, Jewish characters never the wrongdoers, persecution never a mechanic). That reading gave a list of stereotypes and a rule for each. This note does the same for the racialised tropes about Black women, Black trans women and Africa that a game about a West African trans-feminine faith could repeat.

> **Content warning.** The book discusses surgery on enslaved women without anaesthetic (S:1117–1531), sexual violence (S:2960–3011, S:6900–6905), lynching (S:5604–5605, S:7694–7702), the murders of Tamara Dominguez, Brandon Teena, Lisa Lambert and Phillip DeVine (S:120–126, S:7031–7036), police violence (S:6678–6699), a poem's image of filicide (S:4966–4972), and **suicide**: Blake Brockington's death (S:330–337), a surgeon's line that death would be "preferable" for a patient and a note on suicide among women with fistula (S:1402–1413), and a police officer's first guess at the 1993 crime scene (S:7226–7227). The mod's rule stands: **no suicide or self-harm content**. None of this material may be adapted, alluded to or used as flavour.

## 2. Key concepts, in the author's terms

- **Racialized gender.** Following Page and Richardson, gender as the state assigns it along racial lines, producing gender-variant people "as an excluded caste" (S:140–141). Throughout the book, race and gender are one arrangement.
- **Transitivity / transversality.** "Trans" and "black" both name movement and passing from one state to another. They do not name fixed identities (S:558–614). Transversality is Glissant's word for links that lie hidden and need not be seen to have effects (S:758–765).
- **Ungendering; "female flesh ungendered".** Spillers's claim that under captivity "we lose at least gender difference in the outcome" (S:2458–2459). The enslaved body becomes a "territory of cultural and political maneuver" (S:2459–2461), not a man or a woman in the master's order. Snorton holds that "captive flesh figures a critical genealogy for modern transness" (S:2535–2536). That is a claim about violence and its effects, **not** a model to copy.
- **Flesh vs body.** Flesh is what a person is reduced to before law and medicine grant a "body": open to cutting, viewing and use. "[T]here are no bodies (except, tacitly, Sims's), because his 'patients' were nobodies" (S:1566–1568).
- **Fungibility / fugitivity / "fungible fugitivity".** Fungibility is being interchangeable, as goods are. Lucy is called "Betsey" without anyone caring (S:1199–1202). Fugitivity is the escape that the same interchangeability makes possible. They are "two sides of a Janus-faced coin" (S:3664–3667).
- **Scopic availability and pornotroping.** Spillers's term for the captive body made into a sexualised spectacle. White femininity was defined by not being looked at, and Black women by "the unrelenting scopic availability that defined blackness within the visual economy of racial slavery" (S:1643–1644). In a 1912 textbook, "no blackened figure is depicted fully clothed" (S:2194–2195).
- **"Loophole of retreat".** Jacobs's hiding place, read through Hartman as a "space of freedom that is at the same time a space of captivity" (S:3008–3009).
- **Mammy-fication; the "mother-like".** The mammy was a fantasy in which, in Hartman's words, "domination is transposed into the bonds of mutual affection" (S:4987–4988). In Du Bois's image "black people are all mammies compelled to produce white value through their intimate labor" (S:5240–5241). The "mother-like" is "neither a black 'woman' nor a black 'man'" (S:5248–5249).
- **Anatamopoiesis; "human document"; "native informant".** Black writing read as the author's body and the author as an informant on the race (S:4530–4553, S:4633–4638).
- **Shadows, silhouette, countermythology.** Jorgensen as the lit silhouette, the Black press's figures as the shadows. Their refusals become counter-myths, so that "silence becomes countermythological" (S:6221–6222).
- **Restive.** McHarris/Grant's gender, as "a figure may inhabit various gender positions with a sense of sincerity and intransience until otherwise moved" (S:6922–6923).
- **Value (after Barrett).** Value is made by pushing others to the margins. The Black trans figures of the press show the "negativity of value" (S:7007–7012).
- **"Murderous inclusion".** Snorton and Haritaworn's phrase for the politics of inclusion through hate-crime law and prisons (S:345–346, S:7127–7140).
- **Biomythography / invention; "still life".** Snorton calls his DeVine chapter "a narrative composed from fiction, as fiction, and as fiction as “facts”" (S:7256–7258). The book's ethic is "valorizing—without necessarily redeeming—different ways of knowing and being" (S:645–646).

## 3. What transfers (as design analysis, not as history)

These arguments concern how stories about Black and trans people get told. A modern player reads a West African trans-feminine faith through exactly these tropes, whatever the 13th century was. They transfer as **cautions for writing**:

1. **How captivity and slavery work as tropes.** When enslaved people appear in fiction, the story drifts toward treating them as interchangeable, open to anything done to them, and outside gender (ch. 1–2). A game that lets captivity, sale or a master's act turn into gender would repeat the plot of Sims's archive.
2. **The ungendering of the captive body.** Under slavery the captive is placed outside gender, then rearranged. This is the strongest reason, beyond Bhatt and Serano, for Badenya's existing refusal of any "third thing".
3. **Spectacles of Black trans death.** Counting the dead, the deathbed photograph, the martyr (Preface; S:6321–6348; ch. 5). Snorton's refusal to make DeVine a martyr is a direct design rule.
4. **Medical and visual capture.** Examining, undressing, illustrating and verifying the body. The scene recurs from Sims's table (1845) to the five doctors at Lucy Hicks Anderson's trial (S:6151–6153) and the photograph of McHarris/Grant's binder (S:6851–6873).
5. **Fungibility as value.** Gold, prints and press stories in which "white value accrued in relation to black unfreedom" (S:2724–2725). The risk for the mod is any mechanic in which a Black woman's status is bought, or her body is the price.
6. **The joke.** "Lucy was a man" as the punch line (S:6089–6090). The Black press's figures were "framed as jokes, as indications of their supposedly essential disposability" (S:5966–5968).
7. **The mother.** The mammy, the "controlling" mother of the Moynihan report (S:5019–5020), and the matrilineal rule of slavery. Badenya's whole theme is the mother, so this trope is closest of all.
8. **Method.** Snorton refuses to print birth names (S:5972–5975), refuses to make his subjects into "recoverable trans figures" (S:2626–2627), and marks his invention as invention. That matches the mod's practice and supports it.

## 5. What does not transfer

- **No evidence for the Sahel.** The book covers the United States and the British Atlantic from 1836 to 2017. Nothing in it is evidence for Ghana, Mali, the Mande or any part of West Africa before 1453, and it must never be cited in `badenya.md`'s historical sections.
- **"Race" and "blackness" in Snorton's sense did not exist in the 13th-century Sahel.** His blackness was made by the Atlantic slave trade and post-1492 race science (Wynter, McKittrick, S:3196–3206; Hunt and Crawfund, S:3985–4019). The medieval Mande distinctions the sources attest are lineage, freeborn and casted *nyamakala*, free and enslaved, Muslim and not. Do not write "Black" or "African" as an identity in in-game loc, and do not give Mande characters a modern racial consciousness. The guardrails above exist because **players** read through the modern tropes. The characters do not live inside them.
- **Medieval Sahel slavery is not Atlantic chattel slavery.** Ibn Baṭṭūṭa saw enslaved women at the mansa's court, and the trans-Saharan trade was real. But Spillers's "ungendering" and Snorton's "fungibility" are theories of the Atlantic system and US law. Using them as claims about Mali would carry US racial categories back onto the Sahel, so they stay design cautions only.
- **The matrilineal point.** Spillers's matrilineal arrangement is the US rule that a child follows the enslaved mother's status (S:4292–4301). Ghana's and Walata's sister's-son succession (B:20, B:24) is a royal and lineage succession rule with no link to it. Do not read either through the other.
- **Fungibility and fugitivity are not trans gameplay.** Snorton reads cross-gender escape as violence plus survival and refuses to call its people trans (S:2626–2628). Disguise, gender-switching or "passing to escape" must not become a Badenya mechanic or a positive model.
- **The book's trans figures are not precedents for trans women.** Many of them are trans men, gender-variant people of unknown self-identification (McHarris/Grant, S:6914–6980; Jones, S:2791–2798), or not trans at all (the Crafts). None can be cited as a precedent for the House.
- **Note 38 is not a source.** It points to "male wife" categories "in African gender epistemologies" (S:9493–9501), citing Amadiume (Igbo), Murray and Roscoe, and Oyewumi. These are a footnote's pointers, not Snorton's argument, and none concern the Mande. The project has its own note on Oyewumi (`book_oyewumi_invention_of_women.md`). Murray and Roscoe is the kind of "African tradition" anthology Matory warns about (B:56). Do not follow the footnote into a claim.
- **Diaspora spirituality.** The line on "communing with the ancestors" in Black diasporic practice (S:7355–7358) is not evidence for Mande religion.
- **Etymology.** The claim that "mammy" is used in West African compounds such as "mammy-chair" (S:4973–4979) is a dictionary aside about colonial English. It is not Mande vocabulary.
- **Biomythography carries a different licence.** Snorton invents a life for a real man who was murdered, to give back what the archive cut. The mod's invention is a faith no source shows. Snorton supports *marking* invention (B:120–129). He does not license inventing trans lives for real historical people: Sogolon, Tenenba, Qāsā and Sunjata stay as the sources give them.
