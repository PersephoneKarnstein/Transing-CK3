## 2. Key theses of the most relevant chapters

(Ordered by relevance to the mod's three ideological strands and its "veneer of non-anachronism". Read in full: Introduction, Irving, O'Brien, Snorton & Haritaworn, Heyes, Namaste, Serano, Enke, Bettcher, Preciado, Gan, Weismantel, Miranda, Najmabadi, Henry, Gossett, Bassichis/Lee/Spade; head notes plus targeted sections for the rest.)

### Introduction — Stryker & Aizura, "Transgender Studies 2.0" (l. 267-862)
The editors frame "Transgender Studies 2.0" as a self-critique of the field's first wave: it must attack the "implicit whiteness, U.S.-centricity, Anglophone bias" of trans studies and resist becoming an administrative category (l. 437-441). They argue that *man/woman* and *homosexuality* are as culturally constructed and as potentially colonising as *transgender*, and that trans studies should "put as much pressure on the categories of man, woman, and homosexuality, as on transgender" (l. 688-696). They also refuse a "rights-and-representation based framework" in favour of "a transgender political imaginary" and, on health, insist that "Wildness, even more than cultivation or care, should characterize the health of our gender ecologies" (l. 584-585).
- Quote (l. 688-692): "We insist, however, that the ontologization of sex difference through unexamined cultural beliefs about the meaning of biological materiality is itself a modern Eurocentric construct that is imposed onto other cultural phenomena."
- Quote (l. 733-736): "...resist... a common interpretive stance ... that represents non-European gender-variant cultural practices as timeless 'traditions' bound to a particular location to which they are indigenous and authentic... rather than seeing such practices as living, evolving, responsive, and refigurable assemblages".

### Ch. 1 — Dan Irving, "Normalized Transgressions" (l. 863-1643) [Marxism / political economy]
Irving's critical-political-economy reading shows that sexologists (Cauldwell, Benjamin, Money), early transsexual autobiographers, and contemporary trans advocates all legitimate the trans body only insofar as it is *productive* — a working, self-sufficient, tax-paying body. The "real-life test" had an employment requirement; Cauldwell called transsexuals parasites who "would not work". Under neoliberalism (IBM's "managed diversity"), trans difference becomes a "competitive advantage", which fractures trans communities along class/race lines and severs them from anticapitalist alliance. He calls for recovering the militant legacy of Compton's Cafeteria (1966) and Stonewall.
- Quote (l. 915-916): "the integral links between regimes of sex/gender and exploitative economic relations of production as mutually constitutive systems of domination."
- Quote (l. 1246-1247): "Within a heteronormative capitalist society organized around binary sex/gender and exploitative labor relations, transsexuality did not work."
- Quote (l. 1492-1493): "Whose interests are ultimately served by the formation of dutiful, self-sufficient, hardworking transsexual subjectivities?"

### Ch. 4 — Michelle O'Brien, "Tracing This Body" (l. 2957-3418) [Marxism; cult text of trans anticapitalism]
A commodity-chain analysis of her own estradiol and finasteride: insurance exclusions, FDA invisibility, NAFTA-cheapened Mexican/Canadian pharmacies, Big Pharma's WTO suits against generic HIV drugs, and criminalised syringes. Her ethical conclusion is explicitly anti-purist: trans people, HIV+ people and drug users survive by "interfacing global capitalism, but interfacing it improperly"; she rejects "lesbian-feminist separatism" and eco-primitivism as a "politics of purity" and instead embraces Haraway's cyborg. This is the closest thing in the volume to a proto-"transgender Marxism" manifesto.
- Quote (l. 3314-3317): "Our survival is not respected by these corporations, our needs are not taken seriously. We have to meet our needs, not through isolation, purity, or refusal, but through accessing and redefining care in ways counter to the institutions involved."
- Quote (l. 3319-3321): "We are dependent on the very systems that oppress us. We make demands for change, and we appropriate the refuse of capital for our own survival. We live in the flows, suffer in the flows, envision a new world in these flows."
- Quote (l. 3326-3328): "Veganism, do-it-yourself punk ethics, buying natural and local, lesbian-feminist separatism, back-to-the-land self-sustaining agriculture, and especially eco-primitivism ... all frequently rely ... on a commitment to non-participation in global capitalism and certain idealized notions of purity."

### Ch. 5 — C. Riley Snorton & Jin Haritaworn, "Trans Necropolitics" (l. 3419-3980) [biopolitics; trans-of-colour critique]
Using Mbembe's necropolitics, they argue that the deaths of trans women of colour (Tyra Hunter, 1995) are a "resource" from which white "transnormative" politics, hate-crime law, gentrification and the non-profit complex extract value; the dead are "invited back in" only in death (TDOR). They propose "trans of color critique" and warn against a transnational "traumatized citizenship".
- Quote (l. 3463-3464): "this circulation vitalizes trans theory and politics, we claim, through the value extracted from trans of color death."
- Quote (l. 3833-3835): "it is in their death that poor and sex working trans people of color are invited back in; it is in death that they suddenly come to matter."

### Ch. 16 — Cressida Heyes, "Feminist Solidarity After Queer Theory" (l. 9473-10052) [transfeminism]
A non-trans feminist philosopher dismantles Raymond's *Transsexual Empire* and Hausman's *Changing Sex* as orientalising trans people as "dupes of gender", but also critiques Feinberg's gender-voluntarism ("Each person's expression of their gender ... is their own and equally beautiful") for treating gender as an individual aesthetic rather than a hierarchical relation. She proposes an "ethics of self-transformation" (Foucault's technologies of the self) in which SRS, dieting and cosmetic surgery are compared as "family resemblances" — and notes trans women "who resist medical demands further by defining as lesbians".
- Quote (l. 9586-9589): "feminist writing about transgender needs to define and articulate a middle ground in which an ethics of self-fashioning can be developed. Such an ethics should recognize the discursive limits on individual self-transformation without denying agency to gendered subjects."
- Quote (l. 9839-9842): "many MTFs who do not want to engage in penile-vaginal intercourse after SRS (including those who resist medical demands further by defining as lesbians) resist the heterosexist demand to excessively dilate or surgically extend their newly constructed vaginas, or even to have one constructed in the first place".

### Ch. 17 — Viviane Namaste with Georgia Sitara, "Inclusive Pedagogy" (l. 10053-10676) [transfeminism; the Nixon case]
Using Kimberly Nixon's 1995 exclusion from Vancouver Rape Relief, Namaste moves the trans-inclusion debate beyond "is a transsexual woman a woman?" to critique (a) the universalist appeal to a shared "life experience" of womanhood, which effaces race/class; (b) the false gender=race analogy ("a man wanting to be a woman is like a white person wanting to be Black"); (c) VRR's statist appeal to law despite feminist anti-statism; (d) the imperialist history of "protecting" women.
- Quote (l. 10306-10307): "Clearly, an appeal to the lifelong experience of living as a woman is an insufficient ground for solidarity."
- Quote (l. 10406-10408, Marisa Swangha): "An Afrikan/Asian/Native 'man wanting to be' an Afrikan/Asian/Native woman is not 'like a white person wanting to be Black,' it is like a 'man' of a certain color wanting to be a woman of the same color as 'he' already is. Gender is not like race."

### Ch. 18 — Julia Serano, "Skirt Chasers" (l. 10677-11075) [transfeminism; transmisogyny]
Media (and Raymond) depict trans women either as "deceptive" or "pathetic" transsexuals; both archetypes rely on the assumption that femininity is artificial and that trans women transition for a "femininity fetish". Serano names this transmisogyny and argues the disparagement of trans women is sexism against *all* women. She defends trans women who are "not attracted to men" and critiques the Michigan Womyn's Music Festival "womyn-born-womyn" policy.
- Quote (l. 10686-10687, head note): "cultural prejudices against femininity and woman-identification can actually be seen with extraordinary clarity when they are projected against a purportedly male body (a practice she describes as 'transmisogyny')".
- Quote (l. 11040-11041): "Actually, most of us are only a hormone prescription away from being perceived as the 'opposite' sex."
- Quote (l. ~11044, OCR-garbled): "The one thing that women share is that we are all perceived as women and treated accordingly."

### Ch. 19 — A. Finn Enke, "The Education of Little Cis" (l. 11076-11779) [transfeminism; cis/trans]
Enke traces "cisgender" (Defosse 1994, Buijs 1996, Koyama 2002, Serano 2007, Camp Trans) and argues that its uptake as an ally-identity re-stabilises "woman" and "man", reduces trans to the "most oppressed" object, and ignores how cis privilege depends on whiteness, class and ability. Enke's autoethnography (childhood endocrine self-experimentation, becoming a lesbian, a monastery where "I felt my gender 'matched'") refuses to say what s/he "really" is. Cites Feinberg's 1992 pamphlet as a "Marxist and feminist analysis of (trans)gender oppression" (n. 8, l. 11621).
- Quote (l. 11305-11307): "Camp Trans ... began with insistence on self-identification, which was and is a fundamental tenet of trans activism: people's gender identity must be respected, regardless of how they may appear."
- Quote (l. 11583-11585): "As a transfeminist teacher and activist, I have a vested interest in keeping the categories woman, man, and trans* wide open, their flexible morphologies blending into one another".

### Ch. 20 — Bobby Jean Noble, "Our Bodies Are Not Ourselves" (l. 11780-12289) [trans masculinity; class; whiteness]
A white FtM "guy who is half lesbian" uses union/working-class family history (CUPE grandmother; Barnardo "Little Immigrant" grandfather) to argue trans is a class and race issue, seeking "a way to be a man without becoming 'The Man'". Notes gyms as "sites of Marxist alienation".
- Quote (l. 11936-11938): "trans men cannot leave the 'trans' behind and be 'men.'"

### Ch. 22 — Beatriz [Paul B.] Preciado, "The Pharmaco-Pornographic Regime" (l. 12536-13111) [biopolitics]
Post-Fordist capitalism governs sex through "soft" molecular technologies (the Pill, testosterone, Prozac, porn); gender is "a techno-political ecology" invented by Money in 1957. Trans activists who "reappropriat[e] hormonal and chirurgical techniques to construct themselves" are the model for a "gender-copyleft revolution". Also notes that genitals remain "state property (and in extension of this theocratic model, God's property)" — a usable pre-modern/sovereign framing.
- Quote (l. 13009-13011): "not simple economical classes in the Marxist sense of the term, but authentic pharmaco-pornopolitical factories."
- Quote (l. 13016): "They produce self-designed sexes."
- Quote (l. 13022-13023): "Your memory, your desire, your sensibility, your skin, your dick, your dildo, your blood, your sperm, your vulva, your gonads, etc. are the tools of a possible gender-copyleft revolution."

### Ch. 23 — Talia Mae Bettcher, "Evil Deceivers and Make-Believers" (l. 13112-13772) [transfeminism; philosophy]
From Gwen Araujo's murder (2002) Bettcher theorises the "deceiver/pretender double bind": gender presentation is read as *representing* genital status, so trans people are constructed as liars (if passing) or make-believers (if visible). This communicative system is continuous with rape culture ("she wanted it") and racist rape mythology; identity enforcement by forced genital exposure is sexual violence. She ends by reframing "gender deception" as "one laudable tactic of attempted survival".
- Quote (l. 13636-13638): "surely 'gender deception' must be seen as one laudable tactic of attempted survival in what appears to be an exceptionally violent, no-win situation. After all, isn't the adoption of 'honesty is the best policy' only to acquiesce to the morality of oppression?"
- Quote (l. 13640-13642): "in dominant constructions, we are always 'in disguise' while the 'concealed realities' are the multiply resistant community contexts that sustain us."

### Ch. 24 — Jessi Gan, "'Still at the Back of the Bus': Sylvia Rivera's Struggle" (l. 13773-14347) [radical trans history; kinship]
Rivera (Puerto Rican/Venezuelan street queen, Stonewall combatant, STAR House co-founder with Marsha P. Johnson) is reduced by white trans politics to "transgender Stonewall combatant". Gan shows her kinship practices (renaming ceremony, adopted by drag queens, altar to Saint Barbara, "all my children") exceed heterosexual reproduction and identity politics. Also documents Lesbian Feminist Liberation's 1973 attack on Rivera for "parodying womanhood".
- Quote (l. 13931-13934): "It was expected that all of the street queens would give themselves new names, and so Ray Rivera became Sylvia Lee Rivera in a ceremony. Fifty street queens, most of them Latin@ and black, attended the celebration, which, Rivera said, felt 'just like being reborn.'"
- Quote (l. 14177-14179): "Rivera's articulations of kinship, family, and community exceed models of kinship built upon heterosexual reproduction".

### Ch. 26 — Mary Weismantel, "Towards a Transgender Archaeology" (l. 15061-15880) [premodern]
An archaeologist of Moche Peru argues the gender binary is projected onto the record (Vix "princess", Çatalhöyük, Moche "Lady of Cao" and the male "Priestess", Maya cross-dressing kings, the androgynous Maize God) and that premodern ontologies of the "partible", assembled body (detachable clay penises worn as pendants; graves mixing bones) anticipate the transsexual body. See Section 5 for full quotations.
- Quote (l. 15364-15365): "in many ancient societies, ritualized cross-dressing was mandated for political and religious leaders."
- Quote (l. 15551-15552): "To show femininity in this context makes a man more powerful, not less."

### Ch. 27 — Karma Lochrie, "Before the Tribade" (l. 15881-16633) [medieval]
Medieval medicine (Avicenna, Guy of Chauliac, Gilbert the Englishman, John of Gaddesden) knew the clitoris and had "two clitorises"; the "virago" (menstrual retention producing beards, strength and lust) was a non-pathologised form of female masculinity. Key for a medieval "normal wasn't" veneer: premodern sexuality was not organised by norms.
- Quote (l. 15893-15894, head note): Lochrie "seeks to understand 'female sexuality when normal wasn't.'"
- Quote (l. 16528-16530): "The medieval virago did not give rise to the tribade, or 'rubster,' but instead to an otherwise 'heterosexual' woman whose sexual anatomy predicts her masculinity. Her sexuality is anything but heteronormative."

### Ch. 28 — Deborah A. Miranda, "Extermination of the Joyas" (l. 16634-17328) [premodern/indigenous; THE key precedent chapter]
Spanish soldiers and Franciscan missionaries in California waged "gendercide" against *joyas* (third-gender male-bodied people who lived and worked as women, married men, and were the exclusive undertakers/'aqi of Chumash society) by murder (mastiffs), renaming, regendering, and replacement of their death-rituals by Catholic burial. Joya status "does not depend on having a joya parent"; "daughters" of 'aqi were fictive/adoptive kin; the role passed "from mother to daughter". See Section 5.
- Quote (l. 17116-17118): "A joya's conception does not depend on having a joya parent, unlike normative male and female sexes ... as long as enough of the normative population remains alive and able to bear children, the potential for joya gender to emerge in some of those children also remains."
- Quote (l. 17153-17154): "A Two-Spirit person is born regardless of biological genealogy. Thus we will always be with you."

### Ch. 30 — Afsaneh Najmabadi, "Reading Transsexuality in 'Gay' Tehran" (l. 18160-19152) [non-Western; religious sanction]
1970s Tehran had a continuum of "woman-presenting males" (zan-push actors, khanums, iva-khvahar "o'sister"), a 1976 Medical Council ban on sex-change, and Maryam Mulk-ara's pre-revolution fatwa from Khomeini permitting sex-change. After 1979 public gender-segregation bifurcated "gay" from "transsexual", making the latter "the more socially acceptable way of being a non-heteronormative male".
- Quote (l. 18275-18277): "In earlier eras, a male dressing as a woman and opting for a womanly career constituted 'housewifery,' that is, becoming a male kept by a man."
- Quote (l. 18803-18805): "a place where everyone was a woman, that is, even though they were known as males in social norms of recognition, but they were women."
- Quote (l. 18904-18905): "Being transsexual, rather than gay, emerged as the more socially acceptable way of being a non-heteronormative male."

### Ch. 31 — Todd A. Henry, "Between Surveillance and Liberation" (l. 19153-19896) [non-Western; male-bodied women]
Postwar Tokyo *dansho* (cross-dressed male sex workers, many ex-soldiers) fought police in Ueno Park (1948), ran a "dansho school" teaching makeup and female speech, used the "lotus root" technique, and aspired to be "real housewives" of male patrons. A model of a self-organising guild of male-bodied women in a non-Western setting.
- Quote (l. 19249-19251): "Defeat in a hyper-masculine war allowed these individuals, many former soldiers, to reposition themselves as male-bodied 'women' within a society whose sex/gender system remained overwhelmingly hetero-normative."
- Quote (l. 19458-19459): Otoki "categorically stating, 'Mentally, I was born a woman.'"

### Ch. 44 — Che Gossett, "Silhouettes of Defiance" (l. 27742-28265) [radical history; archive]
The NYC "Rainbow Pilgrimage" and the Compton's plaque fold Stonewall/Compton's into neoliberal, pro-police "homonormative" memory, erasing trans women of colour, sex workers and the prison-industrial complex; via Derrida's "archival violence" and George Jackson, Gossett calls for memory that centres survival and abolition.
- Quote (l. 27812-27815): homonormativity involves "the purging of social undesirables—genderqueer, transgender, transsexual, gender non-conforming, people of color, and those who live and work on the street—as a means of obtaining 'rights' and social respectability."

### Ch. 50 — Bassichis, Lee & Spade, "Building an Abolitionist Trans and Queer Movement" (l. 31163-~31830) [transgender Marxism / abolition]
A manifesto contrasting the "official" LGBT agenda (marriage, hate-crime law, military) with "transformative approaches": universal healthcare, prison abolition, "abolish inheritance and demand radical redistribution of wealth", "trickle up" change, collectivism, "for us, by us", transformative justice. Trans people's "impossibility" is reframed as political strength.
- Quote (l. 31222-31224): "For more and more queer and trans people, regardless of marital status, there is no inheritance, no health benefits from employers, no legal immigration status, and no state protection of our relationship to our children."
- Quote (l. 31301-31303, chart): "abolish inheritance and demand radical redistribution of wealth and an end to poverty".
- Quote (l. 31814): "Impossibility may very well be our only possibility."

## 5. Historical / premodern / non-Western precedents (exhaustive for this volume)

**Important caveat up front.** This volume contains **no** material on the Roman galli/gallae, Cybele/Attis, Byzantine or Ottoman or Chinese eunuchs, medieval monks/nuns, or any Christian/Islamic/pagan European precedent for ritual castration or trans-feminine priesthood. The words *galli*, *Cybele*, *eunuch* (in the human sense) and *matrilineal* are absent (see Section 3). The premodern material is: (i) prehistoric/pre-Columbian archaeology (Weismantel), (ii) 18th-19th-c. Spanish-colonial California Chumash/Esselen *joyas*/'aqi (Miranda), (iii) medieval medicine on female masculinity (Lochrie); plus 20th-century non-Western ethnography/history (Najmabadi on Iran, Henry on Japan, Aizura on Thailand, Ochoa/Lewis on Venezuela and Latin America, Stryker on the Philippines, Bailey on Detroit ballroom houses). For the galli/Cybele veneer you must look elsewhere (TSR 1, Roscoe, Beard, Taylor, Ringrose, Tougher). What follows is everything usable, tagged by which planned mechanic it STRENGTHENS or WEAKENS.

Mechanics key: **(a)** castrated men culturally regarded as women; **(b)** adoptive/matrilineal heirship; **(c)** women-only / trans-fem priesthood; **(d)** castration as sacred rite.

### 5.1 Miranda, "Extermination of the Joyas" (ch. 28) — Chumash/Esselen California, 1769-1830s

1. **Joyas lived, dressed and worked as women inside the women's world.** Fages 1775: "those Indian men who ... are observed in the dress, clothing, and character of women—there being two or three such in each village—pass as sodomites by profession ... They are called joyas, and are held in great esteem" (l. 16757-16761). "Joyas were always a part of this women's world and did not cross over into the men's territory" (l. 17021-17022). → **STRENGTHENS (a)** (male-bodied people socially counted as women, "two or three ... in each village" gives a demographic ratio for a trait) — but note **no castration is involved**; joyas are not described as castrated. So it strengthens "male-bodied regarded as women" and **weakens the necessity of (d)** for (a).
2. **Selected in infancy and trained.** Boscana (San Diego): "Whilst yet in infancy they were selected, and instructed as they increased in years, in all the duties of the women—in their mode of dress—of walking, and dancing; so that in almost every particular, they resembled females" (l. 16900-16903). → **STRENGTHENS (a)** and supports a childhood-assigned trait/education mechanic.
3. **Married to men; prized as wives by chiefs.** "The pagan replied that that joya was his wife" (l. 16892); Boscana: joyas "'being more robust than the women, were better able to perform the arduous duties required of the wife, and for this reason, they were often selected by the chiefs and others, and on the day of the wedding a grand feast was given'" (l. 16981-16983). → **STRENGTHENS (a)** (marriage eligibility as women) and gives a flavour hook for a "Cut Consort" marriage interaction.
4. **Exclusive priestly/ritual office of the joya/'aqi.** "death, burial, and mourning rituals were the exclusive province of the joyas; they were the undertakers of their communities. As the only members of California Indian communities who possessed the necessary training to touch the dead or handle burials without endangering themselves or the community" (l. 17047-17051). "the 'aqi, with their male-female liminality, were the only people who could mediate these experiences" (l. 17058-17059). → **STRONGLY STRENGTHENS (c)**: a sacred office restricted to third-gender people plus postmenopausal women — exactly a "women-centred / trans-woman-centred priesthood".
5. **Postmenopausal women share the office — a female-plus-transfem priesthood.** "Asserting that undertakers were exclusively 'aqi or postmenopausal women (also called 'aqi), Hollimon speculates that perhaps 'the mediation between death and the afterlife ... was entrusted by the Chumash to individuals who could not be harmed by symbolic pollution of the corpse, and who were no longer (or never had been) capable of giving birth'" (l. 17067-17071). → **STRENGTHENS (c)**; also gives a theological rationale (non-fertility as ritual qualification) that could be ported onto castration in (d).
6. **De facto matriline and fictive adoptive daughters.** "all undertakers ('aqi') were women, strong enough to carry bodies and dig deep graves, and that the role was passed from mother to daughter" (l. 17088-17090). Hollimon n. 45: "'daughters' of male-bodied aqi were probably fictive kinships (such as adoption) formed with other members of the same guild or role or premenopausal children of women who took up the aqi role late in life" (l. 17308-17310). → **STRONGLY STRENGTHENS (b)**: an office/estate transmitted mother→daughter where "daughters" of male-bodied holders are adoptive. This is the single best precedent in the book for "inheritance through adoptive matrilines".
7. **Non-heritability of the gender itself.** "A joya's conception does not depend on having a joya parent" (l. 17116-17117); "A Two-Spirit person is born regardless of biological genealogy" (l. 17153). → Supports a **randomly assigned trait**; **weakens** any design where the trait is inherited.
8. **Spiritual tools quarantined; payment in baskets.** "Baskets used to scoop up the earth of a grave ... were given to the 'aqi by the deceased person's relatives as partial payment for burial services, but also because they could never again be used for the life-giving acts of cooking or gathering" (l. 17063-17065). → Flavour for a priestly tithe/"funeral fee" mechanic.
9. **Persecution model.** Balboa 1513: "forty indigenous men, all dressed as women ... 'a prey to his dogges'" (l. 16796-16798); mission "regendering" by flogging, stocks, forced male dress (l. 16968-16975). → Flavour for hostile-faith interactions (a "gendercide" casus belli or persecution event).
10. **Weakening point:** Miranda stresses joyas were *not* castrated and that the Spanish read them as "sodomites"; nothing here supports (d) castration as rite. Also joyas "had children in the past" (l. 17151) — they could be fertile, which **weakens** a design in which trans-feminine characters are categorically infertile.

### 5.2 Weismantel, "Towards a Transgender Archaeology" (ch. 26) — prehistory / pre-Columbian

11. **Vix burial (c. 450 BC, Celtic France):** "described variously as a ... 'nomad princess' ... a 'lady' ... a 'man who did not mind wearing women's clothing' ... a 'transvestite priest' ... a 'rich woman and possibly a chief or tribal ruler' ... [or] an 'honorary male'" (l. 15082-15085); possibly "an intersex individual who attained the status of a religious leader or shaman, partly because of her unusual body" (l. 15107-15109). Buried with a chariot, a Greek krater "entirely filled with mead", a gold torc (l. 15113-15118). → **STRENGTHENS (c)** weakly (a gender-ambiguous religious leader in Iron Age Europe, within CK3's map-space); useful for a European flavour event.
12. **Çatalhöyük (7500-5700 BC):** "the absence of gender was a structural feature of Catalhoyük society" (l. 15342-15343); households "genetically unrelated individuals of various ages and sexes ... If these clusters of people represent families, their families were big, variable, and chosen: not small, heteronormative, and biological" (l. 15336-15340). → **STRENGTHENS (b)** (chosen, non-biological households as a deep-time precedent).
13. **Moche Peru (1st millennium AD):** "the 'Lady of Cao' ... a morphologically female corpse—that was buried grasping two enormous gold-headed war clubs" (l. 15380-15382); "one of the 'Priestesses' is actually a biological male; could this be a man who lived and dressed as a woman, or perhaps a cross-dressing priest?" (l. 15394-15396). → **STRENGTHENS (c)** (a male-bodied person interred in the regalia of the "Priestess" class).
14. **Ritual cross-dressing of elites:** "in many ancient societies, ritualized cross-dressing was mandated for political and religious leaders" (l. 15364-15365); "as for the kings of the ancient Maya, cross-dressing was an important component of royal performance" (l. 15425-15426); "Great lords and powerful priests arrayed themselves in elaborate costumes that incorporated aspects of male and female clothing" (l. 15481-15482); "Gender variance without fear of stigma or punishment can be a freedom allotted to the privileged few" (l. 15420-15421). → **STRENGTHENS (c)** and supports making the priesthood an *elite* institution; **weakens** any claim that it was egalitarian.
15. **Androgynous Maize God:** "the lovely face of the Young Maize God, usually described by modern viewers as a very feminine young man. The god was the epitome of perfect beauty—and a perfect androgyne" (l. ~15456-15460); "For ancient Mesoamericans, this—not the inevitability of a fixed biological 'nature'—is the message that the biological world offers to the cultural one" (l. 15478-15479). → Theological model for a goddess/godhead doctrine.
16. **Partible body and detachable penis:** "In the ancient Americas, a basic premise of Pre-Columbian art is that living beings gain power and beauty through appropriating the body parts of others" (l. 15539-15540); "To show femininity in this context makes a man more powerful, not less" (l. 15551-15552); clay penises "were usually pierced for wearing, probably hung from a cord around the neck ... could be attached to any kind of body, including that of a child" (l. 15648-15651); "elements of maleness and femaleness may be partible elements ... exchanged, divided, ingested, or temporarily held within individual persons" (l. 15659-15661). → **STRENGTHENS (d)** indirectly: an ontology in which removing/adding genital "parts" is a transfer of potency rather than a loss — the best philosophical cover for a sacred castration rite in this volume.
17. **Classical Greece (a weakening precedent):** "The same society that produced those statues also put women to death by public drowning or burning alive if their bodies were determined by the authorities to be partly male" (l. 15135-15138). → **WEAKENS** any assumption that Mediterranean antiquity was uniformly tolerant.
18. Weismantel's caution: "The goal of a transgender archaeology is not to re-populate the ancient past with modern trans men and trans women" (l. 15191-15192); and against treating others' histories as "a sort of giant thrift shop" (n. 8, l. 15717-15720). → Design ethics note for the mod's "veneer".

### 5.4 Najmabadi, "Reading Transsexuality in 'Gay' Tehran" (ch. 30) — Iran

### 5.6 Other non-Western / diasporic material

29. **Aizura (ch. 38), Thailand:** "Kathoey is sometimes understood as a 'third sex' ... Kathoeys, or sao praphet sorng, are not ..." universally medicalised (l. 23842-23847); "only around 30 percent of kathoey desire vaginoplasty" (l. 23858-23859). → **WEAKENS** making castration/genital surgery definitional of trans-feminine status.

34. **Preciado (ch. 22):** "the genitals are still enclosed in a pre-modern and almost sovereign regime of power that considers them to be state property (and in extension of this theocratic model, God's property) by virtue of a transcendental and immutable law" (l. 12947-12950); "the systems of matriarchal genderization or of Greek paedophilia" as extinct "gender programs" (l. 12898-12900). → A theoretical bridge: in a premodern frame, who owns the genitals is a *sovereign/theocratic* question — exactly the ground on which a faith could claim the right to cut.
35. **Chen (ch. 13):** "racialized castration, a kind of transing which is not always considered under a trans rubric, except in the case of male to female transsexuality" (l. 8135-8137). → Cautionary: castration imagery is historically a racist weapon; a mod should distinguish sacred self-chosen cutting from imposed mutilation.
36. **Norton (ch. 45):** Japan's Law 111 makes legal gender depend on sterilisation; Norton calls this "a Hobson's choice" and a violation of bodily integrity (l. 28625-28629): "we should neuter the law and not bodies" (l. 28633). → **WEAKENS (d)**: the volume's only sustained treatment of castration/sterilisation-as-condition-of-womanhood condemns it as coercion. If the mod keeps castration sacred, it should be *elective*, with an uncut path to full status (as Heyes l. 9839-9842 and Aizura l. 23858 imply).
