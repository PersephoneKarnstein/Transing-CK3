## 1. The argument in brief

### Adoption as one of three kinships "by arrangement"
- Byzantine marriage treatises classed adoption, baptismal sponsorship and *adelphopoiia* together as kinship "by arrangement" (*thesei*) as opposed to "by nature" (*physei*); all three were "formed by the prayers of a ritual" (pp. 109–110).
- *Adelphopoiia* was "not admissible by law" in the jurists, though a rite existed and was sought after (p. 110). Adoption was the one of the three with full legal standing.
- Monastic typika forbade monks to take godchildren or sworn brothers, but "adoption of a son/daughter is not expressly forbidden" (p. 109 n. 4; p. 115 n. 68). Macrides' explanation: adoption was "essentially non-social", it did not build networks of visiting and feasting, so the typika did not fear it (p. 115).

### Leo VI's three novels (pp. 110–111)
- **Novel 24:** adoption is now "created by the prayers of the church", so marriage between an adopted and a blood child is barred (p. 110).
- **The rite.** The prayers speak of the child "about to be reborn"; the adoptee prostrates at the adopter's feet; the adopter proclaims Ps. 2:7, "Thou art my son, this day I have begotten thee"; they embrace as "spiritual" father and son, "superior to blood parent and child" (pp. 110–111). Some rituals had the adopter tread on the adoptee's neck (p. 111).
- **Novels 26 and 27 open adoption "for the first time" to eunuchs and women** (p. 111). Before, only a woman who had lost all her children could adopt, as a consolation. Leo "gave the right to all women, both virgins and other women who could not have children for whatever reason" (p. 111).
- Macrides' caution: this is less a landmark in women's rights than a sign that adoption "no longer established power or *patria potestas*" (p. 111). Leo writes of children as care and nursing in old age, and as help administering an estate (p. 111).

### What the contracts show (13th–15th-c. notarial formulae, older roots; pp. 111–113)
- Two kinds: contracts between a natural parent (always, in the surviving formulae, an impoverished widow) and the adopter; and contracts between adopter and an **adoptee "twenty-five years of age or older"** (p. 111). Adult adoption was ordinary.
- Adopters were childless couples wanting "an heir and continuator of their line", and also couples with children who took in "an infant in need" (p. 111).
- **The church rite made the adoption; the contract came after** and fixed rights. One adopter: "I took you from the hands of the priest after the holy blessing" (p. 112).
- **Adoption did not automatically mean inheritance.** Without an express clause the adoptee had "a claim only to intestate inheritance". Full heirship had to be written: "from this day we make you an instituted heir of all our property and name you successor to our line (*genos*) and legitimate son" (p. 112). In one case the decision to make an infant adoptee full heir "was made years later" (p. 112). There were "degrees of 'belonging'" (p. 112).
- **Exit clauses.** An adoptee who chose to leave lost the stipulated inheritance; one cast out by the adopters took "one-twelfth of their inheritance" (p. 113).
- Adoption did not cut the child from blood kin: adoptees could still inherit from natural parents (pp. 114, 117).
- Sixth-century papyri show the same pattern, so the formulae probably reflect long practice (p. 113). But Balsamon (12th c.) says Leo's novel is the only place one learns that adoption is by holy prayer, "unknown to many people"; civil adoption before courts or local *archontes* continued (pp. 113–114).

### Charitable adoption, and women adopting girls
- An adoption that gave upbringing and dowry but not heirship could count as a *psychikon*, a deed for the adopter's soul (p. 115).
- **The nun Kallone Pouzoulou** (1401) took in a girl "for the sake of the commemoration of her soul", arranged her marriage and dowered her (p. 115). A nun adopting a daughter.
- **Eudokia Nestongonissa**, the emperor's aunt, raised an adopted daughter from seven and loved her "almost as a legitimate child"; she had not made her full heir, and sued when the girl married without consent (1315; p. 116).
- **Bryenissa**, childless, adopted her first cousin Magistrina after the girl's mother died, arranged her marriage and promised her property; Magistrina sued her adoptive mother for it in 1360 (pp. 116–117).
- **Psellos** adopted a girl after his own daughter died, dowered her with 50 pounds of gold, and used 20 of it to buy his prospective son-in-law the dignity of *protospatharios* (p. 116).

### Adoption as politics, and its fragility
- Adoption for friendship: the Athonite abbot Poimen adopted a monk "remembering an old friendship" and, "wanting to confirm the adoption", sold him his monastery (p. 117). A monastery passing by adoption.
- Alexios Komnenos had Empress Maria of Alania adopt him to get daily access to her at court (p. 117).
- Adoption was a route for the *dynatoi* to take peasant land (Romanos I, Novel of 934; p. 117).
- **Crisis adoptions at the top:** Zoe adopted Michael Kalaphates (Michael V) in 1040 and made him *caesar*; he ruled with her until he tried to remove her (p. 117). Michael VI offered to adopt Isaac Komnenos (1057); Botaneiates offered to adopt Bryennios (1078). All "a weak and temporary expedient" (p. 117).
- **Michael III adopted Basil** (the future Basil I), made him *magistros*; it "ended in betrayal, with Michael III's untimely death" (p. 118). Basil had Michael killed in September 867.
- Conclusion: "adoption was only as strong and long-lasting as the intentions of the parties to it allowed" (p. 118).

## 3. In-world lines for loc

- "Thou art my daughter; this day I have begotten thee." (Take as Daughter completion; adapted from the adoption rite's Ps. 2:7)
- "From this day we make you heir of all we hold, and name you successor to our line." (full-heir confirmation; after the contract formula)
- "The Rule forbids a sister to take a godchild or a sworn brother. It says nothing of daughters." (Elder Sister, Sisters' House succession)
- "The Emperor adopted his groom, and the groom killed him." (Dorleyaian comment on Michael III and Basil, 867)
- "For the remembrance of my soul I take this girl." (charitable adoption; after Kallone Pouzoulou)

## 4. Flags

- **Nuance to existing design:** vanilla adoption = heir; Byzantine adoption ≠ heir unless written. The design should either model the distinction (row 2) or say plainly that the faith always writes the heir clause.
- **Supports** beliefs.md G (g) and §11 ("adoption carries the line; the vow carries the love"): Macrides confirms *adelphopoiia* had no legal force and adoption did.
- **Primary-source cases usable as events or characters:** Michael III and Basil (866–67); Zoe and Michael V (1040); Kallone Pouzoulou the adopting nun (1401); Bryenissa and Magistrina (1360, a woman adopting her cousin); Psellos' adopted daughter (11th c.); Poimen's monastery passed by adoption (c. 1010).
