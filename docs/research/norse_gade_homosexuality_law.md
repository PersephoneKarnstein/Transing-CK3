## 1. The argument in brief

- **Only one Norse law ever punished sex between men, and it was a late political tool.** Gulaþingslög ch. 32 is the single Scandinavian provision: if two men are convicted, both become *úbótamenn* (permanent outlaws, beyond compensation), the accused may clear himself by the hot-iron ordeal, and property is split "the king ... one-half ... and the bishop one-half" (p. 124). Gade dates it to the *nýmæli* (new law) of King Magnus Erlingsson and Archbishop Eysteinn in **1164** (pp. 124-25), against Boswell's later-interpolation view.
- **It was not Church law and not an old Germanic custom.** It is classed with theft, treason and murder, not with crimes against Christianity; the ordeal differs from the church-door oath used for church crimes (p. 130). There is no parallel in the Leges Barbarorum, and Lateran III (1179) only excommunicated (p. 126). Tacitus's *corpore infames* is no firm evidence (n. 4).
- **Its motive was confiscation.** Out of the 1164 alliance of crown and archbishopric (Snorri's "increase God's rights / the king's rights" exchange, pp. 130-31), the clause "must have been introduced to gain control of the property of the accused and as a convenient tool to rid Church and state of their enemies" (p. 131). A likely model is Justinian's half-confiscation (p. 131).
- **It failed.** It never reached Frostaþing, Eidsivaþing or Borgarþing law, nor Grágás, and it vanishes after Sverrir's *christenret* (which added permanent exile and penance, p. 130). Later codes fold "any living being but a woman" into bestiality (p. 128). Gade's conclusion: same-sex acts were "not punishable by Church or secular law, except as a sin mentioned in the penitentials" (p. 135). Bishop Þorlákr (1178) set nine to ten years' penance, the same as bestiality or incest (pp. 126-27).
- **The knife in Norse law is a punishment.** Frostaþing punished bestiality with castration and outlawry, and the animal was drowned at sea (p. 127). This is the only Scandinavian castration penalty.
- **The law punished the word, not the act.** Grágás (Staðarhólsbók, vígslóði ch. 376): "There are three words" (*ragr, stroðinn, sorðinn*) that bring outlawry on the speaker, "A man also has the right to kill for these three words" (p. 132). The general rule: "A man who was said to have been used as a woman was allowed to kill his slanderer" (p. 132). Slandering the dead in these terms also brought outlawry. Gulaþing chs. 138 and 197 carry parallel insult clauses (n. 35-36). All three words name the *passive* partner (n. 37); the active role carried no such stigma (p. 132).
- **The killing words were too dangerous to write.** Skaldic *níð* survives accusing men of bestiality, sodomy and childbearing, but "there are no skaldic poems containing the three infamous words" (p. 133). The verse with the *níðstöng* in *Bjarnar saga* had five and a half lines cut from every manuscript, because the descendants were still alive (p. 135).
- **Níð is a staged image.** The *níðstöng* in *Bjarnar saga* ch. 17 showed two men, one bent before the other, the one in front wearing a blue hat: the attribute of Óðinn, "a god charged with homosexual behavior" (p. 134). Bjǫrn follows it with a verse calling Þórðr "brave as a goat", which plays on *ragr sem geit* (*ragr* as both coward and passive partner, p. 134). Edda flytings aim the charge at Loki and Óðinn, the latter linked to seiðr (p. 133).
- **The insult falls out of later law.** After Járnsíða and Jónsbók (from 1271) the *ragr* clause is gone. What remains is a rule against saying a man "has submitted to disgrace" (*þegit skömm á sér*), which Gade reads as failing to prosecute an injury, not as sodomy (pp. 132-33).
- **Shame was a matter of free status.** Male rape appears only in literature (the priest Bjǫrn in *Guðmundar saga dýra*; the island episode in *Bjarnar saga*), never in law (pp. 133-34). For a thrall "there seems to have been nothing derogatory in a slave playing that part" (p. 136). Gulaþing ch. 198 compensates only the *owner* of an abused male thrall.
