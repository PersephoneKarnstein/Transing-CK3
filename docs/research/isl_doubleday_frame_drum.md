# Doubleday, "The Frame Drum in the Middle East" (1999): design reading

Source: Veronica Doubleday, "The Frame Drum in the Middle East: Women, Musical Instruments and Power", *Ethnomusicology* 43.1 (Winter 1999): 101–134. JSTOR 852696.

**What was read.** All of it: the text (pp. 101–128), the notes (pp. 129–131) and the reference list (pp. 131–134), from a text extraction kept outside the repo. Page numbers are the printed ones. The two figures (pp. 115, 120, 122) were not read; the extraction garbles them.

## 0. Framing warning

- **Two kinds of evidence, of very different value.**
  1. **First-hand fieldwork in Herat, Afghanistan, 1973–77 and 1994** (n. 2, p. 129). This is modern ethnography. It shows patterns, not 1066 practice.
  2. **A historical survey that is secondhand throughout.** The early-Islamic material comes from Farmer (1929), Roy Choudhury (1957) and Robson (1938). Nothing was checked against an Arabic edition.
- **The ancient sections lean on goddess-spirituality writers** (Long 1992; Buonaventura 1983) for "sacred marriage", temple "harlots" and the "Dance of the Seven Veils" (pp. 119, 130 nn. 22–23). Those readings are contested. **Build nothing on them.** The Napoleonic "four hundred *ghawāzī* beheaded" (p. 121) also comes from Buonaventura, and is unchecked.
- **Her broad chronology is dated.** Examples: "purdah" "fully established" 150 years after the Prophet (p. 111, after Levy 1957), and Sufi orders excluding women "by the late tenth century" (p. 113). Treat these as background, not dates.
- **Informants.** Doubleday names her teacher, other musicians and a source for n. 17. This note does not; it calls them "a Herati woman musician" and the like.
- **One modern community, mentioned only.** Note 17 (p. 130) describes eunuchs (*isāk*) in pre-war Kabul who wore long hair, make-up and women's shawls and drummed in the street for tips. The information came from a single informant. It is modern and concerns living people's recent past. **No content is built on it.**

## 1. Findings

### 1.1 The instrument and its names

- **Names:** *duff* (Arabia and the west), *daff/def* (Turkey, Iran and eastward), *dāʾira/doira* (Iran and Central Asia), *ṭār* (Arabia). Also *ghirbāl* (a "sieve"), *mazhar*, *riqq*, and the Hebrew *tof* (p. 102).
- **Abbasid-period terms**, after Farmer: *duff, ghirbāl, bandair, ṭār, mazhar, ṭiryāl, shaqf* (n. 3, p. 129; secondhand).
- **Build:** single-headed and played with bare hands. Bells, rings, chains or metal discs may be added, and the skin may be painted (pp. 101–102).
- **Gender, in general.** Women own no instrument exclusively, not even this one. In Iraq, women traditionally play only idiophones and a few membranophones, the frame drum among them (Hassan, via p. 102). Elite women had a wider range (p. 102). "All-women space" is the main setting for women's music (p. 103).

### 1.2 The *duff* in early Islam (secondhand, via Farmer, Roy Choudhury and Robson)

- **Lawful at weddings.** The hadith "Publish the marriage and beat the *ghirbāl*" (Farmer, via p. 112). ʿĀʾisha has the Prophet send musicians to a wedding she arranged (p. 112).
- **The *duff* is the centre of the lawfulness debate**, being "among the instruments referred to in the sayings of Mohammad" (Poché, via p. 111).
- **Women's earlier public uses** (p. 109):
  - greeting an arrival with improvised blessings (the girls of the Najjār);
  - drumming from rooftops;
  - battle songs (Hind bint ʿUtba at Uḥud);
  - laments for the dead.
- **Lament.** Elegy and lament were "the prerogative of women", sung to the frame drum (p. 109). Little girls drummed and lamented the dead of Badr before the Prophet (n. 7, p. 129, after Robson). In **fifteenth-century Cairo**, female professional mourners used frame drums, and the authorities "periodically banned" them (n. 7, p. 130, after Ahmed 1992 p. 119). That is Mamluk and secondhand: it is not 1066 evidence.
- **Singing-girls (*qaynāt*)** of the pre-Islamic period were "of slave descent, often Greek or Persian", and used frame drums (p. 109).

### 1.3 The *mukhannath* and the drum (Farmer, via Doubleday)

- **Farmer's claim** (via p. 112): after the Prophet's death, women's "commanding position" in music declined. Under ʿUthmān (644–56) came "the first appearance of the male professional musician (*mukhannath*)", "an effeminate class who dyed their hands and affected the habits of women".
- **Ṭuways** (632–710) "accompanied himself on a square *duff* which he carried in a bag or in his robe" (Farmer pp. 52–53, via p. 112).
- **Touma's eunuch claim.** Touma calls Ṭuways the first man with access to the frame drum, a "privilege undoubtedly accorded on account of his being a eunuch" (via n. 9, p. 129). Doubleday herself finds Touma's evidence "unclear". **This is wrong:** see Cross-checks.
- She also guesses that the drum's "patron saint", ʿAmr b. Umayya al-Ḍamrī, who played at Fāṭima's wedding, was a eunuch (n. 8, p. 129). This is a guess with no source.

### 1.4 Zealots against the drum, and patrons who protect it (Robson's Ibn Abī l-Dunyā, via pp. 112–113)

- **Ibn Masʿūd** (d. c. 652) patrolled the gateways of residential quarters "through which women needed to pass", and he and his companions "publicly tore up" the girls' frame drums (p. 112).
- **The tract's hostile sayings** (p. 112):
  - "The angels do not enter a house in which is a tambourine" (Suwayd b. Ghafala);
  - "The tambourine does not pertain at all to the practice of the Muslims" (al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī).
- **ʿĀṣim b. Hubayra** "never saw a *duff* without breaking it". When, in old age, he could not break one, he said: "Not one of their devils has overcome me but this" (p. 113).
- **Doubleday's reading:** the zealots could not touch "musical instruments played at court which were protected by the caliphs" (p. 113). Girls in the street were "easy targets" (p. 113).

### 1.5 Sufis take the drum

- **Exclusion.** Sufi brotherhoods came to exclude women while using frame drums in their own all-male rites (p. 113).
- **Majd al-Dīn al-Ghazālī (d. 1126)** (that is, Aḥmad al-Ghazālī, the brother of the *Iḥyāʾ*'s author; the identification is ours).
  - He allows music only in Sufi ritual. He condemns the instruments "of diversion" but upholds the frame drum as lawful on two "sound" hadiths (Robson, via p. 113).
  - He bars beardless youths from the gathering, or seats them "behind the men", and allows no window "through which women can look" (via p. 113).
  - **His bells.** The drum is "the cycle of existing things", and its five small bells (*jalājil*) are the prophetic, saintly, apostolic, caliphal and imamate ranks (via p. 124).
- **Sacred drums kept apart** (modern, pp. 123–124):
  - Iraqi Sufi drums hang in the *takiyya* when not in use.
  - The *mazhar* "stays in the mosque" and is dedicated to a saint (Touma, via p. 123).
  - The Yazidi *daff* is carried wrapped in cloth by a religious musician. When an outsider touched one, it had to be purified with water (Hassan, via p. 123).
- **Her conclusion, "techniques of exclusivity"** (p. 127). Men take instruments by:
  1. keeping them in all-male space;
  2. passing technique through all-male teaching lines;
  3. physically modifying them so they become "exclusive".

### 1.6 Older and Greek-side material (secondhand)

- **Egypt.** In the New Kingdom, female troupes under women played the drum in temple rites. A *sed*-festival procession is headed by two women drummers (pp. 106–107).
- **Hebrew Bible.** Miriam's *tof* (Exodus 15) and Jephthah's daughter "with tambourines" (p. 107). In Psalm 68's procession into the sanctuary, women play tambourines among the singers and minstrels (p. 107).
- **Cybele.** The frame drum, double flute and cymbals were used by "her priests" in Rome (Scott 1957, via p. 108). Maenads played the *tympanon* for Dionysus (p. 108).
- **Persia.** Little evidence for the frame drum in ancient Persian music. The *Shāhnāma* does not name one (p. 110).

### 1.7 Modern Afghan patterns (fieldwork; not 1066 evidence)

- **Weddings.** They are the main lawful setting for women's drumming. The groom's women must drum, sing and dance, or hire women professionals. Veiled women may drum even in public wedding processions (p. 117).
- **A drum shared** among neighbouring households, not owned by one person (n. 14, p. 130).
- **A seasonal licence.** The spring New Year once allowed women to make music outdoors, until guards stopped it in the mid-1970s (p. 117).
- **Protection.** Drumming after a birth wards off jinn. The forty days after childbirth are a time of danger (pp. 118, 126).
- **Stigma.**
  - Men call the drum a women's toy and a "non-instrument" (pp. 118, 125).
  - Its makers are outsider groups (p. 116).
  - Women professionals collecting tips in the drum is "seen as shameful" (p. 118).
  - She says of the Herati women professionals she worked with: "not prostitutes, but they were labelled as such" (n. 25, p. 130).
- **A defence of the drum.** Two theology students refused to call a man singing to his own drum "music". Their logic, as Doubleday puts it: the drum is lawful, so its sound is not sinful, so it is not music (p. 118).
- **Bans in crisis.** In the refugee camps of the 1980s, resistance leaders banned all music by declaring "permanent mourning". In practice this banned women's drumming (p. 121).

## 2. Cross-checks

- **Ṭuways was not a eunuch.** This contradicts Touma (n. 9). In `hist_rowson_mukhannathun.md`, Ṭuways married and had children (Rowson 1991a p. 681). Rowson argues that his name was wrongly added to the castration list, because he was dead by 711 (p. 691 n. 152; table rows 13 and 34). `isl_mukhannath_recent_studies.md` (Aziz, item 10) records the same error in another author. **Do not use Doubleday nn. 8–9 for eunuch claims.**
- **The square *duff* carried "in a bag or in his robe"** (p. 112, Farmer) matches the novice's bag in Rowson 2003 p. 61 (Baghdad note §2.3, Lawza and Jawza). It also matches Rowson 1991a's square *duff* (`hist_rowson_mukhannathun.md`, glossary). These agree.
- **The lawful wedding drum.**
  - The Fatimid *Daʿāʾim* makes the wedding drum *required*, "the distinction between marriage and debauchery", and forbids the *kūba* (Cairo note §2.2, 2:206–207).
  - Doubleday's hadith (p. 112) is the Sunni side of the same rule. Both legal traditions protect the frame drum at weddings.
- **Al-Ghazālī and his brother.** The Baghdad note §2.6 event 10 has Abū Ḥāmid al-Ghazālī allow the *duff* (Macdonald p. 237). Doubleday's Majd al-Dīn (Aḥmad al-Ghazālī) upholds the frame drum too, but only in Sufi rites with women kept out (p. 113). Both write after 1095.
- **Women drummers in Fatimid Cairo.** Nasab the drummer-woman before al-Mustanṣir in 450/1058 (Cairo note §3.2) fits Doubleday's general claim that elite women had wider access (p. 102). Doubleday adds no Fatimid evidence.
- **The *kūba*.** Doubleday does not mention it. The Baghdad note's verification (al-Ghazālī, *Iḥyāʾ* 2:272) stands alone.
- **Cybele's drum.** `byz_roller_god_the_mother.md` ("Iconography" section, Roller p. 109) records that the tympanum belongs to the Greek and Roman Meter, not the Phrygian Matar. Doubleday's Roman priests (p. 108) are later still. Any Adelphai line using the drum is Greek-and-Roman, not Phrygian.
