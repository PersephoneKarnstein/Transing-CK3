# Liudprand of Cremona, *Complete Works* (trans. Squatriti, 2007): design reading

**Citation (checked against the PDF).** *The Complete Works of Liudprand of Cremona*, translated with an introduction and notes by Paolo Squatriti (Medieval Texts in Translation), Washington, D.C.: The Catholic University of America Press, 2007. c. 300 pp. (index ends p. 298). ISBN 978-0-8132-1506-8 (pbk). Translates Paolo Chiesa's Latin edition. Contents: *Retribution* (*Antapodosis*, c. 958–62), *Homily*, *Concerning King Otto*, *The Embassy of Liudprand* (*Relatio de legatione Constantinopolitana*, 968–69). **What was read:** every passage the index and a full-text search give for eunuchs, castration and *carzimasia* (*Retribution* 3.26, 4.9–10, 5.32, 6.4–6, 6.10; *Embassy* 29, 46, 50, 62–63, with Squatriti's notes), plus the surrounding chapters of the 949 embassy (*Retribution* 6.3–10). The rest was not read. The PDF does not print page numbers; pages below are the printed ones, reconstructed from the index and running heads.

**Read with care.** Liudprand is a hostile, comic, Latin witness. His *Embassy* is a deliberate satire of Nikephoros II's court for Otto I. Use him for what he saw and reports as fact (the gift, the merchants, the pay day); treat his jokes as Latin prejudice.

**Relevance:** high for the boy trade ("buys them as boys") and for prisoner castration; moderate for court eunuchs in the 10th c.; none for adoption or Dorylaion.

## 1. The argument in brief

### The *carzimasia* and the Verdun merchants (*Retribution* 6.6, p. 199)
- On his first embassy (949, for Berengar II), Liudprand had no gifts from his master, so he presented his own as Berengar's: breastplates, gilded shields, silver cups, swords, spears, "and four *carzimasia* slaves, to this emperor the most precious of all these things" (p. 199).
- The definition: "the Greeks call a child-eunuch, with testicles and penis cut off, a *carzimasium*. The merchants of Verdun do this on account of the immense profit they can make, and they are accustomed to bring them to Spain" (p. 199).
- Three facts for the design: (a) these were **children**; (b) the cut was total (testicles and penis); (c) the supply chain ran from Frankish Verdun to al-Andalus, and the Byzantine court was a buyer of the same product. The four boys were the gift Liudprand judged the emperor would value most.
- Context: the reception at the Magnaura with the mechanical lions and singing birds; Liudprand was led in "leaning on the shoulders of two eunuchs" (6.5, p. 198). The envoy who travelled with him from Venice, Salemon, was "a palace butler and a eunuch, who had returned from Spain and Saxony" (6.4, p. 197): eunuch diplomats crossed the same routes as the trade.

### Castrating prisoners as a diplomatic gift (*Retribution* 4.9–10, pp. 146–147)
- Margrave Tedbald (Theobald) of Spoleto, fighting the Byzantines in southern Italy (c. 929–34), captured garrison troops, "amputated their virile members", and wrote to their strategos: "Since I discovered that nothing is more precious to your holy emperor than eunuchs, I respectfully endeavor to send him just these few for now, but will send him some more as soon as possible" (p. 146).
- The counter-story (4.10): a captive's wife came to Tedbald's tent and said he made war on women, for "you remove not what is theirs but what is ours when you turn them into eunuchs". The camp laughed and she won her husband back "intact" with her animals. Her parting line: let Tedbald take her husband's "eyes, nostrils, hands, and feet", but "leave alone what is mine" (p. 147).
- Liudprand treats both as wit. For the design it is plain evidence that castration of captives was a joke and a gift in the 10th-century Latin–Greek frontier.

### Punitive castration in the Latin West (*Retribution* 5.32, p. 194)
- Willa, wife of Berengar II, was accused with her chaplain; the "priestlet" was "sent away with his manly attributes cut off". A Western instance of castration as private punishment for a sexual offence, told as farce.

### Eunuchs in the 10th-century court (as Liudprand saw them)
- **The guardian of the heir.** At his death in 912 Leo VI left his brother Alexander and the child Constantine VII, and "to mind the palace and protect the family property, he entrusted a eunuch in the position of head of the imperial bedchamber, as the custom is there" (3.26, p. 122). Romanos Lekapenos then demanded that this eunuch and the magnates swear for his safety (p. 123).
- **The pay day (6.10, pp. 199–201).** The week before Palm Sunday the emperor paid salaries in gold by rank, from the *rector* and the commanders down to "the immense horde of first swordsmen, swordsmen, swordsmen-in-training, chamberlains, treasurers". Those who received less than a pound were paid "throughout the whole week before Easter" by "the chief of the imperial bedchamber, not the emperor" (p. 201). The chamber eunuchs ran the lower payroll.
- **The eunuch general (*Embassy* 29, p. 256).** Nikephoros II put at the head of his Italian army "a sort of man—I called him 'sort of man' since he ceased to be male and did not become a woman—to insult you." Squatriti's note: eunuchs were used for "their inability to form biological families" and "presumed greater loyalty" (n. 58).
- **The eunuch minister (*Embassy* 46, 50, pp. 267, 269).** "The eunuch Christopher the patrician, who managed affairs in Nicephoros's place", held Liudprand under guard, then received him courteously.
- **Eunuch bishops (*Embassy* 62–63, pp. 278–279).** Romanos I "established his son the eunuch Theophylact as patriarch". At Leukas Liudprand was treated "most inhumanely" by "the eunuch-bishop of that place"; Greek bishops were "capons, that is, eunuchs, which is not canonical" (p. 279).

- **Agrees with the Guilland note** (which quotes Tedbald via Liudprand). This note adds the source's context and the *carzimasia* passage, which Guilland does not cite.
- **Primary-source cases usable as events or characters:** the four *carzimasia* boys (949); Salemon, eunuch envoy back from Córdoba and Saxony (949); Tedbald's letter and the wife's rescue of her husband (c. 930); the chamber eunuch guardian of Constantine VII (912); Christopher the eunuch patrician (968). All are after 867 but inside the game's range.
- **Constraint check:** the *carzimasia* material involves children. Keep events about rescue and adoption; do not depict the cutting.
