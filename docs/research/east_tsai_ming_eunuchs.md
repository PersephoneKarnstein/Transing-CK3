## 1. Book map

**Tsai's thesis (pp. 6–9, 221–230).** Ming eunuchs were not "a minor if bizarre adjunct" but "a fully developed third branch of Ming administration" (p. 221), "a third administrative hierarchy through which emperors could, and did, exercise their power in all areas of government" (p. 9). The Confucian historiography that scapegoats them ("palace termites," p. 8) is itself the product of the scholar-official class's rivalry with eunuchs for control of the imperial apparatus; both groups "were really the pawns of the colossal Ming institutions and absolutism" (p. 7). Eunuchism "could thrive only under the worst kind of governmental system; namely, monarchic absolutism" (p. 221). Rhetorically Tsai is sympathetic to eunuchs as a class but calls castration "the worst form of human exploitation" (p. 221) and repeatedly uses "deficient man," "half-men," "neither men nor women" language himself (pp. 1, 4, 26).

## 2. Institution dossier

### 2.1 The Yuan inheritance and Tang/Song antecedents (as Tsai gives them) **[PRE-1453 ROOT]**

Tsai's pre-Ming sketch is brief (pp. 11–13) and derivative; treat as orientation only.

- **Antiquity.** "Nobody knows when and how eunuchs were first institutionalized in China except that castration was frequently used as a substitute for the death penalty. In China, palace eunuchs were called *huanguan* which was a recognized official title during the Shang dynasty (1765–1222 B.C.) as it appeared on the Shang oracle bones. *Zhouli* (Rites of the Zhou) also used a variety of terms when it referred to this group of castrated men." (p. 11)
- **The stereotype's antiquity.** Pei, the eunuch who "served under three mutually hostile dukes during the sixth century B.C.," became "a stereotype eunuch—a person of sycophancy, ruthlessness, treachery, greed, and luxury." The *Book of Odes*: "Not heaven but women and eunuchs / Bring misfortunes to mankind. / Wives and those without balls / Bleat with similar voices." (pp. 11–12) — **the oldest attested Chinese pairing of women and eunuchs as a single class of court danger.**
- **Qin.** Qin Shihuangdi "established a new agency called Zhongchangshi for the sole purpose of managing the ever-increasing number of court eunuchs." (p. 12)
- **Eastern Han.** "six dowager empresses subsequently promoted court eunuchs to powerful positions"; after He Jin's murder his deputies "put to death more than 2,000 eunuchs as means of retribution." (p. 12)
- **Tang (in-game period, 867–906).** "During the Tang rule, eunuchs were appointed commanders of palace guards and supervisors of the army and allowed to participate in the decision-making process ... Near the end of the dynasty, Tang eunuchs became virtual king makers, because they were instrumental in selecting seven of the last eight Tang emperors ... In 903, the warlord Zhu Quanzhong arrived in Xian and executed 162 court eunuchs and their collaborators. He later arrested and killed 300 more and ordered every eunuch who had been involved in military affairs to commit suicide. With most of the eunuchs gone, so went the Tang dynasty." (p. 12) Cross-ref Hoeckelmann pass; nothing here is new.
- **Five Dynasties / Song.** "very few eunuchs rose to prominent positions" in the Five Dynasties; the Song founder "took measures to keep his castrated servants under control"; but "several eunuchs were given military command and, in fact, a very influential eunuch by the name of Tong Guan, who at one time commanded an army of 800,000 men, was later blamed for causing the downfall of the North Song." (pp. 12–13) Also: "As early as 987 A.D., a Song emperor sent eight eunuchs to Southeast Asia to purchase spice, rhinoceros horns, pearls, and other exotic commodities such as ambergris" (p. 145) — a Song precedent for eunuch overseas commerce.
- **Song law on self-castration (implied).** "In the previous dynasties self-castration was permitted if closely monitored by the officials, who recorded the day of emasculation, examined the wound, and checked every detail during the healing process. However, since the establishment of the Ming dynasty, self-castration had been forbidden" (p. 19). Tsai does not name which dynasties; this is the book's only hint of a pre-Ming *regulated* voluntary castration regime (cross-check against Chiang/Sommer passes).
- **Yuan.** "When China was under the Mongol rule, 1279–1368, eunuchs were rarely active in the political arena. Nevertheless, Kublai Khan (1216?–1294) commissioned a eunuch named Guo Shoujing to construct a section of the Grand Canal near Beijing. And in 1348, Jia Lu was appointed a *Taijian*, or grand eunuch, to manage flood control and maintain China's hydraulic infrastructure. *Taijian*, a unique eunuch title in China's officialdom, was thus created and henceforth became an important element in Chinese history." (p. 13) — Tsai's claim: the title *taijian* is a **Yuan creation (1348)**, i.e. post-game-start but in-period. (Note: Guo Shoujing is normally known as an astronomer-engineer, not a eunuch; Tsai's statement is as given.)
- **Yuan eunuch families.** Zheng He's ancestor "Saiyid Ajall" was appointed governor of Yunnan by Kublai in 1274; the family "served their Mongol rulers with loyalty and dexterity" for a century (p. 155). Yuan Muslim elite → Ming eunuch: a lineage path.
- **Tsai's own Han/Tang vs Ming contrast** (p. 222): Han and Tang apogees arose because "emperors tended to die young, whereas their eunuchs, who were entrenched in powerful positions, enjoyed longevity ... hence also gaining trust from the dowager empresses with whom the eunuchs also had established a long and intimate association." Ming eunuchs by contrast were "more numerous and also had penetrated deeper into practically every governmental agency." "with the exception of Cao Jixiang in the late 1450s, no Ming eunuch dared to depose a crown prince or select his own imperial candidate as did the eunuchs in the Later Han and the Tang." (p. 223)

### 2.2 The Twenty-Four Offices (templates for court positions)

Structure (pp. 31–32, 231–233): **12 Directorates (*jian*), 4 Departments (*si*), 8 Bureaus (*ju*)**. After the 1395 reorganization "Each directorate had a director ranking 4a, two deputies (4b), two assistants (5a), one recorder (6a), plus various numbers of attendants (6a). High-ranking eunuch officials wore specially designed uniforms that would distinguish their positions. For example, eunuchs with 4b rank and above wore red robes, while those with 5a and below attired in blue. Eunuchs under fifteen years of age, on the other hand, could dress only in plain robes and wear a small black hat." (p. 32) Earlier (1384) directors ranked only 7a; Hongwu "had twice promoted the directors of major eunuch offices from 7a to 6a and then to 4a" (p. 32). The *Ming Zhiguanzhi* limited eunuch ranks "to between 8a and 5a" (p. 30).

### 2.3 The Neishutang school and the eunuch career ladder (sponsor/mentor row)

- **Founding.** Xuande "established a classroom known as Neishutang, or Inner Court Study, and appointed four scholars from the Hanlin Academy to teach some 200 to 300 young eunuchs whose average age was about ten." (p. 13) Teachers included Liu Zhong (Ministry of Punishment secretary and "royal reading companion") and grand secretary Chen Shan, who "actually had taught hundreds of teen-aged castrati before he retired" (p. 42).
- **Enrolment rite — the "sage."** "On the day the young castrato, usually around ten years old, was to be enrolled at the school, he first came through the Beian Gate and, after being introduced to all the faculty, he had to kowtow to his own 'sage,' a designated master who would guide him throughout his school years. He was required to pay his sage a white candle, a handkerchief, and a dragon-shaped incense stick as token tuition." (p. 42) **This is the book's clearest sponsor/mentor institution: a one-to-one designated master, sealed by a ritual gift.**
- **Curriculum.** The 100 surnames and 1,000 characters, then "the Filial Piety Classic, the Four Books, poetry, and so on. He recited and copied the lines from the books hundreds of times, sometimes without understanding their meaning. If he was ambitious enough, he would hire a private tutor ... Bright and more advanced pupils often could get part-time jobs in various scribe offices; slower pupils and those who violated school rules always received various degrees of punishment. In general, relationships between the sage teachers and their castrated pupils were cordial and warm." Days off: state holidays plus the 1st and 15th. "Every afternoon, before they were dismissed, each one was required to read aloud a poem he had written. And when they left the schoolyard, the boys walked in droves and always received polite and respectable greetings from onlookers." (p. 42)
- **Output.** "Learned eunuchs were usually assigned to copy documents, scribe imperial proclamations and decrees, and even to draft criminal prosecutions, convictions, and verdicts." (p. 42) Liu Ruoyu "had been studying for the civil service examinations when he got himself castrated in 1589. He was then fourteen years old but quickly found scribal work in the palace" — author of the memoir *Zhuozhong zhi* (p. 42).
- **Model career (Feng Bao, pp. 106–108).** Neishutang student under Hanlin scholars → 1538 scribe at Silijian for fifteen years → 1553 seals office → 1560 *bingbi* (python gown, jade belt, chair, horse) → 1567 Eastern Depot director + Imperial Stables → 1572 *zhangyin* "the highest position any eunuch could hope to attain." Emperor Wanli as a child "used to ride on Feng Bao's shoulders and ... affectionately called Feng Bao his 'Big Companion.'" Fall 1582–83: banished to Nanjing; "his brother and an adopted son, both of whom served as high-ranking military commissioners, were also arrested ... starved to death in the state penitentiary. Eight of his former assistants were also demoted to burn incense in imperial mausolea or do other odd jobs." (p. 108)
- **Model career (Chen Ju, pp. 109–110).** Castrated "before he reached his ninth birthday"; recorder (6a) by 1583; patron of books, "those published during the Han, Tang, and Song dynasties"; "through his influence and inspiration his own brother, Chen Wance, earned a doctorate in 1592"; as Eastern Depot director "the court-yard in the torture chamber was overgrown with weeds." Returned home "to pay respect to his ancestors but took pains to avoid the pomp."
- **Indoctrination.** Ming eunuchs "were, in general, better trained and more heavily indoctrinated with two of the Confucian virtues, loyalty and filial piety. Since childhood, the young castrati were instilled with a deep and abiding affection for the Son of Heaven ... the eunuchs were more willing to dedicate their lives to the service of their 'sage,' as they fondly called their emperor. Like their literati counterparts, the eunuchs studied the teachings of Confucius, Mencius, the Buddha, and Laozi." (p. 224) Note the doubling: the schoolmaster is a "sage"; the emperor is "sage."
- **Patron eunuchs.** Wei Zhongxian "entered the palace service" "through a relative of his mother" (p. 4) and had "his former patron" Wang An "demoted and then murdered" (p. 5). Liu Jin "was adopted by a court eunuch named Liu" (p. 103). Yishiha "worked under two powerful early Ming eunuchs, Wang Zhen and Cao Jixiang" (p. 129). Wang Zhi rose "After serving an imperial concubine for several years" (p. 115); Yishiha likely rose "serving imperial concubines of Manchurian origin" (p. 129) — **palace women as sponsors**.
- **Precarity.** "Practically one out of every two prominent Ming eunuchs ended his career in execution or exile to do hard labor in the frontier." (p. 224) "it became a Ming tradition for a new emperor to wipe the slate clean by launching a purge against all the powerful eunuchs of his predecessor." (pp. 224–225) Demotion "to be only a common castrato" (Zhang Yong, p. 66).

### 2.4 Recruitment, screening, and the "purgatory" of Nanhaizi

- Recruits were "thoroughly examined by the palace head eunuch who made sure that Wei's 'treasure parts' had been permanently severed from his body" (p. 4).
- "the forty-square-li Nanhaizi was primarily an imperial hunting preserve, but also functioned as a reserve camp, where hundreds of thousands of castrati were temporarily detained, waiting to be interviewed for palace work ... all self-castrated males were carefully screened here and not every castrato was permitted to stay. Those who were allowed to stay would be under strict supervision of a eunuch director and four intendant grand eunuchs. These vagabond castrati were then assigned to work in twenty-four different laboring units ... Whenever the Inner Court needed new eunuchs, the prospective castrati were brought to an auditorium in the Ministry of Rites, interviewed, and examined by high-ranking grand eunuchs from the Ceremonial Directorate. Those selected were then led out of a back door of the Ministry to spend a night in a vegetable depot in Nanhaizi. At dawn the next day, they were ushered through the Dongan Gate and again interviewed and carefully checked by the grand eunuchs in the Palace Servants Directorate. If they passed this test, they would then be given each a black wooden tablet and would be assembled in Coal Hill for job assignments ... Nanhaizi might be compared to an earthly purgatory from which the self-castrated young volunteers could climb to the paradise and work for the emperor." (p. 51)
- The unemployed castrate underclass: "A group of less fortunate castrati derogatorily called *wumingbai*, or 'white without name'—implying that they had lost their sexual organs surgically but still could not work for the emperor—always dawdled about there and were ready and willing to scrub, rub, and massage their more fortunate counterparts for meager tips." (p. 48)

### 2.5 Eunuch "families": adoption, nephews, brothers, palace women, property

Tsai has **no section on eunuch marriage or *duishi*** (the term does not occur in the book); the kinship evidence is scattered and mostly agnatic (brothers/nephews/adopted sons). Record for the plausibility matrix:

- **Adoption as norm.** "If each of the 100,000 eunuchs on average had retained two household servants and also adopted a son or supported a nephew, that would mean that the total eunuch personnel the Ming government had to feed would be around 300,000." (p. 34)
- **Adoption into a eunuch lineage.** Liu Jin, "originally surnamed Tan, Liu Jin chose to have a 'self-castration' when he was a small child and was adopted by a court eunuch named Liu." (p. 103) — **eunuch adopts castrated boy, boy takes eunuch's surname.**
- Feng Bao's "adopted son" was a "high-ranking military commissioner" (p. 108).
- **Nephews ennobled.** Cao Jixiang "was made the ceremonial director and one of his nephews, an earl" (p. 61). After Bao Zhong (d. 1548) and Huang Jin (d. 1567) "several of their brothers and nephews received hereditary military positions" (p. 218). Pan Hong (Palace Foods) in 1464 "begged Emperor Chenghua to allow his nephew Pan Gui to buy his salt from the government" (p. 175). Wei Zhongxian "made his nephew Wei Fumin director of the Seal Office" (p. 197); "thirty-five of Wei's relatives were banished to the frontier" (p. 6). Huang Jin "freely brought his own relatives to fill the ranks in both the depot and the embroidered-uniform guard" (p. 106).
- **Castration as family strategy.** Wei Zhongxian "was briefly married to a peasant girl who gave him a daughter" before castration (p. 3). Fathers castrate sons: an elderly princely guard "cut off his son's genitals before asking for a eunuch position for his son" (1424, p. 19); Li An in 1484 "operated on four teenage boys at the request of their fathers" (p. 23); Hongzhi "even had the mother of a self-castrated boy brought to justice" (p. 23). "Destitute men castrated themselves or their children so that they could find employment in the princely establishments." (p. 19)
- **Slavery euphemism.** "When wealthy families purchased slaves, they put in the bond such words of euphemism as *adopted sons* or *adopted daughters* or simply called them employed servants." (p. 27)
- **Palace women as allies/intimates.** Eunuchs "needed to align with powerful eunuchs and collaborate with influential palace women" (p. 5); Wei's rise via wet nurse Mistress Ke, "beaten to death" in 1628 (pp. 5–6); Feng Bao "maintained an intimate relationship with the empress dowager Cisheng" (p. 107); Han/Tang eunuchs' "long and intimate association" with dowagers (p. 222). "Stories about the eunuchs' intimacy with both the palace women attendants and the imperial family are abundant" (p. 5). Palace women and eunuchs are *co-gifted* as a set: Xuande "gave him twenty-five palace women and twenty eunuchs" (p. 20).
- **Property.** Hongzhi "periodically confiscated properties of deceased eunuchs and gave them to the victims of drought, locusts, earthquake, or flooding" (p. 23) — eunuch estates reverted to the crown, i.e. **no secure inheritance**. Cao Jixiang's "relatives were put to death and their properties confiscated" (p. 61). Feng Bao's confiscated household held "musical instruments and artwork of historical importance" (p. 108). Wei left Beijing "with a guard of 800 eunuchs, 1,000 horses, and 40 wagonloads of jewelry" (p. 6).

### 2.6 Religion: temples, cemeteries, Buddhist/Daoist/Lamaist/Muslim/Christian eunuchs, burial

- **Buddhism as the eunuch religion.** "In general, eunuchs who worked in this school [Hanjingchang] had already been converted to Buddhism and functioned like Buddhist monks. Ming eunuchs, as a whole, believed deeply in the future life and made certain that when they died they would be buried in Buddhist monasteries. Some of them became patrons of the monks and contributed to the construction of Buddhist temples. Others even set up their own prayer room inside the Imperial City and offered up prayers periodically to the Buddha. Elderly eunuchs, who had demonstrated religious piety and mastery of Buddhist rituals, were assigned to burn incense in a number of Buddhist temples where His Majesty and his family occasionally visited and worshipped." (p. 55) The Hanjingchang itself was "a special Buddhist school ... where the court ladies learned Buddhist scriptures," with ceremonies on the emperor's birthday and the 15th of the 7th moon at which "honest and pious palace women ... perform Buddhist ceremonies, offer incense, and pray in front of the altar" (pp. 54–55). **Women + eunuchs sharing one liturgical space inside the palace.**
- **Preserved genitals (*bao*), eunuch cemeteries.** **Not in Tsai.** The word *bao* appears only as "treasure" in seal names (*Huangdi fengtian zhi bao*, etc., p. 190) and in "treasure parts" (p. 4). Eunuch burial as he gives it: cremation at the Jingletang pagodas with a copper tablet and coffin from Neiguanjian (p. 49), or burial "in Buddhist monasteries" (p. 55). No cemetery enclave described; Wanyiju "surrounded by a cluster of Buddhist temples" (p. 49) is the nearest thing to a spatial eunuch enclave. (Flag for cross-check against Qing-era sources in other passes.)
- **Lamaism.** "Yongle, Chenghua and Zhengde, were also attracted to Tibetan Lamaism ... invited Tibetan lamas to the Ming capital. The Tibetan high monks were given the title *imperial teachers* ... the Ming court established the Fanjingchang, or the School of Barbarian Religion ... Eunuchs who worked in this school donned Tibetan monk hats, wore red frock coats with yellow collars, and offered candles, oil lamps and incense to a huge black-faced, long-bearded idol." (p. 55)
- **Daoism and exorcism.** "holders of the Zhang Tianshi (also called Heavenly Teachers and previously from the five-bushel-of-rice sect) were frequently summoned to the capital ... The Daoist Heavenly Teacher was admitted into the most sacred chambers of the palace for ceremonies of exorcism ... Eunuchs who studied with the Heavenly Teachers also performed the exorcism rituals, which often lasted three days and three nights, but sometimes as long as an entire week" (p. 55). Hongxi in 1425 "bestowed half of a former military settlement in the suburb of Beijing to a Daoist group" (p. 166). Eunuch Li Guang, Hongzhi's "indispensable eunuch," was "especially known for his black magic. Claiming he could make magic water and immortal pills" — suicide after the 1498 palace fire (p. 229).
- **Eunuch astrologer-priests** at the Lingtai (pp. 53–54; see table).
- **Islam.** "Islam did not appear to have attracted a significant percentage of the Ming eunuch population" (pp. 55–56). Zheng He: born 1371 in Yunnan to a *Haji* father and *Haji* grandfather, "a typical prominent Muslim family" descended from Saiyid Ajall (pp. 154–155); castrated as a war captive after 1382 and "given to Emperor Hongwu's fourth son, Zhu Di" (p. 156); renamed Ma→Zheng in 1404 with "five other eunuchs, all from China's frontier regions" (p. 155). His steles: Celestial Spouse Palace, Liujiagang (14 March 1431) and Three Peak Pagoda, Changle (Dec 1431–Jan 1432) (p. 158). In Sri Lanka "the three religious communities ... Buddhist, Hindu and Islamic—received imperial rewards" (p. 163). Interpreter Ma Huan "was a Muslim" (p. 157). Hong Bao sent "from Calicut to Mecca" (Ch. VII).
- **Christianity.** Late-Ming eunuch converts "Pang Tianshou, known to the Europeans as Achilles Pang, and Wang Ruoshe, or Joseph Wang" (p. 56).
- **Eunuch missionary.** Yishiha (Jurchen) "by constructing a Buddhist temple called Yunning, or Forever Tranquil ... attempted to convert the Oroqens and other ethnic groups of the region into Buddhism. In 1414, he ordered the erection of a stone monument near the Yunning Temple on which he scribed his major activities in four different languages—Chinese, Mongolian, Jurchen, and Tibetan." (p. 130)
- **Tomb-guard commandancies as sacral enclaves.** Fengyang: "eight guards under a eunuch commandant" protected Hongwu's ancestral tombs; "Eunuchs stationed in Fengyang took turns lighting incense and candles every day and kept the fires in the tomb temples burning all the time. On the anniversary of the deaths of Hongwu's parents, eunuchs dressed in smocks mourned and prayed for forty-nine days. As well as such duties, the eunuchs in the central metropolis were also in charge of a very special penitentiary. Ming princes and royal magnates, if convicted of crimes, were sent to the central metropolis for confinement." (p. 57) Hongwu Filial Tomb: "several thousand soldiers commanded by eunuchs were permanently stationed" (p. 57). Tianshoushan: "a special eunuch commandant was created to protect and maintain the tombs" of 13 emperors (p. 57). Xianling: "grand defender of Xianling ... took orders directly from the Ceremonial Directorate; accordingly, local authorities were prohibited from entering the land of the imperial estate" (p. 168). Demoted eunuchs sent "to burn incense in imperial mausolea" (p. 108).
- **Religious taxation.** 1516 Zhengde "authorized his eunuch grand defender in the sacred Mountain Tai ... to levy the so-called incense taxes on pilgrims" at the Bixia Si; by late Ming "the revenues generated from the temple of Mountain Tai alone served to pay the salaries of the government officials of Shandong province" (p. 172).
- **Votive temples.** Jingtai (1452) "ordered the construction of two votive temples, one at Heiyangshan ... and the other at Shawan" to river gods after eunuch-led flood works (p. 204); Li Xing's recommendation of a votive temple approved by Hongzhi, 1495 (p. 209).
- **Eastern Depot shrine.** "By custom, all the names of the eunuchs who had directed this agency were inscribed on a stele inside a shrine, located just west of the main hall" beneath a portrait of Yue Fei and the plaque "Chao Ting Xin Fu," "Heart and Bowel of the Court" (p. 98) — an institutional ancestor-cult of eunuch office-holders.

### 2.7 Castration: procedure, knifers, mortality, law, penalties, self-castration waves

- **Procedure (p. 4, sources Millant 1908, Kuwabara 1936, Stent 1877 — n. 2 p. 243).** "a volunteer like Wei, would first have to seek a specialist for the risky operation. Accompanied by a guarantor, who witnessed the operation and also was responsible for the volunteer's payment and recovery, Wei met his surgeon in a small hut. Before the specialist performed the operation, he ritually asked the young volunteer if he would regret it or not. If the answer was negative and firm, the specialist would immediately flash his blade and, in a few minutes, the castration was completed. In addition to excruciating pain, many would-be eunuchs died of excessive bleeding, urine blockage, or other complications. Wei survived; however, it took him several months to heal from the surgery." **Guarantor = sponsor; ritual question = consent rite.**
- **What was cut.** "Some landlords required their tenants to cut off their penes and scrotums before taking them as household servants" (p. 18); Ming generals "often had their captives' penes and scrotums cut off before taking them as slaves" (p. 156) — full emasculation.
- **Sequelae (pp. 4–5).** Loss of height and beard, broader nose, thicker ear lobes, wrinkled skin, "He quickly underwent a change of voice. He now sounded a bit more shrieking and evidently more feminine than masculine ... He soon became soft and fat." Psychological profile via Chen Cunren and Liu Ruoyu: "extremely sensitive about his 'deficiency' ... often paranoid, petty ... peevish, vindictive ... but gentle and loyal to his friends and generous to his relatives." Nightly "worrying about wetting his bed" (p. 1). "he had joined a group of peculiar people who were neither men nor women" (p. 4).
- **Mortality in mass castration.** "In 1460, a Miao tribe in southwest China capitulated and the victorious Ming commander ordered the castration of 1,565 Miao boys. Of these unfortunate souls, 329 died during the brutal operation" (≈21%); Tianshun "upbraided his governor in Guizhou province for arbitrarily undertaking such a large-scale operation" (p. 16).
- **Knifers / private operators.** Li An (1484) "operated on four teenage boys at the request of their fathers. The emperor believed that Li had committed a crime of venality and had him whipped thirty strokes before sending him to a Manchurian military colony. The fathers ... were ordered to work in an iron foundry for three years while the castrated boys were all confiscated by the central government." (p. 23) Ji Gang, Yongle's battalion commander, "castrated several hundred people and trained them as new recruits" (p. 17). 1599: a Beijing official in Shaanxi "ordered castration of several dozen boys without prior approval of the throne. He was later punished" (p. 17). 1566: Wang Xiang "castrated three suspects before he could prove their culpability ... the emperor reportedly went into a rage and ordered Wang Xiang into exile" (p. 18). Landlords in Fujian/Guangdong: 1372 "Emperor Hongwu threatened to castrate those who castrated their servants" (p. 18).
- **Penal castration — abolished but used.** "Near the end of the Hongwu reign, however, the emperor abolished the tattooing, severing body organs, and castration as forms of criminal justice. As substitute for such baneful punishment, he introduced corporal punishment and various forms of flogging. A similar proclamation was issued in 1425 by the fourth emperor Hongxi, in which Confucian filial piety was cited as a reason for prohibiting the pernicious castration punishment. According to this rationale, a son who could not produce a male heir to carry on the family name committed a serious transgression against Confucian doctrine. But in spite of repeated imperial proclamations, Ming monarchs in the later period often disregarded such prohibitory decrees and resorted to castration for punishing major political criminals and their young male relatives." (p. 17) Huai En "because of his uncle's political problems, was castrated when still a child" (p. 102; n. 17 p. 245: "Young Huai En was castrated for a crime he knew nothing about"). "castration remained a savage weapon readily available for the Ming autocrats" (p. 17). "Castration as a means of punishing prisoners of war became a legacy of Ming-Annamese relations" (p. 16); the Annamese in 1469 killed older members of Southeast Asian embassies "while younger members were castrated and sold into slavery" (pp. 15–16). "At a time when castration was performed as a symbol of conquest and a method of revenge, rulers throughout East Asia, with the exception of those in Japan, also adopted eunuchism and often used castrati as tributes." (p. 14)
- **Self-castration — the law.** Forbidden under the Ming (unlike "previous dynasties," p. 19). Hongxi 1424: a Changsha self-castrate "considered ... a lazy vagabond who had failed to observe the Confucian principle of filial piety and had him banished to the frontier as a soldier" (p. 19). Xuande: self-castrates sent "to the border of Annam"; village heads warned "not to conceal any information" (p. 19). 1436: "a Buddhist monk performed his own emasculation. The new emperor Zhengtong not only annulled the monk's corvee exemption but also exiled him to southern Manchuria." (p. 20) Jingtai: soldier's entire family exiled to a frontier colony; civilian "charged with a violation of filial piety and his village heads and neighbors would also be punished if they concealed knowledge of his act"; then **confiscation** — "he literally confiscated them, took them to the capital and put them under the supervision of the Directorate of Ceremonial ... the emperor was forced to stockpile his own 'inventory' of eunuchs" (p. 21). Chenghua 1474–75: 54 self-castrates "arrested and paraded in public"; 1477: "900 such persons beaten with whipping clubs, thirty strokes each, before ordering them to agro-military colonies"; 1479: 2,000 "invaded the Ministry of Rites ... each receive a hundred strokes and wear a particularly cumbersome cangue for a month" (p. 22; cangue described, "approximately 100 pounds"); "many of these people later changed names, adopted new identities and repeatedly tried their luck; a few of them ultimately were selected to work in the palaces and rose from rags to riches" (p. 22). Hongzhi "actually put to death a few of the self-castrated men" and in 1493 punished the ministers who recommended neither beating nor exile for 300 Ansu self-castrates (p. 23). Zhengde reversed: 1516 "the central government had to accept 3,468 such persons, each of whom received three piculs ... of rice monthly"; 1517 "more than 5,030" (p. 24). Jiajing found "many of these poverty-stricken, miserable people actually practiced cannibalism. Reluctantly, between 1536 and 1566, he took in thousands" (p. 25). Wanli 1601 "picked 4,500 castrated men off the streets of Beijing. He kept 3,000 ... and gave 50 to each of the territorial princes, 20 to every imperial prince ... and 10 to each prince of second degree" (p. 25). 1620: "some 20,000 such people swarmed into the capital ... turned into a rowdy and militant mob"; rejected men "took their own lives because they felt that they had 'lost face'" (p. 25).
- **Numbers.** <100 under Hongwu → ~10,000 in 1520s → "approximately 100,000 near the end of the dynasty" while population only doubled (p. 26); ~1 million employed over the dynasty (p. 7).
- **Who castrates whom (economic).** "For a commoner, unable to climb into the gentry class ... the only means of gaining access to privileges and influence was by means of eunuchism. Consequently, voluntary castration became epidemic." (p. 19) "although ordinary slaves could be bought and sold, castrated eunuchs were an imperial monopoly." (p. 28)

### 2.8 Scholar-official rhetoric against eunuchs

- **Hongwu's instruction to the Ministry of Rites (p. 29):** "Not one or two of these people out of thousands are good. Those who are evil frequently number thousands. If they are employed as ears or eyes, then the ears and eyes are covered. If they are employed as the heart and bowels, then the heart and bowels will be sick. The way to control them is to make them fear the laws and not permit them to have merit. If they fear the laws then they will be attentive to their conduct. If they have merit, then they will be arrogant and lustful."
- **Iron tablet, 1384 (p. 13):** "Eunuchs are forbidden to interfere with government affairs. Those who attempt to do so will be subjected to capital punishment." Eunuchs barred from officials' uniform, rank capped at 4th grade, "Officials of all departments were forbidden to communicate with eunuchs by written documents."
- ***Book of Odes* (p. 12)** — see 2.1; women and eunuchs "Bleat with similar voices."
- **Ming shi verdict (p. 7):** eunuch power produced "one profit but hundreds of damages."
- **Censorial memorials.** Yang Lian "listed twenty-four heinous crimes allegedly committed by Wei" (p. 6). A Jiangxi censor accused Feng Bao of "deceiving the emperor and preying upon the nation" (p. 108). Shang Lu on Wang Zhi: agents "had become ever more arrogant and reckless ... frequently tortured and killed innocent people ... scholar-officials felt uneasy at their posts, traders and merchants felt unsafe ... if Wang was not let go, no one could be sure if the world would be at peace or in peril." Chenghua's reply: **"I merely use a castrato—how would that endanger the world?"** (pp. 115–116). Minister Li Min on plantation eunuchs: they "hired hoodlums and ruffians ... forcibly took over people's land, extorted their money ... and debauched their wives and daughters ... people hated the plantation managers to the marrow of their bones" (pp. 166–167). A garrison commander "swore he wanted to flay Gao's skin and eat Gao's body" (p. 181). Minister of War Zhang Xueyan (1583) on the eunuch battalion: their conduct "was egregious and contrary to all military norms ... the eunuch soldiers refused to turn in their weapons to the storehouses" (p. 67). Confiscation of "tens of thousands of palace apparel items" alleged by censors (p. 45). Civil seal officials "found themselves in scuffles, including verbal exchanges and fistfights, with their eunuch counterparts. Some might treat the eunuchs with contempt" (p. 197).
- **Honorific inversion.** Grand secretaries "ingratiate themselves in front of the ceremonials director by calling the latter *gong gong*, meaning 'grandpa.' On a few occasions, the grand secretaries even were forced to call the most powerful eunuch boss *qiansui*, meaning 'one-thousand-year,' compared to *wansui*, or 'ten-thousand-year,' which they called the emperor." (p. 226) Of 163 grand secretaries, 23 owed appointment to the Silijian and 28 "were actually driven out of office by the eunuch bosses" (p. 227).
- **Derogatory lexicon (pp. 221–222):** *yanren* "castrated persons"; *huozhe* "'persons of fire,' but actually a demeaning transliteration of the non-Chinese name *qoja* or *khoja*"; *siren* "those who serve (originated from *Zhouli*)"; *jingshen nanzi* "young men whose bodies had been thoroughly cleaned"; "beardless courtiers" (p. 224); *wumingbai* "white without name" (p. 48). Tsai's own: "half-men" (p. 26), "deficient man" (p. 1), "neither fish nor fowl" (p. 5), "palace termites" (p. 8, as the cliché).
- **Tsai's counter-rhetoric:** eunuchs were "the creatures not the creators of Chinese absolutist monarchy!" (p. 39); "needed hewers of wood and drawers of water, themselves the victims of a tyrannical system" (p. 8); literati "cultural inertia and inward-looking mentality" (p. 188).

## 4. Belief-plausibility matrix (Chinese setting)

Verdict scale: **STRONG** (directly attested institution), **MODERATE** (attested in part / by analogy), **WEAK** (contradicted or absent). Cite catalogue numbers.

| Belief | Evidence STRENGTHENING | Evidence WEAKENING | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(a) Distinct sex class** | Castrates had their own lexicon (*yanren, huozhe, jingshen nanzi*, p. 221), own uniforms and rank ladder (#20), own schools, infirmary, retirement home and cremation ground (#24, #25), own dossiers (p. 41), and were "an imperial monopoly" unlike ordinary slaves (p. 28). Tsai: "a group of peculiar people who were neither men nor women" (p. 4). Self-castrates "changed names, adopted new identities" (p. 22). | The class is defined by servitude to the emperor, not by sacred status; outside the palace the castrate is a *wumingbai* nobody (#23). | **STRONG** for the existence of a socially distinct third category in China; weak for its being honoured. |
| **(c) Castration as sacred rite** | The knifer "ritually asked the young volunteer if he would regret it or not" with a guarantor present (#60) — a formal consent rite. Pre-Ming regimes "recorded the day of emasculation, examined the wound" (#9). Admission conferred a "black wooden tablet" after a night in a "purgatory" (#22). | Tsai frames castration as "the worst form of human exploitation" (p. 221) and the Ming state as its *penal* regulator; no Chinese source here sacralizes the cut. Confucian law treats it as a filial crime (#15). A monk who castrated himself lost his religious exemption (#50). | **WEAK–MODERATE.** A heretical sect sacralizing the cut would be an inversion of Chinese norms; the consent-ritual and registration provide a ceremonial skeleton to borrow. |
| **(d) Castrated men regarded as women** | *Odes*: "Wives and those without balls / Bleat with similar voices" (#3) — the classical text itself groups them. Palace women and eunuchs are gifted as matched sets (p. 20), share liturgical space (#26) and retirement enclave (#24), and eunuch–dowager alliances are the engine of Han/Tang eunuch power (#5, p. 222). Physical feminization described (pp. 4–5). The Western Depot's founding target was a *transvestite* sorcerer who entered the harem with a eunuch's help (#42). | Eunuchs are consistently "half-men," "deficient men," "neither men nor women" — a *defective male*, not a female. They hold male office titles, command armies (#35, #44), adopt *sons* and ennoble *nephews* (#36, #51–53). | **MODERATE as neighbour-perception** (hostile Chinese rhetoric *already* lumps eunuchs with women); **WEAK as self-understanding.** Good material for Chinese court reaction events: Confucian officials would read the Adelphai as institutionalizing the *Odes* insult. |
| **(e) Castrate religious roles** | Shengongjian temple-wardens (#30); Hanjingchang eunuchs "functioned like Buddhist monks" (#26); Fanjingchang lamaist eunuchs (#27); Daoist exorcist eunuchs (#28); Lingtai astrologers (#29); tomb-cult eunuchs mourning 49 days (#31); Yishiha's missionary temple (#34); Zheng He's steles (#33); eunuch envoys "escorting statues of Buddha" (#57); "Elderly eunuchs, who had demonstrated religious piety ... were assigned to burn incense" (p. 55). | These are palace-cult functionaries and lay patrons, not an ordained castrate clergy; Buddhist monkhood itself is not castrate. | **(f) Separatism (temple/cemetery enclaves)** | Wanyiju "surrounded by a cluster of Buddhist temples," "totally isolated from the outside world" (#24); eunuchs "buried in Buddhist monasteries" (#25); tomb-guard commandancies where "local authorities were prohibited from entering" (p. 168, #31); Eastern Depot's own shrine (#41). | No eunuch cemetery or eunuch-owned temple network is described (Tsai's Ming evidence predates the Qing eunuch temple-cemetery complexes); enclaves are imperial, not self-governing. | **MODERATE.** Spatial enclaves exist but as appendages of the throne. |
| **(g) Adoptive heirship** | Standard: each eunuch "adopted a son or supported a nephew" (p. 34); Liu Jin adopted by eunuch Liu and took his surname (#38); Feng Bao's adopted son (#39); hereditary military posts to brothers/nephews (#52); Cao's nephew an earl (#36); Hongxi's rationale that castration = failure to produce an heir (#15) makes adoption the necessary remedy. | Adoption is patrilineal/agnatic and of *sons*; no matrilineal adoption. The state confiscated dead eunuchs' property (#45), undercutting any heirship. | **STRONG** for adoptive heirship among castrates; **WEAK** for its being matrilineal. |
| **(h) Communal property** | Rice stipends, garments, New Year red envelopes and funerals all issued collectively by bureaus (pp. 49–50, 53); python gowns "had to be returned to this bureau" on death or disgrace (p. 50); confiscated castrates became the emperor's "inventory" (#48). | Property is the *emperor's*, not the community's; prominent eunuchs amassed private fortunes (Wei's 40 wagonloads, p. 6) that were then confiscated. | **MODERATE** (collective provisioning, no ownership). |
| **(i) Non-marital kinship (*duishi*)** | Eunuch–palace woman intimacy "abundant" (p. 5); Wei–Mistress Ke pair punished together (#54); Feng Bao–Dowager Cisheng; eunuchs and women co-gifted and co-housed (#24, #26). Honorific *gong gong* "grandpa" and "Big Companion" (#59). | ***Duishi* is not in Tsai**; no eunuch–maid marriage is described. Wei had a wife and daughter *before* castration (p. 3). | **MODERATE** by inference only; look elsewhere (Qing sources) for *duishi*. |
| **(k) Intra-group hostility** | Eunuch vs literati is the book's spine: 1625 purge of 700 literati, 1628 counterpurge (p. 6); censors' impeachments (§2.8); seal-office "fistfights" (p. 197). Eunuch vs eunuch: Wei murdered his patron Wang An (p. 5); "eunuch turncoats" toppled Feng Bao (p. 108); the "Eight Tigers" bloc (p. 103); Eastern Depot vs embroidered-uniform guard turf wars (p. 100); new emperors purge predecessors' eunuchs (p. 225). | — | **STRONG.** |
| **(l) Outlaw framing** | Self-castrates "paraded in public," cangued, flogged, exiled, occasionally executed (#49); families and neighbours liable (#46, p. 21); knifers exiled (#47); "lazy vagabond" (p. 19); castration itself a filial crime (#15); eunuch corpses dismembered (#54); Western Depot hunts "witches or weird people" (#42). | Simultaneously the state *employs* them: "a window of employment opportunity was opened to the castrated young boys" (p. 23). | **STRONG.** Chinese law treats voluntary castration as a crime even while the court consumes its products — exactly the double bind a heretical castrating sect would face. |
| **(n) Penal castration** | Antiquity (#1); Ming use against "major political criminals and their young male relatives" despite abolition (#15, #37); war captives (#12); Annamese enslavement by castration (p. 16); castration "as a symbol of conquest and a method of revenge" (p. 14). | Formally abolished by Hongwu and Hongxi. | **STRONG** (in practice), with the key nuance that *the law disowns it*. A rehabilitative framing has no Chinese precedent in Tsai — castrated criminals' kin (Huai En) simply became servants. |
| **(o) Sponsor/mentor** | Guarantor at the operation (#60); Neishutang "sage" with tuition gifts (#21); patron eunuchs (Wang An→Wei, Liu→Liu Jin, Wang Zhen/Cao Jixiang→Yishiha); concubines as patrons (Wang Zhi, Yishiha); prince-patron (Zhu Di→Zheng He, #32); the emperor himself as "sage" (p. 224). Sponsor liability: Wei's patron murdered by protégé; Feng Bao's assistants demoted with him (#39). | — | **STRONG.** The sponsor structure is the single best-attested feature for borrowing. |

**Net verdict for a Chinese branch/neighbour:** the *machinery* (sponsor, school, screening, temple service, adoption, enclaves, penal castration, outlaw status) is thoroughly attested; the *theology* (castration as sacred, castrate = woman as honourable status, matrilineality) is absent and would read to Chinese observers as a grotesque literalization of the *Odes* slander. Design the Chinese reaction accordingly: officials denounce it as making law of what the *Odes* condemn; emperors quietly buy its products.
