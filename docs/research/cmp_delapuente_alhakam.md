## 1. The argument in brief

- **Aim.** The pro-Umayyad chronicles still paint al-Ḥakam I as cruel. They do it largely through anecdotes about his slaves. Slaves are "the measuring rod" of the ruler, like servants in Lope de Vega (Concluding Remarks).
- **Terms (Chronicles and Slavery).**
  - In al-Andalus eunuchs are generally called *ṣaqlabī*, not *khādim* as in the East. But not every *ṣaqlabī* was a eunuch; high-ranking white slaves were called that too (n. 9).
  - The claim that the *ṣaqāliba* formed an army unit is disproved (Meouak; n. 10).
  - Chronicles rarely distinguish servant from slave.
- **Male slaves and eunuchs (Al-Ḥakam I's Male Slaves).**
  - Eunuchs were few "due to their high cost". They did the same work as whole slaves (*faḥl*) but could move through the whole palace, "in both the men's and women's quarters".
  - Al-Ḥakam introduced the *maẓālim* magistracy to al-Andalus, and "the first to be in charge of it was Masarra, a eunuch" (n. 15).
  - He built a slave guard of 5,000, 3,000 of them horsemen, called "the mute" because they spoke no Arabic, under a Christian officer, Rabīʿ ibn Teodulf (al-Rāzī; nn. 27–28). Their lack of "family or tribal ties guaranteed him their loyalty" (Concluding Remarks).
  - A *ṣaqlabī* eunuch commander, Manṣūr, died fighting the Franks after 801 (n. 31). A eunuch, Masrūr, was among 72 crucified rebels in 804 (n. 32).
  - After the Revolt of the Suburb (818) he spared rebels' women and children from slavery, but "actually enslaved and castrated some Muslim men" (Male Slaves).
- **Concubines (Concubines, *ummahāt al-awlād* and *qiyān*).**
  - No legitimate wife is recorded. Almost all the emirs were sons of slave women.
  - The favourite, ʿAjab, speaks in the first person in Ibn Ḥabīb's version, which is rare. Ibn Ḥabīb vouched for the story because palace eunuchs, "the only male characters with access to the emir's harem", told it to him (n. 47).
  - ʿAjab and another concubine, Mutʿa, founded mosques, an orchard "as a pious legacy for the sick" and a cemetery. These are "the two oldest pious legacies established by women in al-Andalus known to us" (Marín; n. 53). The orchard was worked by slave labour (n. 54).
  - Al-Ḥakam was the first Andalusi ruler with *qiyān* (singing slave women). Anecdotes show them angry with him, and him yielding: "For excessive love he became a slave" (n. 60).
- **Religion and the jurists (Male and Female Slaves Kept in Order to Display the Religiosity of the Emir).**
  - The jurist Shabṭūn stopped the emir from cutting off a servant's hand by citing a hadith from Mālik on repressing anger (n. 71).
  - A judge ruled that one of the emir's concubine-mothers belonged to someone else. The emir submitted and paid her price, since an *umm walad* cannot be returned, sold or separated from her household, and is freed at her owner's death (n. 72).
  - **Ibn Ḥazm's charge.** Al-Ḥakam "used to castrate the children of his subjects who stood out for their beauty and take them to his palace as servants". Three are named, and mosques and an orchard in Córdoba carried their names (n. 73). One, Naṣr, became a favourite of the next emir. "All legal schools prohibited castration in Islamic territory, both of slaves and free men, although not the purchase or the possession of eunuchs from other territories" (n. 74). For Ibn Ḥazm this made al-Ḥakam "an apogee of regnal cruelty".
- **Repentance.** He was ill for seven years before he died, and repented (n. 76).

## 4. Design implications

- **Event: "The Faqīh Remembers Mālik" (Sunni ruler, Mālikī flavour; al-Andalus or Maghrib).** Fires when a Muslim ruler with a daughter at court gains the angry or wrathful state, or chooses to imprison her. A *faqīh* at court cites the hadith on repressing anger.
  - (a) Listen: the punishment is dropped, ruler gains piety, the daughter gains opinion of the faqīh.
  - (b) Ignore: tyranny, and the faqīh's opinion falls.
  - Loc: "Whoever swallows his anger when he could loose it, God will give him safety on the day of rising." This paraphrases the hadith as the chronicle gives it (n. 71); it is a religious text, so keep it attributed to the faqīh, not the narrator.
- **Event or modifier: "A Legacy for the Sick" (Islamic lord holding a county with a House).** Instead of closing a House, a Muslim lord may let it stand as a charitable endowment in the name of a woman of his household. The House keeps running, loses some autonomy (no Elder Sister election while the endowment stands), and the lord gains piety. Modelled on ʿAjab's orchard (nn. 52–53). *Unverified* whether Mālikī waqf law would let an endowment serve a group like this; treat as design, not history.
- **Opinion modifier for Andalusi Mālikī clergy toward Dorleyism's houses.** "Cut in the Lands of Islam": a large malus if the House is Grandmother's House or if any rite was performed inside a Muslim-held county; a small malus otherwise. Loc: "The schools agree: no knife within our borders."
- **Quotable in-world lines.**
  - "They cut the beautiful sons of his own people, and named mosques after them." (A Sister on Córdoba's past; Ibn Ḥazm, n. 73)
  - "No knife within our borders; what comes from beyond, we may buy." (Mālikī jurist, cynical; after n. 74)
  - "The cut ones go everywhere in the palace, and so they know everything." (Andalusi courtier; after the Male Slaves section and n. 47)
  - "The first judge of grievances in Córdoba was a cut man. Why not a daughter?" (A Sister petitioning an emir; n. 15)
