## 1. The argument in brief

### 1.1 The treatise and its quarrel

- **Why it was written.** Ibn Riḍwān wrote it to refute the Tunisian Ibn al-Jazzār (d. 979/80), who called Egypt unhealthy without having seen it. "He had only heard about it" (p. 77). Ibn al-Jazzār's book is lost except for the excerpts quoted here (p. vii).
- **The thesis.** Egypt's temperament is hot, made moist by the Nile flood, which counts as a "surplus" (p. 87). The result is rot, weak bodies and quick spoilage. But bodies "accustomed" to their air do not fall sick. Illness comes from **deviation from the customary** (pp. 87–89, 103). So Egyptians "are not chronically ill, but they are very susceptible to illness" (p. 103).
- **Dols's verdict.** Ibn Riḍwān sets out to defend Egypt and ends up confirming its unhealthiness: "Ibn al-Jazzar was right, but for the wrong reasons" (p. 53).
- **The date is unsettled.** Dols gives two arguments that do not agree (pp. 52–53):
  - the disastrous epidemic sounds like 1055–62, which would put the treatise between 1062 and 1065;
  - but the treatise never mentions Ibn Buṭlān, which suggests a date before 1049–50.

### 1.2 Tinnīs and the "feminizing" of character (the attribution question)

- **The passage.** It comes in ch. 8, the summary chapter. The Bushmūr marsh people are "grosser" and stupid from coarse food and bad water. By contrast, Alexandria, Tinnīs "and similar places" are improved by the sea, mild weather and the east wind, which "enhance their ambitions" (p. 115). Then: "The fact that Tinnis is surrounded by the sea imposes a predominant humidity on the city and causes the effeminate character of its people" (p. 115; fol. 28a).
- **The Arabic** (Ar. p. 19) reads *wa-iḥāṭat al-baḥr bi-madīnat Tinnīs yūjibu ghalabat al-ruṭūba ʿalayhā wa-taʾnīth akhlāq ahlihā*. That is "the **feminizing (taʾnīth) of the characters** of its people".
- **What the passage does not contain.** There is no *mukhannath*, *takhannuth* or *ubna*. Nothing is said about births, numbers, singing or pleasure, and nothing about any individual. The feminizing is a property of a whole town's air. It sits in a passage that otherwise **praises** Tinnīs against Bushmūr.
- **Tinnīs elsewhere in the book.** Its latitude is "31⅓ degrees", on the edge of the temperate fourth clime (p. 81). It is one of the coastal towns where the healthy east wind blows (p. 83).
- **What Dols says.** Dols does not comment on the passage. He never mentions Abū l-Sarī, the "two hundred *mukhannaths*", or any Tinnīs remark by Ibn Buṭlān. His Tinnīs note (p. 81 n. 16) is only a list of geographical references.
- **Conclusion.** These are two separate statements by two rival physicians (§4.1).

### 1.3 Egyptians' temperament, bodies and character

- **Character follows the body.** "As for the character of the Egyptians, one man resembles another" (p. 93). Their bodies are feeble, so "timidity and cowardice", envy, lying and informing to the ruler dominate (p. 93). Even lions brought to Egypt "become meek and do not multiply" (p. 93).
- **Fusṭāṭ in particular.** Its people are cowardly, ungenerous and slanderers. "Only five officials are needed to drive a hundred or more of their men" (p. 110). Dols notes that other travellers do not confirm this (p. 110 n. 38).
- **The coda softens it.** A foreign student took the book as an attack on Egyptians. Ibn Riḍwān answered that their evils are "simple and uncomplicated" and easy to treat, unlike the wars and murders of other peoples (pp. 148–149). Egypt is worth living in because its people submit to "whoever governs them" (p. 148).
- **The colour line.** Aswan's heat makes its people's "color ... black, and their hair ... kinky" (p. 80). The north is "brown", "mild" in manner, with lank hair (p. 82). This is Ptolemaic climate theory. Ibn Buṭlān turned the same theory on Ibn Riḍwān's own "dark-blackish complexion" (p. 61). See §2 row 11.
- **Sex and gender in the regimen.** These are thin:
  - the six non-naturals can change "the temperament of male and female" (p. 118);
  - item 16 of the physician's 25 checks is "the nature of the patient, whether male or female" (p. 120);
  - menstruation is listed among nature's evacuations (p. 120);
  - sexual intercourse is to be avoided in a hot epidemic (pp. 139, 141);
  - one charlatan wins patients by "talking to the women about what is proper for them in the matter of sexual intercourse" (p. 124).
- **Eunuchs** do not appear in the treatise. In Dols's introduction they appear once, burning incense beside al-Mustanṣir's mule, from Nāṣir-i Khusraw (p. 50).
- **No examination of women.** "Consultations often did not include a physical examination, especially if the patient were female" (p. 39). The family, not the doctor, decided the course of treatment (p. 39).

### 1.4 Climate, seasons and the Coptic year

- **The Nile.** The flood begins in summer and peaks in autumn, carrying carcasses, filth and fish-spawn. At first the water is green, then turbid "like sludge" (pp. 84–85).
- **The four seasons, by Coptic month:**
  - **Spring** (Amshīr, Baramhāt, Barmūda, Bashans): the only balanced air of the year (p. 94).
  - **Summer** (end of Bashans, then Baʾūna, Abīb and part of Misrā): the Nile rises (p. 96).
  - **Autumn** (second half of Misrā, then **Tūt**, Bāba and part of Hātūr): the flood is at its height. The season's first days are "very hot", and it brings the great autumn fish catch (pp. 96–97).
  - **Winter** (second half of Hātūr, then Kiyahk and Ṭūba): "less than three months" long (p. 97).
- **The sickest time** is "the end of autumn and the beginning of winter, in the months of Hatur and Kihak" (p. 98). Health returns at the end of Kiyahk and in Ṭūba (p. 98).
- **1 Tūt is the year's start.** The ancient Egyptians "made autumn the beginning of the year". Their months begin with "Tut, followed by Babih, Hatur" (p. 98). Dols ties this to the Era of Diocletian, which began on 29 August 284 (p. 98 n. 25). The treatise never uses the word *Nawrūz*.
- **Ṭūba water.** Egyptians store water drawn in Ṭūba as the purest. Ibn Riḍwān says to purify it anyway (p. 136).

### 1.5 Fusṭāṭ and Cairo, c. 1050 (ch. 6)

- **The capital's four parts.** The capital is "al-Fustat, al-Qarafah, Cairo, and al-Gizah" (p. 104). Al-Maqrīzī quotes this chapter in the *Khiṭaṭ* (p. 104 n. 9; p. 72).
- **Fusṭāṭ.**
  - It lies in a depression with "narrow" alleys and "high" buildings. Ibn Riḍwān quotes Rufus: in such a city, "flee from it" (p. 105).
  - Dead cats and dogs are thrown into the streets, and dung and latrine sewage go into the Nile it drinks (p. 106).
  - The baths' "large hearths" smoke, and the dust soils clean clothes in a day (p. 106).
  - The worst quarter is "the district around the Old Mosque", down to the river (p. 109).
- **Cairo** has lower buildings and broader, cleaner streets. People drink well water, which latrine seepage still reaches. Stinking pools lie between the two towns (p. 107). Its northern part is the healthiest in the city, and al-Khandaq is bad (p. 109).
- **The rest of the capital.** **Al-Qarāfa** has the best air (p. 107), and al-Gīza is damp and wooded (p. 108).
- **Fish.** Spoiled sea fish are sold in Cairo in winter (p. 109).
- **Ibn Riḍwān's own house** was in the Qaṣr al-Shamʿ quarter of Fusṭāṭ (p. 58).
- **Dols's Cairo, from Nāṣir-i Khusraw (1046–49):**
  - every house, shop and bath in Cairo belonged to the caliph (p. 47);
  - the palace held 30,000 people, 12,000 of them servants (p. 48);
  - 52,000 water camels worked the two towns (p. 48);
  - a cheating shopkeeper was paraded on a camel, ringing a bell and confessing (p. 49);
  - al-Mustanṣir cut the Khalīj dike in plain white on a mule, and "the deaf and dumb" sailed in the first boat for luck (pp. 49–50).
- **No hospital.** "Ibn Riḍwān never mentions hospitals in his description of Egypt", and he neither trained nor practised in them (p. 31). Acute illness was treated at home. For Muslims the hospitals mainly served "the poor and incurable", and Egyptian Jews did not use them (p. 31).

### 1.6 Pestilence, famine and the shidda

- **Five epidemics.** Ibn Riḍwān saw "pestilence occur in Egypt only five times in about twenty years". Only one of them was disastrous (p. 102).
- **The disastrous one.** Wars, "a great fear of the enemy and high prices", and a Nile "extraordinary in both its increase and decrease" combined until "about a third of the people died" (p. 114). Dols dates this to 1055–62 (p. 114 n. 9).
- **Four causes of epidemics:** air, water, food and **psychic events** (p. 112).
  - **Psychic:** "a common fear of a ruler grips the people". In expected famine "they increase their hoarding" (p. 113).
  - **Food:** blight and high prices force a change of diet, and **festival overeating** spreads illness (p. 113).
  - **The dead:** corpses from wars foul the water (p. 114).
- **The remedies are communal.** In an epidemic of fear, "the people hearten one another" (p. 139). In ordinary times, Egyptians should "increase their gaiety and joy" to warm their bodies (p. 117).
- **Dols's crisis chronology** (pp. 50–51):
  - pestilence in 1053, 1054, 1055–62, 1063 and 1065–72;
  - the seven-year famine of 1065–72, when people ate dogs, cats "and even human flesh" (p. 50);
  - a smallpox epidemic around 1062 that killed 21,000 young people "in less than a month" (p. 51 n. 252, quoting Cohen).
- **Ibn Buṭlān on the 1054–55 pestilence.** He listed learned men who died in it, "so that the light of science was extinguished" (p. 51 n. 251).

### 1.7 Ibn Riḍwān's life (Dols, pp. 54–66)

- **Origins.** He was born in 388/998 at Gīza, son of a poor baker. He was self-taught after he found Cairo's teachers merely listened to students read (pp. 55–56). He earned a living early as a "wayside" astrologer (p. 55 n. 267).
- **Office and family.** Al-Mustanṣir appointed him chief physician (*raʾīs al-aṭibbāʾ*) (p. 57). Dols doubts he ran any licensing, because he begs the government to examine doctors (p. 124 n. 19). He married at thirty, and all his children died young (p. 57 n. 277).
- **The orphan.** He adopted an orphan girl "during the famine and pestilence" of 1053. She later fled with valuables worth about 20,000 dinars, and the shock was said to have affected his sanity (p. 58).
- **Galen in a dream.** He dreamed that Galen cured his headache (p. 66).
- **His circle.** His pupils included the Fatimid prince al-Mubashshir ibn Fātik and the Jewish physician Afrāʾīm ibn al-Zaffān (p. 62).
- **Death.** He died in 453/1061 (Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa) or 460/1067–68 (modern consensus) (p. 66). **He may be alive, aged about 68, in 1066.**

### 1.8 The Ibn Riḍwān–Ibn Buṭlān quarrel

- **Ibn Buṭlān.** He was a Christian physician, "probably a priest", from Baghdad. He left in 440/1049 and reached Cairo "the following year", where Ibn Riḍwān attacked him. After three or four years he went on to **Constantinople**, arriving in 446/1054, at the crisis that led to the schism (p. 52 n. 255). He later alternated between Aleppo and Antioch. In 1063 he supervised the building of a hospital in Antioch, and he died a monk there in 458/1066 (p. 52 n. 255).
- **His writings** include a treatise "on how to buy slaves and detect bodily defects" (p. 52 n. 255).
- **The dispute** (441/1049–50) turned on whether a chick is warmer than a chicken, a "jejune issue" (p. 65). Underneath, it was about who was better trained in Greek learning (p. 66). It also touched the remedies Ibn Buṭlān prescribed, which were unsuited to Egypt's air (p. 127 n. 4), and whether one can learn from books without a teacher (p. 64 n. 333).
- **Personal abuse.** Ibn Buṭlān mocked Ibn Riḍwān's dark skin and ugliness. He wrote verses in which midwives cry "Alas, had we only left him in the uterus!" and called him "the crocodile of the demons" (p. 61).
- **What it shows of Cairo's learned life:**
  - public written polemic between a Muslim and a Christian, which Dols says had "nothing to do with religious prejudice" (p. 61);
  - Jewish and Christian physicians at the centre of the profession (p. 45);
  - fierce competition for clients (p. 58 n. 282);
  - physicians who perform status: long grey beards, "five gowns", an astrolabe pulled from the sleeve (pp. 122, 124);
  - druggists "more skilled ... about drugs than the doctors" (p. 122).
- **Ibn Riḍwān's verdict on his colleagues.** Only "four or five" Egyptian doctors are praiseworthy (p. 125).

**Errors and cautions.**
- Dols's two datings of the treatise are incompatible (pp. 52–53), so use "c. 1050–65" if a date is needed.
- Dols's "arrived in Cairo the following year" (1050) is in tension with a controversy dated 441/1049–50 (pp. 52–53). Trivial.
- Ibn Riḍwān's slur on Fusṭāṭ is contradicted by other travellers (p. 110 n. 38). Treat it as his polemic, not as data.
- Dols cites John Boswell for Cairo's "social tolerance" (p. 42 n. 219). Do not cite that as evidence about *mukhannathūn*.

Net relevance: **high** for the Cairo physician register, the Tinnīs attribution, the seasonal calendar and 1060s crisis flavour. **Low** for Dorleyaian doctrine.

## 4. Answers to open questions / corrections to other notes

1. **Tinnīs: whose remark?** Both physicians made one. The two remarks are different, and both are attested.
   - **Ibn Riḍwān's** is in his own treatise (ch. 8, p. 115; Ar. p. 19, fol. 28a). The sea's humidity causes the *taʾnīth akhlāq ahlihā*, "the effeminate character of its people". It is a climate claim about a whole town, inside a passage praising Tinnīs. There is no *mukhannath*, no number and no birth.
   - **The "two hundred *mukhannaths* born every year"** remains **Ibn Buṭlān quoting Abū l-Sarī**, as al-Maqrīzī gives it (Cairo §1.3). Nothing in Dols bears on that passage.
   - **Corrections:**
     - Cortese and Calderini (p. 224) are **right** to give a Tinnīs "effeminacy" remark to Ibn Riḍwān.
     - The Cairo note's "looks like a conflation" (§1.3, "Attribution") should be revised. It is not a conflation.
     - The Cairo note §1.3 and C&C correction 2 can both drop "not checked".
     - Whether Ibn Buṭlān was answering Ibn Riḍwān's line during their quarrel is **unknown**. The Cairo note's gloss "moistness and femininity" suggests a shared *ruṭūba*/*taʾnīth* vocabulary, but that is a suggestion only.
2. **Abū l-Sarī** (Cairo open question 5). **Still open.** Dols never names him. (The "Sari d-Din" of p. 73 is a later manuscript copyist.)
3. **Nawrūz = 1 Tūt.** **Confirmed** as the Coptic year's start: "Tut, followed by Babih, Hatur" (p. 98). The Era of Diocletian began 29 August 284 (p. 98 n. 25). This fits the Cairo note's 29 August 1066 (Julian). But this treatise does not use the word *Nawrūz*. The Cairo note's "Ibn Riḍwān via *Khiṭaṭ* 1:727" must come from another passage, which was not checked here.
4. **Physician voices** (`isl_gesink_intersex_premodern.md` row 2; `isl_arab_queer_trans_feminisms.md`, "The physician" row). Cairo now has two attested physician voices on Tinnīs, not one. Ibn Riḍwān's is **not** a "disease" claim: by his own theory an accustomed body is not ill (p. 103). The row that files "born so; a disease" under "Ibn Buṭlān on Tinnīs" should not be extended to Ibn Riḍwān.
5. **Ibn Buṭlān's dates** (Cairo §1.3 says "in Egypt 1049–53"). Dols's dates:
   - left Baghdad 440/1049;
   - reached Cairo the following year;
   - controversy in 441/1049–50;
   - Constantinople in 446/1054;
   - Antioch hospital in 1063;
   - died a monk at Antioch in 458/1066 (pp. 52–53).
   "In Egypt c. 1050–53" is safer.
6. **Ibn Riḍwān alive in 1066?** Possibly. He died in 453/1061 or 460/1067–68 (p. 66).
7. **The 1060s crisis.** Use Dols's pestilence years 1065–72 and the 1062 smallpox epidemic (pp. 50–51) alongside the famine chronology the Cairo note already has. Dols has nothing on al-Mustanṣir's personal role in the *shidda*.
8. **Hospital.** No evidence of a functioning Cairo *bīmāristān* in Ibn Riḍwān's world (p. 31). Do not add one to the 1066 set.
