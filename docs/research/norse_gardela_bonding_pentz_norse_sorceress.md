## 1. The argument in brief

- **The vǫlur were real, and the texts are late.** The book takes the "atypical" graves as evidence that diviners existed beyond the manuscripts (Bønding, p. 3). It also stresses that *Eiríks saga rauða* was "probably put to parchment at the beginning of the thirteenth century", about an event set in the late tenth (p. 5). Literary scholars read the vǫlva scene as invention. Archaeologists read it as cultural memory confirmed by the graves (pp. 5, 9).
- **The Greenland rite is divination, and the vǫlva is the one in trance.** Þorbjǫrg is greeted ritually and set on a high seat with a hen-feather cushion. She eats a meal of hearts and kid's-milk porridge and sleeps overnight. The next evening the women form "a circle around her while she is elevated on a *hjallr*" (p. 6). Guðríðr, a Christian, sings the *varðlokkur*. The spirits came because the song was beautiful, "though previously they stayed away and would not grant us obedience" (saga, trans. p. 6). **The trance belongs to the vǫlva. The community asks questions afterwards.** Whether it was a shamanic soul-journey is "a possible interpretation, although not the only possible one" (p. 6).
- **Diviners were trusted and feared.** Hávamál 87 warns against a vǫlva who prophesies good. In *Víga-Glúms saga* Oddbjǫrg's predictions track the hospitality she receives, and the Björketorp stone may name a "harm-prophecy" (pp. 7-8). Her otherness (clothes, age, diet, origin) follows her outside the rite (p. 8).
- **The equipment, verbatim from the saga (p. 9).** A dark cloak set with stones; a glass-bead necklace; a black lambskin hood lined with white catskin; a staff with a brass knob set with stones; a belt with a large pouch of charms (*taufr*); calfskin boots; catskin gloves. Schjødt reads this as a thirteenth-century magic worker; Tolley reads it as a parody of a bishop with a crozier; Price and Gardeła read it as broadly trustworthy (p. 9).
- **Staffs are mental weapons, not divining rods.** In the texts staffs "steal one's wits" and mark powerful figures. "There is no justification for using the term 'divination wand'" (Gardeła, p. 421). There are about forty possible staffs, mostly from women's graves, "although items interpreted as staffs also appear in graves that may have belonged to men" (p. 422). Typical features (p. 422):
  - iron, with a cage handle that echoes a distaff;
  - each one unique, made for its owner;
  - some found bent, broken or crushed with stones, as if the staff had a spirit that had to be killed.
  
  Whether a stick was a staff "was always in the eye of the beholder" (p. 430). Price restates the distaff reading: the staff winds "back" the thread of the practitioner's soul as it travels in trance (p. 349).
- **The chair is an elite, female and martial object.** There are 22 chair pendants. "In cases where it has been possible to define the sex of the interred person, we always find a female" (Jessen, p. 451). Several are worn until the loop broke (p. 458). They cluster at royal and fortified sites (Birka, Hedeby, Fyrkat, Lejre, Sarpsborg), where many people "held a martial position" (p. 457). The Lejre pendant lay inside the great hall (p. 457). Jessen links the seeress to war and sacral queenship, citing Veleda, Ganna and Gunnhildr: "who would not want to know the outcome of a possibly fatal confrontation" (p. 458). The chairs are amulets of foresight itself (p. 458).
- **Fyrkat 4: a pagan vǫlva at a Christian king's fortress.** She died c. 980, about fifteen years after Harald Bluetooth's conversion, and was buried in a wagon body beside the ceremonial platform (Roesdahl, pp. 293-96). "She was certainly a pagan – here, at the king's own fortress" (p. 296). The grave held:
  - henbane seeds, possibly in a fur pouch, and a bowl of fat to mix them with (Pentz, pp. 318-19);
  - a silver chair pendant;
  - a small iron "staff" or pin, and a possible staff with a gilt end mount that may have rattled (pp. 320-21);
  - a mirror and toe-rings;
  - a Gotland box brooch reused as a pot of white lead (p. 322);
  - a blue dress with red details (p. 297).
  
  The textile chapter is more cautious. It finds a foot-length blue wool garment with red cords, a silver-brocaded neckband, gold-embroidered triangles on the chest and gold-trimmed shoes, "highly unusual" in Denmark. The 2002 and 2009 reconstructions (strap dress; blue dress with gold-edged veil) are not supported. The sex is uncertain: the outfit "points" to a woman. Yet "there can be no doubt that she wore something extraordinary" (Mannering & Rimstad, pp. 329-36).
  
  Her shiny objects suit "ritual performances in a fire-lit hall" (p. 297). A brooch mount suggests she visited Borgring, "perhaps as part of her military functions" (p. 297). "She must have been one of the last powerful vǫlur in Denmark" (p. 298). Pentz says the grave contradicts "the assumption that all Viking Age women lived under the protection of a man" (p. 323), and adds: "No actions were taken to prevent the deceased from exiting her grave" (p. 324).
- **Hostile treatment of sorcerers' graves.** Sagas stone malevolent magicians, and stones pin bodies in Gerdrup and Trekroner-Grydehøj (Gardeła, p. 264). In *Laxdæla saga* Guðrún finds "dark and evil-looking" bones with a "huge seiðr-staff" under the church floor where she prays. They are moved to where a highway later ran (p. 260). At Kaupang a staff lay pressed by a stone beside a seated woman in a ship (p. 426).
- **A cross-dressing sorceress with an axe.** In *Ljósvetninga saga*, Þórhildr the Widow of Vaðlar meets Guðmundr "dressed in breeches and with a helmet on her head and an axe in her hand". She wades out, strikes the sea, and the water turns bloody. She tells him the cost to herself is too high to do it again (Gardeła, p. 261). Gardeła reads axes in women's graves (Løve, Mårem) as ritual tools, not proof of warriors (p. 261).
- **Gender (Jefford Franks, ch. 3).**
  - Viking society was "unquestionably patriarchal" (p. 31).
  - *Ergi* "is about emasculation – a lack of masculinity, which does not inherently mean the presence of femininity" (p. 36).
  - Seiðr's wisdom, and perhaps spinning, make it a performance of femininity, so men who do it "are also engaging in performances of femininity" (p. 36).
  - Male-bodied people buried as feminine at Klinta (Öland) and Portway (Andover) suggest someone "perhaps not seen to be a man at all, but instead a woman or another feminine gender" (p. 36).
  - Franks finds "no examples of men taking feminine names and pronouns in a way comparable to warrior women" (p. 36).
  - Franks reports that Clover's one-sex model "is no longer recommended by scholars" (p. 34).
- **Price on the Birka "sorceresses" (ch. 26).** The Birka occupants wore women's dress, and none has been sexed. "So far as is known, no burial of a male sorcerer has been reliably identified" (p. 352). Price wonders if he "might in fact be looking at the 'missing' male sorcerers" (p. 352, quoting the 2nd ed. of *The Viking Way*), and adds: "There is no reason to suppose that they all shared an identity, just because they all bore staffs" (p. 352). Other details:
  - Bj. 660 wore a silver crucifix, perhaps hidden under an oval brooch (p. 345).
  - Bj. 834 and 845 were buried seated, facing the town, beside the rampart and a gate (pp. 345-46).
  - In Bj. 834 a lance was thrown over the dead, perhaps a dedication to Óðinn (p. 346).
  
  In his foreword Price says *ergi* brought men "a form of social death" but also "a terrible power that marked such people as forever different" (p. viii).
- **Staffs began with men, and the role was female-coded (Delvaux, ch. 31).**
  - The earliest staffs (before 800) come from weapon burials read as male: Hellebust, Mindre-Sunde, Pukkila-Isokyrö. "The three weapon burials suggest that masculine figures dominated early ritual staff use" (p. 440).
  - Women "established themselves as ritual workers over the course of the 800s as viking violence intensified" (p. 447).
  - Necklaces in staff and snake graves cluster in 915-950, perhaps one or two generations of linked women (p. 443).
  - The specialists "presented themselves as female gendered through the use of material culture" (p. 435). Male figures "might have used female elements to help signal seiðr associations" (p. 445).
  - Axes or other weapons appear in eight of the staff and snake assemblages (pp. 444-45).
- **Gender-crossing is named outright (chs. 27, 29, 16).**
  - The Gutdalen authors: "some seiðr performers had unconventional gender identities" (p. 369). They warn against reading binary gender from grave goods.
  - The Gutdalen staff was bent after the pyre. The mourners "wished to associate the buried person with the community and with the home, rather than to isolate them" (p. 370). Staff graves sit among the ancestors as elite insiders (pp. 369-73).
  - The toolkit chapter reads Þórhildr's trousers as "perhaps a literary allusion to the idea that some of these people had fluid gender identities" (p. 412).
  - Oehrl asks whether the Lejre throne figure is a woman or Óðinn in women's clothing performing seiðr (p. 192).
- **Tools carry the power.** In Vatnsdæla saga the seeress Þórdís gives her staff and cloak to Þorkell, who is no magic-worker, and he uses them to confuse his opponent at a trial (Knutson, p. 22).
- **Naming was a rite, and names could cross gender (Albris, ch. 22).** When "adults changed their names or gained a byname, a gift was given to 'fasten' the name" (*nafnfestr*, p. 284). Many women's names were made by *Movierung*, the feminising of masculine names or name-elements, for example to name a girl after her male kin (p. 284).
- **Season and setting.** Prophetic seiðr in the sagas was mostly women's work, held outdoors at dusk around the *vetrnætur* (Winter Nights) in late October (Gunnell, p. 43). In the hall, "the leader in the high-seat becomes Óðinn ... and the hall becomes Valhǫll" (Eriksen, p. 80).
- **Mother and son in one grave (ch. 28).** At Gerdrup a woman lies with a spear and stones on her body beside a man with his ankles bound. mtDNA shows "they are mother and son" (p. 386). One reading: a vǫlva stoned with her son, as with Katla and Oddr in Eyrbyggja. The newer reading: an elite woman whose spear was "a theatrical prop", her son killed to go with her (pp. 386-87).
- **Deep time, and men too (Varberg, ch. 21).**
  - Bronze Age Hvidegaard (c. 1300-1100 BC): a man buried with a sword, pieces of female dress jewellery and a belt-purse of charms. The first excavator called him a *seiðmaðr* (pp. 268-70).
  - Maglehøj: a woman with a raven's voice-box, a lynx claw and snake vertebrae in her belt-box (p. 271).
  - In the Bronze Age, ritual specialists were "both men and women"; for the Iron Age only women's graves have been identified (pp. 275-76, 280).
  - Varberg suggests Mediterranean prophetesses fed into the vǫlva. Among them are the Montanists of **Phrygia**, where "women are bishops and women are presbyters" (Epiphanius, quoted p. 279).

## 4. Chapter list (one line each)

Foreword (Price): the vǫlur restored to the centre of Norse religion; *ergi* as "social death" with "terrible power" (pp. vii-viii).
Introduction (eds): the National Museum of Denmark project "Thoughts behind Things" (2020-23) and the 2024 vǫlva exhibition (pp. ix-xi).

**Part 1: Rituals, Myths, and Material Culture**
1. Bønding, ritual repertoire: Eiríks saga ch. 4 as divination; ritualised objects and persons as "strange" agents (pp. 3-14).
2. Knutson, burial objects: grave goods as nonhuman agents; Þórdís lends staff and cloak to a man (p. 22).
3. Jefford Franks, gender: hegemonic masculinity, *ergi* as lack of masculinity, Klinta and Portway, no men taking women's names (pp. 31-40).
4. Gunnell, folklore and prophecy: later folk divination as a lens; seiðr at dusk at the Winter Nights; the "Christmas chair" (pp. 41-50).

**Part 2: Places and Spaces**
5. Bønding, introduction: sacred space and the term *hof*.
6. Ljungkvist & Raffield, Gamla Uppsala: no confirmed Viking-Age temple; a great hall burnt c. 800.
7. Eriksen, halls: post-hole foils, abandonment rites, high-seat posts; high seat = Óðinn, hall = Valhǫll (p. 80).
8. Bek-Pedersen, domestic work: spinning and weaving magic; the *dyngja* as a women's space.
9. Egeler, open landscape: Þorbjǫrg's platform and chorus as theatre; Þórhildr cross-dressed with an axe (pp. 99-101).
10. Grundvad, elite sites: hoards at manor gates at Harreby; possible re-enactment of gods.
11. Murphy & Nygaard, processions: Freyr's wagon and priestess; Óðinn arriving with valkyries; wagon burials.
12. Toplak, executions: Adam of Bremen critiqued; Gísla saga siblings stoned for seiðr (p. 153).
13. Moen, women and sacrifice: ibn Faḍlān's "Angel of Death"; Cimbri priestesses who prophesied from blood.
14. Klevnäs, reopened graves: tenth-century grave re-entries as public events; necromancy, Óðinn and the vǫlur.

**Part 3: Animals**
15. Bønding, introduction: animals as agents in religion.
16. Oehrl, birds: *Hliðskjálf* and the *seiðhjallr*; the Lejre figure as a woman or Óðinn in women's clothes (p. 192); swan-valkyries.
17. Evans Tang & Ruiter, horses: the Kaupang staff-woman in a boat; a stallion over the Trekroner woman; Egill's horse-head níð pole.
18. Pluskowski, wolves and dogs: *gandr* as sorceresses' wolf-steeds; a dog in the Kaupang staff grave.
19. Pezzoli, Oseberg wagon: foreign-made hybrid carvings that shimmer in firelight; serpent posts like the Oseberg "staff".

**Part 4: Ritual Specialists**
20. Gardeła, introduction: staff graves, weapons in women's graves, stoned burials; Laxdæla's vǫlva grave; Ljósvetninga's Þórhildr (pp. 259-64).
21. Varberg, pre-Viking specialists: Bronze Age Hvidegaard man and Maglehøj woman; Roman-Iron-Age Blidegn and Årslev; Montanist women in Phrygia (pp. 267-80).
22. Albris, female names: sacral name elements; *nafnfestr* name-fastening gifts for adults; *Movierung* (pp. 283-87).
23. Roesdahl, Fyrkat vǫlva revisited: a pagan at Harald Bluetooth's fortress c. 980, "one of the last" (pp. 293-98).
24. Pentz, Fyrkat 4: full inventory, henbane and fat, staffs or rattle, white-lead brooch, burial reconstruction, the transitional cemetery (pp. 301-28).
25. Mannering & Rimstad, Fyrkat textiles: gold and silver embroidery, gold-trimmed shoes; earlier reconstructions rejected; sex uncertain (pp. 329-38).
26. Price, Birka sorcerers: Bj. 660, 760, 834, 845; the hidden crucifix; seated burials; were they the "missing" male sorcerers? (pp. 339-54).
27. Westlye, Gardeła & Karpińska, Gutdalen: an over-100 cm staff bent after cremation, nine pendants, chicken bones; "unconventional gender identities" (pp. 355-80).
28. Kastholm & Ulriksen, Gerdrup and Trekroner: a mother and son by mtDNA; a beheaded woman under a stallion and boulders; stones alone are not diagnostic (pp. 381-402).

**Part 5: The Sorcerer's Toolkit**
29. Gardeła, Bønding & Pentz, introduction: *taufr*, miniatures, exotica and antiques as esoteric knowledge; trousers and "fluid gender identities" (p. 412).
30. Gardeła, staffs: about forty staffs, no "divination wands", the Fuldby and Gävle pieces, stones and bending (pp. 419-30).
31. Delvaux, necklaces: early staffs in male graves, women's takeover in the 800s, the 915-950 cluster, female self-presentation (pp. 435-47).
32. Jessen, miniature chairs: 22 pendants, female, elite and martial sites; seeresses and war (pp. 451-61).
33. Vang Petersen, wheel pendants: "Freyja's chariot"; protection in pregnancy.
34. Gardeła, miniature weapons: shields, swords, spears and axes, mostly with women and not warriors (pp. 479-91).
35. Vlasatý, weathervane cloak pins: miniature banners; low relevance.
36. Gunnell, masks: masking and voice; Fyrkat's possible white face-paint; Óðinn as Grímnir.
