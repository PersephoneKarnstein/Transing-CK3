# Grass, "A Horse-Driven Court" (2024): design reading

**Citation (verified from the PDF):** Noa Grass, "A Horse-Driven Court: Eunuchs and Horsemen in the Early-Ming Directorate of Imperial Horses", *Asia Major* 3rd ser. 37.2 (2024): 5–22. The running head reads "Asia Major (2024) 3d ser. Vol. 37.2: 5-22". The PDF shows no DOI; none was found and none is supplied here. The author is at Tel Aviv University and the Hebrew University's Louis Frieberg Center. **Read in full** (18 pp., text layer, extracted to the scratchpad).

**Period and relevance.** The article covers Yongle to Zhengde (1403–1521), and the core material is 1403–1460. Only up to 1453 overlaps the CK3 window, which is the early Ming at the very end of the game. It says **nothing about gender, castration, supply or naming**. Its value is narrow: how a eunuch agency became a parallel power with its own land, troops and closed books, and how officials fought for audit access. That serves palace-faction mechanics for a Ming-era endgame only.

Errors in the article: the abstract dates Yongle's reign 1403–1424 but p. 5 gives 1402–1424. Note 7 dates Tsai's *Eunuchs in the Ming Dynasty* to "1991" and calls the press "New York: SUNY"; it is SUNY Press, Albany, 1996 (see `east_tsai_ming_eunuchs.md`). Monkey's post in *Journey to the West* is printed as "bima 避馬" and the office as "DIS" (p. 16); the novel's title is 弼馬溫 *bimawen*.

## 1. The argument

- **Thesis.** The eunuch Directorate of Imperial Horses (*Yumajian* 御馬監, DIH) worked with mostly foreign (Mongol, later Jurchen) horsemen called Braves (*yongshi* 勇士). Together they "carved a path to the center of the imperial court and the close circle of the emperor" through skill, not examinations (p. 7). The DIH "did not merely shadow the horse administration but completed it" (p. 8). The article argues against both the vilifying sources and Tsai's "parallel government" framing (pp. 7–8).
- **Recruitment outside the registers.** Yongle took in soldiers fleeing Mongolia and had them herd horses "in return for food, supplies, wives, and housing" (p. 10). Hongxi ordered frontier garrisons to take in orphans of "barbarians" and send "those who 'know how to rear horses'" to the DIH (p. 11). "This was a precedent born out of contingency" (p. 12). The palace "tapped into an alternative workforce" instead of corvée from the registered population (p. 12).
- **Answerable only to eunuchs and the throne.** The Braves "took orders only from their eunuch commanders and the emperor himself" (p. 14). In 1458 the DIH director had twenty stable boys made Braves "completely independent of the Ministry of War" (p. 13). Hunts and spectacles meant "eunuchs and horsemen were admitted to the emperor's inner circle and enjoyed a degree of proximity that most court officials could never attain" (p. 14).
- **Land and the audit war.** In 1444 a vice-minister and a eunuch of the rites directorate accused DIH eunuchs of seizing (侵占) Zhengcunba land and running "a temple and a kiln" on it. The Zhengtong emperor "did the opposite": he pardoned them and granted each eunuch one *qing*, but ordered annual inspection by a scrutiny official and a censor (p. 20). The tract was 55,342 *qing*, "an astonishing one-third of the total cultivated land in Shuntian" (p. 19). By 1458 the head eunuch requested supplies directly, "a serious procedural breech" (p. 20). The DIH came to draw 500,000 piculs of grain for a reported 30,000 staff. A revenue official said of its horses: "the few became many, and the dead were made alive" (p. 21). Officials won back access only in the early sixteenth century (p. 21).
- **The historiography is hostile on purpose.** The compilers of the 1460s were in "an open struggle to regain control of the DIH", so "they highlighted the DIH as a nest of corruption and crime" (p. 15).
- **Tumu 1449.** Officials blamed the eunuch Wang Zhen for the disaster. Grass suggests the Braves' confidence also pushed the emperor to campaign (p. 22).
