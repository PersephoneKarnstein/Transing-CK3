## 0. Framing warning

- The *Tiruvāymoḻi* and its commentaries are the scripture of the living Śrīvaiṣṇava community. Use the dated statements for the faith's own theology, voiced by invented characters. Never present Śrīvaiṣṇavism as the faith.
- The poems use death-wish lines (9.9.2; 10.3.2–3; and others). Do not quote them in loc. The brief forbids suicide content.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **Date and author.** Piḷḷāṉ's *Six Thousand* (*Ārāyirappaṭi*) is the first commentary on Nammāḻvār's *Tiruvāymoḻi*. It was "written between 1100 and 1150 C.E." by Tirukkurukai Pirāṉ Piḷḷāṉ, cousin and disciple of Rāmānuja (p. xi). That is in period.
- **Its language.** It is the earliest clear evidence that Sanskrit and Tamil devotion were fused (*Ubhaya Vedānta*). It is written in a new mixed Tamil full of Sanskrit nouns (p. xi).
- **The poem.** In "roughly a quarter" of the poem the poet takes the voice of a lovesick girl, or of her mother or friend (p. 98). That is "about twenty-five" of 100 decads (p. 27).

## 2. What matters for the design: the female voice, with dated statements

### 2.1 The poem's girl, mother and friend (ch. 2, pp. 26–32; 9th-century poem)

- **The *akam* stances.** Classical love poetry gives verses to the heroine's mother, foster mother or girlfriend. "Sometimes, the poet speaks of himself in the third person, through the words of the heroine's mother, foster mother, or girlfriend" (p. 27).
- **Nammāḻvār breaks the rules.** *Akam* poems should name no one. "He is named as a person in the love poems" (p. 27).
- **Possession misdiagnosed** (4.6.8). Her family hires dancers to exorcise her. The friend says the dancers worship the wrong god; the cure is the Lord (pp. 28–29).
- **The mother's girl dances** (2.4.1): "Dancing, dancing, her body wilting ... is my girl with a glowing forehead" (p. 29).
- **The mother on a girl possessed by union** (5.6.3, Ramanujan's translation): "How can I explain my girl / to you who see nothing / but this world?" (p. 29). Use one line only.
- **The palmyra horse (*maṭal*)** (5.3.9–10). Riding the palmyra-stem horse was the classical lover's public act of desperation, done by a man (p. 30). In the girl's voice the poet vows to ride it "through every street in town", and "women from all lands will cheer me on" (p. 30).
  - Venkatesan 2007 adds that the grammars allowed only a man to ride the *maṭal*, and that Tirumaṅkai has his heroine threaten it (Venkatesan 2007 n. 11; see `ind_dehejia_venkatesan_antal.md`).

### 2.2 Piḷḷāṉ's own statements (1100–1150)

**What this shows.**
- **A persona.** Piḷḷāṉ, the earliest commentator, treats the girl as the poet's **persona**: a "guise", a likeness ("like a lady"), a voice he speaks "through". Carman and Narayanan agree that the maiden is "a dramatic persona directly expressing his own identity" (p. 100).
- **But he keeps her pronoun.** In the 6.2 introduction Piḷḷāṉ narrates a whole decad in the feminine, and his comments to 10.3 refuse to separate the girl from the poet. The persona carries a woman's pronoun through a whole decad of commentary.
- **"God alone is male" is not here.** The gloss that God alone is male and every soul is a woman does **not** appear in what was read of Piḷḷāṉ or in Carman and Narayanan.
  - Venkatesan 2007 states it as a "fundamental Srivaishnava principle that all souls are ultimately female with the only male". She attributes it to the commentaries "11th century onwards" but cites no passage (Venkatesan 2007 p. 18 and n. 9).
  - **Grade: modern scholarly summary, unverified in a dated text.** The narrator should keep using the 13th-c. *Ācāryahṛdayam* line and Ramanujan's "before God all men are women" (`ind_ramanujan_bhakti.md`), not "God alone is male".

### 2.3 Āṇṭāḷ and the consorts (pp. 75–76)

- **Śrīvaiṣṇava doctrine** recognises three consorts: Śrī, Bhū (Earth) and Nappiṉṉai (p. 75).
- **Āṇṭāḷ "is said to have been married to the image form of the Lord ... at the Srirangam Temple"**. She is treated as a human of special rank, "not a fourth divine consort", though "sometimes ... considered as an incarnation of Bhū" (p. 75).
- **No jealousy.** There seems to have been no "problem of jealousy" between the goddesses and passionate human devotees, "notably Nammalvar himself" (p. 75).
- **The female container** (ch. 12). Citing O'Flaherty, "the being which swallows and then emits another person is in some ways considered female". Tirumaṅkai's *Periya Tirumoḻi* 11.6.6 calls the Lord "existing as a mother" who holds the worlds in his stomach through the flood (pp. 169–70). This is the Vaiṣṇava parallel to Śiva as mother (Shulman, `ind_shulman_temple_myths.md` §2.3).

## 3. Corrections to the candidate

1. **"Every soul a woman before God" needs rewording.**
   - The earliest commentator (12th c.) treats the girl as the poet's guise. He does not say every soul is a woman. Periyavāccāṉ Piḷḷai (13th c.) also says "guise" (Venkatesan 2010 p. 32).
   - The faith's claim is therefore an **inversion** of the commentators: the guise is the truth. The narrator should say so plainly, as `faith_design.md` already does for the Purim dress of the Nehpakhot.
   - Drop "God alone is male" unless a dated source is found.
2. **Mother and friend are the first witnesses.** In the poems and in Piḷḷāṉ, it is the girl's mother and friend who name her: "my girl", "our friend". The 6.5.10 comment is the strongest in-period line for the faith: the girl is known "by her exceeding love". She is not known by a birth or a sign.
3. **The *maṭal* is in-period material**, and a public one. In the girl's voice the poet threatens to ride the palmyra horse "through every street in town" (5.3.9–10; 9th-c. poem; Piḷḷāṉ's commentary is 12th c.).

## 4. Design implications

- **Gate step 1 (the waking) needs witnesses.** The poems give two in-period witness roles:
  - **the mother** (or foster mother), who diagnoses the change ("I cannot diagnose her state");
  - **the friend** (*tōḻi*), who names her ("our friend").
  - **Mechanic:** the seeker's mother, foster mother or closest friend is asked for her word. A friend or mother who speaks for her gives a large bonus to the vow and a memory: "She said: we know you by your love."
  - A refusal sets up a later reconciliation event. The mother might say "I cannot explain my girl to you who see nothing but this world" (5.6.3).
- **New optional event: "The Palmyra Horse"** (public declaration).
  - The daughter declares herself in public, through every street, before she has the rite.
  - Outcomes: large scandal and rivalries, but renown, and women of the faith "from all lands" gain opinion of her (5.3.10).
  - Do **not** depict the *maṭal*'s wounds. Shulman reads the ride as self-mortification (Shulman n. 30), and the brief rules out self-harm; keep it a ride and a cry.
  - This gives the Garland a distinct flavour: the coming-out is a convention of Tamil love poetry turned to the girl's voice.
- **Possession misdiagnosed** (4.6.8). Her family calls exorcists, and the faith's friend says "you worship the wrong god". The event is a branch in which a hostile family treats her change as possession. Resolve it in her favour through a friend, never through an exorcism.
- **Theology lines** (faith voice; ≤ one sentence each):
  - "We know her by her exceeding love." (after Piḷḷāṉ on 6.5.10)
  - "The teachers called it a guise; we call it her face." (narrator marks it as the faith's inversion)
  - "Even the Lord holds the worlds as a mother holds a child." (after *Periya Tirumoḻi* 11.6.6)
- **Doctrine: Śrī mediates.** Piḷḷāṉ's first statement of Śrī as mediator (6.10.10) can ground a faith tenet or modifier. Petition through the Lady: the daughter's requests to a liege or temple go better with a woman patron as mediator, such as a queen, a mother or a senior daughter.
- **Never.**
  - Death-wish lines from the girl poems.
  - "God alone is male" as an in-period quotation.
  - The *araiyar* or living reciters as the faith (`ind_narayanan_vernacular_veda.md`).
