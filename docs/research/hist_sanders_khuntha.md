# Sanders, "Gendering the Ungendered Body" (1991): the *khuntha* in Islamic law, design reading

Source: Paula Sanders, "Gendering the Ungendered Body: Hermaphrodites in Medieval Islamic Law", ch. 5 in Nikki R. Keddie and Beth Baron, eds., *Women in Middle Eastern History: Shifting Boundaries in Sex and Gender* (New Haven and London: Yale University Press, 1991), pp. 74-95 (text pp. 74-89, notes pp. 89-95).
Read in full, notes included, from a text extraction of the chapter kept outside the repo. Quotes verbatim; page numbers are the book's.
Sanders's legal base (n. 14, pp. 91-92) is mostly eleventh-century Hanafi law: al-Sarakhsi (d. 1090), *al-Mabsut* vol. 30, "Kitab al-khuntha" (all unattributed block quotes are his, n. 16). She checks it against al-Quduri (d. 1036), al-Marghinani (d. 1196) and the Shi'i jurist al-Tusi (d. 1067), and finds "minor variations" but no "different conception of the hermaphrodite". The dates suit the 867 and 1066 starts.

**Scope warning.** The chapter is about intersex people: people born with ambiguous genitals, or with none. It is not about trans women, eunuchs or *mukhannathun*. The mod should use it as a **contrast** that shows how the neighbouring law handles sex. It is not a precedent for the daughters. Do not let the *khuntha* stand in for a trans woman in loc.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **A binary drawn harder than Europe's.** "No aspect of life in medieval Islamic societies was free from considerations of sex" (p. 74). The male-female boundary was "so impenetrable" that Islam "differed from medieval and early modern European societies, where the boundary between male and female was more permeable" (pp. 75-76). Other boundaries could be crossed: Muslim and non-Muslim by conversion, slave and free by manumission. "The sexual boundary was different, since it could not be crossed legitimately simply by an act of will" (p. 76).
- **The medicine was Galenic, and still binary.** Muslim physicians held that both parents contribute semen and "the child would have the sex of the parent whose semen dominated" (p. 76). "The preference for the Hippocratic-Galenic over the Aristotelian tradition seems to have been due largely to its consistency with the classical Islamic understanding of the Quranic doctrine of creation". Qur'an 51:49, "we have created of everything a pair", was read as male and female (p. 76). "If the semen of neither parent dominated, the child would be a hermaphrodite" (p. 77).
- **Never both, at most neither.** Al-Sarakhsi says male and female "are not combined in one person, because they are dissimilar by way of being contradictory". Sanders: "Nowhere in these texts is there even the slightest suggestion that a khuntha is both a man and a woman. Human beings had to be either male or female; sometimes they seemed to be neither, but they could not be both" (p. 77). Every person had "a true sex—known at least, or perhaps only, to God" (p. 77).
- **How a *khuntha* is assigned.** The jurists ran a fixed series of tests:
  - *Urinary orifice.* The rule is *al-hukm li'l-mabal*, "the judgment is attributed to the urinary orifice", backed by the hadith "the inheritance is awarded to the urinary orifice" (p. 77). If the child urinated from both, the first to flow decided. Some jurists used the greater quantity instead. Al-Tusi used the last to stop (p. 78).
  - *Extra organs.* Once sex was assigned, the other organs became *'aib*, "defect", with "no legal value", and "they could be removed surgically" (p. 78, citing Avicenna, n. 17).
  - *Fringe tests.* Counting ribs, or "the inclination of its nature", were offered too. Sanders: "This is the only instance where anything other than strictly biological measures were suggested ... it does not reflect the conventional juridical wisdom" (p. 78).
  - *Puberty.* Failing all that, the child waited for the signs of puberty. For a man these were penile intercourse, a beard and nocturnal emissions. For a woman they were breasts, menstruation, vaginal intercourse, conception and lactation. Any one sign settled both sex and majority. The *khuntha*'s own report of the sign was believed, "because no one else could know about it" (p. 78).
  - *Irreversibility.* "Once the sex of a person had been established, that judgment was irreversible, regardless of any evidence that might be produced to the contrary" (p. 79). If breasts grew after a nocturnal emission had settled the case, they "had no legal consequence" (p. 79). In al-Sarakhsi's words the judgment "is not changed by urination from the other organ" (n. 21, pp. 92-93).
- **The *khuntha mushkil*.** This is the "technical legal term for a hermaphrodite who had passed the age at which puberty normally occurs without manifesting any of its signs" (p. 79). The label stuck: having admitted that its sex could not be told, the *mushkil* could not later claim puberty (p. 79). Al-Sarakhsi glosses it as "having no sex or its not being known whether it is a male or female" (p. 83).
- **"Gendering" instead of a third category.** The jurists could not find a true sex for the *mushkil*. They still "had to assign the khuntha mushkil to one of these categories in order to either prescribe or proscribe, because the rules for men and women were different" (p. 79). Sanders calls this "gendering" (p. 79), using the modern word knowingly. "This strategy was possible not because new categories were invented, but by virtue of the structure of Islamic law itself. If sex was not arranged on a continuum for medieval Muslims, the moral universe encoded in Islamic law was" (p. 80). The tool for this was *ihtiyat*, precaution: choosing the course "founded on certainty rather than uncertainty" (p. 80). "No new principles of law were introduced to accommodate the khuntha" (n. 18, p. 92).
- **Prayer position: an in-between place inside the hierarchy.** "It should stand behind the men and in front of the women as a precaution" (p. 80). "Should it turn out to be a man, he would simply constitute the last row of men; should it turn out to be a woman, she would be the first row of women" (p. 81). Funerals keep the same order: man nearest the imam, then *khuntha*, then woman. In a shared grave the man is placed nearest the qibla, "because facing the qibla is an honor" (p. 81).
- **When in doubt, the lower rank.** "When in doubt, the rule seemed to be to accord the inferior status to hermaphrodites. What was important was that access to the higher status of men be successfully protected. The rules assured that no hermaphrodite would attain the status accorded to men unless it could be demonstrated that he was, indeed, a man" (p. 81).
- **Inheritance.** "A hermaphrodite inherits in the same proportions as the female, because it must accept the lesser status of the two" (p. 81). A son and a *khuntha* split the estate "in three shares: two for the son and one for the khuntha" (pp. 81-82). In disputes, the oath of the party with nothing to gain decides (p. 82).
- **Dress and modesty: read as female.** "When the concerns of jurists were centered on sitr al-'aura, the hermaphrodite was almost always gendered as female" (p. 82).
  - *Prayer.* An adult *khuntha* veils to pray. A man may veil, and a woman must (p. 82).
  - *Burial.* If no one may lawfully see its body, the *khuntha* is given the dry ablution (*tayammum*) instead of the full bath. It is shrouded "like a girl, because this is closest to the sitr" (p. 83).
  - *Pilgrimage.* Pilgrim dress defeated the method. A man in *ihram* may not wear seamed clothing and a woman must, so both choices were "equally forbidden". Al-Sarakhsi "admitted that he did not know the answer" (pp. 83-84). Others ruled that the *khuntha* should dress as a woman, and al-Shaibani called doubt about its sex "one of the gravest [extenuating] circumstances" (p. 84).
- **Examination and circumcision.** A man looking would break the rules of modesty, and so would a woman. So "a slave girl who is knowledgeable in these matters is bought for him from his own money to examine him". If the *khuntha* is a woman, a woman looks at a woman; if a man, "it is a slave [mamluka] looking at her master" (p. 84). On the same reasoning a *khuntha* "was circumcised by a slave girl purchased for him either from his own funds or, if he was indigent, from the funds of the public treasury" (p. 85). Physicians "do not seem to have been participants in this process" (p. 84). Sanders: "what could be more in the interests of the public good than the knowledge of whether someone was male or female?" (p. 85).
- **Marriage needs an owner and an owned.** A *khuntha*'s marriage could not be judged valid until its sex was known. Sanders says the reason was not inheritance, incest or modesty. It was that "the male entered marriage as a malik (owner), whereas the female became mamluka (owned)" (p. 87). "Two people cannot both enter a marriage as owners. One must enter as the malik (owner), one as the mamluka (owned). And this means that one must be male and one female" (p. 87). If a father married a *khuntha* to a man and it then showed male signs, "the nikah is null" (p. 87). The incest rules were applied too: a man who kissed a *khuntha* "with lust" may not marry its mother until its sex is known (p. 88).
- **No anxiety about homosexuality.** Sanders notes "the complete absence of the anxiety about homosexuality that pervades the European texts on hermaphroditism" (p. 88). The jurists worried about modesty and incest instead.
- **What it says about a binary legal order.** The jurists' "overriding anxiety" was "maintaining the gendered integrity of their world as a whole". "In this, medieval Muslims were closer to modern Americans than, say, to the ancient Greeks" (p. 88). A person with no assigned gender "had no gender and, therefore, no point of entry into the social world" (p. 88). "Hermaphrodites were usually gendered in the world of ritual as female" (p. 88). One ungendered person suspends the kin network around it: "the ungendered person was not only unsocialized, but could desocialize everyone else" (p. 89). Conditional divorces and manumissions that turned on a child's sex were held "in abeyance" (p. 85). Closing line: what was at stake was "gendering the most important body: the social body" (p. 89). The jurists did not think biology was constructed. They thought the boundary mattered "because it implied a difference in social anatomy" (n. 33, p. 94).
- **How the *khuntha* differs from the *mukhannathun*.** Sanders never mentions the *mukhannathun*. The contrast below combines her chapter with `hist_rowson_mukhannathun.md` and `hist_marmon_eunuchs_islamic.md`.
  - *Khuntha.* Sex is **unknown**, and the problem is legal classification. The jurists treat it neutrally, as a matter of procedure and precaution, with no moral blame. It is born, not made.
  - *Mukhannath.* Sex is **known**, and he is male. The problem is conduct: dress, henna, access to women. He is cursed and banished, and he is one of the boundary-violating behaviours. Sanders lists "transvestism" among the "abominations" alongside sodomy and adultery (n. 8, p. 90).
  - *Eunuch.* Made, not born. Their "legal status was clearly defined; they were slaves", created "outside the lands of Islam" (n. 8, p. 90). Marmon (n. 256) sets out the same three-way split: the hermaphrodite "confuses" the path to gendered adulthood, while the eunuch stands "completely outside of it".
  - *Shared root.* The words share a root. The *Kitab al-'Ayn* derived *mukhannath* from *khuntha* (Rowson p. 673). The law kept them apart.
  - *Same middle position.* The two practices still meet in one place. The *khuntha*'s place between the men's and women's rows matches Rowson's Jamila concert, where "Tuways and his companions" sit between the men and the women. In both, the middle position is a rung in a hierarchy, not a third category.
