## 1. Baumgarten, "Prier à part ?" (2016)

### The argument in brief

- Scholars have treated the 13th century as the moment women were marginalised in Jewish and Christian worship alike (p. 45). Grossman and others explain the change as a male "revanche" against women's economic power.
- Baumgarten calls that reasoning circular (p. 46). She asks instead what women actually did and where they stood (p. 44 n. 4).
- Her answer: women stayed **visible and present**. Exclusion was real but "bien plus graduelle" ("much more gradual"), and came after the 13th century (p. 58). Even women who were ritually impure "restaient à l'extérieur du bâtiment mais elles étaient néanmoins bien présentes" ("stayed outside the building but were nonetheless very much present", p. 58).

### What matters for the design

- **Separate women's synagogues** were built at Worms (1212/13), at Speyer (second half of the 13th c.), at Cologne (1281), and at Nuremberg and Prague (p. 46).
  - Scholars disagree about what they mean. Elbogen and Abrahams read them as a sign of women's strength: women "auraient exigé d'avoir leurs propres lieux de culte" ("would have demanded places of worship of their own", p. 47). Others read them as marginalisation (p. 47; Grossman 2004: 181–182).
  - Baumgarten's own view: these buildings predate the hostile rulings of the late 13th century (p. 54).
- **Dulcea of Worms** (d. 1196–97). Eleazar's elegy (in Marcus's translation, pp. 47–48):
  - she makes wicks "pour les synagogues et les écoles" ("for the synagogues and the schools") under Prov. 31:18, "her lamp does not go out by night";
  - "dans toutes les villes elle a enseigné aux femmes à chanter" ("in every town she taught the women to sing");
  - she "vient tôt à la synagogue, et y reste tard" ("comes early to the synagogue and stays late");
  - she prepares the candles.
- **Dulcea prayed in a building with no women's annex.** Worms had none until 1212/13, so she prayed "dans le même bâtiment et dans le même espace que les hommes" ("in the same building and the same space as the men", pp. 48–49). Baumgarten adds that it is unclear **where** she taught women to sing (p. 48).
- **Purity** (pp. 49–50).
  - In the 11th–12th centuries a few very pious women chose to stay out while menstruating. Ra'aviah says they stood outside, and he praised impure **men** who did the same.
  - By the next century **all** women in Germany were asked to stay out, while impure men kept coming.
  - The asymmetry came from the need for a men's minyan, traditional tolerance of male impurity, and "la pureté rituelle des femmes comme symbole de la spécificité juive" ("women's ritual purity as a symbol of Jewish distinctiveness", p. 50).
  - Some sources let menstruants in so long as they would not see the Torah scroll (p. 50).
- **The ba'alat brit (godmother)** (pp. 51–54). Maharam (R. Meir of Rothenburg, late 13th c.) objected to a woman sitting "parmi les hommes dans la synagogue" ("among the men in the synagogue"). His pupil complained that no one listened: "Est-ce sans raison que la section réservée aux femmes a été séparée ?" ("Is it for nothing that the women's section was set apart?", p. 52). By Maharil (15th c.) women carried the infant only to the door (pp. 52–53).
- **The Speyer door.** Speyer had a small door between the women's and the men's synagogue, put in during the late 13th century. Baumgarten thinks it may have let women be **called to the Torah when there were not enough men** (Teshuvot Maharam no. 108; p. 54).
- **Women donors were blessed by name.**
  - A 14th-century Regensburg prayer book has a *mi sheberakh* for women who pledge charity for the memory of the dead: "Celui qui a béni Sarah, Rebecca, Rachel et Leah devrait bénir Plonit b. Ploni" ("May He who blessed Sarah, Rebecca, Rachel and Leah bless N. daughter of N.", pp. 56–57).
  - The Nürnberg Memorbuch lists almost as many women as men as donors for the dead (p. 55).
  - In the 15th century the blessing for a married woman was folded into her husband's (p. 57).

### Corrections

1. **Reading list item 12 says Dulcea "led women's prayer before the separate building existed (§§6–7)".** That overstates the article. [B] says she taught women to **sing** in every town, prayed daily, came early, stayed late and prepared wicks and candles. Where she taught is unknown (p. 48). "Led women's prayer" is Grossman's broader reading, not Baumgarten's. Also, the Clio text has page numbers, not numbered paragraphs, so cite pp. 47–49.
2. **Date error in [B].** Isserlein is dated "au milieu du XIVe siècle" ("in the mid-14th century", p. 50). He lived c. 1390–1460 ([M] p. 10). Use the 15th century.
3. **Finding 1 is confirmed in substance.** [B] supports separate women's buildings with a connecting door or opening, which fits the Arles "scola … desubtus" (the school below).

### Design implications

- **The Speyer door and the Arles stairs.** The women's synagogue joined to the men's by a door or window is the setting the Turning should use. *Suggestion* for `tm_hipukh.0010.desc`: *"In the women's synagogue below the men's, [tm_zekenah] stands at the little door and gives the women the reader's words …"*.
- **Leah in the women's blessing** (*suggestion*, a new option in `tm_hipukh.0020` or a once-only event after the Turning).
  - When the turned daughter first gives to the synagogue for the dead, the cantor blesses her by the women's formula, "He who blessed Sarah, Rebecca, Rachel and Leah".
  - The blessing names the matriarch whose prayer turned Dinah, and it is the community, not the sect, publicly blessing her as a woman.
  - Effect: +piety and a modest +opinion with the Jewish community. Mark the formula as Ashkenazi (Regensburg, 14th c.); its use in Provence is *unverified*.
- **Wicks for the women's synagogue** (*suggestion*): a flavour line for `tm_hipukh.0020.a`. *"Trim the wicks for the women's synagogue, as Dulcea of Worms did."* This joins the third lamp (the Sabbath lamp) to the synagogue's lights.
- **Called to the Torah when the men are too few** (*suggestion*, a rare event in a small community). Grossman (ch. 8 §4) records Maharam's ruling that in a community of kohanim only, women may be called up and read. This could become a small-community event in which a learned daughter is called. Use only in a county with `tm_kahal` low. This is a design stretch; keep it rare.

## 2. Marienberg, "Menstruation in Sacred Spaces" (2004)

### The argument in brief

- Classical halakhah has "no restrictions on women from entering a synagogue while being in the state of Niddah" (abstract, p. 7).
- The prooftext for exclusion is Lev. 12:4, which is about a woman after childbirth, read through the *Baraita de-Niddah*. That text bars the menstruant from "a house prepared for a prayer" (p. 9).
- **Stages:**
  - 11th–13th centuries: staying away is a praiseworthy custom, not a duty (Rashi's circle, Ravyah, Zedekiah Anav; p. 10);
  - 14th century: stricter voices (Isaac Duran; p. 10);
  - 15th–16th centuries: Isserlein and then Isserles let women in for the High Holidays out of sorrow (pp. 10–11);
  - later: the holy object is the scroll, not the building (pp. 12–14).
- **The women's solution.** Women "could enter the synagogue and only avoid looking towards the scroll" when it was shown (p. 14). Jacob Landau (15th c.) reports that women "pray and they respond to every sacred saying". They are careful only not to look at the scroll (p. 12).

### What matters for the design

- **Rashi's circle:** "it's a purity custom for them and they do well and are to be praised" (p. 10).
- **Benjamin Ze'ev of Arta** (16th c.): women stay away "because of honor not because it is forbidden" (p. 11).
- **Shabbetai Be'er** (17th c.) found "no base to this custom". If impurity mattered, it would be "more reasonable to prescribe restrictions upon impure men", who handle the scrolls (p. 11).
- The synagogue as *mikdash me'at*, a "small temple" (p. 9).
- Most medieval authorities **permitted** entry. Stricter practice came from custom, often women's own (pp. 10, 13). The custom's "socially problematic" cost was loss of "the experience of the communal service" (p. 7; p. 14).

### Corrections

- None to the built content. Note that [M]'s sources are Ashkenazi, Italian and Greek. **None is Provençal or Catalan.** The reading list's description of [M] ("Which women stayed away … when") holds.

### Design implications

- **Answer for the sect** (with `jud_grossman_pious_rebellious.md` §4.1).
  - Through the whole game period, the mainstream position was that a menstruant **may** enter, and that staying away is a praised custom.
  - A daughter who sees no blood is therefore never in question.
  - Born women who stay out are keeping a custom the sect honours. This is the frame for "The Women at the Door" (Grossman note §4.2).
- **Not looking at the scroll** (*suggestion*): flavour for women who keep the stricter custom in the women's synagogue. The scroll is shown, and the women who are in their days turn their eyes away. This is 15th-century evidence; use it only from the 1400s.
- **Opinion modifier wording.** A Jewish observer's −10 (`tm_jewish_daughter_opinion`, "what came forth male is male") stays as built. [M] supplies **no** argument that a turned woman is barred from the women's synagogue. The bar a rabbi would raise is that she is a man (b. Nazir 59a's rationale is excluded by the hard limits), not that she is impure.

## 3. Roos, "Cross-dressing among Medieval Ashkenazi Jews" (2017)

### The argument in brief

- *Sefer Ḥasidim* (13th c.) **permits** cross-dressing in danger:
  - women travellers may dress as nuns or as Christians (§261), or as men, and "gird themselves with swords" (§206);
  - a beautiful woman travelling with her husband made "a fake beard from human hair" and "was saved" (§207);
  - "young boys 'who have not yet grown a beard'" may dress "as Christians or women … 'in order to be saved'" (§207; pp. 4–5, 8).
- Roos finds no earlier Jewish source with such a permission (p. 8).
- Aquinas allows the same in necessity, "to hide from enemies" (*ST* 2-2.169; p. 10).
- **Thesis:** the permissions **reinforce** the binary. Disguise is "a ferry which goes between two shores, without the possibility of stopping in between" (p. 16), "a ferry line with only two stations and no in-between stops" (p. 19).

### What matters for the design

- **Deut. 22:5 in the Talmud and the commentators.** "Merely wearing the clothes of the other sex is not an abomination". Doing so "in order to mix with the other sex in an improper way" is. Rashi and Rashbam follow this. R. Eliezer ben Yaakov reads it as weapons and adornment (p. 8).
  - **Hard limit:** this is the b. Nazir 59a "slip in among women" rationale. Narration never voices it. Hostile text never voices it.
- **The Christian side.** Christians tolerated women dressed as men better than men dressed as women. They suspected men in women's dress of seeking access to women, or of effeminacy or witchcraft. But "men dressed as women having comical functions during festivals or carnivals or … in the medieval theatre" were accepted (p. 10).
- **Disguise against identity.** Roos's definition: the *Sefer Ḥasidim* cases mean "hiding one's true identity" and entail no "desire to assume that other identity". She contrasts modern cross-dressing, which "rather tries to reveal a person's true identity, or certain aspects of it" (p. 6).
- **Women's synagogue and women cantors** (p. 17, after Grossman 180–182).
  - Women's prayer was praised, and the term "women's synagogue" points to "a separate building for women", where "some women also served as cantors".
  - Menstrual avoidance grew from the late 12th century and was credited to "the women themselves" (p. 17).
  - Roos reads it as piety that "strengthened the image of men and women as two separate and distinct categories" (p. 17).
- **The godmother** (p. 17). Women who took men's ritual roles were pushed back; women who intensified women's piety were praised. This is the same ceiling as in the Grossman note §2.2.
- **Dress and Arles.**
  - Roos lists the **Council of Arles, 1234** among the councils that ordered distinguishing dress for Jews (p. 12 n. 10, after Grayzel 1933: XXIX).
  - Jewish women "in many parts of Europe were forced to wear two blue stripes in their veils" (p. 12, after Yeivin 2007). Roos does not name the places, so its use for Provence is *unverified*.
- **Takkanot Shum** (13th c.) forbade Jews to dress like gentiles (pp. 5, 11).

### Corrections

1. **Reading list Finding 5 is confirmed, with one detail.** §207 (Wistinetzki) holds **both** the fake-beard woman and the beardless boys (p. 8; boys p. 5). Margaliot's numbering is §201.
2. **No Purim in Roos.** The article never mentions Purim. For "cross-dressing beyond Purim", the period evidence is only:
   - the danger permissions (temporary, and taken back once the danger passes);
   - Christian festival and theatre cross-dressing (p. 10).
   - **No source here permits keeping the dress.** The sect's act in `tm_hipukh.0011.a`, "Keep the dress on when the joke is over", has no permission anywhere. That is correct as built: it is a claim, not a licence.

### Design implications

- **The ferry, in the faith's voice** (*suggestion*, `tm_hipukh.0003` or a ḥevrah saying).
  - *"The sages say there are two shores and a ferry between them, and that whoever crosses must come back. We crossed. We do not come back, and we did not stop in the middle."*
  - This keeps the built refusal of a third category ("we are not a third thing; we are daughters", `jud_strassfeld_trans_talmud.md`). It is also Roos's thesis turned round: the sect takes the binary at its word and lands on the far shore.
- **Disguise against revelation** (*suggestion*, a line for `tm_hipukh.0011.desc` or option `.a`). *"At Purim the dress hides the young men. On me it hides nothing."* This uses Roos p. 6 in the sect's voice. The narrator does not say it.
- **The worthies' concession, for reaction only.** A worthy may cite §207 to say the law lets a beardless boy pass as a girl only to save his life (reading list Finding 5). *Suggestion*, worthy's line in `tm_hipukh.0003.desc`: *"The pious allowed a beardless boy to pass for a girl on the road, to save his life. Purim is over, and no one is chasing you."* Do not use §206 (women with swords) or the fake beard. Both are female-to-male and stay reaction data only.
- **The Christian bailiff and the women's sign** (*optional; for the user to decide*).
  - After the Turning, under a Christian liege, a bailiff must decide which Jewish sign she wears: the men's badge or the women's veil-mark.
  - It is a sharp point, because her womanhood would be made legible by a mark of persecution.
  - Risks: it puts a persecution device at the centre and is *unverified* for Provence. If used, keep it dry, with no mockery, as with other Christian reaction (no "turning" puns).

## 4. Guerson and Lightfoot, "Crises and community" (2019)

### The argument in brief

- The article draws on about 584 notarial records from 181 registers in Girona, 1390s–1420s, and on about 60 royal chancery registers (p. 95).
- It follows Jewish women and *conversas* through marriage, family and credit after the riots of **10 August 1391** and the Disputation of **Tortosa** (1413–14, with conversions in Girona from 1415).
- At least 40 Jews were killed in Girona in 1391. The community survived in the **Gironella tower** (p. 96).
- Women, above all widows, were central economic actors. They dealt with Christians as well as Jews and used procurators for practical reasons, not out of modesty (pp. 112–124).
- The article is about **economy and family**. It says nothing about the synagogue, prayer, niddah, Purim or dress.

### What matters for the design

- **Naming practice.** "We have found no Jewish women or conversas in the notarial records from late medieval Girona without such identifiers" as "wife of", "widow of" or "daughter of" (p. 99). A woman's legal name was always relational.
- **Catalan Jewish women's names** in the article (as printed):

  Blanca, Constança, Francina and Elionor appear here as baptismal names of *conversas* (Astruga Falcó became Blanca, p. 108). Exclude them from a Catalan Jewish list. Blanca stays on the Provençal list (Shatzmiller).
- **Hebrew and the vernacular.** In 1392 Astruga had her *ketubah* translated into Catalan because only someone "in letris ebraycis sufficienter eruditam" ("sufficiently learned in Hebrew letters") could read it (p. 103 and n. 42).
- **A confraternity is attested at Girona.** Abraham Ravaya was "an administrator for the only Jewish confraternity in Girona" (p. 120). This is a second Western Mediterranean *ḥevrah* beside the Arles *holim* of 1401. No women's membership is recorded.
- **Women as procurators.** Astruga, widow of Ferrer Bonanasch, acted for her son and for another woman (p. 121). This is a legal standing women could hold for one another.
- **Wives and conversion.** After Tortosa, conversions "initially involved husbands but not their wives", and some wives stayed Jewish for years (p. 106). In 1391 Tolrana, on the steps of the Gironella tower, refused to return to her converted husband or to leave Judaism (p. 110).
- **Barrenness and bigamy.** By the late 14th century, royal licences for a second wife were limited to cases of infertility. In 1415 a Girona Jew asked for one because his wife was past childbearing (p. 101 nn. 31, 33).
- **Grossman cited with pages** (useful for the Grossman note): ch. 1 at pp. 10, 14, 18; the gap between image and reality, p. 31 (pp. 100–101).

### Corrections

1. **Reading list item 11 and the reading brief call the article "(Catalan)".** It is written in English, with a Catalan title and abstract.
2. **README "The world" and `faith_design.md` §13 Open, women's names.** The open question of a source for names is now answered for Catalonia as well as Provence (Shatzmiller). See the table above.

### Design implications

- **A Catalan "name of the street"** (*suggestion*). `tm_give_daughter_name_effect` could pick from a Catalan list when the seeker's culture is Catalan or the county is in Catalonia: Astruga, Bonafilla, Regina, Dolça, Estel·lina, Bellaire, Orfila, Tolrana, Amoretes, Desiyada, Preciosa, Briona. Regina overlaps with the Provençal list. All are attested, but only c. 1390–1420. Earlier Catalan names are *unverified*. Kalonymus wrote *Even Boḥan* in Catalonia (Rosen), so a Catalan branch of the sect is plausible in its own voice.
- **"Daughter of"** (*suggestion*, `tm_hipukh.0002`). A notary would write the new name with a relation. A flavour line: *"The notary will write her as 'daughter of' her mother's house."* This marks that a woman's name was always relational. Do not script it into the name itself.
- **The translator event** (`tm_hipukh.0030`) gains a period touch from Astruga's *ketubah*. A woman herself asked for Hebrew to be put into the common tongue (p. 103).
- **End-of-era Catalonia** (*suggestion*, for the late-game arc): 1391 and Tortosa.
  - Keep it to events the article documents: tax relief, the tower, wives who stay Jewish.
  - Do **not** show forced baptism of a sect member on screen.
  - Do not add conversion pressure mechanics without a separate decision by the user.
  - Tolrana's refusal on the tower steps is a model of a woman holding her ground in a dry legal record. It could inspire an elder's backstory, but she must not be claimed as a member of the sect: she is a real, named private person in an archive.

## 5. Answers across the four (for the brief)

- **The women's section.** A separate women's synagogue, sometimes joined to the men's by a door or window, is attested in Ashkenaz from 1212/13 [B pp. 46, 54; R p. 17]. At Arles it was the lower room (Hildenfinger). **Drop "gallery".**
- **Women's prayer and leadership.**
  - Women prayed daily and on the High Holidays, and gave and were blessed by name [B pp. 47–57].
  - Women's prayer leaders sang for women [R p. 17; Grossman].
  - Women who took on men's roles met resistance [B pp. 51–53; R p. 17].
- **The three commandments as practice.** None of the four discusses ḥallah or the lamp as such. [B] gives the lamp a synagogue form: Dulcea's wicks and candles (pp. 47–48). Niddah practice is the subject of [M], and of [B] pp. 49–50.
- **Niddah and access.**
  - Through 867–1453 the main position was that a menstruant may enter, and staying away is a praised custom [M pp. 10, 13].
  - In 13th–14th-century Germany, staying away became general for women and not for impure men [B pp. 49–50].
  - A daughter who sees no blood is never in question. The faith's answer is in `jud_grossman_pious_rebellious.md` §4.1: she keeps the days "as the *zekenah* keeps them".
- **Cross-dressing beyond Purim.**
  - Only for danger, and temporary [R pp. 4–8]. Christian festival and theatre cross-dressing was tolerated [R p. 10].
  - Nothing licenses keeping the dress, and the built content is right to treat it as the sect's claim.
