**Chapter list:**
- **Introduction** (pp. 1–22). The corpus; the Guiyijun period; against the Sino-centric "liberation" narrative; manuscripts as artefacts.
- **1 Multiple-text Manuscripts.** Codices, concertinas and pothis of short scriptures and spells, copied by several hands in a family for apotropaic and funerary use. Skimmed.
- **2 Manuscripts Written by Students.** Lay students from elite families studying secular texts (Analects, Classic of Filial Piety) in monasteries. The verso "scribbles" are assignment records. Skimmed.
- **3 Writing from Left to Right.** Tibetan, Sogdian and Uighur influence on Chinese scribal habits: mandalas, student margins, and donor inscriptions on votive paintings. Skimmed.
- **4 Circulars and Names.** She documents and circulars, and onomastics. Read in full.
- **Concluding Remarks.** Guiyijun Dunhuang was "much less 'Chinese' than normally believed" (p. 249).

## 1. Argument

### The regime (starting-situation facts for the Shazhou seat)
- **Tibetan rule, 786–848.** Dunhuang fell to Tibet "at the end of a long siege in 786" (p. 7). As a result it was "spared from the persecution of foreign religions" of 844–846 (p. 8). The Huichang suppression never reached it, so the confiscation of 845 does not apply at the seat.
- **Zhang Yichao 張議潮 (798/799–872)** led the uprising of 848 and was appointed military commissioner of the Return to Allegiance Circuit (Guiyijun 歸義軍). Galambos dates the circuit 851 to the mid-11th century (p. 1). Zhang "effectively ruled as a king" (p. 9).
- **867, the game start.**
  - Zhang's brother Zhang Yitan, a hostage at the Tang court, died in Chang'an in 867.
  - The court then summoned Zhang Yichao to the capital, "and he had no choice but to comply" (p. 10).
  - He left his nephew **Zhang Huaishen 張淮深 (831–890)** in charge. The court withheld the commissioner title until 888. In 890 Huaishen "was assassinated along with his wife and sons" (p. 10).
  - So at 867 Shazhou is held by an acting nephew whose title Chang'an refuses to confirm, while the founder sits in Chang'an as a hostage.
- **910: the Jinshan state.** Zhang Chengfeng 張承奉 proclaimed the Jinshan state 金山國 with himself as emperor. It was soon forced to become a vassal of the Ganzhou Uighurs (p. 10).
- **914: the Cao family.** **Cao Yijin 曹議金** (d. 935) "took the throne by force" and restored the Guiyijun. He married daughters to the Ganzhou Uighur khagan and the king of Khotan. Cao rule lasted "until the mid-eleventh century, when the expanding Tangut state brought the Hexi region under its control" (p. 10).
- **The library cave was closed shortly after 1006** (pp. 3, 11).
- **The end date is disputed.** Hao 2021 (p. 77) gives the Cao Guiyijun as 914–1002, the year Cao Yanlu fell. Rong's *Eighteen Lectures* (reading list item 58) gives 848–1036. Galambos keeps the vague "mid-eleventh century". The design brief's "867–1036" is the conventional range. Treat 1036 as the Tangut conquest, with a possible Shazhou Uighur interlude before it; this needs checking.
- **Shazhou was not a liberated Chinese city.**
  - The Zhang "had held official posts in the Tibetan administration and rebelled only when the decline of Tibetan political power threatened their interests" (p. 14).
  - Tibetan stayed a working language, at 30–40% of the corpus (p. 13).
  - Many surnames are Sogdian (An, Kang, Cao, Shi, Mi; p. 222).
  - Galambos reads the "bandit turmoil" of P.3544 (855) as Zhang Yichao's own revolt, not Tibetan oppression (p. 202).

### Associations (ch. 4)
- **Officers.** She were "governed by the Three Officers (*sanguan* 三官), comprising the President (*shezhang* 社長), the Manager (*sheguan* 社官) and the Secretary (*lushi* 錄事). In some cases, an officer called Elder (*shelao* 社老)" was also present (p. 199).
- **No names.** "The she in Dunhuang did not have names … it was the list of recipients that defined the group" (p. 199). Outsiders referred to a she by an officer's name: "Eighty-two people of the she of Gao Zhu'er" (Дх-2149).
- **Members.** Mostly lay, with some clergy members; in rare cases a she was entirely clergy (S.5139) (p. 200).
- **Families.** Members of one family often belonged to the same she (S.5698, P.3319). Given names repeat across surnames, so Galambos infers in-laws: "extended family networks must have played an important role" (p. 227). Members also lent to each other (P.2817, p. 225).
- **Purpose.** The P.3544 bylaws (855) give three aims: the emperor, cave-building, and "deceased parents", with *zhui xiong zhu ji* 追凶逐吉, "funerals and weddings within one's own family" (p. 203). He judges these "among the association's most important functions."
- **Joining (P.3266v).** An applicant "does not have any friends, has lived his entire life without a purpose", and so seeks a she (p. 206).
- **Leaving (S.5698).** Luo Shennu and his sons were released for poverty. Afterwards, "if … there are cases of death or birth in the family … these will not concern the members of the she anymore" (p. 207).
- **Circulars (S.1453, 886; pp. 208–215).**
  - Each circular names an assembly point: a hermitage gate, a city gate, a bridge, or a host's house.
  - Fines run in wine: one beaker for late arrival, half a jar for absence. Irrigation-channel associations used the cane: seven strokes (P.5032).
  - The model bylaws S.5629 give seventeen "painful strokes" for disobeying the officers. Galambos suspects such penalties were often commuted to fines (p. 212).
  - The circular returns to the office "to be used as evidence for declaring the fines" (p. 212).
  - The marks 知 and 不知 were probably entered by a messenger, so delivery may have been oral (p. 215).
- **A death circular (P.2817, pp. 224–225).** On Suo Baoding's death the she sends "one jar of ritual ale", and each member brings a peck of millet, a bolt of brown cloth and a bundle of firewood, gathering at dawn.
- **S.527 (pp. 203–206).** The bylaws are an original document. "Treat the older ones as their elder sister, and the younger ones as their younger sister" (p. 204).
  - The Manager is a nun; the President, Secretary and members are lay; the Elder is "simply Nüzi 女子".
  - Fifteen women signed with personal marks. The Secretary signed with a cross although she wrote the documents.
  - Galambos: "the notion that most commoners, especially women, were illiterate is an assumption that has yet to be researched and proven" (p. 206).
  - He reads the bylaws' Confucian quote as claiming that "the bond between members was just as important as biological ties, if not more so" (p. 205).

### Names (ch. 4.6)
- **Apotropaic and theophoric names** were the norm: *Chou* 醜 "ugly", *Gou* 狗 "dog", and *-nu* 奴 "servant (of)" as in Fonu 佛奴 "Servant of the Buddha" or Shennu 神奴. Many translate Sogdian *-vandak* names (pp. 233–245). A daughter could be Qingbi 清婢, "Servant of Purity" (p. 240).
- **Names changed with age.** Zhang Yichao's nephew Zhang Huaicheng 張淮澄 (848–868) had the childhood name Fonu and "began to be called Huaicheng only from the age of seven" (p. 245). In Stein painting 54, only the grandchildren bear *chou* names. Galambos's caveat: some adults kept such names.
