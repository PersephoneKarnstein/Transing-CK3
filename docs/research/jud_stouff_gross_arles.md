**Relation to existing notes.** This note is the companion to `jud_hildenfinger_arles_documents.md`, which reads the Arles notarial documents Stouff builds on. `jud_shatzmiller_jews_of_provence.md` covers the Provençal tax union, the ban licence and the Manosque women. ## 1. The argument in brief

**Stouff, "Chrétiens et juifs"** (pp. 347–365). The relations of Christians and Jews in Arles fall under "segregation, insertion, persecutions" (§4).
- **Segregation.** The community was a world apart "politically, religiously, topographically" (§5). It had its own laws, the *mispatim* (§8). Its streets were the great and little *carriera* in the parish of Saint-Martin, with 58 *hostals* and 80 families in 1437 and 40–60 families by mid-century (§22). It had two cemeteries (Crau, and the Plan du Bourg) (§14). Repeated bans covered sex, food, physicians and public office (§§25–29).
- **Insertion.** Jews were brokers (56 Jewish to 5 Christian, 1435–45), seamstresses, builders and porters (§§32–36). They were physicians to the archbishop himself (§38). They made three-quarters of the town's loans, 1436–40 (§39). The convention of 1385 declared them **citizens** (§51). Converts kept ties with their Jewish kin (§55).
- **Persecution** grew after the 1420s and 1430s: Carnival extortion (1433), mendicant preaching (1457), harvest-worker violence, the destruction of the *carrière* on 7 June 1484, and expulsion in July 1493 (§§3, 59–65). The town's elite protected the Jews to the end; the attacks came from seasonal workers and "people of small condition" (§§60–61).

**Stouff, "Les juifs d'Arles et leurs relations…"** (pp. 317–331). Provençal Jews belonged to the Mediterranean Jewish world. After about 1300 they no longer had direct ties with North Africa. Arles's links ran to **Catalonia** (old and close) and, newly in the 15th century, to **Italy** (§§16–20).
- Of 39 Arles dowry contracts of 1425–50, about **70%** joined a local Jew or Jewess to an outsider (§14).
- After 1391 Arles was "a refuge" for Catalan Jews (§17). The town expelled "the Catalan Jews" in 1438, when Catalans were the enemy (§16; "Chrétiens et juifs" §58).

**Stouff, "Isaac Nathan et les siens"** (pp. 333–346). One notable family of physicians, translators and lenders, followed over 141 years (1353–1494): 33 descendants of the physician-translator Bonjues Nathan across four generations (§6). It practised geographic exogamy and family endogamy (§§9–11). Its women were active. It survived conversions without a break between kin (§27).

**Gross, *Gallia Judaica*.** An alphabetical gazetteer of every French place named in Hebrew sources. Each entry gives the Hebrew spellings, a short history, and a numbered list of the scholars tied to the place. Gross's history of Arles is older and in places wrong (§3, corrections).

## 2. What matters for the design

### 2.1 Women in the council and the assembly

- **Five women in the council, 1401.** In October 1401 the *baylons* told the viguier that the Jews had "taken the habit of holding a council and naming councillors, and of calling **five women** to it, because what concerns all must be approved by all" ("Chrétiens et juifs" §10; ADB 404 E 127, f. 150). Stouff takes this as a sign of "the especially important role of women in Jewish society". The maxim is the legal *quod omnes tangit*.
- **The 1407 assembly.** Esther de Caylar (Hildenfinger's "Estes de Cailario"), granddaughter of Bonjues Nathan, "is authorised to sit in the general assembly of heads of family" that dealt with naming the schoolmaster. She sat "beside another woman, Regina, widow of the physician Abraham Avicdor" ("Isaac Nathan" §20). Stouff's reading is that she was there to represent the Nathan lineage while Isaac was still a child.
- **Seats.** In 1409 Dulcia, widow of Astruc de Clermont, sold a seat "in the school of the Jews, in the men's places, on the upper side" ("Chrétiens et juifs" n. 24). This confirms Hildenfinger's 1452 deed: the men sat above.

### 2.2 The street's alms ("Chrétiens et juifs" §19)

The testaments let Stouff list the alms (*elemosina* or *confratria*) of the *carrière*:
- **the alms of the lamp of the synagogue** (*l'aumône du luminaire de la synagogue*);
- the alms of the poor Jews;
- **the alms of poor Jewish girls to be married**;
- the alms of the Crau cemetery, and of the Plan du Bourg cemetery;
- the alms of the sick (the *holim*: "to care for the sick, clothe the naked, bury the poor");
- the alms of the Jewish *affanatores* (labourers), called in Hebrew *menu* (Stouff's transcription, *unverified*);
- an "*helemosina filiolorum*" that he cannot interpret (testament of Samuel Mosse, 4 Jan. 1428, n. 27);
- the **thacana** (*taqqanah*), funded by a tax on ritually slaughtered meat.

Many testators simply left something "to each of the alms and confraternities of the street of the Jews". Stouff names no members and no officers for any of them, so whether women kept the lamp alms is *unverified*.

### 2.3 Women's work and wealth

- **Venguessona Nathan**, mother of Isaac Nathan ("Isaac Nathan" §§11, 14–17):
  - She married her great-uncle Crescas Nathan, was a widow by 1406 and died in 1436.
  - She was the town's leading lender. With her son she passed 326 loan contracts before notaries in 1431–40, three times the next lender.
  - She kept a shop of goods, filed "among the drapers and linen-sellers", and left it and her credits to two grandsons.
  - "Even the women have books": her testament shows she owned books **in Hebrew and in Latin** (§14).
  - She named her son executor, "trusting with good reason in the probity … of Isaac Nathan my son and heir" (§17).
- **Seamstresses.** The proclamations of 1435–37 name **sixteen Jewish seamstresses (*sartoresses*) and no Christian ones**. Christian women used them ("Chrétiens et juifs" §33). Clothing and cloth were "par excellence" Jewish trades at Arles.
- **Regina, wife of Ruben Vidal Pater**, a Catalan Jew of Perpignan ("Les juifs d'Arles" §16). In 1422 her husband paid an Arlesian, Louis d'Anjou, to go to Barcelona and bring back Regina and their six-year-old son. He was to get 25 florins if she came. If "Regina refused to come", he got 7 florins and had to bring a letter from the Barcelona court or a physician there. Regina was to pay her own and her son's travel. The contract allowed for a wife who might refuse.
- **Mayrona**, daughter of Crescas Cassin, married at Valencia to Salamias Nassi; the couple lived at Arles in 1385 (§16). This is the Mairona of Shatzmiller's 1387 case.
- **Insult and dress.** In 1418 the wife of Isaac de Blavas was denounced for "insulting and horrible words" against Christ ("Chrétiens et juifs" §62). In 1349 the viguier let the community tax "certain young Jews and their wives" who gambled and dressed lavishly (§12). Reaction data only.

### 2.4 Institutions and lords

- **Under the archbishop, then the count** (§6). From Boso and Louis the Blind (921) to the mid-13th century the archbishop held the Jews. Under him, **every year at Sukkot**, the community chose three members to enforce Jewish law, levy taxes and judge, with appeal to the archbishop. Charles of Anjou refused to give that jurisdiction back in 1276. What survived was the Palm Sunday rent of wax, pepper and lampreys "for the guard of the synagogue" (§6).
- **Officers** (§9): variable councillors, auditors (3 or 5), and four *baylons*, "the real leaders". Rule by the rich drew a royal letter in favour of Jews "of middling and lower estate" against "the greater Jews" (§10).
- **The ban**: licensed by the viguier in 1349 and by the archbishop in 1405 (§11).
- **Marriage cases** were settled inside the community under licence (1386/1390, §12).
- **The synagogue's own baylons** (*bayloni scole synagoge*) commissioned a Torah crown, *atara*, from a Paris silversmith in Avignon in 1439 (§17). Christian craftsmen were bound not to work on the Sabbath or Jewish feasts (§20).
- **The school**, reorganised in 1407, had a Bible master and a Talmud master (§18). Its deed was drawn up "in the Occitan tongue" (§54).
- **Kalonymus** described Arles Jews' foods for Ḥanukkah, Shavuot and Passover in *Even Boḥan* (§20). Stouff dates him "1286–after 1328" ("Les juifs d'Arles" §1).

### 2.5 Purim and Carnival

- **1433**: the town's "abbot of youth" extorted the *pelote* from the Jews at Shrove Tuesday. The seneschal's lieutenant ordered the pledges returned (§59).
- **1450**: Stouff reports a young Jew, son of a mason, who wounded a priest and had earlier, with friends, "wanted to give a performance of the Passion of Christ", for which there had been a trial (§63). He asks whether it was "a festive demonstration on the occasion of Purim, a sort of carnival" and leaves it open (§§63–65).
- **Do not use the 1450 case.** It sits next to a ritual-murder accusation of 1478 that Stouff quotes from a Hebrew chronicle (§64). Any Purim-parody-of-Christ event would reproduce an anti-Jewish charge. It is the nearest thing in these sources to a Purim masquerade at Arles, and it must stay out.

### 2.6 Gross: Arles

- **Hebrew names** (p. 73): ארלי, ארלדי, ארלש, and the adjective ארלדי (Isaac ben Sheshet). An Arles Jew is "Arli". The Crau appears in Hebrew in Gerson ben Solomon's *Sha'ar ha-Shamayim* (p. 74).
- **Community** (pp. 77–79):
  - About 200 families c. 1165 (Benjamin of Tudela).
  - A synagogue in the rue Neuve.
  - Three "rectors" appointed under the archbishop's ordinance of **1215**, who judged, taxed and watched observance "like the consuls in the town" (p. 77). Their Latin names are Durantus, Saletus(?) and Ferrerius (p. 80).
  - Labour on the Crau bridge commuted for a rent in 1178 (p. 77).
- **Scholars**, numbered by Gross (pp. 79–89):
  - no. 3: the six leaders Benjamin met c. 1165, Moses, Tobias, Isaac, Solomon, Abba Mari and Nathan.
  - no. 6: **Samuel ibn Tibbon finished his Hebrew translation of Maimonides' *Guide* at Arles on 30 November** 120[4] (p. 80). The OCR reads 1201, a Turin manuscript gives 1205, and the usual date is 1204, *unverified* which Gross prints.
  - no. 8: Meir and his son Kalonymus, *nesi'im* of Davidic descent, mid-13th century (pp. 80–81).
  - no. 13: Gerson ben Solomon, *Sha'ar ha-Shamayim*, c. 1280–1300 (p. 82).
  - 1306: Abba Mari of Lunel stayed some months at Arles after the expulsion from France and was not warmly received (p. 83).
  - no. 17: **Kalonymus ben Kalonymus**, born at Arles "towards the end of 1286", alive there in 1328 (pp. 84–85). He **studied at Salon** under Abba Mari ben Eligdor (Sen Astruc de Noves) and Moses of Beaucaire. He wrote *Even Boḥan* "in December 1322 and the first days of 1323", in Catalonia. Immanuel's "nasi Samuel" urged him home (p. 85).
  - no. 18: **Bendich Aym (Abin)**, physician to Queen Joanna at Arles in 1369. He "knew Arabic, Greek and Latin" and "gave public lectures on mathematics" in Arles (p. 85). He is Hildenfinger's Bendig Aym, the father of four daughters.
  - 1387: the Duran de Cadenet marriage scandal (pp. 87–89; Shatzmiller's Arles 1387).
- **Names.**
  - **Meirona** (Mairona, Mayrona) "corresponds to the biblical name of Miriam" (p. 88). So does the Narbonne **Matrona** (p. 404).
  - **Salva** is a woman's name, the feminine of Salves (p. 87).
  - *Cregud* and *Cregudon* are Provençal men's names (p. 88). So Hildenfinger's "Cregudon Cohen" of 1431 is probably a man.

### 2.7 Gross: Lunel, Montpellier, Narbonne

- **Lunel** (pp. 277–280):
  - Called in Hebrew by the moon in its name: Yeriḥo, "Jericho" (ירחו, from *yareaḥ*, moon). Its rabbis took the byname **Yarḥi**. Abba Mari called a book *Sefer ha-Yareaḥ*, and Zeraḥiah ha-Levi called his *Ma'or*, "Luminary", for his name and his town (p. 278).
  - Benjamin found 300 Jews there; its rich men supported **foreign students in its schools** (p. 278).
  - It was "the residence of the Torah" and "the forecourt of the Temple" (p. 279).
  - Meshullam ben Jacob and his five sons (p. 279): Judah ibn Tibbon translated Baḥya for him. Aaron defended Maimonides. Asher lived as an ascetic.
  - Maimonides' letter on the *Guide* went to the sages of Lunel, Jonathan ha-Kohen above all (p. 325).
- **Montpellier** (pp. 322–325):
  - Called simply **Har**, "the Mount". Its Jews called it **Har ha-Bayit**, "the Temple Mount". Yedaiah ha-Penini called it **Har ha-Qodesh**, "the holy mountain", and "compares it to Jerusalem" (p. 324).
  - Its Jews contributed "for a large part" to the medical school (p. 324).
  - Its scholars took the byname Harari, as Jacob ben Makhir ibn Tibbon did (p. 323).
  - Benjamin praised its sages c. 1165 (p. 325).
- **Narbonne** (pp. 401–408):
  - Hebrew נרבונה; the byname **Narboni** spread as far as Algiers (pp. 401–402).
  - A funerary inscription of **689** names Justus, **Matrona** and **Dulciorella** with "peace upon Israel" and a seven-branched candlestick. Reinach and Gross take them as Jews (pp. 403–404).
  - The legend has Charlemagne bring **Makhir** from Babylon to head the community. Gross treats this as legend, but holds that Makhir's descendants were the *nesi'im*, "Jewish kings", with lands (pp. 404–405). The line runs Todros, Kalonymus the Great, Todros, Kalonymus (c. 1165), Moses, Levi (pp. 406–407).
  - About 2,000 souls before the war that followed Viscount Aymeric's death (1137 in Gross; the usual date is 1134, *unverified*). Many left, and Benjamin found 300 (p. 405).
  - Its masters were held equal to "the heads of the old Babylonian academies" (p. 406).
  - Levi the *nasi* probably presided over the 1215 assembly at Saint-Gilles against the Lateran decrees (p. 407).

## 3. Corrections to current docs

1. **"A women's *ḥevrah*" / "the sick-watch as cover"** (README, faith_design §13, `judaism_reading_list.md` finding 2). Stouff names eight alms in the street and gives none a female membership. The *holim*'s statutes name only men (Hildenfinger). What **is** attested for women is the **council** (five women, 1401), the **assembly** (1407), wealth and books (Venguessona), and the seamstresses' trade. Model the sect on those (§4.2).
2. **Gross's date for the expulsion**, "1488" (p. 79), is wrong. Hildenfinger prints Charles VIII's letters of July 1493, and the last Jews were put out in September 1494. Stouff: 7 June 1484 for the sack (Hildenfinger has 8 or 10 May), July 1493 for the expulsion. None of this falls inside 867–1453, so it touches docs only.
3. **"Salonica"** (`judaism_reading_list.md` Part B, finding 5) is confirmed as Salon: Gross p. 85.
4. **The 1215 leaders.** Shatzmiller's "three … in the manner of the Christian consuls" matches Gross (p. 77). Stouff adds that they were chosen yearly at Sukkot (§6). Gross has the archbishop "prescribe" the election. Both agree that the archbishop held the Jews until 1276.
5. **Holy-site loc.**
   - `holy_site_tm_montpellier_effects` ("The translators' country") is fair, since the Tibbonids were "Harari" of Montpellier. Gross gives a stronger, period epithet (§4.4).
   - `holy_site_tm_narbonne_effects` is confirmed (pp. 404–407).
   - `tm_arles` "where Kalonymus ben Kalonymus was born" is confirmed (p. 84).
6. **Mairona = Miriam** (Gross p. 88). The street list's top name is also the vernacular of a scriptural one. Note this in the comment in `tm_rite_effects.txt`.

## 4. Design implications (proposed, not approved)

### 4.1 `tm_hipukh.0010` (see `jud_hildenfinger_arles_documents.md` §5.1)

Stouff's 1409 seat ("men's places, on the upper side") is a second witness that the men sat above and the women below. Use "the women's synagogue, below the men's". Do not use "gallery".

### 4.2 The sect as a circle of women, not a women's *ḥevrah*

Recommended frame, in the faith's voice: **"the women of the lamp."** These are householders of the street who sit together in the women's synagogue below. They give to the *holim* and the dowry alms, and they keep the alms of the synagogue lamp (Stouff §19; the lamp's place is in the 1452 deed). Mechanics:
- **`tm_hipukh.0003` "Before the Elders".** Add a `triggered_desc` when the county has `tm_kahal`:

  ```
  tm_hipukh.0003.desc_council:0 "Purim is over, and I am still wearing the dress. The baylons have called me before the council, and five women sit with them, as the custom of this street has been since the year of the holim: what concerns all must be approved by all. One reminds me that the law sets lashes for a man who adorns himself as a woman; another, that one does not cite miracles."
  ```

  In `0003.a`'s win chance: `modifier = { add = 10 location ?= { county ?= { has_variable = tm_kahal } } }`. The date gate is optional: the custom is attested only from 1401, so `current_date >= 1401.1.1` keeps it honest. Before that year, use the plain desc.
- **`tm_hipukh.0020` (Three Lamps).** New option, "Give oil for the synagogue lamp." Effect: small gold, +piety, +5 opinion with Nehpakhot courtiers. Loc: "The women of the lamp keep the synagogue's light as I keep the Sabbath's."
- **Dowry alms.** An option in `0020` or a yearly event: "Give to the alms for poor girls to be married." This grounds Shatzmiller's Reillanne "marriage of a daughter or sister" in Arles too.
- **`tm_hipukh.0011` "The Revel".** The dress can come from the street's own trade. Desc variant: "…and one puts on a woman's dress for the joke, sewn by the seamstresses of the carrière." Stouff counts sixteen Jewish seamstresses in 1435–37 (§33).
- **Venguessona as the template for the elder.** A widow with a shop, a lender, a reader of Hebrew and **Latin** books. `tm_create_hevrah_elder_effect` could give `education_stewardship` as an alternative to learning. It also supports `tm_hipukh.0030`'s woman translator as plausible, though not attested: Venguessona owned Latin books, not Arabic ones.
- **Mobility.** About 70% of Arles Jews married outsiders (Stouff "Les juifs d'Arles" §14), and Arles took in Catalan Jews after 1391 (§17). A small Adar-season event: "A kinswoman from Barcelona". A seeker of the Turning arrives through a marriage contract and may refuse to come, as Ruben Vidal Pater's wife could (§16). Gate it after 1391 in the Crown of Aragon. Expulsion of "the Catalan Jews" from Arles in 1438 could be a hostile follow-up for a Provençal lord at war with Aragon.

### 4.3 Officers and lords (supports `jud_shatzmiller_jews_of_provence.md` §5)

- Rename "the worthies" in loc to **"the baylons and the council"** where it means Arles: `0003.desc`, `0003.a.win` and `.lose`, `0040.c`.
- Before 1276 the archbishop of Arles held the Jews. A county lord who is the **archbishop** (a theocracy holding `b_arles`) is historically right for the 867–1276 window. `tm_turning_niddui_effect` already lets the lord refuse the ban.

### 4.4 Holy-site loc

```
 holy_site_tm_arles_effects:0 "The Jewish street of Arles, where Kalonymus ben Kalonymus was born and Samuel ibn Tibbon finished the Guide in Hebrew. Its women sit in their own synagogue, below the men's, and the Nehpakhot keep the alms of its lamp."
 holy_site_tm_montpellier_effects:0 "“The Mount”, its Jews called it, and one of their poets likened it to Jerusalem: the town of the translators and the physicians."
 holy_site_tm_narbonne_effects:0 "The great Jewish community of the Midi, whose nasi, of the house of David as they said, held fields no lord could take. A stone of the year 689 says “peace upon Israel”."
```

The Ibn Tibbon date is 1204, *unverified* in Gross's OCR. "Of the house of David as they said" keeps the claim in the community's voice (Gross pp. 80, 405; Shatzmiller p. 130).

**Lunel**, if a fifth Provençal site or an event location is wanted: "Yeriḥo, the moon's town, whose rich men kept the students of other lands; Maimonides wrote to its sages." (Gross pp. 278, 325). It is not proposed as a holy site; Hamadan (Esther's tomb) carries the Purim theme better.

## 5. Content to keep out

- The 1450 "Passion" case and the 1478 ritual-murder charge (§§63–64). The 1418 blasphemy charge (§62).
- Stouff's figures on lending (75% of loans) can inform the economy, but no event may make lending a motive or a trait.
- The 1484 sack and the 1493–1501 expulsions fall after 1453. They should not be foreshadowed in event text.
- Gross's account of the Jewish soldier of 508 accused of treason (p. 76). Gross himself judges the charge invented. Do not use it.

## 6. Open questions

- Who kept the lamp alms? Stouff's testaments (ADB 405 E 90 and others) are the place to look.
- The 1401 council document (ADB 404 E 127, f. 150) deserves a transcript: were the five women named, and what did they vote on?
- Gross's Arles scholars after p. 89, and his Lunel, Montpellier and Narbonne lists past the pages read, for any woman or any Purim custom.
