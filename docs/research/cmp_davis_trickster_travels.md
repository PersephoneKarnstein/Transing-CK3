## 1. The argument in brief: how reliable is Leo?

- **The text we have is not the text Pory translated.**
  - Al-Hasan al-Wazzan (Yuhanna al-Asad, "Leo Africanus") finished the manuscript in Italian on 10 March 1526. "We know the book best and most accurately from the sole surviving copy" (ch. 3).
  - Ramusio's 1550 edition is the base of every early translation. Ramusio made the Italian more literary and made "brief omissions and additions". Some of his changes alter "content and self-presentation" (ch. 3).
  - Ramusio "was certainly not going to let this prize African appear unpersuasive for his European readers" (ch. 3).
  - Davis treats John Florian's Latin (1556) and Pory's English (1600) as following Ramusio's printed text, including its added anti-Islamic wording (ch. 6, nn. 6-7). **Pory, and so the Hakluyt pages the reading list cites, carries Ramusio's changes.**
- **An Italian audience first.** Leo wrote "with two audiences in mind". His "primary audience was in Italy", but some local detail was kept for possible Maghribi readers (ch. 3, n. 59).
- **He placed same-sex life in particular places, not in "Islam".** Leo puts same-sex relations in "special institutions and places (certain hotels, wine houses, and government-taxed bordellos)" and in particular religious practices. He does not blame "some general Muslim miasma or 'infection'", which was the European stereotype in Spandugino (ch. 7).
- **Ambivalent, not simply hostile.** Leo calls the men "accursed", yet says he was at Sufi banquets "many times" (Ramusio cut the phrase), praises Ibn Sahl's love poems to a man as "most elegant and most sweet", and "had known something of these networks" (ch. 7, nn. 27-31).
- **His sexual vocabulary is partly Roman street slang.**
  - Leo calls the women diviners "le Fregatrice" (CGA 175r). This was Italian argot he "seems to have picked up" in Rome. Ramusio changed it to the learned Latin *fricatrices* (ch. 7, n. 45).
  - "Keep a man like a husband" echoes the Florentine phrase "keep a boy like a woman" (ch. 7, n. 44).
  - "Giottoni", Leo's word for the cross-dressers, was Italian slang for sodomites (ch. 7, n. 44).
  - **Design consequence:** the sexual charges in these passages are framed by Leo's Italian setting.

## 2. The Fez passages

### The hostelry men ("el cheva")

Davis reads CGA 147r-v (Ram 168-169; Ép 191) in ch. 4 (block quote, n. 29) and ch. 7 (n. 26). The account:
- **The name.** "That 'breed who call themselves el cheva' (perhaps we should read el chena)". Davis says Leo was transliterating "either alhiwa, an Andalusian word for 'sodomites,' or al-khanith, mukhannath, 'effeminate men'" (ch. 7).
- **Their life.** They live in their own hostelries. They "go about dressed as women and adorn their persons like women, they shave their beards and talk like women, and they spin" (ch. 4). Each "takes another man as a husband" (ch. 4).
- **Their exclusion.** They are barred from the mosques, scorned by scholars, merchants and "right-thinking artisans", and the populace wishes them dead (ch. 7).
- **Their civic roles.** They "served as cooks for the sultan's army and as lamenters at the death of the city folk in Fez, beating drums and singing verses in praise of the deceased" (ch. 7).
- **What Ramusio changed.**
  - He "adds more negative remarks to CGA" (n. 26).
  - He inserted a sentence on women in the hostelries, which weakens Leo's comparison of the men to European brothels (ch. 4, n. 29).
  - He replaced the argot "keeps a man like a husband" with the plainer statement that each keeps a concubine and uses him as a wife uses her husband (ch. 7, n. 73).

### The women diviners ("sahacat")

From CGA 174r-176r (Ram 192-193; Ép 216-218), in ch. 6 (n. 42) and ch. 7 (n. 24):
- **The practice.** Fez women claim ties with demons "of different colors—red, white, black". They summon them "by special perfumes", ventriloquise their voices, and answer clients' questions for a gift (ch. 6).
- **Leo's sexual charge.** Leo says they are *suhaqiyat* (*sahacat*), "tribades". "Assuming the voices of demons, they lured beautiful women into their lascivious company, singing, dancing, and having sex with them, enticing them to trick their husbands along the way" (ch. 7).
- **Ramusio's edit.** Ramusio **shortened** the diviners section (n. 42; DAR 39v).
- **The healing frame.** Davis calls them women "who ... foretold the future or served as healers" (ch. 7).

## 4. Design implications

- **The drum belongs to both groups.** The candidate gate ("drum night") was built on the women's possession society. Davis shows the drum in the **men's** hands too, at funerals.
  - This matches Rowson's *tubul al-makhanith*, the "*mukhannathun*'s drums" of tenth-century Baghdad (`isl_rowson_gender_irregularity.md`, p. 61).
  - **Proposal for a Fez faith, if built:** the Houses' civic duty is the **lament**. Here is a concrete mechanic: when a courtier or vassal of the ruler dies, the House can be asked to lament, which gives a stress loss to the bereaved and a county opinion bonus. A zealous Maliki lord forbids the lament and pays a small opinion cost with commoners.
  - Leo's men are not attested as women. The faith may claim them in its own voice ("the mourners of Fez were ours"), and the narrator must not.
- **A Maliki modifier: "Barred from the Mosque".** For daughters living under Sunni Maliki rulers in the Maghrib (CK3: Fes, Tlemcen, Ifriqiya), with a small piety and opinion malus. This follows Leo's mosque ban, which is milder than Ibn 'Abdūn's expulsion. Use expulsion for the Almoravid era and the mosque ban for the Marinid and Wattasid era.
- **Hostile reaction text, Maghrib:**
  - "She speaks in a borrowed voice, like the women of the perfumes." (Maliki faqih on a daughter's declaration; from Leo's ventriloquism charge, ch. 6)
  - "Let them cook for the army and wail for the dead, and keep out of the mosque." (Fez notable; from ch. 7)
- **Islamic neighbours (built).** Leo's Fez confirms the README line that Islamic courts make room for a daughter as a servant (army cook, mourner) while barring her from honour (the mosque).
  - The hostelry men "shave their beards" and dress as women; no veil. This supports correction #1 in `isl_islamicate_sexualities.md`: change "a man in a woman's veil" in `tm_isl.0002.desc`.
- **Pacing.** The best evidence is still c. 1500, after the game ends. Davis does not move any of it earlier. The assessment of the expansion as weak to moderate stands.
- **Other reaction data found in passing.**
  - In Cairo in 886/1481, al-Suyuti won the razing of a "house of dissipation" beside a mosque, against the objections of other judges (ch. 7, n. 26). This is a Mamluk scholar split usable as two opposed qadi voices.
  - Large harems with "black eunuch guards" were "a routine part of court life" in the Maghrib (ch. 7).
  - An Anti-Atlas chief sent the sultan of Fez "ten eunuchs" among his gifts (ch. 1).
  - The glossary defines *kahina* as "diviner, male and female". This supports the reading list's caution about using the Kahina.
- **Ha-Nehpakhot.** Nothing here bears on the built Jewish sect. Leo's Jewish collaborator in Italy (Jacob Mantino, the 1524 dictionary) is after 1453 and Italian. Leave it out.
