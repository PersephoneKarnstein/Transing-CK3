## 2. Eunuch dossier by source

### 2A. Tang (Hoeckelmann, 618–907; focus 779–903)

**Scale and structure of eunuch power.** From Dezong (779–805) "until the year 903, when all but thirty eunuchs within and outside of the twin capitals of Chang'an 長安 and Luoyang 洛陽 were slaughtered at the behest of the Military Commissioner (jiedushi 節度使) Zhu Quanzhong 朱全忠 (852–912). In the roughly 120 years in-between, eunuchs had taken control of the palace armies, the religious institutions of the capitals, as well as branches of the provincial armies and civil administration." (H p.2–3). "The mainstay of their power were the 'Four Worthies among Mighty Castrates' (quan yan si gui 權閹四貴): the two Protectors-in-Chief of the Left and Right Army of Divine Strategies (zuoyou shencejun hujun zhongwei 左右神策軍護軍中尉), the most formidable among the Northern Armies (beiya 北衙 or beijun 北軍) in the late Tang, and the two Commissioners for State Secrets (shumi [yuan] shi 樞密[院]使), who acted as intermediaries between the inner and outer court." (H p.3). Military Surveillance Commissioners (jianjunshi 監軍使) in the provinces "later on often allowed their incumbents to forge close ties with the provincial elite. One way of forming alliances was by intermarrying with and adopting from military families, both practices widely spread among eunuchs of the late Tang." (H p.3). Earlier, under Xuanzong (713–756) "the number of ranking eunuchs rose to the thousands" (H p.2).

**Eunuch marriage.** "It is well known that eunuchs in imperial China could marry and adopt children, practices attested in nearly all tomb epitaphs and stele inscriptions for eunuchs. That these family ties were not fiction is evidenced by the existence of epitaphs for eunuch wives and a number of joint burials (hezang 合葬 or fuzang 祔葬) of eunuchs with their wives." (H p.3). "At the same time, eunuchs also married the adopted daughters of other eunuchs on a regular basis." (H p.3) — i.e. an endogamous eunuch marriage market. Criticism "of eunuch marriages and adoptions as a source of political instability" arose only under the Northern Song (H p.3; Wu Ji 吳及, "Lun huanguan yangzi" 論宦官養子, n11). Earliest marriage evidence: Liu Yu's 劉瑜 memorial of AD 168: "又常侍、黃門，亦廣妻娶" ("Even the (Eunuch) Attendants and Gatekeepers now widely take wives", H p.7). Eunuch wives: Gao Lishi's Lady Lü 呂氏, daughter of a commoner clerk whose career Gao then advanced (H p.14); Liu Honggui's "State Lady of Mi of the Li clan from Longxi" (密國夫人隴西李氏), possibly of the imperial clan (H p.19); Yang Ting 楊珽 (777–830), courtesy name Qionghua 瓊華, granddaughter of a eunuch, daughter of a eunuch, wife of a eunuch Shence Protector-in-Chief, mother of two eunuchs (H p.22). "The citation of a courtesy and/or personal name for a eunuch wife, even in her own epitaph, is very unusual" (H p.22 n105).

**Eunuch adoption and inheritance of office/property.** Legal history: "The first legalization or official recognition of eunuch adoptions dates to the Eastern or Latter Han 東/後漢 (AD 25–220), when Emperor Shun 順帝 (r. 125–144) allowed them to adopt heirs to hand down their titles and estates in AD 135." (H p.6). Liu Yu, AD 168: "今中官邪孼，比肩裂土，皆競立胤嗣，繼體傳爵，或乞子疎屬，或買兒市道，殆乖開國承家之義" — "The evil of palace eunuchs today: shoulder to shoulder do they divvy up the land, all competing to install their heirs, inheriting their positions and bequeathing their titles, some begging for sons from distant relatives, some buying boys on the markets and in the streets, they are on the verge of perverting the meaning of 'establishing a fief and prolonging a family.'" (H p.7). The Tang Code: "即養異姓男者，徒一年；與者，笞五十。其遺棄小兒年三歲以下，雖異姓，聽收養，即從其姓。" — "If it is the case of fostering a boy with a different family name, the punishment is one year of penal servitude; the child-giver receives fifty beatings with the light bamboo stick. If it is an abandoned infant of three years or below, although he is of a different family name, it is permitted to take him in and foster him if he assumes their (the foster family's) family name." (H p.7). Dezong's edict of 791 (first Tang mention of eunuch adoption): "内侍五品以上，許養一子，仍以同姓者，初養日不得過十歲。" — "Inner Attendants (eunuchs) of the fifth rank and above are permitted to foster (or adopt) one son, for which they, as before, take those of the same family name and who, on the first day of fosterage, ought not to have exceeded ten years of age." (H p.7–8). "Judging by the evidence, those rules were widely ignored, as the biographies, epitaphs and stele inscriptions of eunuchs indicate time and again that they adopted or fostered more than one son and/or daughter. The meaning of yang was also highly ambiguous and ranged from 'adopting as an heir' to 'fosterage' and 'patronage.'" (H p.8). "Note that there is no mention of any limitations for the adoption of daughters." (H p.8 n35). Adoption as institutional reproduction: "adoptions into eunuch households (and subsequent or preceding emasculation) appears to have been a way of reproduction for the whole eunuch institution, both in the sense of replenishing the ranks at court and of inheriting social and even, in some cases, political status." (H p.3). "In the case of eunuch clans it is appropriate to speak of political dynasties, as they controlled the emperor and court and, other than career officials, were able to perpetuate the power of their clans across generations via adoptions" (H p.10 n47).

**Why eunuchs wanted families — the gendering of Tang eunuchs.** "Why would eunuchs want to have families and even adopt normative family values, given that they were unable to consummate a marriage? … the social stigma and religious anxiety associated with childlessness were so severe that they were tantamount to the social death of slaves in other periods of Chinese history. Having no male offspring meant the severance of the ancestral line so no one could perform ancestral rites for oneself after death. It testifies to the strength and stability of male gender identity in medieval China that even eunuchs abided by its requirements." (H p.8). "epitaphs for eunuchs do not disclose, at least not in unequivocal terms, that their dedicatees were anything but normal male members of the bureaucratic elite. Only their offices betray their identity, because all eunuchs had to hold posts in the Palace Domestic Service (neishisheng 內侍省) under the Tang" (H p.8). Epitaph writers "emphasize their subjects' masculine qualities by the use of expressions such as 'not falling short in the civil or the martial' (wen wu bu zhui 文武不墜), 'having civil and martial qualities' (you wen you wu 有文有武), 'perfecting both the civil and the martial' (wen wu jie quan 文武皆全), or 'fully embodying the civil and the martial' (wen wu bei ti 文武備體)" (H p.8–9). High eunuchs "moved out of the palace walls and into representative mansions in the residential wards of Chang'an … becoming (and presenting themselves as) heads of their own households … That shows that eunuchs wanted to appear to posterity and to their contemporaries as gender-normative men who complied with their filial duty to produce male offspring and continue the ancestral line." (H p.9). Conclusion: "The authors of commemorative writings went to great lengths to construct a gender-normative male identity for their eunuch subjects." (H p.26). Only one hint of a feminine framing: Gao Lishi's stele says of Empress Wu "則天……選內官而母之", "which may indicate that the empress acted as a kind of foster or godmother for Lishi" (H p.14 n66). Late-imperial eunuchs "referred to themselves as 'slaves' (nubi 奴婢)" (H p.8 n38). Terminology of the operation: "We know from late imperial times that both the penis and the testicles were removed in China, so the term 'emasculation' seems more appropriate than 'castration'" (H p.3 n12). "Given the high mortality rate of full-scale emasculation, they also may have adopted boys from non-official families, which in any case involved a change of surname." (H p.27).

**Penal emasculation (宮刑) and its marginality.** "Punitive emasculation, known under the name gongxing 宮刑 and others, never played a major role in providing eunuchs for the palaces of imperial China. Those put under the knife under the Shang 商 Dynasty … were mostly prisoners taken during raids against the people generically called the Qiang 羌, and their emasculation may have served ritual purposes. Moreover, emasculation as a punishment and the institution of eunuchs are not perforce related: convicted criminals may have provided palace eunuchs for the early imperial courts but by Tang times, emasculation as a regular punishment had all but disappeared." (H p.4–5). "It was only used in exceptional circumstances" (H p.5 n20). The one in-period legal channel: "The legal practice of emasculating boys below the age of fourteen, whose fathers had been found guilty of 'great sedition and impiety' (dani budao 大逆不道), originated in the late fifth century with the Northern Wei 北魏 (386–534) Dynasty. Although the abolishment of the practice dates to 547 under the Western Wei 西魏 (535–556) and was reaffirmed under the Northern Qi 北齊 (550–577), and emasculation was no longer part of the legal codes of the Sui 隋 (581–618) and Tang, Yuanyi's case seems to confirm that emasculated boys were still sent as 'tribute' from the south to serve in the imperial palace during the Tang, although probably not on a large scale." (H p.13–14).

**"Voluntary" emasculation, boys from the south, captives.** "Most eunuchs throughout Chinese history entered the palace through so-called voluntary emasculation (zigong 自宮), which in practice meant that their families chose them for that kind of palace service." (H p.5). Biographies "mention for example that he entered the palace as an 'emasculated boy' (yan'er 閹兒) or that he was 'privately emasculated' (sibai 私白)." (H p.5). No Roman-style rule that eunuchs be foreigners: "there also was no preference for non-Chinese boys to become eunuchs comparable to the injunction in later Roman law that eunuchs had to come from outside the Roman Empire" (H p.5). The Fujian/Lingnan trade, from Yuquanzi 玉泉子 (after 890): "諸道每歲送閹人〔，〕所謂私白者，閩為首焉，且多任用，以故大閹已下，桑梓多係閩焉。時以為中官藪澤。" — "Each year, the regions presented eunuchs, so-called 'privately emasculated' ones, and the Min region was foremost among these; furthermore, many were appointed, which is why, from the high eunuchs downward, many had native ties to Min. At the time, it was considered the prime territory of the eunuchs." (H p.5–6). Du Xuanyou, who owed his post to eunuch patrons, sent officials each Cold Food Festival "to sacrifice at the burial mounds of the eunuchs … Thus, contemporaries nicknamed him 'Imperial Commissioner for Looking after Tombs' [勅使看墓]" (H p.6). Hoeckelmann's correction: "evidence such as this for a southern 'slave trade' in eunuchs remains anecdotal and … most high-ranking eunuchs in the second half of the Tang came from the north." (H p.6). Captive route — Gao Lishi: born Feng Yuanyi 馮元一 in Panzhou (Lingnan), of the Northern Yan royal line; after a punitive expedition against his family's hereditary southern governorship, the Commissioner "Li Qianli 李千里, presented Yuanyi and another child named Jingang 金剛 as emasculated boys (yan'er) to the court … Junheng and the adult male members of his family were most likely executed, while Yuanyi and at least one other boy were emasculated." (H p.13). Yang Fuguang's "original surname was Qiao 喬 and he came from Fujian (Min)" (H p.23–24).

**Eunuch "great clans" and adoptive lineages.** Dezong "increased the number of eunuchs from 'reputable' (i.e., officeholding) families (liang zhou ru shi 良胄入侍)" to improve the institution after Gao Lishi, Li Fuguo, Yu Chao'en (H p.10). "Given the shortage of attractive positions … it seems highly probable that an elite family could have chosen one son or at least have adopted an orphan to send him into the palace as a eunuch. Indeed, many of the epitaphs of first-generation eunuchs indicate that they were the younger sons and hence not the principal heir of an official." (H p.10). Choronyms (junwang 郡望) claimed: Pengcheng Liu 彭城劉, Longxi Li 隴西李, Hongnong Yang 弘農楊, Fufeng 扶風 (Ma Cunliang), Anding 安定 (Liang Kuangren) (H p.9, 17–19, 22–23). "since eunuchs from the second generation onward were certainly adopted, but inscriptions list their ancestors through (presumably) adoption as if they were biological kin, their pedigree … is difficult to establish." (H p.9).
- *Gao lineage*: Feng Yuanyi adopted under Wu Zetian by the eunuch Gao Yanfu 高延福, "whose surname he assumed"; epitaph: "帝曰俞以汝為內侍高延福男" (H p.14 & n66). Gao Lishi's sons: "one, Chengyue 承悅, is designated as 'nephew' (youzi 猶子, literally 'like a son'), the other, Chengxin 承信, as 'adoptee' (yangzi 養子)." (H p.14). Fifth/sixth-generation descendant the eunuch Gao Kecong 高克從 (784–846): "開元中，開府儀同三司內侍監齊國公知省事諱力士者，即公之五代祖也。" (H p.15); Gao Kefang 高可方 (809–850), palace-guard officer, "a son of Kecong, but the grandson of Lishi" (H p.15) — contradictions typical of adoptive genealogies.
- *Pengcheng Liu lineage*: Liu Honggui 劉弘規 (775–827), Shence general; great-grandfather a county magistrate, grandfather a garrison commander, father a guards colonel in the defunct militia system — "which may explain why he decided to send one of his sons as eunuch into the palace" (H p.18); "the epitaph states that Honggui was admitted at the age of 15, i.e., around 791 … sending a boy to serve as palace eunuch was a way of preserving status." (H p.19). Five sons, "at least four were eunuchs" (H p.19); grandsons Liu Zhongli 劉中禮 (805–872) and Liu Zunli 劉遵禮 (815–867), both eunuchs; "Zhongli had five sons, of which at least four were members of the Palace Domestic Service, and Zunli four, of which at least two were eunuchs." (H p.20). Grand Counselor Liu Zhan 劉瞻, himself a Pengcheng Liu, wrote Zunli's epitaph — "other members of the elite were willing to accept claims to great-clan descent made by eunuchs" (H p.20).
- *Hongnong Yang lineage*: Yang Zhilian 楊志廉 (744–807) "must have been adopted, because his father was the Eunuch Palace Attendant (neichangshi 内常侍) Yang Yanzuo 楊延祚" (H p.21); seven sons, two or three eunuchs; son Yang Qinyi 楊欽義 Commissioner for State Secrets c. 840; grandsons Xuanyi 玄翼, Xuanjia 玄价, Xuanshi 玄寔; great-grandsons Yang Fugong 楊復恭 (d. 894) and Yang Fuguang 楊復光 (842–883). JTS: "復恭，即玄翼子也。以父，幼為宦者，入內侍省。" — "Fugong thus was Xuanyi's son. Because of his father, he became a eunuch at an early age and entered the Palace Domestic Service." (H p.23). "It is unclear whether that means that he was emasculated at the behest of his adoptive father, that his biological father sent him to undergo the procedure, or even that his father was emasculated together with his son." (H p.23 n114). Fugong's "original surname was Lin 林" (H p.24).

**Adult adoption as patronage: the "600 sons".** Fuguang's biography: "諸假子：守亮，興元節度使；守宗，忠武節度使；守信，商州防禦使；守忠，洋州節度使；其餘以守為名者數十人，皆為牧守將帥。" — "His adopted sons were: Shouliang, Military Commissioner of Xingyuan; Shouzong, Military Commissioner of Zhongwu; Shouxin, Defense Commissioner of Shangzhou; Shouzhong, Military Commissioner of Yangzhou; and several dozen more taking a name with the character 'Shou' in it, who were all prefects or military officers." (H p.25). "Fuguang most likely had not adopted these imperial commissioners as 'foster sons' (jiazi 假子) when they were children, but taken them in when they were already adults. That kind of adoption or fosterage was wide-spread, especially among the military elite in North China, as a way of forming patron-client relations." (H p.25). Fugong: "復恭以諸子為州刺史，號「外宅郎君」；又養子六百人，監諸道軍。" — "Fugong made Prefects of his sons, calling them (his) 'princelings outside the city'; he further had six hundred adopted sons who surveilled the armies of the regions." (H p.25). Tian Lingzi 田令孜 "bestowed commissions to the palace guards, adopting all of them as his sons" (令孜以復光故，纔授諸衞將軍，皆養為子, H p.24 n120). "the medieval Chinese terms for adoption or fosterage (yangzi or jiazi) were broad in meaning and usage, encompassing not only adoption as a legal term … but also patron-client relationships … It was that tolerance for ambiguity … that facilitated strategies of eunuchs to reproduce their social and political status over long periods of time" (H p.25–26).

**Eunuchs and religion.** Eunuchs held the Commissionerships for the Cultivation of Religious Merit (gongdeshi 功德使) of the Left/Right Streets of Chang'an, controlling "the religious institutions of the capitals" (H p.2–3; Liu Honggui "Commissioner for the Cultivation of Religious Merit of the Left Side of the Capital (zuojie gongde shi 左街功德使)", H p.16; Yang Ting's husband likewise, H p.22; Gao Lishi posthumously credited with the title, H p.15 n76). Eunuch burial in native Fujian and tomb cult (H p.6). Gao Lishi ordered to strangle Yang Guifei "in front of a Buddha Hall under a pear tree" (命高力士縊貴妃於佛堂前梨樹下, H p.12).

**The hostile narrative.** "perceptions of eunuchs are still marred by moral prejudices, which often stretch back to the Five Dynasties (Wudai 五代, 907–960) and Northern Song, when historians construed the narrative of the evil eunuchs who, together with factions and military commissioners, and pitted against the upright few, brought about the collapse of the dynasty with their micromanagement (and even murder) of emperors … led to an image of Tang eunuchs as nothing but a malicious group of scheming cronies." (H p.10–11). "Most historians ignore the claims of eunuchs to great-clan descent, while some reject them on moralistic grounds, because they believe that elite families would have found it shameful to be related to eunuchs." (H p.9). Li Bai "ordered Gao Lishi to pull off his boots in the imperial presence" (後對御令高力士脫靴, H p.12) — the poet humiliating the eunuch.

### 2B. Ming (Tsai, 1368–1644) — **LATE; in-game window only 1368–1453**

**Numbers.** "at the end of the fifteenth century, there were approximately ten thousand eunuchs in the Forbidden City and in various Ming princely establishments. At the time of the establishment in 1644 of the Qing dynasty it was estimated that the total number of eunuchs in China had reached a hundred thousand in a total population of about 130 million people." (T p.121). "of the estimated one million eunuchs employed by the Ming emperors throughout their 276 years of rule, only a handful actually rose to powerful positions." (T p.125). Growth "from a mere hundred at the beginning of the dynasty to approximately 10,000 in the 1520s, and eventually reaching the astonishing figures of 100,000" while population "increased only twofold" — "the number of castrated men had jumped by a staggering thousandfold" (T p.144). Hongwu "decided to limit the number of court eunuchs to fewer than 100. Even though he was later to increase the number of palace servants to more than four hundred, he reportedly also decreed that no eunuch be permitted to learn books or to give advice on political matters." (T p.123). 1384 iron tablet: "Eunuchs are forbidden to interfere with government affairs. Those who attempt to do so will be subjected to capital punishment." (T p.123). Eunuchs' "rank should not exceed the fourth grade. Officials of all departments were forbidden to communicate with eunuchs by written documents." (T p.124). Yongle (1402–24) "had to rely on the help of the eunuchs because the court ministers had remained loyal to his nephew" and rewarded them "even putting some of them in charge of military affairs" (T p.124).

**Training school.** "Emperor Xuande, Yongle's grandson, established a classroom known as Neishufang, or Inner Court Study, and appointed four scholars from the Hanlin Academy to teach some two to three hundred young eunuchs whose average age was about ten. Consequently, some of these educated eunuchs were to become secretaries of the emperor" (T p.124). [Xuande r. 1425–35 — **in-period**.]

**Duties of the rank and file** (T p.125): procurement of metals and timber, building, "burn incense, light the altar candles, and offer food and sacrifice to the emperor's ancestors", kitchens, animals "horses, cows, elephants and giraffes", seals and archives, robes, gates, firewood, fire-fighting, drums, acting and puppet shows, firearms, wine, flour, garments, gardening, warehouses, "even as dog and cat sitters". "their hard labor and long subservience provided the emperor and his relatives with luxury, beauty, safety, and political intelligence." (T p.125).

**Foreign supply — captives.** 1368: Mongol princes captured "Together with their eunuchs" (T p.127). 1371: Mai-di-li-ba-la, grandson of the last Yuan emperor, detained in Nanjing "Accompanied by hundreds of Mongol officials, women, and eunuchs" (T p.127). 1381: Lan Yu and Fu Youde in Yunnan "took 380 Mongol and Muslim prisoners of war. Among them were many young castrated boys, including possibly the future great Ming maritime explorer Zheng He, who was then eleven years old." (T p.127). "the struggle between the Chinese and the Mongols continued for the next two centuries while hundreds of prisoners of war were castrated and used as hostages, diplomats, or spies." (T p.127). Eunuch-ambassadors to the Mongols 1375 (one Chinese, one Mongol) and 1379 (T p.127). 1460: "a Miao tribe in southwest China capitulated and the victorious Ming commander ordered the castration of 1,565 Miao boys. Of these unfortunate souls, 329 died during the brutal operation. News of the tragic proportion and unusual number of casualties reached Beijing and Emperor Tianshun (1457–1464) was reported to have upbraided his governor in Guizhou province for arbitrarily undertaking such a large scale operation." (T p.130) — a 21% operative mortality. 1453: Guangxi regional commander "was faced with a surplus of castrated persons. Consequently, the emperor ordered him to distribute the young castrati among the royal family members and the meritorious government officials in the region." (T p.131). "The best way to preserve [the imperial line] and to keep court secrets was to use foreign-born eunuchs such as Shin from Korea and Wang Zhi, a captured Yao tribesman." (T p.130).

**Foreign supply — tribute.** "At a time when castration was performed as a symbol of conquest and a method of revenge, rulers throughout East Asia, with the exception of those in Japan, also adopted eunuchism and often used castrati as tributes. Likewise, in addition to luxury products from their native lands, most tributary missions also included young handsome castrated boys and virgin girls." (T p.128). "most of the servants from Korea, Vietnam, and Ryukyu Islands were meant to be used as imperial eunuchs. In 1408 when the king of Borneo visited the Ming court, he was ushered in by a gaggle of eunuchs from China's various ethnic tribes as well as castrated men from Mongolia, Korea, Vietnam, Cambodia, Central Asia, Siam, and Okinawa." (T p.128). Vietnam: "In 1383, Tran Wei, the king of Vietnam, sent a mission to the Ming court, and among its tributes to Emperor Hongwu were twenty-five beardless castrated boys, most of whom bore the last name of Nguyen. Similar missions were undertaken every two years as Tran presented thirty eunuchs to the emperor in 1384 and again nineteen castrati in 1385." (T p.128–129). War 1406–1427: "Hundreds of thousands of prisoners of war were either executed or castrated." (T p.129); 1427 the Chinese killed "Le Loi's chief eunuch named Le Mi" (T p.129); 1469 Vietnamese captors of Southeast Asian envoys: "Older members of the mission were all killed while younger members were castrated and sold into slavery" (T p.129). "Castration as a means of punishing prisoners of war became a legacy of Sino-Vietnamese relations." (T p.129). Korea: missions "once every three years, but they gradually increased in frequency, often as much as three times a year. No one knows how many Korean castrati and virgin girls were included in these missions, but Ming documents suggest that, after working for a certain period of time, Korean eunuchs were allowed to return home with honor and pensions. A few of them gained favor with their Chinese masters and became confidants of Ming emperors. One such person was Shin Gueisheng who was to become Emperor Hongwu's 'ears and eyes.' Other Korean-born eunuchs were entrusted with such important assignments as managing fiscal and monetary affairs for the emperor's inner court. Between 1370 and 1634, the Ming government sent a total of twenty-eight eunuch-led missions to Seoul, primarily for the purpose of installing new Korean kings. More than half of the eunuch-ambassadors were Korean-born." (T p.129–130). "Korean eunuchs enjoyed more power than any other foreign group in the Ming court." (T p.128).

**Domestic supply — quotas and private castration.** "local officials were often required to offer a certain number of castrated men to the imperial palace. In 1449, a regional commander presented 108 men, who had already been castrated, to Emperor Chengtong. In 1451, a Fujian proctor presented fifty-nine castrati to the Directorate of Ceremonial, and a year later, the commander of Nanjing sent to the capital four emasculated persons. Many officials, however, performed castration without imperial authorization and kept such castrated men for their own use." (T p.131). "throughout most of the Ming period, castration remained a common practice in the southeastern provinces of Fujian and Guangdong. Some landlords required their tenants to cut off their penes and scrotums before taking them as household servants. In this way, the landlord could make sure that chastity and purity would prevail among his ladies and concubines … In 1372, upon hearing of such a practice in Fujian province, Emperor Hongwu threatened to castrate those who castrated their servants." (T p.133). Civil-war recruitment: "Zhu Di trusted a self-seeking opportunist named Ji Gang and made him a battalion commander. Ji castrated several hundred people and trained them as new recruits to bolster Zhu Di's strength. When Zhu Di assumed the throne in 1402 he promoted Ji to be the commander of the imperial bodyguards. Two years later, one of Zhu Di's officials was accused of kidnapping and castrating young boys in Beijing, but the emperor … decided not to punish the alleged culprit." (T p.132). Demand from the princely establishments: princes "were given territorial titles, manors, and retainers and servants ranging from 3,000 to 19,000 persons" (T p.133); imperial clansmen "had grown to an estimated 100 000 and demand for castrated servants appeared to be insatiable." (T p.134). Yongle's gifts to loyal princes included "eunuchs who were professionally trained to work as 'ears and eyes' of the emperor in the provinces" (T p.136); 1425 wedding gift "twenty-five palace women and twenty eunuchs" (T p.137). "while ordinary slaves could be bought and sold, castrated eunuchs were an imperial monopoly." (T p.146).

**Penal castration and its abolition.** "castration was frequently used as a substitute for the death penalty" in antiquity (T p.122). "Near the end of the Hongwu reign, however, the emperor abolished the tattooing, severing of body organs, and castration as forms of criminal justice. As substitute for such baneful punishment, he introduced corporal punishment and various forms of flogging." (T p.131). "A similar proclamation was issued in 1425 by the fourth emperor Hongxi, in which Confucian filial piety was cited as a reason for prohibiting the pernicious castration punishment. According to this rationale, a son who could not produce a male heir to carry on the family name committed a serious transgression against Confucian doctrine. But in spite of repeated imperial proclamations, Ming monarchs in the later period often disregarded such prohibitory decrees and resorted to castration for punishing major political criminals and their young male relatives." (T p.131–132). "A case in point was the story of Huan En whose cousin was a Vice-Minister of War but was executed by Emperor Xuande on a charge of treason. Young Huan En was castrated for a crime he knew nothing about." (T p.132 n17). "In 1566, for instance, a criminal justice official, by the name of Mao Xiang, castrated three suspects before he could prove their culpability. Upon hearing the news, the emperor reportedly went into a rage and ordered Mao Xiang into exile." (T p.132). "castration remained a lethal weapon readily available for the Ming autocrats when and if they or their surrogates chose to use it against their enemies." (T p.132).

**Self-castration: the ban and its evasion.** "Destitute men castrated themselves or their children so that they could find employment in the princely establishments … For a commoner, unable to climb into the gentry class either by passing the difficult civil service examinations or by purchasing a degree, the only means of gaining access to privileges and influence was by means of eunuchism. Consequently, voluntary castration became epidemic." (T p.134). "In the previous dynasties self-castration was permitted if closely monitored by the officials who recorded the day of emasculation, examined the wound, and checked every detail during the healing process. However, since the establishment of the Ming dynasty, self-castration had been forbidden" (T p.134–135). 1424: "a report from the city of Changsha that a man had cut off his own genital organ and offered himself to serve in the palace. The emperor considered this person a lazy vagabond who had failed to observe the Confucian principle of filial piety, and had him banished to the frontier as a soldier. One month later, an elderly guard from a princely manor cut off his son's genitals before asking for a eunuch position for his son. Emperor Hongxi rebuked the father and made his son a soldier." (T p.135). Xuande "ordered the Minister of Rites to send all such men to the border of Vietnam" and "warned the village heads not to conceal any information of wrongdoings or acts that were clearly in violation of Confucian filial piety." (T p.135). "In 1436, a Buddhist monk performed his own emasculation. The new emperor Chengtong not only annulled the monk's corvée exemption but also exiled him to southern Manchuria." (T p.135). Xuande yr 1: "nine civilian and military personnel in Shanxi province practiced self-castration and then presented themselves to the princely establishment of Jin … the emperor … ordered that all nine persons be punished according to the penal code. The senior administrator of the princely establishment of Jin … was arrested and tried in the capital." (T p.136). 1449: "a total of thirty-four requests by the princes and princesses for the supply of eunuchs. Of the thirty-four requests, Emperor Chengtong granted fourteen, denied one, but placed the other nineteen requests in limbo … the emperor had no more eunuchs to spare. Consequently, he chose to condone a new practice by allowing his relatives to purchase self-castrated men from private sources." (T p.137). Jingtai (1449–57): "One measure was to exile the entire family of a self-castrated man to a frontier military colony if such a person was a soldier. If, on the other hand, the self-castrated man was a civilian, he was to be charged with a violation of filial piety and his village heads and neighbors would also be punished if they concealed knowledge of his act." (T p.137). Then "whenever he discovered illegal eunuchs who had practiced self-emasculation, he literally confiscated them, took them to the capital and put them under the supervision of the Directorate of Ceremonial. In other words, the emperor was forced to stockpile his own 'inventory' of eunuchs" (T p.138). Post-1453 (out of game range but pattern-defining): 1474–75 fifty-four petitioners "arrested and paraded in public" (T p.138); 1476 seventy-six exiled; 1477 "900 such persons beaten with whipping clubs, thirty strokes each" (T p.138–139); 1479 "some two thousand self-castrated men once again invaded the Ministry of Rites … each of them receive a hundred strokes and wear a particularly cumbersome cangue for a month" (T p.139); "many of these people later changed names, adopted new identities and repeatedly tried their luck; a few of them ultimately were selected to work in the palaces and rose from rags to riches" (T p.139). 1484: "a scoundrel by the name of Li An operated on four teenage boys at the request of their fathers … whipped thirty strokes before sending him to a Manchurian military colony. The fathers of the four young boys were ordered to work in an iron foundry for three years while the castrated boys were all confiscated by the central government." (T p.139). Hongzhi (1487–1505) "actually put to death a few of the self-castrated men", 1493 "some 300 self-castrated men from Ansu county, Hebei province, hit the so-called 'attention drum' of the Ministry of Rites" (T p.140); "he even had the mother of a self-castrated boy brought to justice" (T p.141); "he periodically confiscated properties of deceased eunuchs and gave them to the victims of drought, locust, earthquake, or flooding" (T p.140). 1516: "the central government had to accept 3,468 such persons, each of whom received three piculs (about 400 pounds) of rice monthly" (T p.141–142); 1517 "more than 5,030" (T p.142); Jiajing found "many of these poverty-stricken, miserable people actually practiced cannibalism. Reluctantly, between 1536 and 1566, he took in thousands of new eunuchs to fill in sinecure offices" (T p.142). 1601 Wanli "picked 4,500 castrated men off the streets of Beijing" (T p.143). 1620 "some 20,000 such people swarmed into the capital … turned into a rowdy and militant mob … Many of these people, after failing to gain employment and having been driven back to their villages, took their own lives because they felt that they had 'lost face' with their relatives." (T p.143). "one may wonder if the Ming government was truly a government of men or merely a government of half-men." (T p.143).

**"Eunuch family" and adoption.** Tsai has little on eunuch adoption as such; the relevant note is the slave market's euphemism: "When wealthy families purchased slaves, they put in the bond such words of euphemism as 'adopted sons' or 'adopted daughters,' or simply called them employed servants." (T p.146) — and the Hongzhi confiscation of "properties of deceased eunuchs" (T p.140), implying eunuch estates with no state-recognised heir. Korean eunuchs "allowed to return home with honor and pensions" (T p.130). No Ming eunuch-adoption law is cited; **for eunuch adoption use Source A** (and the Song critique it cites).

**Scholar-official hostility (verbatim rhetoric for hostile text).** "Chinese bureaucrat-scholars, who constantly locked into an adversary position against the eunuchs, had a tendency to portray their archrivals as rapacious, wicked, sly, and duplicitous. They attributed all the dynastic evils to the despised and hateful eunuchs" (T p.121). "Pei was to become a stereotype eunuch — a person of sycophancy, ruthlessness, treachery, greed and luxury." (T p.122). *Book of Odes* as Tsai renders it: "Not heaven but women and eunuchs / Bring misfortunes to mankind. / Wives and those without balls / Bleat with similar voices." (T p.122) — the classical Chinese pairing of women and eunuchs as one category of evil. Confucius "so irked by the evil example of a prominent eunuch named Yun Chu of the state of Wei that, after staying only for a month, he decided not to serve the Duke of Wei." (T p.122). "Although Chinese society generally viewed these people as anathema, their status had by the beginning of the Christian era been firmly established." (T p.123). Mingshi: eunuchs' rise brought "one profit but hundreds of damages" (T p.125). "Of the eighty-three various Chinese dynasties … more than two-thirds were founded by ruffians, religious charlatans, warlords, monks and nuns, thieves, and people from lowly strata, but none by the eunuchs." (T p.126). Eastern Han: "they put to death more than two thousand eunuchs as means of retribution" (T p.123); Tang 903: "Zhu Chuanzhong arrived in Xian and executed 162 court eunuchs and their collaborators. He later arrested and killed 300 more and ordered every eunuch who had been involved in military affairs to commit suicide." (T p.123).

### 2C. Goryeo (Kim, 918 – c. 1100)

**The central finding: naesi 內侍 were NOT eunuchs.** "잘 알려진 사실이지만 高麗의 內侍는 中國의 唐⋅宋이나 朝鮮의 그것과는 實體가 전혀 달랐다 後者가 宦官內侍임에 반해 前者는 주로 文武官 出身의 朝官內侍였다" — "As is well known, the *naesi* of Goryeo were entirely different in substance from those of Tang and Song China or of Joseon. Whereas the latter were eunuch-naesi (宦官內侍), the former were chiefly court-official naesi (朝官內侍) drawn from the civil and military officialdom." (K p.151). Their work: "이들이 宮中의 雜事보다는 奉命使臣⋅奏事⋅使行 및 執禮와 같이 비중 있는 업무에 종사할 뿐 아니라 개별 內侍마다 職銜에 따르는 고유한 직무까지 수행했던 것" — "they engaged not in palace menial chores but in weighty tasks such as carrying royal commands as envoys, presenting memorials, embassies, and conducting rites, and each naesi additionally performed the specific duties attached to his own office title" (K p.151–152). **Who did the menial palace work:** "高麗에서 宮中의 雜事는 內侍가 아니라 이른바 宦官給事로 불리는 부류의 몫이었다" — "In Goryeo the menial affairs of the palace were the lot not of the naesi but of the category called *hwan-gwan geupsa* (宦官給事, 'eunuch attendants')." (K p.152). "이처럼 文武官 出身의 內侍는 高麗 때만 있었던 아주 특징적인 존재였다" — "Naesi of civil/military-official origin were thus a highly distinctive phenomenon that existed only in the Goryeo period." (K p.152).

**On the 'preventing eunuch evils' explanation.** "짤막하게 宦官 弊害의 防止 를 거론한 견해가 있기는 하지만 論證을 缺如하고 있을 뿐 아니라 宦官內侍를 두었던 朝鮮時代에도 별다른 宦官의 弊害는 없었다는 점에서 타당성을 인정하기 어렵다" — "There is a view that briefly cites 'the prevention of the evils of eunuchs', but it lacks demonstration, and since even in the Joseon period, which did have eunuch-naesi, there were no particular eunuch evils, its validity is hard to accept." (K p.152). [Kim thus rejects the idea that Goryeo deliberately kept eunuchs out of the inner service to avoid Tang-style abuses; his explanation is institutional — the dual 內廷/朝廷 structure inherited from Silla.]

**Early-Goryeo naesi as household (內廷) officials.** Taejo year 1: "守義刑臺卿인 能律을 廣評侍郞으로 삼고 廣評侍郞인 職預를 內侍書記로 삼았다" — "Neungnyul, acting Minister of the Board of Justice, was made Vice-Minister of the Gwangpyeong-seong, and Jikye, Vice-Minister of the Gwangpyeong-seong, was made *Naesi-seogi* (內侍書記, Secretary of the Inner Attendants)." (K p.153). Seongjong [year digits lost]: "景宗이 죽은 후 王妃가 자기 집에 나와 거처하였는데 郁이 드디어 情을 통하여 王妃가 임신하게 되었다 일이 발각되자 成宗은 郁을 泗水縣으로 流配시키며…內侍謁者 高玄에게 명하여 押送케 하였다" — "After Gyeongjong died the queen went out and lived in her own house; Uk at last had relations with her and the queen became pregnant. When the matter came to light, Seongjong exiled Uk to Sasu county … and ordered the *Naesi-alja* (內侍謁者, Inner Attendant Usher) Go Hyeon to escort him." (K p.153). The next year the same Go Hyeon, as 內謁者監, delivered Uk's son (the future Hyeonjong) to his father (K p.153 n). Both posts are held by ranking officials who transfer freely to and from the regular bureaucracy: Jikye "廣評侍郞에서는 해임되고 內侍書記를 새로 맡게 된 것" — "was dismissed from Vice-Minister and newly took up Naesi-seogi" (K p.162). Wi Su-yeo 韋壽餘: "光宗 때부터 司膳으로 있었으나 오랫동안 자리를 옮기지 못하더니 穆宗 때 여러 번 陞遷하여 門下侍郞平章事가 되었다" — "From Gwangjong's reign he was *Saseon* (司膳, Provisioner), unable to move post for a long time, but under Mokjong he was promoted several times and became Munha-sirang-pyeongjangsa [a chief-minister rank]" (K p.158); elsewhere he is called a "近臣" put in charge of "御廚의 일" (the royal kitchen) (K p.154). Choe Ji-mong 崔知夢: a dream-interpreter whom Taejo rewarded with "비단 옷과 供奉의 관직" ("silk robes and the office of *Gongbong* 供奉") and who "항상 征伐에 나설 때마다 隨從케 하여 左右에서 떠나지 않도록 하였으며 統合 이후에는 禁中에서 왕을 모시며 顧問에 대비토록 하였다" — "was made to accompany him on every campaign, never leaving his side, and after the unification served the king within the palace as an adviser" (K p.158–159); as 司天供奉 he warned Hyejong of Wang Gyu's plot (K p.159). A physician-attendant: "大王 光宗 이 尙醫供奉侍郞 直文에게 명하여 특별히 仙藥을 가지고 가서 朝夕으로 보살피게 하였다" — "The Great King (Gwangjong) ordered Jikmun, *Sang-ui Gongbong Sirang* (尙醫供奉侍郞, Attendant Physician), specially to take immortal medicine and tend [the master] morning and evening" (K p.159). Kim's synthesis: "麗初의 內侍는 결국 그 공통분모라 할 內廷官署의 官員에 대한 汎稱이었다" — "the early-Goryeo naesi was in the end a general term for the officials of the royal-household offices (內廷官署), their common denominator" (K p.162); "麗初의 內侍制는 內省을 정점으로 하는 內廷官制에 다름 아니며 後代의 內侍院과 같은 內侍의 事務機構 또한 따로 존재하지 않았다" — "the early-Goryeo naesi system was nothing other than the household officialdom headed by the *Naeseong* (內省), and no separate naesi bureau like the later *Naesi-won* existed" (K p.162). Household offices named in the 始定田柴科 land-grant regulation of Gyeongjong year 1 as "雜業": "殿中⋅司天⋅延壽⋅尙膳院 等" (Palace Service, Astronomy, Longevity [medicine], Royal Table) (K p.155), modelled on Silla's 內省 and its 供奉卜師 / 供奉醫師 / 肉典 (K p.156). Note Silla precedent: a Silla king sent a 中使 to supervise the funeral of the monk Nanggong (K p.162) — household officials as the king's agents to the Buddhist clergy.

**Status and recruitment of the naesi.** Full-time (專任職) household posts in the early period, becoming seconded posts (派遣職) from the regular ministries into a 內侍省/內侍院 after Mokjong (K p.162, 163, 171). Movement between household and court was unobstructed: "이들 內侍와 朝廷官署의 官員 즉 朝官 사이의 人事交流는 꽤 활발했다 … 교류에 별다른 장벽이나 차별은 없었던 것으로 보인다" — "personnel exchange between these naesi and the court officials was quite active … there appear to have been no particular barriers or discrimination" (K p.162); "內侍를 지낸 인물이 후일 門下侍中과 같은 朝廷의 高位職에까지 오를 수 있었던 것이다" — "a man who had served as naesi could later rise even to the highest court offices such as Munha-sijung [chancellor]" (K p.171). Under Munjong (1046–83) the memorial of Im Wan recalled that "文宗 때는 유능한 자만을 內侍로 가려 뽑아 [digits lost]여 명으로도 宮中의 일을 무난히 처리할 수 있었다" — "in Munjong's time only the capable were selected as naesi, and with some [number lost] men the affairs of the palace could be handled without difficulty" (K p.162–163); Im Gae "內侍로서 大倉의 일을 관장했다" ("as naesi managed the affairs of the Great Granary", K p.163); Munjong summoned Choe Sa-chu, "名家子弟이며 博學多聞한" ("a son of a famous house, widely learned"), to the 內侍省 to converse (K p.163) — i.e. naesi recruited from aristocratic (名家) sons, later the pool for the 門閥 aristocracy (see the title of Pak Han-nam's cited article 「高麗內侍와 門閥貴族의 形成關係」, K p.151 n). The 內侍省 "仁宗 [ ]년 이후로는 內侍院으로 개칭" — "renamed Naesi-won after Injong year [lost]" (K p.163). Contempt from the regular bureaucracy toward the king's household men already under Gwangjong: Seo Pil "朝廷官員인 式會를 두둔하고 內廷官署의 관원으로 이해되는 國王 側近의 近臣을 힐난한 사실" — "defended the court official Sikhoe and berated the king's close 'near servants' (近臣), understood to be household officials"; Kim reads the label 雜業 ("miscellaneous occupations") as reflecting "後者 集團에 대한 前者의 輕蔑的 認識" — "the contemptuous perception of the latter group by the former" (K p.157 n). Precedence quarrel at a court ceremony between a court official (廣評郞中 柳問律) and a household officer (直省官 朱瑄劼) in Taejo year 1 because "하나는 朝廷官職이고 다른 하나는 內廷官職이어서 兩者의 官職上의 高下라든가 先後가 분명치 않았기 때문" — "one was a court post and the other a household post, so their relative rank and precedence were unclear" (K p.160–161 n).

**Castrated hwan-gwan 宦官 in Goryeo — what the source does and does not say.** Kim establishes (i) that a castrated category existed and is called 宦官給事 in his usage, (ii) that it did palace "雜事", and (iii) that it was *not* the naesi. He gives **no date for the appearance of castrated hwan-gwan, no recruitment mechanism, no numbers, and no mention of the Yuan period or of eunuch tribute**; his article ends at the formation of the 朝官內侍制 around Mokjong (997–1009) and Munjong. He does cite his own later article 「高麗後期 內侍制의 變化」 ("Changes in the late-Goryeo naesi system", K p.152 n) as the place where later developments are treated. **Action item for the parent:** the Yuan-period Goryeo eunuch story (and the question of when hwan-gwan gained power) must come from another source; the only cross-source datum here is Tsai's record that Korean tribute missions to Ming included castrati and that Korean-born eunuchs served and returned "with honor and pensions" (T p.129–130), which post-dates 1368.

[#25, #28, #47, #55] | Mixed: **STRENGTHEN** for "distinct class" (China always treated them as a legally and socially bounded group with their own bureau, marriage market, and tombs); **WEAKEN** for "sex class" (the class is a *service* status, and the eunuchs themselves fought to be read as men) | Locals will instantly recognise "a bounded caste of the cut" — but will file it under servants, not under a third sex. Chinese text should call the Sisters' castrates 閹人/宦官 and be baffled that they rule rather than serve. |
| (c) | Castration as sacred rite | Shang captives "may have served ritual purposes" (H p.5) — the only ritual hint, prehistoric. Tang eunuchs *administered* religion (功德使) but castration itself is never religious (H p.2–3, 16). Ming: a monk's self-castration (1436) is punished as a corvée dodge, not read as piety (T p.135); state ideology makes castration *anti*-sacred: unfilial (T p.132). Fujian 私白 is an economy, not a cult (H p.5–6). [#1, #24, #38, #33] | **WEAKEN** strongly | No East Asian frame treats the knife as holy; Confucian doctrine treats it as the destruction of the ancestral line. A Chinese writer will call the Sisters' rite 自宮 ("self-emasculation") — the word for a vagrant's livelihood trick — or, at best, barbarian sacrifice. Any East Asian branch must argue *against* filial piety head-on. |
| (d) | Castrated men regarded as women | Tang eunuchs "modeled their (after)life according to traditional male gender roles" (H p.1); authors "went to great lengths to construct a gender-normative male identity" (H p.26); they marry women, head households, continue the line (H p.8–9). Only two feminine-adjacent hints: Empress Wu "母之" to Gao Lishi (H p.14 n66) and late-imperial self-designation 奴婢 (H p.8 n38). *Odes* pairs women and eunuchs as one evil (T p.122). [#8, #9, #47] | **WEAKEN** for the eunuchs' self-view; **STRENGTHEN** only for *hostile* pairing (women + eunuchs = inner-palace misrule) | Chinese eunuchs would be the Sisters' fiercest *doctrinal* opponents: men who paid the price and still insist they are men and fathers. Hostile scholars, by contrast, will happily agree that castrates "bleat" like wives — and damn both. Write the Chinese eunuch as offended, the Confucian as contemptuously agreeing. |
| (e) | Castrate priesthood | Tang eunuchs held the Commissionerships for the Cultivation of Religious Merit over the capitals' Buddhist/Daoist establishments (H p.2–3, 15–16, 22); rank-and-file Ming eunuchs "burn incense, light the altar candles, and offer food and sacrifice to the emperor's ancestors" (T p.125); a Ming monk self-castrates (T p.135). No source shows a *castrate clergy*; eunuchs supervise clergy or perform household rites. Goryeo: household diviner 司天供奉 and the king's 中使 to monks were intact officials (K p.158–162). [#24, #38, #51] | **STRENGTHEN** weakly: eunuchs as *ritual functionaries and ecclesiastical administrators* is attested; eunuchs as *ordained religious authority* is not | A Chinese branch could plausibly grow out of eunuch 功德使-type patronage of temples (castrates who already run the monasteries' affairs), not out of monastic ordination. Flag: Buddhist monastic rules on castrates are *not* in these sources — check Vinaya separately before writing a Buddhist-eunuch clergy. |
| (f) | Separatism / withdrawal | Tang high eunuchs moved *out* of the palace into Chang'an mansions and buried themselves beside the elite (H p.9); Fujian eunuchs buried in native land, graves tended by clients (H p.6); Ming castrates lived in princely establishments empire-wide (T p.125, 133–134). Eunuchs are never separatist — they are the court's inner ring. [#23, #10] | **WEAKEN** | East Asian castrates sought *centrality*, not withdrawal. A separatist castrate community would read to Chinese eyes as a bandit-sect (cf. the 1620 "rowdy and militant mob" of 20,000 castrated men, T p.143) — useful as the hostile frame. |
| (g) | Adoptive heirship | The strongest row. Han 135 legalisation (H p.6); Tang Code (H p.7); Dezong 791 (H p.7–8); 宦官世家 across 4–6 generations: Gao, Pengcheng Liu, Hongnong Yang (H p.10, 15, 20–24); "adoptions … a way of reproduction for the whole eunuch institution" (H p.3); Song critique (H p.3). Ming: slave bonds euphemised as "adopted sons" (T p.146). [#2, #3, #6, #10, #11, #13, #16, #19, #27] | **STRENGTHEN** strongly | Adoptive succession by castrates is *the* East Asian eunuch institution. It is legible, legal within limits (same surname, ≤10 years, one son), and produced genuine political dynasties. The Sisters' adoptive matriline mirrors it with the sex flipped: Tang eunuch houses pass through adopted *sons* and are *patrilineal in form*; the Sisters pass through adopted daughters/castrates in a matriline. A Chinese observer would say: "宦官養子 — we know this; the Song forbade it for good reason." |
| (h) | Communal property | Eunuchs held *private* estates and titles passed to adopted heirs (H p.6–7, 17); Hongzhi confiscated "properties of deceased eunuchs" for famine relief (T p.140); Liu Yu complains eunuchs "divvy up the land" (H p.7). No communal holding anywhere. [#2, #3, #46] | **WEAKEN** | No precedent; but the Hongzhi confiscation shows a ruler treating eunuch wealth as escheatable to the state — a mechanic for seizing a faith community's common property on the death of its head. |
| (i) | Non-marital kinship | 猶子 "like a son" vs 養子 "adoptee" (H p.14); 假子 "foster sons" as adult patron-client bond (H p.25); shared generation-character 守/重/仕/渶 among adoptive brothers (H p.20 n100, 23 n108, 25); 600 sons (H p.25); Tian Lingzi's guards (H p.24 n120); eunuchs marry other eunuchs' adopted daughters (H p.3); Empress as 母 (H p.14 n66). [#8, #9, #17, #20, #21, #22] | **STRENGTHEN** strongly | Tang culture's "tolerance for ambiguity" in kinship terms (H p.26) means fictive kin networks among castrates are entirely familiar. The Sisters' sponsor-daughter/sister bonds will be understood as 養/假 kinship — and attacked, as the Song attacked it, as a cover for faction. |
| (k) | Intra-group hostility (eunuchs vs scholar-officials; castrates vs castrates) | "constantly locked into an adversary position" (T p.121); eunuchs portrayed as "rapacious, wicked, sly, and duplicitous" (T p.121); Song "evil eunuch" narrative (H p.10–11); 903 and Han massacres (H p.2; T p.123); Hongwu's death-penalty tablet (T p.123); Goryeo court official Seo Pil's contempt for household 近臣 and the 雜業 label (K p.157 n). Castrate-vs-castrate: Fugong/Fuguang vs Tian Lingzi factions implied (H p.24–25); none of the sources gives a doctrinal castrate schism. [#14, #26, #27, #28, #53] | **STRENGTHEN** (for literati hostility) | Scholar-official hostility to any castrate power is the default East Asian stance and needs no invention. Additionally, Chinese eunuchs who insist on manhood and fatherhood give a *second* hostile party to the Sisters — a rival castrate identity. |
| (l) | Outlaw framing | Tang Code: cross-surname adoption = 1 yr servitude (H p.7); Dezong 791 one-son cap (H p.8); Hongwu: eunuchs in government = death (T p.123); castrating servants → castration (T p.133); Hongxi/Xuande: self-castration = unfilial, exile/soldiering (T p.135); Jingtai: exile the whole family, punish village heads and neighbours who conceal (T p.137); Hongzhi: executions, mother prosecuted (T p.140–141); private castrators flogged and exiled, fathers to foundry, boys confiscated (T p.139). Goryeo: no castration law in source. [#6, #11, #28, #29, #33, #37, #39, #41, #45, #46] | **STRENGTHEN** (the faith is squarely illegal in both Tang and Ming law) | Tang: the faith's adoptions violate the Code; its castrations are 私白 outside state control. Ming: every element — castration, self-castration, sponsorship, concealment by a community, female sponsor — has a named punishment. The faith would be treated as a 自宮 racket plus unfiliality plus (if armed) sedition; collective punishment of the sheltering village is attested. |
| (n) | Penal castration | 宮刑 "never played a major role" (H p.4); gone from Sui/Tang codes, "only used in exceptional circumstances" (H p.5 n20); Northern Wei castration of traitors' sons <14, abolished 547 (H p.13–14); Ming: Hongwu abolished it, Hongxi re-banned it 1425, later emperors used it on "major political criminals and their young male relatives" (T p.131–132); Huan En (T p.132 n17); Mao Xiang exiled for castrating suspects 1566 (T p.132); Ji Gang castrates recruits (T p.132); Miao boys 1460 (T p.130). [#5, #29, #33, #34, #35, #42] | **Mixed**: **STRENGTHEN** that castration *as punishment* is a known East Asian category ("one of the Five Punishments" framing is the sources' background — Hoeckelmann calls it 宮刑); **WEAKEN** for the faith's *rehabilitative* logic — East Asian penal castration targets the kin of traitors and captives, never the violent man for his own reform | A Chinese jurist would recognise the Sisters' penal path as 宮刑 revived — a punishment his own dynasty prides itself on having abolished, and one Confucian doctrine condemns as an injury to the ancestral line. The faith's sponsor-and-reform framing has no analogue; the only near-fit is Ming landlords castrating servants to secure household chastity (T p.133). |
| (o) | Sponsor / mentor system | Eunuch "fathers" adopting boys who are then castrated: Gao Yanfu→Gao Lishi (H p.14), Yang Yanzuo→Yang Zhilian (H p.21), Xuanyi→Yang Fugong "以父，幼為宦者" (H p.23); family sponsors: Liu Ying sends Honggui at 15 (H p.18–19); mass adult sponsorship: 600 sons, Shou- names (H p.25); Neishufang school, boys avg. 10, Hanlin tutors (T p.124); fathers who commission castration punished, castrator exiled, boys taken into state custody (T p.139); princely households that receive self-castrates prosecuted (T p.136); Korean eunuchs retire home "with honor and pensions" (T p.130). [#8, #12, #13, #16, #19, #21, #36, #37, #39, #45] | **STRENGTHEN** strongly | The castrate "father" who adopts, has castrated, places, and is succeeded by a boy is the backbone of Tang eunuch society; the Ming adds a formal school. The Sisters' sponsor (a woman or castrate who stands surety for a penitent) maps onto the 養父/假父 role with the gender inverted, and the Ming liability rules (sponsor punished, ward confiscated) give a ready-made hostile legal mechanic. |

**Overall verdict.** In an East Asian setting the faith is *legible* on exactly the axes the Tang eunuch institution occupied — bounded caste, adoptive heirship, fictive kinship, mentor chains — and *illegible or offensive* on the axes it adds — sacredness, womanhood, separatism, communal property, rehabilitation. Chinese eunuchs are a mirror that reflects the Sisters' structure while rejecting their gender claim; Confucian officials reject both; Goryeo, which ran its inner court with intact aristocrats, has no eunuch estate powerful enough to be either ally or rival in these sources.
