# Burns, *Bringing Their Mother Home* (2025): design reading

**Citation (verified from the PDF).** Krishni Burns, *Bringing Their Mother Home: Roman Multiculturalism and the Mother of the Gods*. Ann Arbor: University of Michigan Press, 2025. x + 257 pp. ISBN 978-0-472-13363-5 (hardcover); 978-0-472-22235-3 (e-book). DOI 10.3998/mpub.11648173. Open access.

**Read:** ch. 5 "Attis and the Galli: Myth and Reality" (pp. 86–115) in full; the Pessinus section of ch. 1 (pp. 16–18); the Conclusion (pp. 208–10). Each PDF sheet carries two book pages; the page numbers below are the book's.

**Scope.** The book's terminus is Claudius (p. 99: "this study's terminus ante quem"). It is about Rome, not Anatolia, and says nothing on late antiquity or the cult's end. It reads Latham and Roller *against* the "anti-galli school".

## Chapters (one line each)

- Intro (p. 1): the thesis that Rome imported the Mother as deliberate, state-sponsored multiculturalism.
- ch. 1, Phrygian Matar (pp. 5–18): Matar as protector rather than fertility deity; a short Pessinus section, read in full and summarised below.
- ch. 2, Greek Meter (pp. 19–38): Hellenisation, metragyrts, Piraeus.
- ch. 3, Geopolitics (pp. 39–58): Pergamon, Pessinus and the 205/4 BCE transfer as Roman Eastern diplomacy.
- ch. 4, Arrival (pp. 59–85): Claudia Quinta, the Palatine temple, the votive deposit.
- ch. 5, Attis and the Galli: read in full (summarised below).
- ch. 6, Acculturation vs. Orientalism (pp. 116–30): Dionysius of Halicarnassus as a biased source.
- ch. 7–9 (pp. 131–89): Roman myth, worshippers, the Megalesia.
- ch. 10 (pp. 190–207): the late Republic and early Empire.
- Conclusion (pp. 208–10): the "performatively foreign" Roman goddess. Under Claudius the Hilaria replaces the Megalesia, and the cult "maintained its cultural relevance as long as Rome was a polytheistic society" (p. 210).

## 1. The argument

- **Pronouns.** Burns uses **he/him** for the galli. "There are no modern constructions of gender that do reflect the galli's gender", and he/him are simply "the closest English has to unmarked pronouns" (p. 86). She explicitly does not use the feminine. **That is a scholarly choice the mod departs from, and the departure should be knowing.**
- **"Both", not "neither".** On a Claudian relief of the Palatine temple pediment (Villa Medici), two reclining galli wear "long tunicae and stolae". One is flat-chested and one "has visible breasts". "Rather than being 'neither of men or of women,' the galli can be both" (p. 97). This is Burns' answer to Genucius. Note that it is still a *third*-category reading, which Bhatt and the design's Aristotelian rite reject.
- **The "anti-galli school" is overstated.** The pre-Claudian evidence for Roman hostility is only Genucius (Valerius Maximus) and Dionysius' claim that citizens were barred. Both are Julio-Claudian, two centuries into the cult (pp. 98–99). Against them stand the galli carrying the goddess in the Megalesia procession (Ovid), Varro's admiring narrator, and the historians' respectful galli (pp. 99–109).
- **Varro's fragments.** In Varro's satire the galli sing of themselves: "now we half-man galli toss smooth flying hair for you" (p. 103). The narrator praises their "chaste clothing" and "tender appearance" (p. 103). Varro's verb *gallantes*, "galli-ing", appears nowhere else (p. 103). In Burns' reading, "the joke is on the narrator, not the galli" (p. 104).
- **Battakes (102 BCE),** from Diodorus 36.13 (pp. 106–8). He came from Pessinus wearing "a huge gold crown and a brightly colored robe woven with gold thread that indicated a royal rank". The tribune Aulus Pompeius drove him from the rostra. Pompeius then caught a fever, lost his voice, and died in three days. Battakes was "led in procession by many men and women" when he left Rome. The tribune objected to the *kingly* dress, not to his gender (p. 108).
- **Pessinus envoys.** Polybius: galli "sent by Attis and Battakes, the priests of the mother of the gods from Pessinus, wearing figures and images on their breasts", whom the consul received "benevolently" (p. 105). Burns compares them with Mesopotamian eunuch officials (p. 106).
- **Pessinus itself** (pp. 16–17).
  - The earliest settlement dates to c. 400 BCE: "not actually an Iron-Age Phrygian settlement".
  - Attis appears as a priestly name, perhaps "a kind of dynastic name taken on by each Pessinuntine chief priest".
  - "There may have been a time when the Pessinuntine priests of Matar/Cybele were castrated, but there is no positive evidence of it."
  - The *Attabokaoi* suggest a hereditary priesthood, which was certainly father to son by the 2nd century CE.
  - Under Attalid patronage Pessinus "became one of the two great cult centers in Anatolia" for Cybele.
- **The Mother as protector.** Matar's image is "not overtly maternal". She was "a motherly protector of her worshippers ... rather than a mythological mother ... or a source of fecundity" (pp. 17–18). The "mother of the state" was her most enduring persona (p. 18).
- **Castration in practice.**
  - The cut supposedly happened "during the Hilaria", with no evidence before Claudius. Initiates removed their testicles "using a sharpened stone or a sherd of pottery" (Minucius Felix) (p. 114).
  - Pliny twice calls the operation safe. The galli "mostly likely employed gelding techniques that were perfected for animal husbandry" (p. 114).
  - "The tales of wild self-mutilation were probably born out of a combination of the Attis myth and later Christian propaganda" (p. 114).
  - Late Roman law knew four kinds of eunuch: crushed, sterile, born irregular (intersex), and surgically cut (p. 114).
  - Domitian outlawed castrating a boy "without his prior consent" (p. 111). This is a **consent** precedent from Roman law.
- **Why choose it.** Castration let a person stay in "the youthful gender of Roman society" (p. 112). Ovid's Attis vows *semper fac puer esse velis*, "wish always to be a boy" (p. 112). The galli "may have seen their voluntary castration as an expression of selfhood as well as a religious act" (p. 112). Burns compares them with the Vestals: they gave up a normal role and gained legal and religious privileges (p. 113).
- **Self-representation.** On funerary reliefs galli are "more often than not" in **female Roman clothing**, with pectoral placards, Attis' attributes, and a whip "that evokes the galli's custom of ritually harming themselves during the Hilaria" (p. 110).
- **The citizen ban recast.** Citizenship "would have split the galli's identity and compromised their loyalty to the Magna Mater". In 101 BCE a slave who castrated himself was exiled; by dedicating himself to the goddess he "technically stole himself from his prior owner" (p. 113).
- **Survival.** Vermaseren's inscriptions show "steady participation in the cult's priesthoods and rituals well into the fourth century C.E." (p. 110).

## 2. Design pressure

| 1 | The cut are women (Aristotelian main rite); a "third kind" (Galenic splinter) | Burns is the strongest recent scholarly statement of "the galli can be both", and she uses he/him. | 3 | Day of Blood: knife, rename, rise a daughter | Burns places the cut at the **Hilaria** (the joy festival). Ancient sources are silent; the rite was secret. | Design freedom: the March cycle has a mourning day (blood) and a joy day (Hilaria). Proposal: the Day of Blood is the mourning-and-offering day, and the knife and the **new name are given at the Hilaria**, two days later. | 4 | Knife doctrines; the Gift Alone is the strongest long-run doctrine | Domitian's law forbade castrating a boy without his consent. | 5 | Refusal of the court eunuch system | Burns gives a Roman typology of eunuchs: crushed, sterile, born so, cut. That typology became Byzantine law's. | Use as event flavour in a Byzantine jurist's reaction: "Of the four kinds of eunuch in the Digest, yours is none." No mechanical change. |

**Primary-source cases usable as events or characters.** Battakes against Aulus Pompeius (Diodorus 36.13; Plutarch, *Marius* 17); Polybius' Pessinuntine envoys; the slave of 101 BCE who "stole himself" for the goddess; the Villa Medici pediment galli.
