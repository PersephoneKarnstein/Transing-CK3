## 1. The argument in brief

### Pagan magic, Christian magic, and a slow conversion (chs 1-2)
- **Pagan and Christian magic used the same toolkit.** Only the source of power differed.
  - A wax image offered at St Katarina's grave counts as a miracle, while a dough poppet is condemned as a heathen custom (pp. 44-45).
  - Bishop Þorlákr heals with songs and calms storms, as Óðinn does (pp. 51, 66).
  - Conversion contests (Poppo's hot iron, Þangbrandr's fires) work as a "metalanguage": both sides accept that magic is real and argue only over whose power is greater (pp. 32-37).
- **Magic was learned, not innate.** *Fjölkyngi* means "manifold knowledge". Practitioners name their teachers, and the texts describe apprenticeships. Magic was "something ... one consciously acquired as part of a learning process, not an innate ability" (p. 46).
- **Conversion was gradual.**
  - Þorgeirr's ruling (999/1000) outlawed open heathen practice but left secret practice unpunished. "Within a few years" secret practice was banned too (p. 38).
  - In *Grettis saga*, old Þuríðr lives among "many heathen sparks" (*margir gneistar heiðninnar*, p. 94).
  - Hjalti Skeggjason was outlawed for a verse calling Freyja a bitch (p. 29).
- **Óðinn survived as a devil.**
  - A 14th-century Bergen rune-stick calls on Óðinn, "greatest among devils", to name a thief (pp. 12, 66).
  - Late Stockholm trials charge men with having "served Óðinn for seven years" (pp. 50, 173).

### Charms, galdr and performance (ch. 2)
- **Galdr is song.** The word comes from *gala*, "to crow; to chant, sing" (p. 72).
- **Charms were performed:** spoken, then carved, with the speaker turning (*snúa*) toward the target. Mitchell's examples are Skírnir, Busla, Egill's níð-pole, and the woman in dark blue before Örlygsstaðir in 1238 (pp. 68-71, 102).
- **A curse had to be declared publicly to work.** Ragnhildr tregagás (Bergen, 1324) hid a sword and other objects in a bridal bed and spoke her curse in secret. The next day she announced at the feast that the groom would be impotent. "Performance matters" (p. 72).
- **Love magic was gendered.** Charms tied to men aim to coerce sex; charms tied to women aim to prevent it (p. 52).
- **The noun *ergi* is cast at women as a curse.** Skírnir threatens Gerðr with *ergi oc œði oc óþola*. A 14th-century Bergen rune-stick calls down "wolfish lechery and lust" (*ylgjar ergi*) on a woman (pp. 53-54). In Snorri, Mitchell glosses *ergi* as "lewdness, lust" (p. 80).
- **Charms passed from teacher to pupil.** Ragnhildr learned hers "in her youth" from Sörli Sukk (p. 170). Margit (1490) learned hers from a woman called both "Anna the singer" and "Anna the Finn" (p. 173).

### How the sagas use seiðr (ch. 3)
- **Magic marks the past.** The further back a saga is set, the more magic it contains. The contemporary sagas (*Sturlunga*) have almost none (pp. 85, 97-104). Writers ascribe sorcery to the past "using it to represent ... the very essence of what that world was like" (p. 103). Saga "testimony must be sipped cautiously" (p. 104).
- ***Eiríks saga* in full (pp. 94-97).**
  - Mitchell translates *varðlokur* as "warlock-songs". The manuscripts also spell it *varðlokkur* and *vardlokkvr*.
  - Guðríðr: "I am neither a sorceress nor a witch, but in Iceland my foster-mother Halldís taught me a song"; and "This is the sort of affair I want no part of, for I am a Christian woman" (p. 96).
  - Some scholars think the scene was invented to show the sanctity of Guðríðr, an ancestor of bishops. Strömbäck doubted the *varðlokkur*. Mitchell thinks the overall impression is probably right (p. 97).
- **The völva did not work alone.**
  - Heiðr in *Örvar-Odds saga* travels with fifteen girls and fifteen boys. The older manuscript says she needs them for chanting, and she goes "out" with them to perform seiðr (p. 130).
  - Þorbjörg is "one of nine sisters", and *systir* "can also refer to elective affinities (i.e., 'nun', 'sister of charity')". The saga may have left out her troupe so that Guðríðr would be needed (p. 131).
- **The platform was also used for weather-working.** Two *seiðkonur* climb a "high sorcery-scaffold" to raise a storm, then fall and break their backs (p. 91). Kotkell's whole family mounts a platform to raise a storm (p. 93).
- **Massed sorcerers are men.** When witches and sorcerers appear "in massed, armylike scenarios in opposition to Christianizing kings, they are male" (p. 192). Óláfr Tryggvason burns sorcerers at a feast and drowns Eyvindr kelda's crew of *seiðmenn* on a skerry (pp. 33, 190-191).

### Myths of the late period (ch. 4)
- **Kirkjubær 1343: a nun is burned in Iceland** (pp. 122-123, 171).
  - One annal says she gave herself to the devil in writing, threw the host into the privy, and slept with laymen. Another says she was burned for blaspheming the pope.
  - It is the only medieval Nordic trial with a written pact.
  - It came after eclipses, an eruption of Hekla, and the arrival of new bishops.
- **The sabbat came late** (pp. 125-129).
  - 1410: Blåkulla is named as a peril to shipping, with no witches yet.
  - c. 1480-1490: church murals show flying witches.
  - 1555: the full sabbat appears in Olaus Magnus.
- **The witch-ride changed.** In older manuscripts the *gandreið* is a solitary ride to gather news or attack. In 15th-century manuscripts it becomes a journey to an assembly (pp. 132-133).
- **Seiðr's sexuality does not fit the sabbat.** The sexuality of seiðr ("ergi 'unmanliness'") "does not seem to map well" onto the sabbat orgy, which was imported (p. 136).
- **Church-porch murals.** More than 140 scenes show the milk-stealing witch, by then always a woman (pp. 137-141). Sko-Ella, "the woman worse than the devil", is painted at church doors (p. 137).

### Law (ch. 5): the era arc in detail

**Early codes (12th-13th c.)**
- **Witchcraft first appears in law as a slander.**
  - Norwegian codes count calling someone a witch among the gross insults. The accused goes to the ordeal: the kettle for a woman, hot iron for a man. If she is cleared, the accuser is a slanderer unless he can plead common rumour (p. 150).
  - The Older Västergötland Law (c. 1225) lists the slander of claiming to have seen a woman ride the pen-gate "with your hair loose, and in a witch's shape", between night and day. The Younger Law adds "loose-girdled" (pp. 134-135, 150-152). The parallel list for men covers cowardice, homosexuality, bestiality and incest (p. 151).
- **Gulaþing church law** (pp. 160-161):
  - Soothsaying: outlawry, with goods split between king and bishop.
  - Heeding a soothsayer: 40 marks.
  - Sorcery: exile from the realm.
  - Defence for a man: a sixfold oath.
  - Defence for a woman: six good housewives, three on each side of her, swear "that she knows neither sorcery nor witchcraft". The test is whether "her peers" regard her "as being incapable of doing it". If the oath fails, her heir takes her out of the realm.
- **Borgarþing** (p. 161):
  - Felonies: sitting out to rouse trolls; travelling to Finnmark for prophecy; hair, nails or frog's feet found in a bed.
  - Fines of 3 marks each: biting off a child's finger for long life; raising a child heathen.
  - Same six-women defence.
- **Eiðsivaþing** forbids keeping a staff or a heathen altar, and forbids belief in "Finns". A woman charged with "riding" a man may clear herself by oath (p. 163).
- **Grágás** (pp. 67, 159):
  - Galdr, *gerningar* and *fjölkyngi* bring lesser outlawry, and this includes having charms said over oneself.
  - Magic that causes sickness or death (*fordæðuskapr*) brings full outlawry.
  - Both are tried by a jury of twelve.

**The turn (13th-14th c.)**
- **1247:** ordeals abolished (p. 163).
- **Amended Gulaþing:** a woman found to be "troll and man-eater" is taken out to sea and "hewn in the back" (p. 156).
- **Sverrir's Christian law (1269-73):** anyone who "raises a pole and calls it a scald-pole" loses his goods and must confess, or else goes into exile (p. 164).
- **An Icelandic text of debated age names seiðr directly.** Men or women known to "perform seiðr or raise trolls to ride men or livestock" are drowned at sea (p. 164, n. 126).
- **Church statutes:**
  - 1326 Niðaróss confession ordinance: lists "working seiðr" (*magnar seið*) beside sitting out and galdr (p. 166, n. 139).
  - Archbishop Páll (1334-46): "herbs, runes and galdr".
  - Archbishop Árni (c. 1347): rune-users are cursed heretics (pp. 166-167).
- **Capital punishment arrives.**
  - c. 1300, Norway: "Norwegian laws that once called for witches to be exiled now demand capital punishment" (p. 125).
  - 1328, Scanian municipal law: a man who kills by witchcraft goes to the wheel; a woman is burned (p. 154).
  - c. 1350, Magnus Eriksson's Swedish national law: witchcraft moves into the high crimes, beside treason. Women are burned (some manuscripts say stoned) and men broken on the wheel. Witchcraft "is no longer simply a perversion but represents a serious danger to social stability" (pp. 156-157, 198-199).
- **1442, Kristoffer's law:** the slander list now pairs male and female terms: *trulkarl* / *trulkonna*, *kettare* / *ketterska* (pp. 157, 192).

**Denmark and Iceland were milder.**
- Danish law is quiet on witchcraft and deals mainly with rights of defence (p. 157).
- Grágás never lists witchcraft among slanders, and never mentions women in that context. Hastrup's explanation: in Iceland magic meant knowledge, and knowledge was male, which is why Iceland's early modern witches were mostly men (p. 153).

### Trials and gender (chs 5-6)
- **Women and men were charged and punished differently.**
  - Women: "the precipitating crisis ... tends to involve sexuality", meaning love or anti-love magic. They were acquitted, sent on pilgrimage, or exiled. "there is no evidence of capital punishment among women charged" (pp. 169, 197).
  - Men: charged with apostasy, devil-worship and church theft, and executed in every case whose outcome is known (pp. 169, 174, 196-197).
- **Ragnhildr tregagás, Bergen 1324-25** (pp. 123, 169-171). Bishop Auðfinnr called her case heresy. She had been held in fetters and was said to be "not in full command of her faculties". Her sentence: lifelong fasts and a seven-year pilgrimage outside Norway.
- **Kolgrímr, Greenland 1407.** Burned "after the judgement" for winning a married woman by "the black arts". This is the one male love-magic case (pp. 67, 171-172).
- **1080:** Pope Gregory VII tells the Danish king not to blame women (that is, witches) for misfortunes (p. 169).
- **1193:** Philip II of France claims Ingeborg of Denmark bewitched him on their wedding night (p. 169).
- **Reputation was the defence, and false accusation was itself punished as slander.** In Stockholm in 1478 a woman who had called another a heretic was nearly given a heavy sentence. The "better women" pleaded for her, and she was banished from the city instead (pp. 146-147).
- **Seiðr was too womanly for men.**
  - Mitchell describes "the native Nordic perception of witchcraft as being fundamentally effeminate, that is, the view that seiðr ... was too womanly for men" (p. 199).
  - Bósi refuses Busla's teaching because he does not want his saga to say he won "by trickery instead of relying on his own manhood" (p. 190).
- **The "evil woman".** A late proverb says: "An evil woman is the devil's door nail" (p. 176). The church-porch murals work as conduct books for women. The law codes list the night-riding witch beside abortion and infanticide (pp. 185-188).
- **Birgitta and Vadstena.** Her double monastery was ruled by an abbess, which may have "reinforced fears of a gynococracy, of an organized society of women" (p. 195). Romans called her a witch. In legend, St Ingrid of Skänninge and her companions were taken for witches (pp. 135, 195).
- **Witch trials were rare.** Stockholm's records for 1471-92 hold "only two capital cases of witchcraft, less than 0.1 percent of the total crimes recorded" (p. 204).

## 4. Chapter list (one line each)

- **Preface / Introduction.** The 1100-1525 window. Sagas as "forerunners of the historical novel". Magic defined by what witches *do*. The Waxes' "magical world view".
- **1. Witchcraft and the Past.** Sources and method. Pagan magic and the conversion: Ansgar, Poppo, Óláfr Tryggvason's burnings, Þangbrandr's fires, Þorgeirr's secret-practice clause. Syncretism of single elements, not whole systems.
- **2. Magic and Witchcraft in Daily Life.** Christian and pagan magic as one toolkit. Love and anti-love charms (Skírnismál, Bergen sticks, Ragnhildr, Arboga, Stockholm). Fortune, health, weather, malediction. Galdr as performed song.
- **3. Narrating Magic, Sorcery, and Witchcraft.** Eddic Óðinn and Freyja. Nordal's three degrees of "pastness". *Eiríks saga* in full. Contemporary sagas lack magic. Saxo, *Historia Norwegie*, sermons, Birgitta, *Siælinna thrøst*.
- **4. Medieval Mythologies.** The devil's pact (Kirkjubær 1343). Blåkulla, flight and assembly. The *gandreið* changes from a solitary ride to a sabbat. Sko-Ella. The milk-stealing witch in about 140 murals.
- **5. Witchcraft, Magic, and the Law.** Slander and reputation. Provincial and national secular laws. West Norse and East Norse church laws and synodal statutes. Trials: Ragnhildr 1325, Kirkjubær 1343, Kolgrímr 1407, Arboga and Stockholm 1471-92.
- **6. Witchcraft, Sorcery, and Gender.** "Evil woman" iconography in the church porch. Women tried for sex magic and spared; men tried for apostasy and executed. Birgitta and the fear of rule by women. Seiðr as too womanly for men.
- **Epilogue.** From medieval leniency to the early modern hunts (Sweden 1668-76; the Danish ordinance of 1617).
