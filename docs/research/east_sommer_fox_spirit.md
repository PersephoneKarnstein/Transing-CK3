# Sommer, *The Fox Spirit, the Stone Maiden, and Other Transgender Histories from Late Imperial China* (Columbia UP, 2024) — research dossier

**Purpose:** source material for Adelphai Dorleyaiou neighbour-faith text (a Chinese court meeting the Sisters) and a possible Chinese branch. Design is locked; this file gathers precedent, not argument.

**PERIOD FLAG (read first).** This is a book about the **Ming (1368–1644) and Qing (1644–1912)**; almost every legal case is **18th–19th century Qing**, i.e. 300–450 years after the CK3 end date (1453). Sommer himself says: "'Late imperial' refers to the last two dynasties, the Ming, 1368–1644, and the Qing, 1644–1912" (p. 2). What is usable for 867–1453 is (a) the *categories* (stone maiden, yin-yang person, renyao, "leaving the family," the sodomy and heterodoxy statutes) whose roots Sommer traces back to Tang/Song/Yuan/antiquity; (b) the *Sang Chong* case (1470s, Ming, just after the game's end — the earliest full case, and one with a clear pre-Ming literary ancestry); (c) *narrative shapes* (the exposure, the midwife's examination, the sorcery charge, torture-shaped confession) that are generic enough to be back-projected. Every entry below carries a period tag: **[PRE-1453 ROOT]**, **[MING 1368–1644]**, **[QING]**, **[LATE QING PRESS]**, **[MODERN]**.

Cross-references: Hoeckelmann (Tang eunuch dynasties) and Tsai (Ming eunuch supply) and Chiang (*Transgender China*, *After Eunuchs*) are already filed; where Sommer leans on them I note it and do not re-extract. Page numbers are Sommer's printed pages (markers in the text file).

## 1. Book map

**Sommer's thesis.** Following Susan Stryker's practice-based definition ("people who move away from the gender they were assigned at birth," p. 8), Sommer groups under "transgender" a set of Ming–Qing phenomena normally kept apart: boy actresses (*dan*), Buddhist and Daoist clergy, eunuchs, "stone maidens," intersex "two-formed persons," and above all three Qing prosecutions of "men masquerading in women's attire" (*nan ban nüzhuang*). His central claim: gender-transing was tolerated when "it took place within narrowly defined institutional contexts where it did not threaten the normative order" (p. 11) — the stage, the monastery, the palace — but "gender transgression that was understood to be an act of self-determination provoked paranoid fantasies of heterodoxy and sexual predation and therefore became a target for harsh persecution" (p. 11–12). The Confucian root: *The Book of Rites* makes the distinction of the sexes the thing that separates humans from animals; blurring it is "the way of animals" (*qinshou zhi dao*), which Sommer calls "a good approximation for the concept of heterodoxy (*zuodao yiduan*)" (p. 3). Hence heterodoxy sits under the Board of Rites in the codes, not the Board of Punishment (p. 3). A second structural claim is a "feedback loop" between courtroom and *zhiguai* fiction: the 1470s Sang Chong case spawned a literary subgenre, which then scripted how later magistrates interrogated (and tortured) M–F crossers (pp. 19–20). Sommer uses they/them for the M–F protagonists and for Xinxiang, but he/she for actors, eunuchs and clergy, who "seem to have been identified by others (and at least some themselves identified) primarily with the gender they had been assigned at birth" (p. 16). Note also his observation that classical/vernacular Chinese had **no gendered pronoun** before the 20th century (*ta* 他 covered all; p. 12–13), so testimony could "suspend judgment" about a person's gender without comment (p. 14).

**Sources.** Qing routine criminal memorials (*xingke tiben*) and secret palace memorials (*zhupi zouzhe*) in the First Historical Archive, Beijing; Ming–Qing fiction of "the strange" (*zhiguai*: Li Yu, Ling Mengchu, Pu Songling, Yuan Mei); and late-Qing Shanghai press (*Shenbao*, *Dianshizhai Pictorial*), pp. 17–22. That "men masquerading in women's attire" cases were reported through the *secret* memorial channel normally reserved for war and sedition "reflects the weight of the ideological implications such cases bore" (p. 18).

## 2. Case dossier

Format: date/place · what the person did · how neighbours vs. court categorised them · verdict · gendering logic. All quotes are Sommer's translations unless marked (Chinese romanisation in parentheses is his).

### 2.1 Sang Chong 桑沖 — the locus classicus **[MING, executed 1477; narrative roots pre-Ming]**
- **Date/place.** Shanxi commoner; executed by dismemberment (*lingchi*) in Chenghua 13 = 1477 per the *Ming shilu* (compiled c. 1488–1505, "the closest we have to a contemporary account," p. 62). Wanli-era gazetteers (1612) redate it to Zhengde (1506–21) and inflate the numbers; "The two gazetteer summaries: both carry the title 'A Male Sorcerer Harms Public Morality' (*nan yao shang hua*)" (p. 64). Shanxi Provincial Gazetteer adds "that Sang Chong was the adopted son of one Sang Mao" (p. 63).
- **What they did (per *Ming shilu*).** "In the beginning, there was a man in Shanyin County, Datong Prefecture, who practiced women's handiwork and disguised himself as a woman (*xi nügong, wei furen zhuang*), in order to seduce and debauch daughters and wives of good families (*yi youyin liangjia nüfu*). If any refused him, he would use black magic (*momei*) to make them submit... Sang Chong learned all this man's techniques, and then recruited seven followers whom he instructed in turn. Sang Chong traveled through more than forty departments and counties, debauching more than eighty women in all, and no one suspected his disguise. But when he reached Jin Department, a man who tried to rape Sang Chong discovered his fraud and reported him to the authorities" (pp. 61–62).
- **Training as transition (Lu Can, 1510s fiction).** "Chong pledged Gu Cai as his master (*toubai wei shi*). Gu Cai tweezed and shaved Chong's eyebrows and face to look like a woman..., rolled his hair up in a topknot (*jiuji*) in the style of a married woman, and then dressed him in women's clothing and jewelry. Then Gu Cai taught Sang Chong how to do women's work: how to draw and cut patterns, how to embroider the tops of shoes, how to cook meals, and so on" (pp. 65–66). Sommer: "In the Ming dynasty, the standard hairstyle for men was to wear the hair long; thus, Sang Chong's transition would have required no radical modification of the hair" (p. 66). **Relevant to pre-Qing setting: no queue to grow out.**
- **Exposure.** By a would-be rapist (Gao Xuan or his son-in-law Zhao Wenju): "Groping Chong's body, Zhao realized that Chong had no breasts, and instead had a scrotum! So he seized Chong and delivered him to the authorities" (p. 67). "in no version is that man ever held criminally liable for the attempted rape" (p. 67).
- **Categorisation.** A *renyao*/*yaoren*: "The character *yao*, which I have translated in this context as 'sorcerer,' typically means 'demon'... *Yao* has the female radical *nü*, thus implying there is something feminine about these demons. In Ming-Qing legal texts, *yao* is used interchangeably with *xie* (heterodox)" (p. 64). "The term '*renyao*' (or '*yaoren*') has long carried a wide range of meanings associated with gender-crossing, demonic shape-shifting, and black magic" (p. 64). Fictional variants give the sorcerer "a retractable penis" (p. 64).
- **Verdict.** *Lingchi* in the marketplace "on the grounds that his repulsive, vile crimes had injured the public morality (*you shang fenghua*)" (p. 62); seven accomplices hunted.
- **Gendering logic.** "the M–F cross-dresser was a fundamentally masculine, hypersexual predator whose disguise as a woman was intended to facilitate his crimes" (p. 61). Sommer's reading: a torture-shaped confession "that confirmed a preexisting 'script' in the minds of their interrogators" (p. 20); the historical Sang "was a gender-transing person not unlike Xiong Mumu" (p. 71). Note the cult structure: "its weird techniques being passed from master to disciples across three generations" (p. 64).

### 2.2 Xiong Mumu 熊姆姆 / "Xiong Ersheng" — the midwife **[QING 1744, Hunan]**
- Born c. 1680, Wuling County, Hunan; left home c. 1706 at 26 *sui*, "grew out his hair, pierced his ears, bound his feet, changed into women's attire (*xufa chuan'er chanzu, gaizhuang wei nü*)" (p. 51). Name *Mumu* = "Elder Sister-in-Law"; "*mu* could also mean 'nurse' or 'governess'" (p. 51). Cover story on arrival in Anfu County 1723: a chaste widow fleeing in-laws who forced remarriage, who "had shaved her head and become a Buddhist nun, but had recently returned to lay life, growing out her hair and earning a living as a midwife (*wei renjia chanfu shousheng*)" (p. 51). "everyone believed this story (*ren gong xin zhi*)" (p. 52).
- **Kin-building without marriage.** Adopted Peng Tianruo as "successor son" (*jizi*) to the fictive dead husband, paid his bride-price; he adopted a successor son in turn; pledged three "dry daughters" (*gan nüer*); "a family of eight people in all" built "by formal successor adoption (*liji*), acquisition of women through marriage, and the more informal pledging of dry kinship" (p. 52). Amassed 32 *mu* of paddy; "at least a 'rich peasant,' if not a landlord" (p. 53).
- **Exposure.** Self-inflicted: sued brother Erliang for half the paternal estate as "his older sister's property (*di zhan zi chan*)" under the name "Xiong Shi"; Erliang's counterplaint "exposed their sibling's masquerade (*shouchu jia zhuang qingshi*)"; "Magistrate Zhang then ordered 'Xiong Shi' to strip and 'be examined in court' (*dangtang yanming*) by a midwife" (p. 53). Inheritance logic: "if Xiong Mumu wanted to be treated as a woman, then in household division they should receive a daughter's share (i.e., nothing)... Xiong Mumu wanted to live as a woman without surrendering the privileges of a man" (p. 59).
- **Court's categorisation.** Governor Jiang Pu: "we can be sure without even bothering to ask that he must have seduced and raped women on a routine basis (*ze pingri jianwu zhi shi bu wen ke zhi*). Someone who destroys family relations and injures the public morality in such an egregious manner cannot be tolerated in Your Majesty's sacred realm!" (p. 54). Yet "careful investigation had thus far uncovered no further evidence of 'heterodox teachings, sorcery, or bewitching and deceiving people' (*xiejiao, yaoshu, guhuo renxin*)" (p. 54). Dry-daughter pledging "must have looked suspiciously similar to a disciple's pledging themself to a master, which was the basic means of recruitment in proscribed sects" (p. 76).
- **Verdict.** Governor asked for beating to death; Qianlong: "This culprit's crimes are not yet serious enough to warrant death (*shang bu zhi si*), but he cannot be allowed to remain in place" — ordered to "shave off his hair (*ti qi fa*)" into the male tonsure and sent to military slavery in Heilongjiang (p. 55). Brother beaten as co-concealer ("abetted and concealed Ersheng's treachery (*pengjian rongyin*)"); kin network dissolved "and then returned to their respective natal lineages"; property confiscated: "there is no one who is entitled to receive it" (p. 55).
- **Gendering logic.** Genital inspection = truth: "if they had male genitals, then Xiong was 'really' a man, and their presentation as a woman was a 'masquerade' (*jia zhuang, jia ban*)" (p. 70). Punishment included "coercive regendering" — deadname, tonsure, presumably "forced to take out their earrings, unbind their feet, and put on men's clothing" (p. 71). Sommer's counter-reading: "Another possibility is that everyone knew the truth, but no one cared. I believe this is actually the most likely scenario" (p. 57); "nobody from Xiong's own community reported them" (p. 57).
- **Sponsor/teaching.** "we must assume that Xiong learned midwifery after transitioning and presumably was understood to be a woman by whoever taught them" (p. 58); midwifery "was learned through apprenticeship, and... often ran in families, being passed from mother to daughter" (p. 58).

### 2.3 Zengliang 增亮, the monk **[QING 1819, Beijing]**
- Young monk, sodomised by older monk Jiekuan then by layman Lü Yushan; beat Jiekuan; "at the urging of Lü Yushan, he attempted to disguise himself by 'changing into woman's attire' (*gaiban nüzhuang*) and fled" (p. 79). Board of Punishment: "in previous cases of men masquerading in women's attire (*nan ban nüzhuang*), if the perpetrators had debauched women (*jianyin funü*) or deceived the masses in order to cheat them out of their money (*huozhong hanqian*), then they would be sentenced to strangulation according to the statute against 'deviant ways and heterodox doctrines' (*zuodao yiduan lü*)" (p. 79). But even with neither motive: "for a monk to be willingly polluted and debased in this manner (*gan shou wuru*) was itself weird and perverse (*guiyi*)" — 100 blows, life exile 3000 *li*, two months cangue, laicised; i.e. one degree below strangulation (p. 79). **Key rule:** cross-dressing alone = heterodoxy-minus-one-degree; "for clergy simply to dress in lay attire was itself a crime, not to mention adopting the attire of the opposite sex" (p. 81).

### 2.4 Zhang Yaoguniang 張幺姑娘 / "Peng Ziren" — the beggar faith-healer **[QING 1816–18, Hubei/Henan]**
- Orphaned landless beggar, b. 1781, Tianmen County. Transitioned 1816 at 35 *sui*: confessed motives "Because I could not beg enough food... I got the idea to change into women's dress, hoping that people would pity me more... I also hoped to become intimate with women and seize opportunities to have illicit sex with them and kidnap them" (p. 110) — Sommer thinks the second is the interrogators'. Took **mother's natal surname** Zhang: "they symbolically abandoned not only the social role of a man but also their patrilineal ancestry... they chose the mother over the father, the matrilineal connection over the mandated patrilineal one" (pp. 110–11).
- **Healing lineage.** Saved from choking by itinerant smallpox doctor Zhu Da's charmed water; "pledged him as master to learn how to make talismans and incantations (*bai ta wei shi xuexi fuzhou*)" (p. 112). Incantation: "Princely Heaven, command and bestow your numinous perception in abundance!... I beseech the Great General to descend into this talisman and provide protection!" (p. 112). Treated two snakebites for 800 and 1200 cash; patient Xiong Gui "testified that he had indeed been cured of snakebite by a 'beggar woman' (*gaifu*)" (p. 113).
- **Marriage to a man.** Fellow beggar Wang Shixian (younger by 7 years) saw through them; Zhang "offered to let me sodomize him and to acknowledge me as his husband and himself as my wife (*qingyuan ting xiaode jijian, ren wei fufu*)" (p. 117). Sommer: of hundreds of male-male cases, "the sole exceptions are the relationship between Zhang Yaoguniang and Wang Shixian... and the relationships between Liu Xing Shi and their two husbands... they are best understood not as 'male same-sex relationships,' but rather as what the couples themselves called them: marriages between husband and wife" (p. 119).
- **Verdict.** Prefects: "For Peng Ziren to masquerade in women's attire was already in itself weird and perverse (*yi shu yaoyi*). But then he employed this scheme in order to have illicit sex with women and abduct them; moreover, he falsely claimed to cause spirits to descend (*shendao jianglin*)... his crimes meet the statutory definition of 'using deviant ways to deceive the masses' (*zuodao huozhong*)" (pp. 123–24). Strangulation, raised on review to "immediate" (p. 124). Wang Shixian as accomplice: deported to Xinjiang "and given as slaves to the greater or lesser begs" and "tattooed on the face" (p. 124). Note what did NOT matter: "Zhang's sexual role as the penetrated partner did not fit the paradigm... same-sex acts had no bearing on the statute against heterodoxy" (p. 124).
- **Interrogation script (Sommer's reconstruction).** "'You decided to masquerade in women's attire so that you could seduce and rape women, isn't that so? How many women have you seduced and raped?... Have you ever summoned or channeled spirits?... Who taught you how to do these things? Have you pledged anyone as master...? Have you recruited any disciples yourself?'" (pp. 132–33). Final denial: "nor did I transmit my methods to any disciples (*chuan tu*), nor did I practice any other heterodox teachings or sorcery" (p. 121). Eighteen-month investigation; ankle press likely (p. 133).

### 2.5 Xing Shi 邢氏 / "Xing Da" — the fox spirit medium **[QING 1807, Beijing]**
- Orphan from Renqiu; sodomised from 14 *sui* by cook Li Si; at 17 *sui* Hong Da "told me to grow out my hair and count myself his wife (*jiao wo liule toufa, suan ta nüren*)" (p. 138); lived 12 years as Hong's wife; 1802 Hong (ill) sold them in marriage as his "widowed younger sister" for 25 strings of cash to grain-hauler Liu Liu, a fraudulent widow-sale (pp. 139–40). Wedding night: "I covered up my inconvenient place and said that I was ill and could not have intercourse, but I was willing to let him sodomize me instead" (p. 140).
- **Fox calling.** 1805 "visited in a dream by a 'fox spirit' (*huxian*) and realized that they could serve the spirit as a medium (*dingxiang*)"; a grateful father "painted a picture of a female fox spirit (*nü huxian*)" for their altar (p. 140). Ritual: incense circled three times round the head, "pretending that the fox spirit had descended into me (*jiazhuang huxian jiangxia*)", vague diagnostic dialogue, tea-and-ginger medicine, 100–200 cash "incense money" (pp. 140–41). In-laws objected; Xing "pretended to be mad and possessed by a demon, and made a wild scene in order to scare them (*jiazhuang fengmo sapo xiahu*)" and the couple were disowned (p. 141). "After I began worshiping the fox spirit's image I never again had sexual relations with Liu Liu" (p. 141).
- **Exposure.** A neighbour plucking facial hair "reached out and groped Xing Shi in the crotch" (p. 141) → couple moved. Constables surveilling for heterodoxy: "Although she wears women's clothing and her hair and facial features are the same as a woman's, there seems to be something suspicious about her manner and comportment (*guan qi juzhi xingji, si you keyi*)" (p. 137); a neighbour woman sent as fake patient confirmed she "really does not appear to be a woman"; midwife: "is in fact a male (*shi xi nanzi*)" (pp. 137–38).
- **Categorisation.** Gendarmerie: "the civilian Xing Da, despite being a man, grew his hair out long, masqueraded falsely as a woman, was married to Liu Liu as wife, worshiped a picture of a fox spirit... He is truly lawless in the extreme (*mu wu faji*)!... how is it possible that for five years he never realized that Xing Da had a man's body?" (p. 142). Board: "On top of that, he shockingly dared to grow out his hair and wear women's clothing and accessories! It is obvious that he has committed crimes of debauching women" (p. 143) → ankle press. Under torture Xing denied sex with women to the end: "He asks why he would endure torture in order to lie about this one thing, after having confessed everything else?" (p. 144).
- **Verdict.** Strangulation as ringleader under the heterodoxy statute, "immediate"; Liu Liu: "life exile in Heilongjiang, where he shall be given as a slave to the Oroqen or Daur (*Suolun, Dahu'er*) banner troops," face tattooed; Liu's father 80 blows under "doing things that ought not to be done — severe cases (*bu ying zhong*)" for not reporting (p. 145). Arrest to execution under a month (p. 146).
- **Gendering logic.** Officials "clearly had nothing to do with male same-sex attraction" in mind (p. 124); the torture-rewrite recast Xing as "lecherous seducer" who got Liu to alternate roles — "the alternation between sexual roles by male partners would have implied a switching or even transing of gender roles" (p. 158). Fictional afterlife: Wu Chichang, "Yaoren Xing Da" (1850), judgement: "For a man to exchange his attire for a woman's temporarily is something known to happen on occasion; but for a man to do so permanently is something rarely seen in all of history!... If they take turns playing husband, then there is no distinction in rank between yin and yang (*bi zhang fu, wo zhang fu, yinyang diti*)... The law treats such deviants as *yaoren*, and they should be strangled" (p. 169).
- **Medium as gender role.** "In various parts of China, male mediums have been known to channel female spirits or deities, and female mediums to channel male ones, and some wear the clothing of the opposite gender while conducting these rituals" (p. 154). "the four sacred animals were especially venerated as protective deities by three categories of gender nonconformists in North China: the eunuchs in the imperial palace, who made offerings to them twice a month...; actors...; and prostitutes" (pp. 154–55). Hijra comparison explicit (p. 155).

### 2.6 Press and fictional cases **[LATE QING PRESS / MING–QING FICTION]**
- **"A Perverse Nun Is Exposed" (*yaoni bailou*), *Shenbao* 1890, Ren Village near Tianjin.** Young "nun" medium who could "look at incense... to ward off disasters and cure illnesses... Countless numbers of ignorant women worshiped her like a goddess, pledged her as their dry mother (*gan anai*), and became her Buddhist disciples"; "she was not a female, but a male! (*fei ci er shi xiong ye*)"; "kicked and beaten nearly to death" on arrest (pp. 77–78). Press links it to Sang Chong "along with his twenty-seven disciples" who "pretended to be a spirit medium healer (*jiatuo wuyi*)" (p. 77).
- **Zhu Daguan, *Shenbao* 1911, Zhenjiang.** *Renyao* in his twenties married soldier Wang; wedding-night excuse: "I am possessed by a messenger from the court of the underworld, so I cannot engage in intercourse (*qie yin yinsi you chaishi zai shen bu neng tong fang*)... If it doesn't go away within a year, then I will get you a concubine" (pp. 160–61); earned by fortune-telling; exposed by a household-registration check; beaten, paraded in cangue to the four gates, executed "in order to uphold public morality"; husband "remains trapped in his own delusions" (p. 161).
- **Pu Songling, "Renyao" (17th c.).** Sang Chong's disciple Wang Erxi, posing as a masseuse fleeing abusive in-laws, is caught by Ma Wanbao who "castrates him instead and promises to conceal him from the authorities as long as he serves Ma as a concubine until the end of his days... he sleeps with Ma every night and faithfully serves Ma's wife by day" (p. 168). "castration 'disarms' Erxi, and he is domesticated... transformed from a sexual predator into a model concubine" (p. 168); Zeitlin: "a perverse Confucian 'rectification of names'" (p. 168).
- **Li Yu, "A Male Mencius's Mother" (17th c.).** You Ruilang "castrates himself in order to serve his older lover, the widower Xu Jifang, as his concubine. The castration wound miraculously heals in the form of a vagina"; he binds his feet and renames himself Ruiniang (p. 168). "In both tales, male anatomy is posed as an obstacle to the fulfillment of a woman's sexual and social roles. The solution is what amounts to gender confirmation surgery" (p. 168).
- **Wang Sen, 1622 White Lotus.** Fox taught him an "occult technique of empowerment"; "allegedly used knockout drugs to gain control over women so that he could rape them" (p. 148) — fox + heterodoxy + rebellion, no cross-dressing.
- **Wu Huguo, Guangxi 1767.** "Ghost master" (*guishi*) who drugged children with datura/azalea and charged to "dance the ghosts" (*tiao gui*); beheaded under the robbery statute's clause "If drugs are used to stupefy or render someone unconscious (*yi yao miren*)"; governor banned the profession province-wide (pp. 127–28).

### 2.7 Stone maidens (*shinü* 石女) **[QING cases 1739–1865; category older — see §3]**
- **Xie Shi, Tianmen County, Hubei, 1739** (homicide case). Second husband Dong Xianzhao: "Five or six days after I joined him in marriage, Dong Xianzhao tried to have intercourse with me..., but he couldn't (*bu neng jin*), and he told me that I am a stone maiden (*shinü*)" (p. 177). Matchmaker's son: "Xie Shi was married into the He family for several years, and I never heard anyone say she was a stone maiden then... how come she's suddenly a stone maiden?" — insult to the groom → fight → groom killed (p. 178). Midwife: "Even a finger can't penetrate (*zhitou dou jin bu qu*) Xie Shi's lower body... She is definitely a stone maiden. On her upper body, her breasts are collapsed flat (*pingta de*), like a man's breasts (*yu nanren de ru yiban*)" (p. 178); magistrate "personally examined her breasts" (p. 178). Verdict language: "Xie Shi's body is that of a stone maiden (*shen xi shinü*), so she cannot engage in sexual intercourse (*bu neng renshi*)" — literally "cannot engage in human activity" (p. 178). **Placement:** no fraud found (she alone knew her "secret illness (*anbing*)"), so she was returned to the dead first husband's family as a chaste **widow** — "a role that required only the ritual and social dimensions of wifehood" (p. 180), with the widow's rights "to have an appropriate nephew appointed as her husband's heir" (p. 180). Gendering: "no one involved in Xie Shi's case doubted she was a woman"; midwife not coroner = "a priori categorization of Xie Shi as a woman" (p. 180); "Xie Shi was not a male, but a female with a problem — a deficient female" (p. 182).
- **Zou Shi, Nanbu County, Sichuan, 1865.** Groom (16 *sui*): "she is a two-formed cripple and cannot bear children (*erxing fei ren, bu neng shengyu*)" (p. 183) — note *erxing* used as a synonym for stone maiden. Magistrate ordered her father to pay 30 strings so the groom could **buy a concubine** while Zou Shi stayed main wife: "your daughter will have sons even though she cannot bear them herself (*wu zi er you zi*), she will have successors and her incense fire will continue" (p. 184). Sommer: polygyny lets the main wife keep "the social-ritual role only" (p. 185).
- **You Shi, judged by Yuan Mei, Jiangning, 1740s.** Groom "mistakenly taken a stone maiden for his wife (*wu qu shinü*)"; father knew and concealed → marriage fraud; Yuan Mei instead made him refund the bride-price so the groom could "also take a concubine (*ling qu xiaoxing*)" and You Shi could "retain her position as main wife (*reng wei zhengshi*)" — praised as a "marvelous judgment (*miao pan*)" (p. 185).
- **Liao Zhanggu, Ba County, Sichuan, 1851.** Bride 13 *sui*; "vagina is solid (*yin shi*)" → "impossible to use her as a wife (*nan yi wei qi*)" (p. 186). Settlement: groom's family paid 15 strings as temple endowment and she would "shave her head to enter a temple as a nun (*pi ti ru miao wei ni*)" (p. 186). "In social practice, clerical celibacy seems to have been the solution of choice" (p. 187). Fiction: "Sister Stone" (*Shi Daogu*) in *Peony Pavilion*: "Heaven denied me woman's proper parts and so my sole recourse was to the Way, to don the shaman's robe" (p. 187); her husband "did temporarily substitute anal for vaginal intercourse" (p. 187).
- **Jiang Shi, Shunyang County, Henan, 1743.** Too poor for concubine or endowment; husband "murdered the unfortunate young woman" (p. 188).
- **Sommer's contrast:** "Every effort was made to find a place for the stone maidens, but the normative gender system furnished no place for M–F crossers; no official suggested, for example, that they be allowed to join the celibate clergy... the focus of official opprobrium was transgressive behavior, rather than anatomy" (p. 188).

### 2.8 Husbands with "problem" penises **[QING 1748, 1864–66]**
- **Ren Mei, Licheng, Shandong, 1748.** Wife: "A guy like you shouldn't expect to get married, because you only harm someone else by doing so!" (p. 189); he killed her. Magistrate "examined Ren Mei's penis (*xiati*): it appears impotent and small (*wei xiao*)" — echoing Li Shizhen's "natural eunuch" (*tianhuan*) whose "penis is impotent and useless (*yang wei buyong*)" (p. 190). No fraud charged → marriage valid; "Male impotence in and of itself seems to have carried no legal significance during the Qing; it was certainly not grounds for a wife to seek divorce" (p. 190).
- **Chen Erchuan, Jia County, Henan, 1864–66.** Penis "rotted away, leaving him a deformed cripple (*jingwu huan chuang kuilan, zhi cheng canfei*)"; concealed it; bride Yue Shi cut his throat: "Chen Erchuan was crippled and could never have children, so in the future I would have no one to support me at the end of my life (*cheng fei bu neng shengyu, jianglai zhongshen wu kao*)" (p. 192). Board of Punishment: his concealment was marriage fraud, "This marriage was not legitimate"; she was sentenced for murder of an "unrelated party" with execution "delayed" (pp. 192–93). "wives, too, were entitled to expect sexual intercourse and procreation from marriage" (p. 193).

### 2.9 Two-formed persons / yin-yang persons **[QING forensic manual 1807; fiction 17th c.; press 1888–1912; Li Shizhen 16th c.]**
- **Zhou Sijie, Wu County, Jiangsu** (in *Xi yuan lu*, 1807 ed., appendix "A Two-Formed Person"): Ma Yunsheng's wife "had illicit sex and spent the night (*jiansu*) with Jin Sanguan's wife Zhou Sijie... inside her vagina (*chanmen*) there was a soft, fleshy protruberance... when aroused (*jufa*) it would extend about two or three *cun*... She could use it to have illicit sex with women (*ke yu furen tongjian*)" (p. 194). Classified under Li Shizhen's "changeling" (*bian*), "a category of 'deformed male'" (p. 195), yet "the text also seems to represent Zhou as a woman, albeit an anomalous woman" (p. 196). "I have seen not a single reference to *jian* between women in any other legal text from China's imperial era" (p. 195).
- **Suzhou 1912 (*Shenbao*).** Person "had the overall appearance of a woman, but also possessed a tiny phallus"; jailed with women, had sex with inmates; confessed "during the daytime (the period of yang) their body assumed female form, but upon nightfall (the period of yin), it would become male"; "The magistrate labeled this individual a *renyao* and condemned them to death in the standing cangue" (p. 197).
- **Li Baozhen, Shanghai Mixed Court 1888–89.** Monk accused of being "a nun" → "a person of two sexes" (*ci bu xiong*) → police: "in fact, they are a yin-yang person (*shi xi yinyang ren*)" (p. 100); second trial as "Li Huizhen" in women's dress wanting "to meet a man in order to get married (*jiaren*)" (p. 102); magistrate: "Are you a female or a male? (*er shi ci ye xiong ye*)" (p. 104); sentence: a month in a conjoined cangue paraded with the barber, sign reading "A man masquerading in woman's attire, paraded through the streets to be displayed to the people (*nan zhuang nü ban, youjie shizhong*)" (p. 105). *Shenbao*'s opening line: "for a woman to masquerade in men's attire, and for a man to masquerade in women's attire, are both strictly prohibited by law (*nü ban nanzhuang, nan ban nüzhuang, jun gan lijin*)" (p. 99) — **note: Sommer elsewhere says the Qing code has no such explicit article (p. 72); the paper is paraphrasing practice.**
- **Fictional shape-shifting nuns.** Ling Mengchu (late Ming), abbess Wang: midwife "Although she does not exhibit male form, there's something about her that is not quite like a woman (*yu nüren you xie liang yang*)"; oil + dog's tongue → "suddenly a stiff pole popped out" (p. 93); confession: trained by a master sorcerer in "extracting the bodily fluids of others (*caizhan*) and extending and shrinking his penis (*shen suo zhi shu*)", White Lotus adherent; executed, corpse exposed (pp. 93–94). Chen Fangsheng (late 17th c.), **set in the Song dynasty**: nun tells the daughter "I have two forms (*erxing*), so that when I encounter male I am female, and when I encounter female, I am male (*feng yang ze nü, feng yin ze nan*)" (p. 94); precedent nun Dong Shixiu: judgment "The Way of Heaven has yin and yang, and the Way of humankind has male and female... Dong Shixiu's body takes two forms, being neither exclusively male nor female: that is, what is known as *yaowu*, a monster (*shen dai erxing, bu nan bu nü, shi wei yaowu*)"; sentence: 20 blows, "tattooed on the forehead with the characters *erxing*", 10 days cangue, chained at an army camp indefinitely (p. 95). Sommer: *renyao* = "a natural but monstrous phenomenon", *yaoren* = "sorcerer" (p. 95).

### 2.10 Nuns, novices, eunuchs as rape victims — the sexed body wins **[QING 1745, 1747, 1741, 1788]**
- **Zhao Wan, Huaining, Henan, 1745.** Nun gang-raped; midwife: "definitely a virgin who has just been penetrated for the first time (*shi xi chuzi chu poshen*)"; ringleader beheaded; "handled in exactly the same manner as the rape of a chaste wife or daughter" (pp. 198–99). 1747 edict canonised a murdered Daoist nun "even though she does not fall within the ranks of wives and daughters of military personnel or civilians (*bu zai bing min funü zhi lie*)" (p. 199). "Chastity and rapability were two sides of the same coin" (p. 200).
- **Male rape victim.** 1788 Board: anal examination "can certainly be extended by analogy (*yi ke tuilei*)" from the virgin's vaginal exam (p. 201). Plausible victim = "a son or younger brother of good/commoner family (*liangjia zi di*)", i.e. a boy; "A male could be imagined as vulnerable to rape, like a woman, only by being imagined as powerless, like a woman" (p. 201). Rape of a male novice punished like rape of a lay boy; "it seems we can extend this logic to eunuchs as well" — the runaway eunuch Liu Jinxi (15 *sui*) "handled in exactly the same way as the rape of an intact male youth" (p. 202).
- **Liu Jinxi (runaway eunuch) and Jiao Laiyi, Beijing 1741.** Castrated at 13 *sui* by his father, sold "for a 'body price' (*shen jia*) of five taels of silver"; renamed "Jinxi" by a supervising eunuch, "a very common eunuch name"; ran away "because he broke a teapot and feared punishment" (p. 84). Drugged with azalea/human-brain tobacco ("striking flowers with the hand (*pai hua*)") and raped by a defrocked Daoist; returned to the princely estate; Jiao beheaded under "using drugs to stupefy or render someone unconscious in order to steal their property (*yong yao miren tucai*)" (p. 84).

### 2.11 Xinxiang 心祥 / Zhao Jianlin — the hustler **[QING 1746–48, Shanxi/Beijing]**
- Monk (given to a temple as a child), then "sang for three to four years as a performer of female roles in opera" in Beijing, then monk again, sex worker, con artist with eight personae; executed for impersonating "Imperial Censor Zhang Chun" (ch. 7). Yongji County 1746: government student Wang Xu reported "To my surprise, Xinxiang told me that he is actually a woman (*kou cheng xi nüren*), and he behaved wildly and without regard for propriety"; Xinxiang "confessed that it was true he had intended to sell illicit sex (*yitu maijian shu shi*)" and was beaten and deported (p. 213). Final sentence: beheading after the assizes under the "fraud and forgery (*zhawei*)" statute: "Whoever fraudulently claims without any basis (*pingkong zhacheng*) to be a minister in close attendance on the Emperor... and thereby incites and deceives the common people (*shanhuo renmin*), shall be beheaded after the assizes" — same phrase as the heterodoxy statute (pp. 216–17). Sommer on the boy traffic: "Qing dynasty Beijing was the site of an extensive, multifaceted traffic in young boys that supplied the imperial household's eunuch establishment, the boy brothels ('barbershops')... and, of course, the opera" (p. 222). Cross-ref: Tsai on Ming eunuch supply.

### 2.12 Zhang of Shandong — the gender-ambiguous medium **[MODERN, 1930s–1990s; epilogue]**
- Assigned male, dressed as a man, farmed badly, sewed with women, squatted to urinate; villagers called them *eryizi* = "half male, half female (*ban nan ban nü*)... a 'yin-yang person' (*yinyang ren*)" (p. 253). Healed as a toddler by a female medium who made them her godson and declared "this child was born to serve the spirits (*zhege haizi shengxialai jiushi cihou shenxian de*)" (p. 255); possessed by a "white animal spirit" as a teenager, later by the Flowery Maiden, attendant of Grandma Taishan; "When possessed, Zhang's voice was exactly like a woman's voice (*hen nüren*)" (p. 256). Men: "He was not really a man, he only dressed up in men's clothing (*ta you bu shi ge nanren, guang chuanzhe nanren yishang*)" (p. 254). Wife rumoured to "borrow seed (*jie zhong*)"; couple adopted an abandoned girl (p. 255). "Zhang was accepted and respected by their community and even protected by local cadres, because they played a valuable social role" (p. 257). **Relevance:** the one case where the community's category (yin-yang person + medium) is benign and protective — model for a village-level "sympathetic neighbour" culture against the elite script.

### 3.2 Punishment forms with gendering content
- **Coercive regendering**: deadname in record; "shave off his hair (*ti qi fa*)" into the male tonsure (p. 55); presumed removal of earrings, unbinding of feet (p. 71). *Note for 867–1453: no queue; Ming men wore hair long (p. 66), so regendering would be hairstyle/clothing/jewellery only.*
- **Public display**: conjoined cangue parade with placard (p. 105); standing cangue to death (*lijia*; pp. 97, 197); dismemberment (*lingchi*) for Sang Chong (p. 62); face tattooing of accomplices (pp. 124, 145); forehead tattoo *erxing* in fiction (p. 95).
- **Slavery as sentence**: Heilongjiang military slavery (Xiong Mumu; Liu Liu "given as a slave to the Oroqen or Daur"); Xinjiang "given as slaves to the greater or lesser begs" (p. 124).
- **Dissolution of chosen kin and confiscation**: Xiong's adoptive family "returned to their respective natal lineages"; property "there is no one who is entitled to receive it" (p. 55).

### 3.4 Marriage, property and the "changed" spouse
- **Inheritance rule**: "all sons receive equal shares (*zhuzi junfen*)... daughters were excluded from direct inheritance via household division" (p. 59). A son-living-as-daughter therefore has, in the brother's logic, "a daughter's share (i.e., nothing)" (p. 59). No statute addressed it; the court never ruled on Xiong's claim because the examination mooted it.
- **Stone maiden wife**: marriage valid absent fraud → she stays wife (polygyny) or widow (heir appointed for her) or becomes a nun (temple endowment paid by groom's side) (pp. 180–87).
- **Husband without penis**: marriage "not legitimate" → wife treated as "unrelated party" (p. 192).
- **Man-living-as-woman married to a man**: never recognised; the husband is an **accomplice** to heterodoxy if he knew (Liu Liu, Wang Shixian), punished with life exile/slavery and face tattoo (pp. 124, 145); if he did not know, the marriage is simply void and he is a dupe (Liu Liu's first story; Zhu Daguan's soldier). The couples' own term was *fufu* 夫婦, husband and wife (p. 117); Sommer: "There was no model based on marriage" for male pairs (p. 118) — only *ganqin* adoption or sworn brotherhood.
- **Wife sale / widow remarriage as cover**: "Since widow remarriage was structured as a form of wife sale... there was no way for a prospective buyer to learn the truth, as long as the woman kept the secret" (pp. 139–40).
- **Dry kinship** (*ganqin* 乾親; *gan nüer*, *gan erzi*, *gan anai*) and **successor adoption** (*liji* 立繼 / *jizi* 繼子 / *guoji erzi* 過繼兒子): the only legal tools by which a non-procreating person built a household (Xiong Mumu, p. 52); read by the state as cult recruitment (p. 76).
- **Eunuch adoption**: "prohibited" in Qing (p. 16); adopted boys "referred to as 'nephews' rather than 'sons'" (p. 43); Jay's five cases of eunuch adoption, "only one of which dates to the Qing"; Hoeckelmann on Tang, Gilbert Chen on Ming, Li on Han (n. 78, p. 295) — **cross-ref Hoeckelmann/Tsai files; Sommer adds nothing new on pre-Qing eunuch kinship.**

## 7. Candidate CK3 content

Period reminder: Chinese branch content should sit in **Song/Yuan** idiom (867–1453). Use *renyao* in its 13th-c. sense (prodigy / sex-anomaly), *zuodao* for the charge, long male hair (no queue), midwife (*wenpo*) as examiner, *chujia* and Quanzhen ascesis as the religious frame. Avoid *yinyang ren* (unattested early in Sommer) in dialogue; it is fine in modern-flavoured tooltips.

### 7.2 Neighbour-faith text (a Chinese court meeting the Sisters)
- **Confucian official, hostile (orthodox):** "Distinction between the sexes is what sets humankind apart from the birds and beasts. These western priestesses unmake men with the knife and then call them women — that is the way of animals (*qinshou zhi dao*). They gather crowds to burn incense by night and claim to heal; the code calls this *zuodao yiduan*, the left way and heterodox principles, and the ringleader is strangled." (pp. 2–3, 72–73)
- **Confucian official, pragmatic (Yuan Mei model):** "A body that cannot do a wife's work still has a place: as widow, as main wife beside a concubine, or in a nunnery with an endowment. If they will register their house as a convent and keep women from mixing, let them be. If they recruit disciples, they are a sect." (pp. 184–87, 45)
- **Daoist (Quanzhen) cleric, curious-sympathetic:** "In the first generations of our order some adepts cut themselves to reach non-desire at once. The adept's member withers and vanishes with cultivation; theirs is gone by the knife. Crude, but not foreign to the Way." (pp. 39, 42)
- **Buddhist nun, cool:** "We who have left the family are not women as laywomen are women. But they keep their bodies and call them changed; we know the body is not changed by a name." (pp. 37–38, 46)
- **Village medium / fox-cult household, warm:** "The Hu Transcendent chooses whom she will. One who is half-and-half is often chosen — born to serve the spirits. Bring them incense money, not the magistrate." (pp. 150, 255)
- **Literati gossip (*zhiguai* register):** "They say the abbess in the western temple is a *renyao* — meets a man and is a woman, meets a woman and is a man. The prefect should bring a dog." (pp. 94–95)
- **Eunuch courtier, uneasy recognition:** "They cut as we were cut, but they call it holy and we call it the price of rice. We burn incense to the Four Households twice a month that the masters be kind. What god do *they* burn it to?" (pp. 9, 154)

### 7.4 Flags for the design team (not relitigation — source limits)
1. Sommer gives **no Chinese case in which a court treated a male-bodied person as a woman**; the nearest are fiction (#28–29) and the ambiguous Zhou Sijie (#35). A Chinese branch's legal recognition must be invented, and the invention should be flagged as such in flavour text.
2. Sommer does **not** connect Li Shizhen's "five non-males" to Buddhist Vinaya categories; do not cite him for that.
3. *Yinyang ren*, *nan ban nüzhuang* as a set phrase, and the "predator" sense of *renyao* are all post-1453 in his account; for 867–1453 use *renyao* = portent/anomaly (Cheng via n. 51).
4. Everything on eunuch kinship pre-Qing is secondhand (Hoeckelmann, Chen, Li) — already covered by earlier passes.
