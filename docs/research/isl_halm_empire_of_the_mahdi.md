Searches of the whole text for *mukhannath*, effeminate, *khuntha*, hermaphrodite, castration, singer, *muhannak* and *tahnik* found nothing.

**Quotation convention.** Halm quotes Arabic sources in his own German, which Bonner put into English. A primary text quoted here (the *Sira* of Jawdhar, the *Sira* of Ja'far, al-Nu'man's *Majalis*, and so on) is therefore **secondhand**: it was seen only in Halm's rendering. The primary source is named after Halm's page.

**Scope.** The book ends in 973, when al-Mu'izz entered Cairo. It is **background** for the Cairo 1066 set: the household, offices, teaching and ceremonial that al-Mustansir's court inherited. It says nothing about Egypt after 973, Rasad, the Pearl Pavilion, or *mukhannathun*. Halm's later volumes cover what comes after: *Die Kalifen von Kairo* (2003) for 973-1074, and *Kalifen und Assassinen* (2014) for 1074-1171.

**Relation to existing notes.**
- `isl_el_azhari_queens_eunuchs.md` and `isl_tolino_eunuchs.md` use Jawdhar and Ja'far but read them through secondary work. Halm worked straight from the *Sira* of Jawdhar and the *Sira* of Ja'far, and he gives the career detail they lack: offices, manumission, property, letters, and how the eunuchs fared in successions.
- `isl_cortese_calderini_fatimid_women.md` covers the Egyptian harem. Halm adds the North African household it grew out of.
- `tsq_almarri_khuntha.md` and `later_starts/mukhannathun_fatimid_cairo.md` §2 read al-Qadi al-Nu'man's law. Halm gives the man himself: his career, his books, the palace sessions, and the *Ta'wil al-Da'a'im* as the text of those sessions.

## 1. The argument in brief

Only the threads that bear on the design are given here.

### 1.1 The Slavs, the Rum and the palace slaves

- **One label for all the white slaves.** The Aghlabid slave troops were Black (*sudan*) or white, and the white ones were called "Slavs" (*saqaliba*) or "Romans" (*Rum*), "regardless of their actual origins" (p. 152). Of Jawhar, Halm says that "Slav" is "more a designation of type than of provenance" and that a Byzantine origin "does not say much" (p. 339).
- **Taken over, then bought.** When the Aghlabid amir fled in 909, his Slavs and Blacks were taken over by the new regime, not killed (p. 122). Al-Mahdi took over "both the black and the Byzantine [military] slaves" (p. 150). He inherited the Aghlabid Slav guard and then "refurbished it through new purchases" (p. 278).
- **Freedom as clientage.** "These slaves had been carried off in childhood, and even after they were formally set free, they remained in a relationship of clientage" with their former master for life (p. 278).
- **Names.** A Slav freedman can be recognised by a single "speaking" name with no patronymic, and by the epithet *al-Fata* ("the young hero"), which marks freedmen of officer's rank (p. 279). Halm's examples are Mas'ud al-Fata and Ghars al-Fata (parasol bearers), Nasim al-Fata and Sabir al-Fata (governors of Qayrawan), and Maysur, Sandal and Qudam (pp. 279, 283, 316).
- **Why.** The Slavs were "known for their unconditional loyalty to the Fatimids". Halm surmises a deliberate policy of reducing the power of the Kutama chiefs (p. 279). By 958 they formed "the core of the army", and al-Mu'izz had to urge the Kutama to live in brotherly concord with them (p. 397).
- **The palace city.** Al-Mahdiyya was inhabited "solely by the imam-caliph, the heir to the throne, and the princes with their families", plus their slaves and freedmen. Gatekeepers, part of the garrison and the secretaries of the *diwans* were all recruited from these slaves and freedmen (p. 219). The Slav troops "belonged to the Imam in person" (p. 220).
- **Eunuchs in command.** The fleet of 919 was led by the eunuch Sulayman (p. 207). The eunuch Bushra commanded at Beja in 944 (p. 301). The "Slavic eunuch Faraj" led the Sicilian armada of 950 (p. 334). On the Abbasid side, the eunuchs Mu'nis and Thamal defended Egypt (pp. 203, 208, 235). Halm reports all of them without comment on their sex.

### 1.2 Jawdhar: the career

Halm's main source is the *Sirat Jawdhar*, compiled after Jawdhar's death by his secretary and freedman from his papers (p. 279; list of sources). It contains "anecdotal recollections, sermons, documents, letters and rescripts" (p. 279). With al-Nu'man's *Majalis*, it is "the most important immediate testimony" for al-Mu'izz's reign (p. 342).

**How Halm genders him.** Halm calls Jawdhar "the eunuch", "the intendant", "the majordomo" and "the old eunuch". He describes no body, no voice and no dress beyond the borrowed shrouds, and the *Sira* as Halm uses it does not discuss Jawdhar's sex. This matches Tolino's finding that chronicles and biographies do not mention gender (`isl_tolino_eunuchs.md` §1.1).

### 1.3 Other household eunuchs and freedmen

- **Ja'far the chamberlain.** Al-Mahdi's milk-brother: "the same wet-nurse had suckled them both" (p. 61). He was a eunuch who rose to chamberlain and dictated his memoir in old age (pp. 58-59). On the wedding night of al-Mahdi he waited with the women at the door of the bridal chamber. When the bloodied sheet was thrown out, he "danced around with the sheet raised over his head, in the circle of the delighted women" (*Sira Ja'far*, 108ff., secondhand) (p. 61). In 946 he preached the victory sermon in Qayrawan at 72 or 73 (p. 316).
- **Su'luk.** A eunuch, Ja'far's cousin, who guarded al-Mahdi's household women on their flight from Salamya. He was a "fearsome ruffian" who had killed three men (p. 73). He later became the chamberlain Ja'far b. 'Ubayd and led the raid on Apulia in 925 (pp. 154, 237).
- **Sandal and Muslim.** On his way west al-Mahdi bought "a young eunuch from Aleppo, named Sandal, and a Greek goldsmith who acquired the name Muslim" (p. 95). Muslim was bought from a lady in Sijilmasa and served the heir. He became the chamberlain Abu Sa'id 'Uthman (p. 154).
- **Bushra.** A Slavic eunuch bought by the da'i Abu 'Abdallah and given to al-Mahdi. Halm quotes Ja'far's memoir, in which Bushra greets the household: "I am now, God be praised, one of you" (*Sira Ja'far*, secondhand) (p. 137).
- **Greek eunuchs as a charge.** At Sijilmasa the Kutama were said to have found around al-Mahdi "silk and brocade clothing, gold and silver vessels, Greek eunuchs, and indications of the use of alcohol" (p. 161). The source is the hostile 'Abd al-Jabbar. Halm suspects anti-Fatimid Qarmati informants (p. 161 n. 66).
- **Jawhar.** Al-Mansur's freedman and later the conqueror of Egypt. He was owned first by the eunuch Sabir, then by "a lady named Khayzuran", then by the eunuch Khafif, who gave him to the caliph (p. 339). He began as a secretary: "a young, strongly perfumed dandy" (p. 339). He was freed, rose to a vizier-like post (p. 340), and as viceroy received honours "nearly befitting a ruler" (p. 411). **Halm never calls Jawhar a eunuch**, and "the wife of Jawhar the Slav" owned land jointly with a eunuch (p. 363).
- **The curtain-drawer.** Al-Mu'izz's curtain-drawer was "Khafif al-Saqlabi" (p. 351 n. 58, citing al-Maqrizi, *Itti'az* 1:97f.; secondhand). A "Slavic curtain-drawer" was sent to ask the Kutama chiefs to pay taxes (p. 365).
- **The parasol-bearers.** Al-Mahdi's parasol-bearers were the Slav freedmen Mas'ud al-Fata and Ghars al-Fata (p. 279).

### 1.4 The household women

- **The *harim*.** In 910 al-Mahdi's women were brought from Tripoli: "his mother, both his daughters, two nieces, and his son's wife" (p. 148). In Salamya the household also had a Christian steward (*qahraman*) and his wife, the Christian housekeeper Umm 'Ali (p. 73).
- **The Aghlabid concubines.** The fleeing amir left his *harim* behind, except the concubines who had borne him sons (p. 119). The da'i reserved the rest for al-Mahdi. Their woman overseer asked for wine, and added that "they want from men that which men want from them". He answered that only their lord was entitled to that (p. 122). Al-Mahdi kept a few, gave some to his son, and divided most among the Kutama chiefs (p. 148). His seven daughters and six younger sons came from these concubines (p. 276).
- **Mothers of sons.** One of al-Mahdi's *ummahat awlad* hoped her son would reach the throne (p. 277, citing *Majalis* p. 542f.). Al-Mansur was said to have preferred the mothers of al-Mu'izz's brothers (p. 342). He left "three 'mothers of sons,' who became free upon his death" (p. 337).
- **Successions decided in the harem.** Al-Mansur came to the throne through Jawdhar, his mother Karima and his wet-nurse Salaf (p. 311). Al-Mu'izz came through "a court clique", which probably included al-Nu'man (p. 342). Halm's general verdict: these accessions were "settled in a manner that was anything but transparent, behind the palace walls" (p. 348).
- **A woman within earshot.** One cold morning al-Mu'izz received some Kutama shaykhs among his books. He told them what he had said that morning to "the princes' mother ... she is somewhere around here now, so that she can hear me" (p. 367; Ibn Muhadhdhab via Ibn Sa'id and al-Maqrizi, secondhand).
- **Al-Nu'man's women.** Al-Mu'izz gave al-Nu'man slave girls from the booty, and he passed them to his two sons as concubines. When the household grew, the family asked for larger housing but asked not to be separated, "because the women might then pay each other visits and keep one another company without being seen" (p. 341; *Majalis*, pars. 280-281).
- **Women's property.** When al-Mahdi confiscated the property of those who had fled with the amir, he let their wives keep their legal share and gave them protection and maintenance (p. 150). The Shi'ite chief judge enforced "the daughter's right to inherit" (p. 153).
- **Women who stood guard.** At the Sunni *ribat* of al-Monastir, "women standing guard" (*al-nisa' al-murabitat*) lived on the square south of the fort (p. 229; al-Bakri, who wrote in 1068).
- **Mourning women.** Al-Qa'im forbade loud lamenting over the dead, and under al-Mansur a wailing woman was imprisoned (p. 244 n. 367). In 960 al-Mu'izz had al-Nu'man order mosque staff to "outlaw the use of mourning women at funerals" (p. 401).

### 1.5 Women in the *da'wa*

- **From the start.** Abu 'Abdallah's "sessions of wisdom" (*majalis al-hikma*) among the Kutama (before 909) included women: "they used to take part in the sessions, and listen to the wisdom" (p. 106; al-Nu'man, *Iftitah*, secondhand).
- **Aflah's sermons to women.** After 911 the Malusa chief Aflah b. Harun, chief da'i and judge of Raqqada, "held teaching sessions (*majalis al-hikma*) for men and women" (p. 153). His colleague Ibn al-Haytham describes his method: "When he spoke to the women, he would usually draw his example from a jewel or a ring, from earrings and diadems". He also drew on necklaces, anklets and bracelets, clothing and kerchiefs, spinning and weaving, and hairdressing. He spoke the same way to craftsmen about their own trades: needle and thread for the tailor, staff and flock for the shepherd (p. 375; Stern, *Studies*, 103, secondhand).
- **Al-Nu'man's Friday sessions.** Al-Mu'izz sent al-Nu'man "books with secret knowledge" to read out every Friday in a palace hall. The crowd overflowed into the courtyard. When a separate hall was proposed for newcomers, they took it as a slight. The imam told al-Nu'man to carry on as before, since each hears according to capacity, "just as a pot which one puts in the rain catches only the water which falls into its opening" (p. 376; *Majalis*, par. 201, secondhand). The lectures were written down at the imam's order as the *Ta'wil al-Da'a'im*. The imam took the oath from initiates in person (p. 376). Halm does not say whether women attended these Friday sessions or met separately.

### 1.6 Outer and inner: the teaching

- **Ta'wil.** For the Isma'ilis the Qur'an is "in code". Its text is "merely the outer (*zahir*) shell, concealing the authentic, inner (*batin*) meaning" (p. 16).
- **The gendered creation myth.** In the oldest myth, God's omnipotence (*Qadar*) is the masculine principle and *Kuni* ("Be!", feminine imperative) the feminine. *Kuni* "takes herself to be God", and her hubris sets creation and matter in motion (p. 17). Only one early tract tells it as a story, and Halm compares it to gnostic myth (p. 17). It is pre-Fatimid.
- **The law stays.** Antinomians who ate pork and drank wine in Ramadan in 921 were arrested, and most died in prison (pp. 247-249). From then on the official line was that "observance of the outer (*zahir*) commandments and ritual prescriptions remained obligatory, even for initiates" (p. 249). Al-Nu'man's *Da'a'im* puts devotion (*walaya*) to the imam first among seven pillars and insists that believers keep every ritual rule (p. 372). The abolition of the law is put off to the end of time and spoken of only in esoteric writings (p. 350).
- **Sexual slander as polemic.** Halm three times reads charges of sexual licence against Isma'ilis as a stock polemic. Adversaries "strove tirelessly to defame" them as dissolute (p. 21). "Polemicists were always ready to come up with accusations of sexual misconduct" (p. 49). On the Qarmati "god" of 931, said to have ordered pederasty and incest: "caution is appropriate on this point" (p. 259).
- **The *Da'a'im*.** Al-Nu'man began as a collector and copyist of books (p. 370). He cited only the imams up to Ja'far al-Sadiq, and turned to rulings of al-Qa'im or al-Mu'izz only where no older precedent existed, for example on whether slaves may inherit and testify (pp. 370-371). Twelver jurists later called him a crypto-Twelver (p. 373). He fixed the end of Ramadan by count, not by sighting, which set off clashes with Sunni judges (p. 373), and in Fustat in 969 (p. 415).
- **Manners at court.** Al-Nu'man's *Himma* tells the believer to appear before the imam freshly washed, with clean hair, best clothes and the best perfume, standing with eyes down, speaking only when asked, and smiling rather than laughing (p. 351). Halm notes that "the people of the court are always announced by a penetrating fragrance before they become visible" (p. 339).

### 1.7 Ceremony, festivals, music, punishment

- **The curtain.** In Salamya the da'i Abu l-'Abbas took his vow before a curtain. It was then drawn back to show "the Imam, the Mahdi and the Qa'im" (pp. 58-59; *Sira Ja'far*, 122, secondhand). At al-Mansuriyya the imam "sits for the people" on a throne, "when the curtain-drawer literally uncovers him" (p. 351).
- **Hidden caliphs.** Al-Qa'im stayed hidden in al-Mahdiyya throughout his reign and left his palace gate on horseback only twice (p. 278).
- **Insignia.** Throne, crown, canopy and parasol (pp. 351-352). The parasol was carried by a rider who kept it at the ruler's height. Ibn Hammad heard that the Byzantine ruler of Sicily (*sic*) had the same, and may have given it to the Fatimids (p. 352; secondhand). Halm thinks it more likely that all tenth-century courts drew on a shared late-antique ceremonial (pp. 352-353). Proskynesis was introduced, banned by al-Mansur and allowed again by al-Mu'izz; al-Nu'man defended it as reverence, not worship (pp. 351-352).
- **Festivals.** On the two great feasts the imam left the palace city to preach on the open prayer-ground. At the feast of sacrifice he wore red and slaughtered camels himself, then fed the palace (pp. 220, 354).
- **The circumcision feast of 962.** Boys across the empire were circumcised in one month. In the palace court, people from Sind distracted the boys with antics, "and afterwards they accompanied the ones who had already been circumcised to their houses with music". Gifts were graded by the father's rank (pp. 402-403; *Majalis*, secondhand).
- **Music as a charge against Baghdad.** In a sermon at Alexandria in 915, al-Qa'im attacked "the harem household of the child-caliph in Baghdad" and its governors, who taxed "singers, musicians, thieves and falsifiers of weights" (pp. 204-205).
- **Parades and display:**
  - In Abbasid Fustat in 920, the captured Fatimid admiral and 117 captains "were led on camelback around al-Fustat" (p. 208).
  - In 946 a beardless youth who had claimed to be a prophet was "led about the camp" and then flayed (p. 317).
  - In 947 Abu Yazid's corpse was carried on camelback through every city, with trained apes beating it (p. 322).
  - In 960 two captured rebels were shown on rotating platforms that al-Mu'izz designed. They were held in the palace gate through Ramadan (p. 400).
  - In 971 Jawhar had 3,000 heads carried around the streets of Fustat (p. 416).
- **No *mukhannath* material.** The book has nothing on *mukhannathun* or effeminate entertainers. Court entertainment appears only in the Sind performers of 962, and in al-Mu'izz's denial that he spent his days "drinking wine and singing" (p. 367).

### 1.8 Byzantium, captives and Sicily

- **Captives in bulk:**
  - 11,000 from Oria in Apulia, with a Byzantine *patrikios*, in 925 (p. 237);
  - 18,000 from the Adriatic coast in 930 (p. 238);
  - "thousands" of Sicilians carried to North Africa after the revolt of 937-941 (p. 288).
- **Ransom and exchange** were routine on the Byzantine frontier. In 908 Baghdad ransomed 3,000 Muslim men and women, and in 938 the Byzantines offered 6,300 men and women at the Taurus (p. 235). In 961 a Greek named Socrates came to Palermo to redeem prisoners (p. 396). The Fatimid court poet Sa'dun had himself been a Byzantine captive (p. 235).
- **Tribute and truces:**
  - Byzantium paid for peace in Calabria from 931 or 932 (p. 238). Halm notes this let the caliph claim the tribute that Qur'an 9:29 demands.
  - The truce was renewed in 946, 948, 958 and 967 (pp. 316, 333, 395, 407).
  - In 958 a *patrikios* brought jewelled gold and silver vessels, brocade, silk, horses, and Muslim prisoners from the east. Al-Mu'izz refused a truce without limit, citing the law of jihad. He rebuked the envoy for an "anthropomorphic" phrase (*tashbih*), probably calling Christ the Son of God (pp. 403-404).
- **A captive at work.** The *patrikios* Nicetas, captured in 965, spent two years in al-Mahdiyya copying the homilies of St Basil. He later gave the manuscript to a church in Constantinople; it is now Paris, BnF, grec 497 (p. 407).
- **Asylum at Reggio.** The truce of 952 made the Byzantines accept a mosque in Reggio where Muslim prisoners "no matter whether they had apostasized" could claim asylum (p. 335). A Byzantine squadron destroyed it in 957 (p. 395).
- **Other Rum at court.** The Jewish physician Moshe ben El'azar from Oria served al-Mansur, al-Mu'izz and al-'Aziz (p. 358). Christian deportees from Sardinia were settled at Sardaniya, where the court camped in 972 (p. 418).

### 1.9 What carried over to Cairo

- **The plan.** Al-Mahdiyya had two palaces, for the imam and the heir, "facing one another, separated by a square"; "an arrangement which will be found again afterwards in Cairo" (p. 218). Cairo, first called al-Mansuriyya, repeated the names of the North African palace city: the Zawila Gate, the Victory Gate (*Bab al-Futuh*), and al-Azhar (pp. 346, 415-416). Zawila was named after the Black guard regiments, called "the people from Zawila" after the slave market in the Fezzan, and the name moved to Cairo with them (p. 220).
- **The arrival, 973.** Al-Mu'izz entered the palace city on 10 June 973 with his sons, brothers, uncles and "the other descendants of al-Mahdi", bringing the coffins of his three predecessors. The city was renamed al-Qahira al-Mu'izziyya (p. 420). The princes kept under arrest by Jawdhar thus became Cairo's princely household.
- **Institutions that continued:**
  - government by petition and rescript (p. 354);
  - Slav and Black slave corps loyal to the imam in person (pp. 220, 278);
  - eunuch intendants of treasury, *tiraz* and arsenal (p. 339);
  - the curtain-drawer and parasol-bearer (pp. 279, 351);
  - the *majalis al-hikma* and the *Ta'wil* read in the palace (p. 376);
  - the *Da'a'im* as state law (p. 372);
  - the Isma'ili reckoning of Ramadan (p. 415).
  
  Halm shows each of these only up to 973. Their form in 1066 needs Egyptian sources.

## 4. Corrections / answers to open questions in other notes

Do not edit these files from this note. The changes they should get:

**`isl_tolino_eunuchs.md`**
- §1.3, Jawdhar's purchase of a blessing: "He paid 20 dinars" (T2018 p. 256). Halm quotes the *Sira*: "Give me ten dinars" (Halm p. 346; *Sira Jawdhar* 37f.). Check Tolino's source before using either figure. Halm's account also has no later blessing from the Mahdi: the blessing passes by the sale itself.
- §1.3, Ja'far's wedding-night dance: Tolino has him put "the thrown veil on his head". Halm has "the bloodied bedsheet ... raised over his head" (p. 61; *Sira Ja'far* 108ff.). Prefer Halm, or read the *Sira*.
- §1.3, "Halm suggests ... an intrigue of Jawdhar, the prince's mother and his wet-nurse" (T2018 p. 257 n. 67). **Confirmed**, with names: the mother was the slave Karima and the wet-nurse Salaf (Halm p. 311).
- §1.3, "freed by al-Mansur": confirmed and dated. He received his manumission document on 15 August 947 (Halm pp. 321-322).
- §1.4, *fata* "not found ... a Western usage" (T2023 p. 79): consistent. Add that it was the dynasty's own usage in Ifriqiya before 973, the epithet of Slav freedmen of officer rank (Halm p. 279). Halm never applies it to Jawdhar, whom he calls *ustadh*.
- §2 row 4 (re-casting Sawsan): add the curtain-drawer alternative (row 4 above). The office is attested for the caliph, held by a Slav (Halm p. 351 n. 58).
- §2 row 6 (tutor's bond): **confirmed** for Jawdhar ("his pupil al-Qa'im", Halm p. 347; assigned to the heir, p. 279).
- §2 row 7 (adoption barred): consistent with Halm p. 363. A eunuch's granted land reverted to the ruler as an ownerless estate.

**`isl_el_azhari_queens_eunuchs.md`**
- "Jawdhar, a Saqlabi from Spain" (El-Azhari p. 255): Halm gives no origin, and treats "Slav" and "Rum" as labels of type, not of provenance (Halm pp. 152, 339). Mark "from Spain" as El-Azhari's claim, *unverified*.
- "A dynasty run through eunuchs 'from Jawdhar ... to Jawhar'" (p. 254): Halm never calls Jawhar a eunuch, and mentions "the wife of Jawhar the Slav" (Halm p. 363). Do not cast Jawhar as a eunuch on El-Azhari's phrase alone.

- Kafur: consistent. Regent from 946, governor 966, died April 968 (Halm p. 409).

**`isl_cortese_calderini_fatimid_women.md`**
- §1 "Religious education", "The da'i al-Malusi taught women through jewellery, spinning and dress (p. 30)": this is Aflah b. Harun al-Malusi, chief da'i and judge of Raqqada after 911. His colleague Ibn al-Haytham reported his method (Halm p. 375; Stern, *Studies*, 103). Add that women attended *majalis al-hikma* before 909 (Halm p. 106) and Aflah's sessions "for men and women" (p. 153).
- "Women's voices ... al-Nu'man's household ... asked 'not to be scattered' (p. 79)": Halm gives the context. The women were slave girls from booty, given by al-Mu'izz to al-Nu'man, who passed them to his sons. The request was made while al-Mansuriyya was still short of housing (Halm p. 341; *Majalis*, pars. 280-281).
- §1 Rasad: Halm adds the North African precedent for succession made in the harem. Al-Mansur was raised by the eunuch Jawdhar, his mother Karima and his wet-nurse Salaf (Halm p. 311). This supports C&C's reading of Rasad as working an *old* system (C&C p. 113).

**`later_starts/mukhannathun_fatimid_cairo.md`**
- §1.2 (*tashhir*): add precedents. In Abbasid Fustat in 920 a Fatimid admiral and 117 captains were led on camelback around the city (Halm p. 208). Fatimid display punishments followed in 946, 947, 960 and 971 (Halm pp. 317, 322, 400, 416). None of these involves a *mukhannath*.
- §2.3 (*Ta'wil al-Da'a'im*): add the setting. The *Ta'wil* is the written text of al-Nu'man's Friday esoteric sessions in a palace hall, authorised by the imam, who took the initiates' oath in person (Halm p. 376). The "ranks of adornment" reading was court-sanctioned teaching, not a private gloss.
- §2.4 (cycles of crackdown): add the Fatimid bans on loud lament and on mourning women (al-Qa'im; al-Mansur; al-Mu'izz in 960) (Halm p. 244 n. 367; p. 401). Whether they held in Egypt is *unverified*.
- §4.4 (openings) and §4.5 (guardrails): add that an argument from inner truth against outer law is the heresy the Fatimids themselves punished (Halm pp. 247-249). Treat sexual-licence charges against Isma'ilis as stock polemic (Halm pp. 21, 49, 259).
