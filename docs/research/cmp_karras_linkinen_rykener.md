## 1. The argument in brief

- **Origin (p. 111).** Karras says that if she rewrote the 1990s articles with David Boyd she would call Rykener "transgender" rather than "transvestite". She had "thought of making the case via fiction" but, with no experience of fiction, "returned to the archives". Linkinen had already written a conference paper making the same case and co-wrote a puppet play, *John-Eleanor* (Turku, 2011).
- **Terms.** "Transgender" in Stryker's broad sense, any "movement away from an initially assigned gender position" (p. 111 n. 2). Rykener's life was "transgender-like", after Bennett's "lesbian-like" (p. 112). The authors use ze/hir throughout (p. 112 n. 6).
- **What the record shows (pp. 112–113).**
  - Anna taught hir the "vice" "in the manner of a woman"; Elizabeth Brouderer "first dressed him in women's clothing" (p. 112, quoting Boyd and Karras). It suited Rykener to blame others, so this cannot be taken at face value.
  - **Women's work was not a way to earn more.** Women's wages were lower, so "it is unlikely that ze chose to live as a woman because of these earning opportunities". Embroidery and tavern work were what was open "once ze had chosen to wear women's clothing not just on the street as a prostitute, but daily", moving to Oxfordshire "where ze was not known and could pass" (pp. 112–113).
  - Rykener also confessed to sex "as a man with many women" (p. 113).
- **Limits (pp. 113–114).** Medieval people did not separate sex, gender and orientation. They recognised intersex bodies and bodies with the "other" desire only as exceptions, "monsters because they did not fit" (p. 113, citing Cadden). Rykener "most likely would have thought of hirself as either a man acting as a woman, or as a woman; but we do not know which" (p. 114).
- **Beyond clothing (p. 115).** In the receptive role a man could already be called effeminate. But Rykener "went beyond the gender-crossing medieval people saw as entailed in hir sexual behavior" by wearing women's clothes and doing women's work. Bennett and McSheffrey (2014) found other London cross-dressing cases, almost all women dressed as men and one other man, and none recorded beyond clothes or hair. They read those as temporary, not attempts to pass. Rykener "may not have been unique; but ze is unique to our knowledge" (p. 115).
- **Passing (p. 116).** Rykener "wished to pass as a woman". The record mentions "three unsuspecting scholars" in Oxford who slept with hir "often", so "hir passing was truly effective".
- **Against Dinshaw (p. 116).** Dinshaw tied Rykener's choices to "queer desires". The authors object that desire for men is not what makes someone a woman, and suggest "it is even possible that Rykener was the medieval equivalent of a lesbian transwoman".
- **Two fictions (pp. 117–121).**
  - The puppet play deliberately "avoided depicting Rykener as a victim", making "a survivor's story in which Rykener becomes the hero/ine of hir own life" (p. 119). It ends as an interrogation, not a trial, with Rykener leaving to "carry on with hir life" (p. 118).
  - Bruce Holsinger's novel *A Burnable Book* (2014) has an "Eleanor/Edgar Rykener" whose pronoun follows hir clothes. He calls hir a "swerver", a word "Holsinger seems to have invented (it does not appear in the *Middle English Dictionary*)" (p. 119).
- **Conclusion (p. 121).** "Rykener was caught in a binary because of the society in which ze lived." Through clothing ze staked out "not a middle position, but a flexible one that went back and forth". The authors call hir "a survivor working within the limits of the possible".
- **Unread lead.** Goldberg 2014 argues the record was "a critical allegory against Richard II"; it came too late for them (p. 121 n. 36).

Brewer (an imprint of Boydell & Brewer), Cambridge. Full volume subtitle: *Essays in Honor of E. Jane Burns*. It is now read; the TARA repository copy is no longer needed.

- **`medtrans_henningsen_eleanor.md`.** See row 1 above.
- **Date.** Karras and Linkinen give the arrest as 1394 (p. 112), matching our files. Wingard's 1395 is the roll's date (see `cmp_wingard_trans_middle_ages.md`).
