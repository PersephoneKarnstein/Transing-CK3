## 1. The argument in brief

- **Bhakti as embodiment.** The Tamil Śiva hymnists (7th–9th c.: Campantar, Appar, Cuntarar, "the three", *mūvar*) present bhakti as a "theology of embodiment". Worship is grounded in place, in pilgrimage and in community (pp. 6, 9).
- **Each later age re-embodied the hymns.**
  - The Chola imperial temples (10th–12th c.) turned the hymns into **paid liturgy**, and the saints into **images and stories** (p. 10).
  - The *Periya Purāṇam* (c. 1135) "embodied" bhakti as the 63 saints (*nāyaṉmār*) (pp. 10, 117).
  - The 14th-c. Śaiva Siddhānta philosophers made the hymns a forerunner of their system (pp. 10–11, 154–155).
- **Against romantic readings.** Prentiss rejects Stein's "religion of peaceful peasants". She also rejects any thesis of "devotion to female tutelary deities" in the hymns (p. 93).

## 2. What matters for the design

### 2.1 The singers of the hymns were a male, hereditary, paid office

- **Pallava era.** A Tiruvallam (Bilvanātheśvara) inscription, probably of Nandivarman III (c. 852), sets aside paddy for "the singers of the Tirupatiyam". Their service is called "great service" (*palapaṇi*) (pp. 88–89).
  - The singers are a group of "partners or even family" (*uḷḷiṭṭār*), not the poets themselves (p. 89).
  - Other inscriptions claimed for the Pallavas are actually Chola (p. 88).
- **Early Chola.** At Sembiyaṉ Mahādevī's Tirunallam temple (Uttama Chola's reign), the grant lists two *tirupatiyam* singers.
  - They get 90 *kalam* of paddy each, against 120 for the priest (p. 99).
  - They get houses in the temple quarter (p. 99).
  - They rank in the "middle" of temple space, between the sanctum and the periphery (p. 99).
  - Parāntaka I's Andanallur list has one singer (p. 99).
- **Rājarāja I's Thanjavur temple**, inscription of his 29th regnal year (pp. 101–102):
  - forty-eight singers and two drummers, paid in paddy from the treasury;
  - each singer is named, some after temples and some after the three poets, and each is given a **second name ending in -Civaṉ** (Śiva);
  - the post passes to a relative at death or departure; if no relative is qualified, the group (*nyāyattār*) chooses;
  - Prentiss: the office "had come to resemble a jati", likely tied to the Vēḷāḷa caste (pp. 101–102).
- **Women's songs are a separate track.** These singers are men. The temple women's purchased right to sing the *Tiruvempāvai* (Orr) is the women's track.
- **The canon story.** The 14th-c. *Tirumuṟaikaṇṭa Purāṇam* says Nampi set the three poets' hymns to tunes "with the help of a female musician" (p. 144).

### 2.2 A male Śaiva hymnist sings as the girl

- **Appar 6.25.7** (*Tēvāram*, 7th c.). An *akam* poem in the voice of a girl (*talaivi*) "mad for Him". She leaves her parents, forgets her own name and lays her head at the Lord's feet (pp. 53–54).
- **Appar 6.13.2** (Tiruppurampayam), in Umāpati's 14th-c. anthology. "He counts my shining glass bangles one by one while I sleep" (p. 168). Prentiss calls it the *nāyaka–nāyakī bhāva* genre, "in which the poet is the female lover to God" (p. 168, note 4.4).
- **Appar 6.13.6** (same shrine, same anthology). The Lord comes in sleep with "half His form as the lady", announcing "I have a mixed body". The speaker wakes frightened (p. 168).
  - Prentiss notes that the Tamil phrase is obscure; one translator takes it as a place-name (note 4.2, p. 168).
  - The god's own half-female body is a stock Śaiva image (Ardhanārī).
- **So the Śaiva column has more than Māṇikkavācakar.** Appar, one of "the three", already sings as the girl in the 7th century, and a 14th-c. Śaiva anthologist chose such a verse.

### 2.3 Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār in Prentiss

- Dated with the earliest Tamil devotional poets, 5th–6th c. "with a question mark" (p. 54). She is "almost certainly earlier than" Appar (p. 110).
- **Cuntarar's list** (*Tiruttoṇṭattokai*, 8th–9th c.): "I am the servant ... of the Demoness [*pēy*]" (p. 112).
  - In Cuntarar the word *pēy* does not specify gender. Nampi's text does (p. 234 n. 90).
- **Nampi Āṇṭār Nampi** (reign of Kulōttuṅga I, 1070–1122; p. 110).
  - She walks to Kailāsa on her head. Śiva calls "the lady" "my Mother" (p. 112).
- **The story** (summary in Appendix A note): her husband fears her intimacy with the Lord after the mango. "The lady asked to be released from her womanly body-beauty" to see the dance at Ālaṅkāṭu (p. 176 n.).
- **On the *Tiruvaruṭpayaṉ*.** Prentiss glosses the *pēy* as a "socially constituted antisocial being". Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār "pleads to Siva for the removal of her flesh (her femininity)" (p. 196, note 8.7).
  - *Prentiss's parenthesis, not the 12th-c. text's words.* See `ind_pechilis_karaikkal.md` for the verse.

### 2.4 Royal women and the imperial temple

- **Sembiyaṉ Mahādevī** (temple endowments c. 941–1001) was the foremost early Chola patron of Śiva temples.
  - She rebuilt temples in stone on sites the three poets sang (pp. 96–97).
  - A relief shows her kneeling in worship (p. 97).
  - Inscriptions present royal women "virtually exclusively as agents of religious beneficence" (p. 96, after Orr).
- **Rājarāja I turned away from the feminine.** He built his Thanjavur temple around the liṅga and an Āgamic, male priesthood, "largely eschewing" goddesses and royal women (p. 100). This is Prentiss's reading.
- **Saint images as royal piety.**
  - Rājarāja's vestibule murals show Cuntarar's call and ascent (pp. 102–105).
  - Donated bronzes include the three poets, Cuntarar's wife Paravaiyār, the king and his chief queen Lōkamahādevī (p. 106).
  - Images were "becoming increasingly important as emblems of religious salvation" (p. 108).
  - *Do not use* the Ciṟuttoṇṭar image group (a child killed and restored, pp. 107–108).

### 2.5 A 14th-c. Śaiva Siddhānta line

- **Umāpati's *Tiruvaruṭpayaṉ* 8.2** (1307): "There will not be any fruit of bliss between two women; it is experienced between a man and a woman" (p. 195).
  - It is a simile for the soul's union with grace (p. 206).
  - Couplet 8.7 counsels sitting still "until the nature of a *pēy* arises" (p. 196).

## 3. Corrections

1. **Prentiss is no source for the pāvai vow or Mārkaḻi.** Do not cite her for gate step 2.
2. **Year.** The copyright page says 1999. "Prentiss 2000" in the reading list is a citation convention; keep it, with the ISBN.
3. **Not every temple song was a woman's.** The Śaiva hymn-singers of 852–1014 were men, organised like a caste office. The women's *Tiruvempāvai* right (Orr, 12th c.) is the exception, not the rule.
4. **Rājarāja's masculinised temple** argues against making Thanjavur the faith's warmest site. It holds the four hundred women (SII 2 no. 66) *and* a cult the king built away from goddesses and queens. Both facts are useful.

## 4. Design implications

### 4.1 The cross-tradition current

- **Support.**
  - The Śaiva column now has Appar's girl-voice poems (7th c.), not only Māṇikkavācakar (8th–9th c.). The "men sing as the girl" claim holds on both sides of the sectarian line in Tamil.
  - Pallava kings "patronized many religious activities", building Śiva and Viṣṇu temples (p. 86). This fits a Kanchipuram seat (§15).
- **Risk.**
  - The Chola state cult was Śaiva and royal.
  - A crossing faith reads to Chola temple managers as a claim on a hereditary office. Use this for the sectarian friction already planned.

### 4.2 The pāvai vow gate

- **Step 2, the song.** Add a pre-step or alternative for a seeker still living as a man.
  - **"Sing among the -Civaṉ"**: a court or temple position "Singer of the Sacred Verses" (*tirupatiyam*).
  - The seeker joins the male hereditary choir. That makes her **Marked**: she has sung the girl's verses (Appar 6.25.7) in public.
  - At the vow she moves from the men's choir to the women's *Tiruvempāvai* verse she has bought (Orr). This mirrors the *araiyar* route on the Vaiṣṇava side.
- **Step 4, the dream.** Appar's bangles poem (p. 168) is in period and fits better than an invented dream: "He counts my bangles while I sleep."
  - Use it as the sign-event text, cited to Appar, *Tēvāram* 6.13.2.

### 4.3 The staff initiation

- Prentiss adds no initiation rite.
- Her singers' **renaming** ("-Civaṉ", given by the temple; p. 101) is precedent for a temple-given name at entry. That supports step 5's "a name".
- The faith's version: the seeker's male choir-name in -Civaṉ is set aside, and the staff gives a woman's name (Nanda-, Goodall) or a shrine name (Tanjavur women named after shrines, `ind_tanjavur_inscription.md`).

### 4.4 Events, modifiers, reaction lines

- **Event "The Two Singers' Houses".** The temple grant gives a singer a house in the temple quarter (p. 99). If the singer becomes a daughter, the temple managers must decide whether the house passes to her.
  - Options: petition the queen-patron (diplomacy); buy the right (gold); yield.
- **Modifier "Sung Place"** (*pāṭal peṟṟa talam*, "a place that obtains singing"; p. 90): a county with a temple the poets sang. Small piety bonus for Garland holders.
- **Reaction lines.**
  - *Śaiva Siddhānta scholar* (after 1307): "There is no fruit of bliss between two women. So Umāpati teaches; so the union is taught."
  - *Faith answer:* "Then let her be a woman before the Lord, as Appar was, counting bangles in his sleep."
  - *Faith, on the Lord's own body* (after Appar 6.13.6): "He came half a lady and said: I have a mixed body." Use with the note's caution on the phrase. Do not let it become a "third thing": the Garland reads it as the god making room for the bride, not as an in-between sex (Bhatt).
  - *Chola temple manager:* "The office passes to a kinsman. Is a daughter a kinsman?"
- **Never.**
  - Do not make the -Civaṉ singers or their Vēḷāḷa descendants (*ōtuvārs* today) into the faith.
  - No Ciṟuttoṇṭar child episode.
