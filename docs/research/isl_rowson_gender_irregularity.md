## 0. Headline finding: the al-Jahiz "realm" sentence is verbatim here

`hist_ayalon_eunuchs_caliphs.md` (headline) and beliefs.md G3 consequence 15 say that "castration transfers eunuchs from the realm of male characteristics to that of females" is not in al-Jahiz as quoted, and is a paraphrase (attributed to Karras, Marmon or Pellat). **That is wrong.** The phrase is Rowson's own translation of al-Jahiz, *Kitab al-Hayawan*, ed. Harun, 1:136 (Rowson n. 23), printed on p. 55 of this chapter. Karras almost certainly took it from here. The passage, in two short extracts:

- "What is astonishing is that, despite their transferral from the realm of male characteristics to that of females, they are not susceptible to effeminacy (*takhnith*)." (p. 55)
- "I have never seen a eunuch who was a mukhannath, or heard of such a thing ... at first glance it would appear that it should be universal among them." (p. 55)

So al-Jahiz does say castration moves eunuchs into the female realm, and **in the same breath** says eunuchs never become *mukhannathun*, while passive sex (*hulaq*) is "common" among them (p. 55). Rowson adds that al-Jahiz elsewhere treats eunuchs "anatomically" as "a third sex" (p. 55). He also lists them as a third object of desire: "Some women prefer women, others prefer men, yet others prefer eunuchs" (*Hayawan* 1:167; p. 54).

**Action:** correct beliefs.md consequence 15 and the Ayalon note's headline. The doctrine (d) anchor is now first-hand: al-Jahiz via Rowson 2003, p. 55. Ayalon's *Hayawan* I, 116 (*khidma*) passage and al-Mas'udi's refutation still stand as further anchors. Edition page numbers differ between Ayalon (I, 116, 124-125, 135) and Rowson (1:136, 1:167); both use Harun's edition, so 1:136 is the place to cite for this sentence.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **Institutionalised and salaried.** At the ninth-century caliphal court "both male and female transvestism were not only tolerated, but institutionalized, and even salaried, as forms of professional entertainment" (p. 46).
- **Two institutions, side by side.** Male *mukhannathun* (attested from the Prophet's time) and female *ghulamiyat* (from al-Amin's reign, 809-13) (pp. 46-47).
- **The *ghulamiyat* began as a queen mother's move against eunuchs.** Al-Mas'udi: when al-Amin "favored the eunuchs ... notably Kawthar", his mother Zubayda took shapely slave girls, "put turbans on their heads", bobbed their hair and dressed them in close-fitting robes and sashes, and sent them to him. He "brought them into public view", and "both elite and commons" copied the fashion (p. 47; *Muruj* 4:318).
- **What they imitated.** A *ghulamiya* is "ghulam-like". The look was an adolescent boy or a *shatir* (street rogue), not a eunuch (p. 48). Painted sidecurls and sometimes painted mustaches; no attempt to hide the breasts (pp. 48-49). "It was surely intrinsic to the appeal of the ghulamiya that she was a woman" (p. 49).
- **Imposed, not chosen.** "The ghulamiya identity was usually an imposed one, adopted at the behest of the slave girls' masters, although it could also be freely adopted" (p. 51). 'Arib, the century's most famous singer, began as one of al-Amin's *ghulamiyat* (p. 51). They were not tomboys and not lesbians: the institution "was a function of male sexual tastes, not female ones" (p. 51).
- **Masculinity as act.** Ninth-century Baghdad "defined 'masculinity,' not in terms of 'sexual object choice,' but rather of 'sexual act'" (p. 53). Boys "are like women, in their physical softness ... and most obviously in their lack of facial hair" (p. 53).
- **Eunuchs and al-Amin.** A Baghdad satire: "The former plunges into Kawthar" (p. 54). Rowson: eunuchs "shared the beardlessness and, usually, the bodily softness of boys and women". Al-Amin's taste was "more a function of availability than of taste" (pp. 54-55).
- **The Medinan summary.** Under 'Abd al-Malik and al-Walid, "a group—one might almost say a corporation—of male transvestite singers" flourished in Medina (p. 56). They played the *duff*, wore women's jewellery, were admitted to the women's quarters and worked as marriage brokers. Some were married (p. 56). The *mukhannath* was "an institutionalized irregular gender role" felt "as an identity with both personal and corporate dimensions" (p. 56).
- **Date of the castration order: 717.** Sulayman ordered "that all the mukhannathun of Medina were to be castrated" in **717** (pp. 46, 57). The reasons given are women's chastity, never homosexuality or feminine dress in itself (p. 57). "The mukhannathun survived their castration, of course" (p. 57).
- **Abbasid *mukhannathun*: wit over music.** "It is their wit, not their musicianship, that makes them valued" (p. 57). They played the *tunbur* (long-necked lute) at court and drums everywhere. A tenth-century source distinguishes "parade drums" from "mukhannaths' drums" (*tubul al-makhanith*) (p. 61). They were "notably not the object of love poetry" (p. 57).
- **'Abbada.** The son of a cook at al-Ma'mun's court, he "became a mukhannath" after his father's death. Al-Ma'mun sent him to entertain Zubayda. He was jester to al-Mutawakkil, was banished several times, and survived al-Mutawakkil's murder in 861 (pp. 57-58). He mocked 'Ali at al-Mutawakkil's parties with a pillow under his robe (p. 58). Offered a eunuch as a partner: "I don't board a ship without a rudder!" (pp. 58-59). Offered a wife if he gave up *takhannuth*: "Are you a caliph or a marriage-broker?" (p. 59).
- **Princes and the style.** Al-Amin danced all night on a hobby-horse (*kurraj*) among slave girls and *mukhannathun* with drums (p. 60). His vizier al-Fadl b. al-Rabi' set a boy to stop his son plucking his beard; the son did it in his sleep and answered with Qur'an 68:20, "as if plucked" (p. 60). The future al-Mutawakkil asked leave to grow his hair "in the style of the mukhannathun"; his brother the caliph sent a barber, who cut it, spoiled his black robe and slapped him with the hair (pp. 60-61).
- **Guile and argot.** The poet Abu l-'Atahiya began as a *mukhannath*, carrying their "provision-bag" (*zamilat al-mukhannathin*). Later he said he carried it "to learn their tricks (*kiyad*) and memorize their speech (*kalam*)" (p. 61). *Kalam* is probably an argot. Their mimicry was feared: "I will act out your mother in a skit!" (p. 62).
- **Gentleness as the other face.** Ibn Shada was nicknamed "the Mukhannath" because he "never composed abuse poetry about or defamed anyone ... the most well-mannered of people" (p. 62). The Mu'tazilites were mocked as "the mukhannathun of the Kharijites" for lacking nerve (p. 63).
- **Very little dignity.** "The mukhannathun had very little dignity, and their status ... sank noticeably under the Abbasids" (p. 63). They had "joined the ranks of the not-male—boys, who would grow out of this status, and women, who could not help it" (p. 63). The lost dignity "freed them ... to serve as clowns" (p. 63).
- **The philosophers.** Al-Kindi (contemporary of al-Mutawakkil) lists the *mukhannath* among those who rejoice in their vices. Despite "plucking his beard and decking himself out as a woman", he is "joyous and proud", counting it "the most precious and delightful of gifts" (pp. 63-64). Miskawayh (10th c.) puts it down to bodily temperament: "treatment" can succeed, and if refused, "imposition of the stipulated penalties upon him is incumbent" (p. 64).
- **Public shaming (*tashhir*).** Captives entering Bukhara (late 10th c.) were met by "mukhannathun with tambourines and spindles, rather than swords and spears" (p. 64). A baker convicted of treason was paraded behind a troupe of *mukhannathun* (p. 65). In 11th-c. Egypt a *mukhannath* was paraded for procuring (p. 65; n. 62: al-Musabbiḥī, *Akhbār Miṣr*, ed. Millward, Cairo 1980, p. 187). Another, a rich musician who kept singing slave girls, was murdered (p. 65; n. 63: same work, ed. Sayyid and Bianquis, Cairo 1978, 1:104). Notes 62–63 verified 2026-10-26; see `later_starts/mukhannathun_fatimid_cairo.md` §1.1–1.2.
- **The conclusion.** The two institutions are not parallel. The *ghulamiya* lost no prestige among slave girls. The *mukhannath* lost a great deal, having "voluntarily relinquished" his manhood (p. 65). Both "subverted the rules of a rigidly hierarchical gender structure for the delectation of those ... securely established at the top" (p. 66).
