# Hao Chunwen, "The Social Life of Buddhist Monks and Nuns in Dunhuang" (2010): design reading

**Citation (verified from the PDF):** Hao Chunwen 郝春文, "The Social Life of Buddhist Monks and Nuns in Dunhuang during the Late Tang, Five Dynasties, and Early Song," *Asia Major*, 3rd series, 23.2 (2010): 77–95. The article sums up Hao's monograph 《唐後期五代宋初敦煌僧尼的社會生活》 (Beijing: Zhongguo shehui kexue chubanshe, 1998); see p. 78 n. 2. No translator is named in the PDF. No DOI was found; the copy is from the Academia Sinica IHP site (`sources_pending/china/SOURCES.txt`).
**Read:** the whole article, pp. 77–95.
**Caution:** all of this is about *Buddhist* clergy. The Way's nüguan are Daoist. The argument here is that at the faith's seat, clergy who live at home, own land and adopt are the local norm. That makes the Way ordinary for Dunhuang, not exceptional. Galambos (2020, p. 4, Ikeda's table) puts Daoist texts at 0.3–6.1% of the four main collections, so the Way would be a small minority in a Buddhist town.

## 1. Argument

- **Most clergy did not live in their monasteries.** Many monks and nuns "lived at home with their children, uncles, nieces, nephews, and servants; they engaged in farming and other non-religious commercial activities" (p. 77). Hao adds: "many — perhaps most — monks and nuns in northwest China resided with their families at home" (p. 82).
- **Circulars prove it (P.4981, pp. 83–85).** A monastery damaged by flood had to summon its own monks by a passed-round circular. The list includes laypeople by nickname (Chou'er 醜兒 "Ugly", Gounu 苟奴 "Dog Servant"). Officers bring an axe and basin and the "dispersed assembly" brings shovels. The last two to arrive get seven strokes of the stick, and absentees are fined a jar of wine. Hao notes these penalties match those of lay societies (p. 85).
- **Monks kept their land and their sons.**
  - The monk Zhang Yueguang 張月光 divided the family property with his brothers and kept a house, 48 *mu* of farmland, an orchard and waste land (P.3744, p. 86).
  - In 852 he exchanged land "with father and sons", meaning his three sons (P.3394, p. 86).
  - Hao's summary: "monastics are referred to by their secular surnames" (p. 86).
- **Nuns too.**
  - The nun Wubianhua 無邊花 "lived mainly with her secular family" (P.3730, p. 87).
  - A Dharma-Vinaya master Zhang lived with his daughter (P.3578, p. 87).
- **Adoption (P.3410, pp. 87–88).** The will of the monk Chong'en 崇恩, secular surname Suo, a senior saṅgha official c. 825–875:
  - The adopted daughter Wachai 娲柴 "came from infancy as my adopted daughter; she has never gone against my wishes".
  - He leaves her the maid he bought: "She should no longer be a low person."
  - He also leaves cattle and wheat to the monk Wenxin, who "has known Chong'en's family affairs inside and out."
  - Correction for the reading list: Chong'en is a monk, not a nun.
- **The summer retreat needed room-finding (P.6005v, p. 88).** Saṅgha officials ordered monasteries to house, for the retreat, the monks and nuns "who normally live at home".
- **Terms.** Clergy living out are the *sanzhong* 散眾, "dispersed assembly". Living with one's family is *gonghuo* 共活 or *tonghuo* 同活 (p. 89).
- **Inside the nunnery, every woman kept her own kitchen.**
  - **Haijue 海覺 (P.3730, p. 89).** She and "her secular sister" became nuns and tried to live at home, farming. Hard times came, so they asked to enter the nunnery and set up their own kitchen.
  - **Guangxian 光顯 at Puguang Nunnery (S.542v, p. 90).** A new nun built her own kitchen, diverted the water to it and refused to share. The sister nuns petitioned the saṅgha.
  - **Dasheng Nunnery** (p. 91) had 173 nuns but one cauldron and two stoves; Jingtu Monastery, with 22 monks, had two cauldrons and three stoves. Meals were private.
- **Numbers (p. 92).** Dunhuang had 20,000–25,000 people, 13–17 monasteries and nunneries, and 406–1,500 registered clergy (average c. 1,000, about 5%). At Dunhuang monks "normally paid taxes and took part in corvée labor — and some even performed military service."
- **A wage hierarchy (pp. 92–95).**
  - There were five ranks: monk, nun, probationary nun (*shichani*), novice monk, novice nun.
  - Clergy were paid *chenli* 儭利 ("dakṣiṇā wages") once every three years, plus fees for temple and lay services.
  - The top fifth could be rich, and the lowest novices got 15 *chi* of cloth a year.
  - A novice petitions for full ordination because he "utterly lack[s] holy accomplishments" (p. 94).
