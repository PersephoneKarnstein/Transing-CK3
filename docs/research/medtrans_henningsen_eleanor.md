**Primary source:** Corporation of London Records Office, Plea and Memoranda Roll A34, m. 2 (1395). It records the examination of 11 December 1394 before Mayor John Fressh.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **The case.** "John Britby of the county of York and John Rykener, calling [herself] Eleanor, having been detected in women's clothing" were brought before the Mayor of London (p. 249). They had been found in Soper's Lane committing "that detestable, unmentionable and ignominious vice."
- **Thesis.** Scholars have read the case as sodomy and male transvestism. Henningsen argues that "Rykener ought to be understood as a transgender woman" because she lived and worked as a woman for periods of her life and "other people in her social milieu accepted her as such" (p. 250).
- **She named herself in a hostile court.** She "insists on 'calling [herself] Eleanor' even within a hostile juridical context" (p. 250). By this "she strategically—and perhaps even defiantly—inscribes herself into the historical record as a woman" (p. 251).
- **Women's work.** She worked six weeks as a **tapster** at the Swan in Burford, and five weeks in Oxford "in women's clothing and calling himself Eleanor, worked as an embroideress" (pp. 251-252). Sewing was "the quintessential" women's labour (p. 252). She made "no mention of doing any form of 'men's' work" (p. 260).
- **Women made her a woman with her.** A certain Anna taught her; "a certain Elizabeth Brouderer first dressed him in women's clothing" and called her Eleanor (p. 252). "In modern terms, we might say that Anna and Elizabeth helped Eleanor socially transition" (p. 252). The tavern-keeper John Clerk hired her as a woman.
- **Pronouns in the record.** Of 35 pronouns, "two are feminine (her), thirteen are male ... and twenty are indiscriminate or missing" (p. 251 n. 6). The scribe was "unclear about how to gender Rykener." Britby's own testimony slides from masculine to feminine (p. 261).
- **Woman as a series (Iris Young).** Women are a collective "without identifying common attributes that all women have" (p. 256). People belong to a series through a shared orientation toward objects (clothes, pronouns, the sexual division of labour), like people at a bus stop, who share no essence.
- **Gender labor (Jane Ward).** "The affective and bodily efforts invested in giving gender to others" (p. 257). It has three forms. **Alliance**: Anna, Elizabeth and John Clerk. **Being "the girl" / "the man"**: her clients approach her "as he would a woman" (p. 260). **Forgetting**: partners set aside her body to know her as a woman.
- **Legal liminality.** She was apparently never charged. A whore was "first and foremost a sinful woman," and a man who took money did not fit the charge. Sodomy charges fell mainly on the penetrating partner (p. 262).
- **Sex with women.** She "often had sex as a man with many nuns and also ... with many women" (p. 263). Henningsen reads this as possibly "a bisexual or lesbian transgender woman" (p. 264). He argues a trans woman may "derive pleasure from using her penis" and still be a woman (p. 264 n. 45).
- **Against "transgender-like" and "via fiction."** Karras and Linkinen later called her a "transgender person" or "transgender-like" and considered and turned down making the case "via fiction" (Karras and Linkinen 2016; see `cmp_karras_linkinen_rykener.md`; Henningsen's summary reads it as a proposal) (pp. 253-254). Henningsen: that "replicates the logic whereby transgender people, especially transgender women, are frequently ... figured as pretenders or as living 'fictional' lives" (p. 254).
- **Not an "ahistorical equivalency"** (p. 252, citing DeVun and Tortorici). We write about medieval "women" although the category fits imperfectly, and the same can be done with "trans".
- **Agency under constraint.** As a trans woman, Eleanor "would not have had access to either marriage or motherhood," and so may not have had a choice about sex work (p. 259 n. 34).

## 2. What it changes in the design

**Relevance overall:** high. This is the best-documented medieval trans woman inside CK3's timeline (1394), and the argument maps directly onto the recognition mechanics.

### Methodological cautions (should shape how the mod presents history)

- **Claiming historical figures.** Four readings of Rykener are in print: male transvestite (Karras and Boyd 1995/96), "transgender-like" or genderqueer (Karras and Linkinen 2016, using ze/hir), trans woman (Henningsen), and satire (Goldberg 2014, p. 250 n. 2). The mod should present **her own act**, that she called herself Eleanor, and let that carry the claim. Loc should say what the record says ("she gave her name as Eleanor"), not "Eleanor Rykener, a trans woman of 1394." Henningsen's warning cuts against the mod as well: fiction is exactly where trans lives get filed as make-believe. So cite the roll in the event text (a tooltip line "Plea and Memoranda Roll A34, 1395") to mark her as real, not invented.
- **Not an "ahistorical equivalency".** No modern vocabulary (trans, transition, dysphoria) in her event. Use the record's own terms and the faith's.
- **Sexual content.** The roll is frank, and much of the scholarship dwells on her clients. Keep her event about the name, the work and the court. Do **not** list her clients or make jokes about priests from the record. That would repeat the sodomy-file reading Henningsen argues against.
- **Later cases** (Thomas(ine) Hall 1629, Mary Jones 1836, Mary Ann Waters 1851) fall outside CK3's timeline and are noted only for completeness. Hall is better read as intersex, and the mod's Marked seed should not be applied to intersex figures.
