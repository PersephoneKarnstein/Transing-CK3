## 1. The record in brief

- **The court.** The Mayor, John Fressh, and the Aldermen of the City of London, on 11 December in the eighteenth year of Richard II (1394). The two were "brought here before" them (*ducti fuerunt hic coram*) (pp. 461–462). The roll's heading is dated 1395 (Henningsen); the OCR of the heading reads "1385", which is an OCR error.
- **The arrest.** City officers (*per quosdam dicte civitatis ministros*) found the two by night, between eight and nine, on a stall in Soper's Lane, "last Sunday". They were held in prison until the hearing (*carcere vero mancipati hucusque*) (pp. 461–462).
- **The procedure.** A "separate examination" of each (*pro seperali examinatione*). John Britby of Yorkshire spoke first. He said he had approached her in Cheapside "thinking he was a woman" (*ipsumque mulierem fore suspicantem*). Rykener confirmed his account. She was then asked who had taught her, for how long, where, "and with what persons, masculine or feminine." She answered under oath, swearing "willingly on [her] soul" (*in animam suam sponte iuravit*) (pp. 461–463).
- **She came to court dressed as a woman.** The Latin says she was "brought here in woman's clothing" (*in veste muliebri hic adductus*) (pp. 461, 463).
- **Her name, in the Latin.** The record opens *Johannes Rykener, se Elianoram nominans veste muliebri detectus*. In *se Elianoram nominans* ("naming self Eleanor") the reflexive *se* has no gender; the "[himself]" in the translation is the editors' supplement. The masculine is carried by *detectus* ("detected"), a participle that agrees with *Johannes* (p. 461). In Oxford she worked as an embroideress "calling [herself] Eleanor" (*nominans ipsum Alianoram*), where *ipsum* is masculine (p. 462). The editors' note 12 (p. 464): the Latin uses the feminine for her only twice, both in indirect speech. They render the indeterminate forms as masculine.
- **A wife's standing.** When a rector demanded back two gowns she had taken, she said she "was the wife of a certain man" and that her husband would sue him if he pressed for them (pp. 462–463).
- **Her movements and work.** Five weeks in Oxford as an embroideress before Michaelmas; six weeks in Burford as a tapster at the Swan for John Clerk; a stay in Beaconsfield; back to London (pp. 462–463). This is already in the Henningsen note.

## 4. Design implications

- **No new event.** The edition sharpens the two planned ones.
- **"A Woman Calling Herself Eleanor"** (1390–1400): add the line "She came before the Mayor in her own dress." Its ending stays open, because the record's does.
- **"The Mayor's Problem"** (Catholic city court, c. 1300–1453): three options.
  - (a) Let her go: small piety loss.
  - (b) Invent a charge: tyranny.
  - (c) **Send the case to the bishop**: no cost, and the case leaves the event chain. This is the clerk's real exit, and it shows the jurisdiction split in the record.
- **Hostile record loc**: "Johannes Rykener, *se Elianoram nominans*." Tooltip: "Naming herself Eleanor. The Latin does not say 'himself'; the clerk said that with another word."
- Nothing here bears on the Jewish sect.
