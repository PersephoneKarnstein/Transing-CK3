# Bey, *Cistem Failure* (2022): guardrails for Badenya

**Citation.** Marquis Bey, *Cistem Failure: Essays on Blackness and Cisgender*. Asterisk: Gender, Trans-, and All That Comes After (series eds. Susan Stryker, Eliza Steinbock and Jian Neo Chen). Durham and London: Duke University Press, 2022. ISBN 978-1-4780-1580-2 (hardcover), 978-1-4780-1844-5 (paperback), 978-1-4780-2303-6 (ebook). LCCN 2021047318.

**Sources read.** The whole book, in full: preface, acknowledgments, seven essays, notes, bibliography and index (`scratchpad/books/bey_cistem.txt`, 6,277 lines). Cited below as **CF l.N**, by line of that text file. The companion book is cited as **BTF l.N** (`bey_btf.txt`).

**Quotes.** Every quote is verbatim, 25 words or fewer, and was checked by script against the cited lines (soft hyphens and line-break hyphens removed, running heads skipped). Everything else is paraphrase.

**Pronouns.** Bey writes that they use they/them "for now", but: "I am, ultimately, pronoun indifferent" (CF l.876–877) and "Just don't gender me" (l.880–881). This note uses "Bey" and they/them. Bey describes themself as nonbinary in relation, "not to say I 'am' nonbinary" (l.873–876), and as read by others as a cis Black man. That is how they are described here, and no further.

**Purpose and limits.** This note uses the same method as `book_bey_black_trans_feminism.md`. The book is read for the racialised tropes about Black women, Black men, Black trans people and Africa that the game could reproduce, with a rule against each. **It is not evidence about medieval West Africa.** Its archives are the United States, from the 18th century to 2020: Jim Crow bathrooms, J. Marion Sims, high-school football, Philadelphia vernacular, cartoons. Its central thesis is that cisgender is a property of whiteness, and that "Black people can't be cisgender" in some sense. That thesis concerns the modern Atlantic racial order. It cannot be carried back onto the Sahel of 867–1453 (§5).

**Left out on purpose.**
- Private people. The book names or initials friends, relatives, a coach, a former girlfriend, a man who pressed sex on the teenage Bey, and an email and text correspondent "D" (e.g. CF l.115–120, l.1733, l.3872, l.4342–4346, l.4754–4805). None of them is named or quoted here.
- Sexual coercion (l.3862–3902) and an anecdote about a teammate (l.1769–1772). Not used.
- See §6 for content warnings.

**Relation to existing notes.** `book_heaney_feminism_against_cisness.md` §2.7 reads Bey's "Faceless" (on confession) and sides with Bhatt (A3). The essay "Notes on (Trans)Gender" here (l.2571–2591) restates that argument; Heaney row 4 already covers it, and this note adds nothing to it. The companion note covers Black women seen only through violence, the "mammy" figure, Africa as spirit, the golden past, fugitivity, the punchline and deadnaming. This note covers what is distinctive to *Cistem Failure*: Black womanhood as "not-quite-woman", Black masculinity, the claim that "Black people can't be cisgender", Black vernacular, colonial-origin claims, medical display, and family and inheritance. Rule numbers refer to `docs/faith_design.md` §16.

## 2. Key concepts, in Bey's terms

- **Cistem.** The gender binary as a system that hides its own making. Its "failure" is the aim (preface, l.187–253).
- **Cis as whiteness.** "Gender is always a racial arrangement" (CF l.1208–1209). "cisgender is irrevocably, fundamentally antiblack" (l.1216–1217). Bey then softens the slogan: "So maybe it's not quite 'Black people can't be cisgender.' It is, rather, that blackness undermines the embedded coherency" (l.3014–3016). Bey is clear that this is not a claim about Black people's attitudes: "it is not because I believe that black people are somehow more 'accepting,'" and "they, we, are not, I don't think" (l.2240–2243).
- **Made, not born.** "I was not born cis; I was made, diligently, maliciously, cis" (l.1122–1123). Bey rejects "born this way" narratives (l.1058) and the confession of a gender (l.2571–2591; "Confess your gender, again and again, or you will be deemed deceptive", l.2579–2580).
- **"Trans" as prefix and politics,** open to anyone who misaligns. Following Wilchins: "you don't have to be transexual to be a Menace" (l.3409) and "We need a movement without identities" (l.3430–3431). Bey extends Blackness the same way: "everyone whom blackness claims, which is to say everyone, can claim blackness" (l.3025–3026).
- **Gender abolition as coalition:** "Gender abolition is about coalition for me; it is about a refusal of individuation" (l.5053–5054). "Gender is the harm" (l.5069–5070). One can work against sexism without settling who is a woman: "We need not settle 'woman' or even the parameters of 'gender' in order to advance a politics" (l.5071–5072).
- **Materialist moment:** "The oppression created the class of women, not the other way around" (l.5092–5093). This is the Wittig, Bhatt and Heaney line.
- **Kinship over family.** Slavery voided the legal family: "Family, as a term given through the juridical, was rendered evacuated of meaning in the context of blackness" (l.3999–4002). From this Bey concludes that "there is salvation in reformulating family around kinship" (l.4007). Inherited lines are to be refused: "To fail at our inheritance is to spit in our ancestors' faces" (l.3599–3604). But violent ancestors cannot be disowned: "They can, though, be disavowed" (l.3616–3617).

## 4. Where Bey bears on the mod's own framing

**4.1 "Woman" as a violence, and trans women's womanhood as a "lusher prison."** This is the sharpest disagreement with the mod.
- *Bey's position.* "'Woman,' categorically, is also a violence" (CF l.3773–3774), and "The aim is not just for men to 'become' women" (l.3771–3772). Discussing trans women who find liberation in womanhood, Bey writes: "I am uninterested in more roomy, responsive, lusher prisons" (l.5469–5470), and "I am still hesitant to clap for the trans woman's 'liberation' if she chooses a differently textured womanhood" (l.5474–5481).
- *Bey's own qualifications.* Bey adds at once: "This is not to denigrate the chosen prison" (l.5474–5475). Elsewhere they grant that names "can be a home for us" (l.5426–5429) and that "gender is a kind of prison at one time and a kind of liberation at another" (l.5450–5452, reporting a friend).
- *The mod's position.* The mod holds A3: daughters are women, not a third thing. Bhatt's reply to Bey is in the Heaney note (§2.7): refusing a category does not stop others forcing you into it, and a trans woman forced out of womanhood is degendered, not freed.
- *Where they agree.* On the cause, Bey and the mod agree: "The oppression created the class of women" (l.5092–5093) is Bhatt's and Wittig's materialism. They part on the remedy. Bey would abolish the class at once. The mod's faiths make room inside it, in a world (867–1453) where outside womanhood lay degendering, not freedom.
- *Verdict.* No change to A3. Bey's universal claim, that anyone can claim trans as they can claim blackness (l.3025–3026), runs into rule 18 ("every woman was made too", never "every woman is one of us"). The rule stays.

**4.2 Inheritance and ancestors.** Bey refuses the paternal line and its wealth. Rejecting cisgender "means we must reject, therefore, the familial line" (l.3975–3976). Bey would spit in the faces of violent ancestors, while conceding they can only be "disavowed" (l.3599–3617).
- *Where this cuts against Badenya.* Badenya is built on inheritance. The heir pays "the sister's share". Adopted daughters "inherit as daughters of the blood". A jeli sings the ruler's line. The mother's name goes first.
- *Bey's side.* Inheritance reproduces property and the order that made it. A faith that wins kingdoms through the sister's line is still a dynasty.
- *The mod's side.* Badenya already reorders inheritance away from the father toward claimed and sororal kin. That is Bey's "kinship over family" (l.4007) in medieval dress. In the 13th-century Sahel, standing came through lineage; a faith that refused all inheritance would leave its daughters with nothing.
- *Guardrail.* Rule 19 already makes foremothers kin, not precedent. Badenya's genealogy songs praise the mother's line without claiming it was always right. The jeli's admonition (Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's bird-masked bards) keeps the right to criticise rulers.

**4.3 Identity and Houses.** Bey calls identity "the result of the carceral project of individuation" (l.5035–5036) and wants "a movement without identities" (l.3430–3431). The Menace is open to anyone (l.3409). Bey quotes McKenzie Wark: "I don't want an identity" (l.5165).
- *Tension.* Badenya admits through a gate and calls its members "Daughters of the House".
- *Mitigations already in the design.* Sisters are sisters before the knife (rule 8). Houses are open. Coalition across faiths is an alliance, not one people (rule 6). Bey's "Coalition's nonexclusivity means that we cast no one out, that no one is disposable" (l.5157–5158) supports a design point already in place: closing a gate does not cast a seeker out of the House's care.

**4.4 Houses and home.** "Trans… never tries to build a house" (l.520–522, after Awkward-Rich). Bey's childhood house "was not always a place of solace" (l.488–502). This is the same disagreement as BTF note §4.5. The mod's House is chosen and can be left. Rule (from that note): a daughter may leave a House without penalty or shame.

**4.5 Modern vocabulary.** Bey's subject is "cisgender", a 1990s word (l.1444–1477) that Bey links to whiteness and the Atlantic (note at l.5827–5847). The mod already bars "cis", "trans" and "gender" from in-world text (Heaney note §7). Bey's account of how "cis" is *made* ("guys by design", l.1110–1117) is useful for writing hostile pressure on daughters: drilling, mockery, "be a man". That pressure belongs in a Mande form (*fadenya* rivalry, the duty to marry and beget heirs), not a football locker room.

## 5. What does not transfer

- **The archives.** Slave-ship accounting, plantation "hands", Jim Crow "Colored" restrooms, the 2007 Farmer case (l.1303–1312, l.2747–2803); Sims (l.2732–2744); Ann Fisher's 1745 grammar (l.2127–2145); 1990s Usenet "cis" (l.1465–1477); US cartoons, films and football. All modern and American. None is evidence about Mali.
- **The race–gender thesis.** "Cisgender is antiblack" describes a racial order made by the Atlantic trade and US law. In the Sahel before 1453, the lines that mattered were lineage, *nyamakala* caste, free or slave status, Muslim or not, and language. Mande women of free lineages were women in their society's own terms. To call them "not-quite-women" because of a modern racial theory would be the very trope row 1 forbids.
- **Lugones' "colonial/modern gender system"** (l.2709–2712), the claim that gender is "a concrete colonial practice" (l.4303–4304), and "cultures… already in gender abolitionist territory" (l.5384–5386). These do not fit a pre-colonial West Africa with its own documented patriliny (row 5).
- **Black vernacular "they"** (essay 3). This is a modern African American usage. Whatever the grammar of Mande pronouns, no claim about gender ideology is drawn from it, and neither point appears in loc.
- **Jefferson's "cis-Atlantic"** (l.5827–5847) as an etymology of cisness. A US political coinage.
- **Narnia, Christ and Lucifer** (essay 5). Christian and British frames.
- **What may be used, as inspiration only:**
  - Bey's account of how a gender is *made* by drill and mockery (l.1726–1862, l.3810–3933) can inform hostile pressure in a Mande key (§4.5).
  - Bey's grandmother's playful "Who you? Do I know you?" (l.5584–5603) and the love "in, precisely, the not knowing" (l.5606–5607) fit the *My Mother's Child* claiming: being known anew.
  - Neither is cited as evidence.
