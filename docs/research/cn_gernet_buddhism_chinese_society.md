# Gernet, *Buddhism in Chinese Society* (1995): design reading

**Citation (verified from the PDF imprint):** Jacques Gernet, *Buddhism in Chinese Society: An Economic History from the Fifth to the Tenth Centuries*, trans. Franciscus Verellen. Translations from the Asian Classics. New York: Columbia University Press, 1995. ISBN 0-231-07380-1. Translation of *Les aspects économiques du bouddhisme dans la société chinoise du Ve au Xe siècle* (Saigon: EFEO, 1956). Page numbers are the printed pages.

**Read in full:**
- ch. 2, "Secondary Facts: The Fiscal Deficit" (pp. 29–62): the fiscal status of monks and of monastic assets, confiscation and the official name-panel, the 845 resettlement, the sale of ordination certificates;
- ch. 3, "The Indian Heritage" (pp. 65–93): consecrated property (*changzhu* 常住), the rights of inheritance of Chinese monks, the Dunhuang wills and auctions;
- ch. 6, "Commerce and Usury", from "Commercial Activities of the Chinese Communities" to the end of "Loans Granted to Monks" (pp. 167–179);
- ch. 7, "The Circuit of Giving", the sections "The Inexhaustible Treasuries" and the start of "Charitable Activities" (pp. 210–220);
- ch. 9, "The Popular Social Environment", the section "Organized Associations" (pp. 259–277).

**Read in part:**
- ch. 1 (pp. 3–25): the clergy numbers and the 845 metal seizures (pp. 7–12, 21–23);
- ch. 4, "Lands and Dependents": the Dunhuang monastery households (pp. 105–111) and pp. 133–141 (allotments to clergy, the 845 land figures, Song confiscations);

**Caveat on age.** The book was written in 1951–55 (preface p. xi). Hao Chunwen 2021 (p. 84) rejects Gernet's view that clergy were the religious masters of Dunhuang she, and Hao's corrections stand (see `cn_hao_she_associations.md`). Gernet is still the standard account of *changzhu* property, the inexhaustible treasuries and monastic lending.

## 1. Argument

### Monastic property: permanent, inalienable, and usable

- **Permanent assets.** Everything given to the Three Jewels was "permanent" (*changzhu*), "present, past, and future, as the Buddha himself" (p. 67).
  - The Vinaya lists five things that "may neither be owned, sold, or divided": the land, the buildings, the utensils, the fruit trees, and the flowers and fruit (*Mahīśāsaka*, p. 67).
  - Daoxuan sorts *changzhu* into four kinds; only estates and the "light goods" of dead monks were permanent in the strict sense (pp. 68–69).
- **Taking from the permanent store damns you.** The popular sanction was rebirth as the community's own property.
  - A steward who lent ten pieces of monastic cloth to a magistrate, and the magistrate who never paid them back, were both reborn as monastery slaves, "with the characters Chih-kuei imprinted on his back" (p. 72).
  - Anyone who diverts the permanent assets becomes "oxen, farmyard animals, or slaves … reborn in the monasteries to become part of the saṃgha's permanent assets" (pp. 72–73).
  - This is the faith's own defence of common property: the ledger curses the thief.
- **Sacred, but invested.** "The sacred nature of the permanent assets did not stand in the way of their investment in the lay world", provided the profit went back to the Three Jewels (p. 73).
  - Over time *changzhu* came to mean above all the fungible goods in the treasury: grain, cloth and coin (p. 169).
  - "To dedicate their assets as 'inexhaustible wealth' was a current means of ensuring the continuity of religious foundations" (p. 169).

### Clergy held private property, and their kin inherited part of it

- **Separated from, not divested of.** Gernet quotes Lingat: "The rules of the order required a monk to be separated from, not divested of, his property" (p. 74).
  - I-jing's notes from India (671–695) show monks appointing heirs and leaving debts and loans. The Vinaya rules "only applied to monks who died without leaving a will" (p. 76).
- **In China, monks were pushed toward movable wealth.** Clergy who had "left the family" had in theory no right to farmland, so they held "fungible goods (foodstuffs, cloth, and money)" (p. 79).
  - The law of 722 granted regular clergy personal share land: "Taoist monks are provided with thirty mu and Taoist nuns with twenty mu; the allocation is identical for Buddhist monks and nuns" (pp. 133–134).
  - By Tang times "the individual property of the religious was at least as extensive as that which was consecrated to communal use" (p. 134).
- **The will of the nun Linghui 靈惠, 865** (pp. 81–82). This is the best primary case for the Way.
  - Falling ill, she declares to her family that she makes her dispositions "in full consciousness".
  - Her only slave, Weiniang, "born in her family", goes to her niece Panniang, who is to see to the funeral. "Having made these dispositions, her family will not be entitled to intervene."
  - The witnesses are her younger brother, a So granddaughter, her niece the nun Linggui, a niece who signs with two fingerprints, several nephews, a water official and a military governor.
  - Gernet's reading: the family could claim goods that came from the family patrimony, but not what she acquired as a nun.
- **The will of Chong'en 崇恩 (P.3410)** (pp. 82–83; also in Hao 2010).
  - Estates on four canals went to Jingtu Monastery. His cell goods went "to the community of the entire town". The goods from his two terms as monastic administrator ("outstanding loans") went to the treasurer.
  - Personal legacies went to his nephew the monk, his cousin the general, the nun Yanding, the novice Yiniang, and his adviser Wenxin.
  - "Little Wa-ch'ai, whom he had raised from infancy … he now marries off". The servant he bought goes to her "in order that she may not be demeaned" (p. 83).
- **Entering religion split the household's property at Dunhuang** (pp. 84–85). In the dispute of the monk Longzang (Qi Zhou), the brothers divide land, grain and animals when the younger "has entered religion". Gernet ties this to the break-up of large households in Shazhou, where land was cut into plots "of mostly less than half a hectare" (p. 84).

### The Dunhuang saṅgha as an economy

- **Size.** Seventeen large establishments. Nunneries were bigger than monasteries, "a fact that may be peculiar to Tun-huang": Puguang Nunnery had 127 nuns, against 24–40 monks at the monasteries (p. 9).
- **Shared income was paid out in cloth.**
  - In 933–936 the Shazhou saṅgha auctioned clothing left by the dead (officials, nuns, "Madame Yin", "the grand prince") and gifts for services. The total was 58,502 *chi* of cotton cloth (pp. 89–90).
  - The proceeds were shared out to the whole clergy of the district: 60 *chi* to each monk or nun, 30 to each novice. Expenses included a gift to "the Empress of Khotan" (p. 90).
  - The account carries the seal of "the controller general of monks of Sha-chou" (p. 91).
- **Monastery households (*sihu* 寺戶, *changzhu baixing* 常住百姓).** Dunhuang monasteries held hereditary serf families.
  - The families could marry only within their class. A child fathered on an outside woman "shall be assigned to the property held in perpetuity" (pp. 106–107).
  - A Dunhuang proclamation calls the endowment "as a mountain and cannot be displaced" (p. 106).
  - They borrowed seed grain from their own monastery in groups (*tuan* 團) **without interest**, repaying "after the harvest" (pp. 108–109). Some petitions promise double repayment if late (p. 110).
  - A census of the Bao'en Monastery households, dated 876 or 936, survives (p. 107).

### Loans

- **Two kinds of lending** (pp. 174–175).
  - Loans of cash or cloth against a pledge went to officials, the gentry and donors. They were long-term, sometimes without a contract, and "one relied more on their trustworthiness and piety" (p. 178).
  - Grain loans went to peasants, at **50 percent interest** due at harvest (p. 177).
  - Tang law capped interest on pledge loans at one-fifth and forbade interest above the principal; ordinary loans ran in practice at one half (p. 172).
- **Dunhuang contracts.**
  - A monastery-household head, Yanjun, borrows three *shi* of wheat from the Lingtu Monastery's "Buddhist accounts". If he is late, his movables are seized; if he is absent or dead, the guarantors pay. A monk and an abbot witness (p. 176).
  - Li Sisi pledges an iron cauldron (p. 176). Loans to the households of *other* monasteries were also interest-free, "a mutual understanding that linked all the monasteries at Tun-huang" (p. 177).
- **Usury was a third of revenue.** At Jingtu Monastery in 930, more than 60 of 136 *shi* of grain income came from interest. "The Ching-t'u monastery derived more than a third of its revenues from usury" (p. 178). Grain was lent in small amounts of a few *dou*, recorded in ledgers with each borrower's residence and a cross for a signature (p. 177).
- **Monks borrowed from their own permanent assets** and repaid in the presence of the community (P. "Liang fa tuan ru zhai wenshu", pp. 178–179).
- **Debt for land.** In 907 a commoner who owed a monk grain handed over five *mu* for two years of sowing, keeping the tax liability himself (p. 135).
- **Long Life Treasuries** (*changsheng ku* 長生庫, Song). These were monastic pawn banks funded by members' subscriptions.
  - At one, monks pooled 33 strings of cash and bought lamp oil with the interest. The treasurer showed the accounts monthly (p. 174).
  - At another, novices pooled their money to buy their own ordination certificates (p. 174).
  - Gernet links this "subscription by their members to a mutual fund" to the she (p. 174).

### The Inexhaustible Treasuries (*wujin zang* 無盡藏)

- **Founding.** The Sect of the Three Stages (Sanjie jiao) founded one at Huadu Monastery, Chang'an, early in the 7th century (pp. 210–211).
- **Use of the funds.** They were split three ways: restoring monasteries across the empire, relieving "all those in the realm who went hungry", and rites (p. 211).
- **Lending.** Loans were made without a deed: borrowers "simply repaid their debts when they were due" (p. 210).
- **Gifts.**
  - The rich gave on the fourth day of the first month. Donors left "cartloads of money and silk … without the [donors'] names being known" (p. 211).
  - The poor gave a set daily mite, as little as one *fen* a day (p. 213). Branch "merit offices" opened at the Ghost Festival (p. 213).
- **"Inexhaustible" means the giving, not compound interest.** There are "two inexhaustibles": the field of compassion, and donors who "never cease giving for a single day" (p. 214). "Giving is contagious" (p. 214). The system aimed "not at an accumulation of goods but at their redistribution and circulation" (p. 217).
- **Confiscation, 713.** Xuanzong banned the gifts as "excess and fraudulence" and seized the treasuries (p. 211). The holdings were redistributed to Buddhist *and Daoist* monasteries in the capital, as permanent assets that "may not be divided among the private cells", the poorest first (p. 212).

### Associations (she 社 / yi 邑)

- **Earlier and later forms.**
  - The 6th–7th-century *yi* 邑 / *yihui* 邑會 known from steles were large (7 to 300 members) and loose. Rich members paid for whole statues (pp. 263, 271–272).
  - The Dunhuang *she* were smaller and more legal: "contributions were regulated by statute", and defaults were fined (p. 272).
- **Fictive kinship.** Members of 6th-century *yi* called themselves "brothers in the Law". At Dunhuang some she were called "society of brothers" or "family society" (p. 267).
- **Hereditary membership, with no adoption from collaterals.** A Dunhuang document: "When [a member] dies, his sons and grandsons shall take the succession", and "if this succession comes to an end for lack of descendants, it shall not be permitted to detach a member of a collateral branch" (p. 268). This is a design pressure (§2 row 4).
- **The kinless joined in order to have rites kept for them.**
  - "Being orphaned or without a male descendant were considered favorable qualifications for being admitted" (p. 268).
  - The petition of Ho Qingqing: he has no kin "or a wife to maintain [the offerings for] his deceased father and mother", and asks the three officers and the secretary to enrol him (p. 268).
- **Burial and travel dues.** An added article gives a bolt of cloth per member at the death of a member's close kin. It also gives wine for a member's kin leaving on a journey of over 1,000 *li*, and two jars on their return (pp. 267–268).
- **Banquets.**
  - A host (*zhaizhu* 齋主) received the members' grain, flour and oil and gave the feast in his own name, then took his turn as a giver (pp. 269–270).
  - There were six small feasts a month and three great ones a year (1st, 5th and 9th months) (p. 270).
- **S.527 in Gernet** (p. 273). He dates the women's statute to **13 February 959** and paraphrases the feast fine and "three blows of the bamboo" for leaving. He translates *shezhang* loosely as "the president" whose orders must be obeyed.
- **Rulers distrusted the she.**
  - In the 5th century a memorial warned that people "west of the passes" held Buddhist banquets "to incite each other to sedition" (p. 269).
  - The 40,000 small sanctuaries were "the private sanctuaries of a multitude of popular associations", and "in the eyes of the rulers monks were held to be creators of 'parties' … (p'eng-tang)" (p. 275).

### Confiscation and its limits

- **The name-panel protected a monastery.** An official name-panel (*e* 額) "was the safeguard against all future confiscations and even the destruction of the sanctuary" (p. 43). Monks bribed officials for them (p. 46).
- **Unrecognised sanctuaries went first.** They "were the first to be closed or destroyed" (p. 44): in 727 (village chapels), in 843–845 (hermitages and "rustic establishments" first, p. 45), and in 955 (all houses "wu chih-e", without a name).
- **Immunity was never absolute.**
  - Assets were "never entirely tax-exempt" (p. 43).
  - Shops, mills and loan agencies were assets "over which the government was reluctant to relinquish control" (p. 47).
  - Local officials confiscated dead monks' goods "ostensibly because of legal disputes", against the rule that they pass to the community (pp. 47–48).
- **845 (Huichang).**
  - Clergy who were allowed to stay were moved into large monasteries; the rest were laicised (pp. 40–41).
  - Bronze images went to the Salt and Iron Commission to be minted, iron ones became farm tools, and gold and silver went to the Board of Finance (p. 22).
  - The act of grace of 845 attacks rich monasteries that lend out their shop rents "to snatch away the property of people who are defenseless" (p. 169).
  - 150,000 slaves were freed; each slave received 100 *mu* of the confiscated land (p. 139).
  - Only four monasteries were kept at Chang'an and one per circuit (p. 304).
- **The great repressions were mint drives.** Gernet reads the repressions of 574–577, 842–845 and 955 as "measures of economic recovery", ways for the court to get copper for coinage (pp. 21–22).
- **Later seizures.**
  - In 1150 the Southern Song sequestered "the [excess] lands of the Buddhist and Taoist monasteries" of Fujian, leaving enough for the "permanent" revenues and raising 340,000 strings. A minister asked for 60–70% more (p. 141).
  - Gernet calls the Huichang measures the start of a decline "from the middle of the ninth century on" (p. 298).

### Daoists at Dunhuang (incidental, but important)
- **Two provisional ordination documents of 758 survive (Paris)** (pp. 55–56). They are for the new clergy "in the territory of the Six Armies and of the prefectures under our jurisdiction".
  - The fee paid for writing the certificates was 1,465 strings.
  - Totals: [2]27 Buddhist monks, 169 nuns, **137 Taoist monks and 33 Taoist nuns**. The total of 666 is the document's; it includes other categories.
  - Both copies are made out to Shazhou men, of Dunhuang county. The record names "the Buddhist or Taoist monastery to which the postulant will be attached".
- So Daoist nuns (*nüguan* 女冠) were being ordained and attached to *guan* 觀 in the Hexi region, Shazhou included, in 758, before the Tibetan conquest. The 722 law gave them 20 *mu* each, like Buddhist nuns (p. 134).
- Late-Tang Dunhuang bought ordinations in kind: "My elder brother had his daughter P'ing-niang ordained [a nun] by the governor-general An … he required a donkey and a cow" (p. 60).

## 3. In-world loc lines

- "Whoever spends the House's store is born again into it: an ox at the mill, a hand at the press, her name on her back." (after pp. 72–73)
- "She was given to the House, not to the throne; what is given to the House remains." (after the *changzhu* formula, p. 67)
- "I make this in my right mind before my kin. The girl born in our house goes to my daughter, who will bury me. Let no one of the family intervene." (after Linghui, 865)
- "Lend to a sister and ask back the measure. Lend to the county and ask the half." (after pp. 109, 177)
- "Two things are inexhaustible: those who hunger, and those who never stop giving."
- "Our seats do not close when a line ends. We fill them with daughters."
