## 2. Ashū, renamed Berākhā

- **The deed.** On his arrival in India Ben Yijū bought and freed an Indian slave woman. He calls her "Ashū the slave girl, the proselyte, of Tuluva" (p. 55). Friedman thinks it "cannot be far from the truth" that he meant to marry her, and probably did (p. 56).
- **Her name.** When he freed her he gave her a Jewish name, "Berākhā daughter of Abraham". The "daughter of Abraham" refers to the patriarch, "father of all converts" (pp. 56–57). Friedman: among the many Geniza deeds of manumission, "this is the only one that I have seen, which cites the new name given to the freedwoman" (p. 57). Berākhā ("blessing") was also the name of Ben Yijū's sister (p. 57). The formula comes from a later responsum (Asher b. Jehiel): proselytes are written "So-and-so son of Abraham" (p. 57 n. 18).
- **Her status was questioned.** Some authorities held that a slave woman immersed "for servitude" was of "intermediate status, being neither completely Gentile nor completely Jew" (p. 56). In Yemen, around 1140, "self-appointed guardians of public morals" doubted the validity of the betrothal, her status and that of the children (pp. 73–74). The responsa he commissioned in his defence rest partly on a reading of the Mishnah that all known manuscripts contradict (p. 75).
- **Protection by a patron.** Ben Yijū answered in a poem: "They maligned me … / And Madmūn the grandee arose / and clad me in his robes" (II, 37, quoted p. 76). Madmūn, *nagid* of Yemen and representative of the merchants, protected him. Later letters call Ben Yijū "head of the community" and "God-fearing … one who avoids evil", which Friedman reads as rebutting the slanders (p. 77).
- Ashū herself never speaks in the documents. Her name does not appear after the deed (p. 56 n. 16).

## 3. Sitt al-Dār, "Mistress of the House"

- The daughter's name means "Mistress of the House" (p. 70). In Egypt she was called **Umm Yūsuf**, "Joseph's Mother" (p. 84).
- In 1149 her father wrote from Aden to his brothers: "find out who is the best of the sons of my brother Joseph or the sons of your sister Berākhā, so that …" (III, 29, p. 72). He was ready to settle "in Aden or Fustat or Alexandria", if return to "al-Mahdiyya … Tunis or Qayrawān" was impossible (p. 72).
- She spent three years in another merchant's household and was betrothed to his son. Her father broke the match when her brother died and fled with her, and he wrote of "his daughter's depressed state" (p. 78).
- Her uncle Joseph wrote from Sicily: "I shall complete her education in my household, so that we have joy from you and from her" (III, 49, p. 80).
- Her dowry list (Aug. 1156) was written subject to "the well-known conditions imposed by daughters of Israel". Friedman thinks "Perhaps Sitt al-Dār reserved exclusive rights to the use of her dowry items" (p. 82).
- Her husband, the scholar-nephew, was scorned by her father for having "little hollow pomp and little pushiness" (III, 50, p. 82). He became a respected judge, and their line became "the family of scholars" (pp. 83–84). One descendant signed the Damascus ban of 1286 against critics of Maimonides' *Guide* (p. 88).

## 4. The Maghrebi family network

- **Origin and name.** Ben Yijū is a Berber tribal name. "Our Abraham b. Perahyā's grandfather was presumably under the protection of that tribe and took its name", and it survives as Benichou (Goitein, quoted p. 53).
- **Al-Mahdiyya's judge.** From Aden Ben Yijū sends greetings to "Labraṭ, the *dayyān*" of al-Mahdiyya, son of Moses the *dayyān*. He may have been Ben Yijū's teacher, and Ben Yijū wrote a poem praising him as interpreter of the Talmud (p. 74). He is probably of the same family as the Labrat b. Moses b. Sighmar, *dayyan* of al-Mahdiyya, who reported on Nissim in 1061 (`jud_maghreb_nissim_relief.md` §1; the link is *unverified*, and Friedman cites Hirschberg, "Soghmār"). This is the only figure of Maghrebi community authority in the part read.
- **Women of the family.** His sister Berākhā went to Sicily with her husband Marwān and their children (p. 72 n. 64; p. 88). Joseph rented a flat at Mazara from **Umm al-ʿIzz d. Zurʿa** for forty years (p. 72). Daughters "are rarely referred to in their letters" (p. 54).
- **Aden's jurisdiction.** "Aden and India" formed one Jewish "juridical diocese" under the rabbinical court of Aden, which deferred to Fustat (p. 40).
- **Sijilmasa.** Abu Zikri Kohen, a great India trader and representative of merchants at Fustat, came from Sijilmasa (pp. 14, 40). His son's letter of 1148 on the Almohad conquest is in Kraemer (`jud_maghreb_maimonides_fes.md` §1).
- **A lamp alms.** In 1156 Abu ʾl-Khayr Ṣedāqā gave money "for the illumination of Ben Yijū's synagogue in Egypt" (p. 69). This parallels the lamp alms of Arles (Stouff §19).
- **After the Almohads.** Friedman's note cites Stillman: "Already in late Almohad Morocco, Jewish forced converts to Islam were allowed to return to the open practice of Judaism", if they wore the distinguishing clothes (*Dress*, p. 114, quoted at p. 516 n. 14). This is secondhand. Check it in Stillman before use.

## 5. Gender and the body

Nothing on eunuchs, cross-dressing, *khuntha*, Purim or bathing in the part read. One divorce appears: the "little one" whose husband Abū ʿImrān "converted to Islam … and he divorced her". A patron "looked after her rights" (II, 61, p. 500). That case is Egyptian, not Maghrebi.
