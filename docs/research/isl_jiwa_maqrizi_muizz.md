# Jiwa (trans.), al-Maqrīzī's *Ittiʿāẓ* on al-Muʿizz (2009): design reading

Source: Shainool Jiwa (trans.), *Towards a Shiʿi Mediterranean Empire: Fatimid Egypt and the Founding of Cairo. The Reign of the Imam-caliph al-Muʿizz, from Taqī al-Dīn Aḥmad b. ʿAlī al-Maqrīzī's Ittiʿāẓ al-ḥunafāʾ bi-akhbār al-aʾimma al-Fāṭimiyyīn al-khulafāʾ*. Ismaili Texts and Translations 11 (London: I.B. Tauris with the Institute of Ismaili Studies, 2009), xvii + 237 pp. ISBN 978-1-84511-960-7.

**Reign covered.** Al-Muʿizz li-Dīn Allāh, r. 341–365/953–975. Almost all the Egyptian material falls between Jawhar's conquest (July 969) and al-Muʿizz's death (December 975). The translation is of al-Shayyāl's edition, *Ittiʿāẓ* vol. 1, pp. 93–235 (Note on the Translation, p. 49). **It does not reach al-ʿAzīz, al-Ẓāhir or al-Mustanṣir.**

Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo. Page numbers are the book's printed pages. "Jiwa" means her introduction and notes; "al-Maqrīzī" means the translated text, with his named source where he gives one.

**What was read.**
- **In full:** the introduction (pp. 1–47) and the Note on the Translation (p. 49).
- **Translation, by keyword search and the surrounding pages:** eunuch, *ustādh*, *khādim*, Jawdhar; harem, women, mother, *sayyida*, concubine, slave; singing, music, dance, drum, jesters; Nawrūz, festival, Ghadīr, ʿĀshūrāʾ, procession, parade; effeminate, *mukhannath*, *muʾannath* (no hits); Rūm, Byzantine, Greek, Christians, Melkite, Jews, Ibn Killis; *majālis al-ḥikma*; dress, colours, trousers, *ghiyār*; *muḥtasib*, prohibitions and bans. Read through in context: pp. 53–121 (to the start of the Qarmaṭī history) and pp. 181–215 (the last two years and the obituary). The long Qarmaṭī section (pp. 122–186) was searched only. It is heresiography about Iraq and Bahrain and has no Egyptian material in scope.

**Relation to existing notes.** `isl_halm_empire_of_the_mahdi.md` stops in 973, the day al-Muʿizz entered Cairo. This book covers the **first Cairo years (969–975)**, the founding generation of the court Rasad's household inherited. It is also the direct text behind several *Ittiʿāẓ* citations in `later_starts/mukhannathun_fatimid_cairo.md` §2.4 and §3.1, `isl_sanders_ritual_fatimid_cairo.md` §1.3 and `isl_shoshan_popular_culture_cairo.md` §1.2. Women at court: `isl_cortese_calderini_fatimid_women.md`. Eunuchs: `isl_tolino_eunuchs.md`.

## 0. Framing warning

- **Who is speaking.** Al-Maqrīzī (b. Cairo 766/1364, d. 845/1442) was a Mamluk Sunni compiler writing about 450 years after the events. Jiwa p. 32 prints "845/1449", which is a slip for 1441–42. For 969–975 his main source is **Ibn Zūlāq** (d. 386/996), an eyewitness whose *Sīrat al-Muʿizz* he says he read "in his own handwriting" (pp. 45, 212). He also uses Ibn al-Ṭuwayr (12th c.), Ibn ʿAbd al-Ẓāhir (13th c.), Ibn Saʿīd and **Ibn al-Athīr**. He himself flags the last as hostile, one of the "eastern" historians who "say abominable things" about the Fatimids (pp. 45–47, 212–213). Treat dated Cairo entries as near-contemporary, and anecdotes from Ibn al-Athīr or ʿAbd al-Jabbār as polemic.
- **The draft problem.** The *Ittiʿāẓ* is an unfinished draft: "a detailed list of events and reports" with no joined-up narrative (p. 37). Entries are terse. Jiwa's square-bracketed additions are hers.
- **The translator's frame.** Jiwa writes for the Institute of Ismaili Studies. Her introduction stresses Fatimid "tolerance" and "inclusive governance" (pp. 17–19, quoting Daftary). That is a fair reading of the *amān*, but it is not neutral. The text itself also records a dress decree for Jews (p. 101), sectarian street violence (pp. 100–101, 115) and many public punishments. Use the entries, not the frame.
- **Ninety years before 1066.** Everything here is 91–97 years before the bookmark. The **"still in 1066?"** column in §1 marks what can be carried forward. "Unknown" means this book cannot say.
- **No *mukhannath* and no dancers.** The book has no *mukhannath*, *muʾannath*, *raqqāṣ* or professional dancer. Kapitaikin's *Ittiʿāẓ* ii p. 164 (al-Ẓāhir "preceded by male dancers") lies in vol. 2 and is **outside this translation**. It was not checked here.

### 1.2 Royal women, succession and women's property

- **A woman sells a girl she raised.** "The mother of the princes (*umm al-umarāʾ*)" sent "a young girl, whom she had raised in the Maghrib, to be sold in Egypt". Jiwa cannot identify the mother of the princes (n. 163). An Ikhshīdid woman, "Kāfūr's lady", bought the girl for 600 dinars "so as to enjoy her". Al-Muʿizz uses the story as a war cry: if their princesses buy concubines for pleasure, "the virility of the men has weakened". (pp. 63–64)
  - **Status:** C&C's "anecdotal woman" trope (sexuality as a sign of a dynasty's decline). It is Fatimid war propaganda about a rival court. The girl is a sold, owned person.
- **The sickroom.** "It was said" that as al-Muʿizz's illness worsened, **his wife** brought Jawhar, Ibn Killis, ʿUslūj, Aflaḥ and Ṭāriq al-Ṣaqlabī to his bed to ask who held authority. He wept and would not answer (p. 210). A lacuna cuts Jawhar's dress (n. 566). His physician Mūsā b. al-ʿĀzār and "his slave Jawhar" nursed him (p. 209).
- **A concealed death.** His death "was not announced" for months. Al-ʿAzīz entered the room "as if he was consulting" his father and came out with orders (pp. 209–210; n. 565).
- **The caliph's mother.** She died six days before the end of Rabīʿ II 364 (c. 10 January 975). Prince ʿAbd Allāh died nineteen days later; al-Maqrīzī calls her his grandmother, and the dates conflict (p. 196, n. 549). Ibn al-Athīr says al-Muʿizz's mother was an *umm walad*, which Jiwa glosses as "a freed slave-woman" (p. 211, n. 568).
- **A woman's estates restored (365/975).** The rulers of Mecca asked for favour for their sister **Ṣafiyya**, wife of a man who had sided with the Qarāmiṭa; "she had gone into hiding". Al-Muʿizz ordered "her estates and revenues" returned. Ibn Killis had the chief qāḍī and his certified witnesses witness the letter. "Then she came out of hiding and was safe." (p. 206)
- **Women as petitioners.** Ibn Khallikān (n. 198) says it was **the women** of Fusṭāṭ who went to the sharīf Abū Jaʿfar Muslim and implored him to get Jawhar to renew the safety guarantee (969). *Secondhand*: Jiwa's note, not al-Maqrīzī's text. (p. 76)
- **Other women in the text.** Kulthūm and Nafīsa are present only as shrines (§1.4). Women in besieged Damascus "sat by the roadside" (p. 194). The rest are captives in the Qarmaṭī chapters.

- **The trousers decree is new to the notes.** It is a sumptuary rule aimed at women's clothing. The target is extravagance (wide trousers cut from many panels of fine Delta linen), not gender-crossing. The text gives no penalty and no enforcement.
- **The 975 Nawrūz entry in the *Ittiʿāẓ* has no masks, no mock elephants and no camels.** Those details are in the *Khiṭaṭ* version of the same Ibn Zūlāq report (Shoshan p. 49, via *Khiṭaṭ* I:268). The session's Būlāq OCR of the *Khiṭaṭ*, at printed p. 493, has *samājāt*, jewellery, elephant figures and offenders paraded "on camels". Jiwa may be compressing, or the two works may differ. Cite the *Khiṭaṭ* for masks.
- **Shaming parades.** The parade (*tashhīr*) appears throughout. In 974 a captured Qarmaṭī commander was set on a camel "covered in a large hooded cloak on the back of which was written his name and what he had done" (p. 188). In 971 captives were paraded "wearing hooded cloaks and in shackles" while "the people reviled and vilified" them (pp. 190–191). Both scenes go on to flayings and must stay off screen; only the labelled cloak is usable.
- **Humiliation of women as a conqueror's demand.** At Damascus in 970 the Fatimid general Jaʿfar b. Falāḥ would pardon the town only if the shaykhs came "accompanied by your women with their hair uncovered" (p. 93). This is a hostile act by a Fatimid commander, in Syria. **Not for events.**

### 1.5 Christians, Jews and Rūm

- **Population.** Egypt held "Arabs, Turks, Greeks, Berbers, Armenians and Sudanese", with "Copts, Melkites and Nestorians" and "a number of Jewish groups" (p. 14, Jiwa).
- **The *amān* (969).** It promised that "the dhimmis (non-Muslims) will be treated according to previous custom" (p. 71). Jiwa reads more into it, including the right to build and repair houses of worship (pp. 17–18).
- **Jewish office-holders:**
  - **Ibn Killis**, "an Egyptian Jew who had converted to Islam", became the first Fatimid vizier in Egypt. He held study sessions on Ismaʿili law at his house (pp. 18–19, 113–114 and n. 323). He oversaw al-Muʿizz's palace affairs in 975 (p. 206) and stood at the sickbed (p. 210).
  - **Isḥāq b. Mūsā** was "a Jewish physician who accompanied al-Muʿizz from the Maghrib". His father had also been the caliph's physician (n. 330). The court physician named in the lists is Mūsā b. al-ʿĀzār (pp. 113, 195, 209). That he is Isḥāq's father is inferred from n. 330, not stated.
- **The *ghiyār* decree.** In 362/972–73 Jews were ordered to wear *al-ghiyār* (p. 101). This is the only discriminatory measure against Jews in the book. Nothing says how long it lasted.
- **Languages at court.** Al-Muʿizz learned Greek among other languages (p. 65), and a Byzantine envoy had private audiences with him (p. 207). The famous envoy speech, in which the envoy finds the new Cairo "dark and gloomy", is Ibn al-Athīr's. Jiwa n. 560 calls it an example of "eastern" bias, and al-Maqrīzī rejects it (pp. 207, 212).

## 2. Cross-checks

1. **`isl_halm_empire_of_the_mahdi.md` §1.3, curtain-drawer "Khafif al-Saqlabi" (secondhand, *Ittiʿāẓ* 1:97f).** **Verified:** Jiwa p. 61 has "Khafīf, the Slav *ṣāḥib al-sitr* (curtain bearer)". He carries the caliph's words and brings back the answer. Halm's p. 365 "Slavic curtain-drawer" sent to the Kutāma about taxes is the same episode.
2. **Halm §1.4, "A woman within earshot" (secondhand).** Agrees with Jiwa pp. 29 and 57. Jiwa adds the monogamy counsel (p. 58) and the C&C p. 52 reading (n. 149).
3. **Halm §1.7, the circumcision feast of 962.** Halm has performers from Sind; Jiwa n. 147 (al-Nuʿmān via Idrīs) has "jesters to entertain them". These are compatible. The free-or-slave, Muslim-or-*dhimmī* universality comes from the order itself (p. 55).
4. **Halm §1.4, mourning women banned (960).** At ʿAbd Allāh's funeral in Cairo, 975, the *dignitaries* came "without [their] turbans" and the coffin was "surrounded by the dignitaries … screaming and wailing" (pp. 196–197). This is male court mourning, fifteen years after the ban. Women's mourning is not mentioned.
5. **Halm §1.3, "Halm never calls Jawhar a eunuch".** Jiwa does not either. She glosses him "al-Ṣiqillī (the Sicilian) or Ṣaqlabī (the Slav)" (n. 28) and the text calls him al-Muʿizz's "slave" (pp. 67, 209). Consistent.
6. **`isl_shoshan_popular_culture_cairo.md` §1.2, row 364/975 (masks, mock elephants, camel parade; Ibn Zūlāq via *Khiṭaṭ* I:268).** The *Ittiʿāẓ* version (Jiwa p. 205) has none of these. **The Cairo note §3.1 bullet "Nawrūz, 364/975. Three days of masks … paraded some offenders on camels (Ibn Zūlāq via *Ittiʿāẓ* 1:224)" should cite the *Khiṭaṭ* for the masks and camels.** The *Ittiʿāẓ* supports only the three days, the arrests, the parade and the prison.
7. **`isl_sanders_ritual_fatimid_cairo.md` §1.3, Nawrūz bans and Ghadīr 362/973.** Both agree with Jiwa pp. 112 and 195. Sanders's "arrest and publicly humiliate" matches "arrested, paraded and imprisoned" (p. 205).
8. **Sanders §1.1, "The Ṣaqlabī eunuch Muẓaffar carried the caliph's parasol (pp. 64–65)".** Do not confuse him with Jiwa's Muẓaffar (p. 65), a Slav calligraphy tutor killed in Ifrīqiya before 972. The parasol-bearer of 973–975 is Shafīʿ (pp. 108, 113, 195).
9. **`later_starts/mukhannathun_fatimid_cairo.md` §3.1, "Variant: Būlāq reads *al-mughannūn*".** This conflicts with `isl_kapitaikin_palatina_dancers.md` §2 item 2, which gives Būlāq vol. 1 p. 493 as *al-muʾannathūn*. The session's Būlāq OCR at printed p. 493 also reads المؤنثون. **Recheck the Cairo note's variant against the page image** before keeping it. This is tangential to Jiwa but surfaced while checking item 6.
10. **Cairo note §3.2, hostile instruments.** Jiwa adds the labelled hooded cloak (p. 188) and the parading of millers, which shows *tashhīr* applied to market offences, not only crime or sex (p. 87). The bells of al-Musabbiḥī's parade appear here only in the founding of Cairo: bells hung on the ropes, rung by a raven (p. 78). That story comes from hostile later sources (n. 204).
11. **`isl_cortese_calderini_fatimid_women.md`, "Method and hostile tropes".** The Kāfūr's-lady story (p. 64) is a textbook case of their "anecdotal woman". Jiwa gives no comment. Treat it as Fatimid propaganda.
12. **C&C, women's property and confiscation (ch. 5–6).** Ṣafiyya's restoration (p. 206) fits C&C's finding that women were "normally" spared and could recover property. The mechanism is new: a family petition, a caliph's letter, and the vizier having the qāḍī's witnesses attest it.
13. **`isl_lev_state_society_fatimid.md` (Ṣaqāliba supply, p. 74; police and *muḥtasib*, pp. 153–161).** Consistent. Jiwa adds the 975 purchase drive (p. 204), the first *muḥtasib* (p. 84) and the two *shurṭas*. The higher *shurṭa* was moved to Cairo, and the arrangement lasted "throughout Fatimid, Ayyubid and Mamluk times" (n. 199, after al-Shayyāl). Both forces still exist in 1066.
14. **`isl_tolino_eunuchs.md` §1.1.** Jiwa translates *khādim* as "eunuch" in her notes and in "this eunuch [al-Khādim]" (p. 202), but leaves it as a name element elsewhere. Read the gender by context, as Tolino does; do not take Jiwa's English as evidence.
15. **Moreh note §4 item 13 / Cairo note §3.1 ("Never March 21").** Holds. Jiwa n. 352's "21 March" is the Persian feast in an Iraqi context, and her n. 547 gives 1 Tūt for Egypt.
16. **Kapitaikin (*Early Music* 2019, n. 33), *Ittiʿāẓ* ii p. 164.** Not in this book, which covers vol. 1 only. Still unchecked.

## 5. Leads not yet followed

1. **The Arabic of *Ittiʿāẓ* 1:224** (al-Shayyāl), for the 364/975 Nawrūz entry. Does the *Ittiʿāẓ* really lack *samājāt*, or did Jiwa compress? A.F. Sayyid's IIS edition (in preparation in 2009) may have variants.
2. **The trousers decree (Dhu l-Ḥijja 363).** Find the Arabic word (probably *sarāwīl*), and whether the *Khiṭaṭ* or Ibn Muyassar repeats it with a penalty.
3. **The Būlāq variant (Cross-check 9).** Look at the page image of *Khiṭaṭ* Būlāq 1:493 and settle *al-muʾannathūn* versus *al-mughannūn* for the Cairo note.
4. ***Sīrat al-Ustādh Jawdhar*** (ed. Ḥusayn and Shaʿīra 1954; Canard's French 1958; Haji's English edition). It has al-Muʿizz's notes to Jawdhar "about various official and family matters" (n. 292), the best source for how a eunuch major-domo handled the harem.
5. **al-Nuʿmān, *al-Majālis wa-l-musāyarāt*** (Tunis 1978). 292 audiences with al-Muʿizz (n. 65); the women's petition there is already verified in the C&C note. Search it for the curtain-bearer and for women's sessions.
6. **Idrīs, *ʿUyūn al-akhbār* vol. 6** on al-Muʿizz. Jiwa was preparing an English annotated translation (n. 42). It has Ibn Zūlāq material and the political version of the Slavs' fall (n. 168).
7. ***Ittiʿāẓ* vol. 2** for al-Ẓāhir (Kapitaikin's ii p. 164, the male dancers) and al-Mustanṣir (the drummer Nasab, 2:254, already verified in the Cairo note). Jiwa does not cover them.
8. **Jiwa, "Religious Pluralism in Egypt: The Ahl al-Kitāb in Early Fatimid Times"** (MESA 2000, iis.ac.uk; n. 47). It may date the *ghiyār* decree's end, and covers the church at Qaṣr al-Shamʿ.
