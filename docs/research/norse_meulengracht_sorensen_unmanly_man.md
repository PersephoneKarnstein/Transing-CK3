## 1. The argument in brief

**Method.** The sagas are read as thirteenth- and fourteenth-century texts. They speak to the society that wrote them, not to a pagan past (pp. 11-12).
- He grants that "the ideas on which Norse *níð* are founded are ancient in origin" (p. 85). His evidence is Tacitus' penalties for men "cowardly and unwarlike and abusive of their bodies".
- He also finds "no strong evidence" that the surviving examples come from older tradition (p. 81).
- In short, the concept is old but the texts are medieval. Bæddel's "tolerated while pagan" arc is a design choice; this book does not support it as history.
- His summary: "cowardice and effeminacy were two aspects of the same thing ... nothing hits a man harder than the allegation that he is no man" (p. 11).

### The words (ch. 2)
- **Three outlawry words.** *Sorðinn* and *stroðinn* are participles: a man "sexually used by a man - willingly or not". ***Argr / ragr*** names "a quality or tendency" (p. 18).
- **The sexual sense of *argr*:** "willing or inclined to play or interested in playing the female part in sexual relations. ... in this sense it can only be applied to a man" (p. 18).
- **Of a woman** the feminine form *org* means something else: "generally immodest, perverted or lecherous" (p. 18). His example is Venus in Hauksbók, "so lustful, *org* and evil". In Lokasenna, Iðunn is called "the most nymphomaniac" of women and Freyja "the foulest witch" (p. 19). For a woman the word means lust, not unmanning.
- **Three senses, fused.**
  1. Sexual.
  2. "Versed in witchcraft" (p. 19). Heathen sorcery "included (as far as we can see) sexual activities and taboo-breaking; among other things, men appeared as women" (p. 19). He quotes Snorri: seiðr is accompanied by "so much *ergi* that men could not be associated with it without disgrace" (p. 19).
  3. "Cowardly, unmanly, effeminate", which he calls the most important (pp. 19-20).
  - The fusion is the point: "fusion between the notions of sexual unmanliness and unmanliness in a moral sense stands at the heart of *níð*" (p. 20). The proverb *Svá ergisk hverr sem eldisk*, "everyone gets *argr* as he gets older" (Hrafnkels saga ch. 8), joins impotence and cowardice in one taunt (p. 20).
- **The charge is symbolic.** "The substance of *níð* is not sexual perversion, even though it is cast in this form. ... The charge is symbolic" (p. 20).
- **Contempt for women is not the mechanism.** "The female role was ignominious only when it was assigned to a man." *Argr* and *sorðinn* "signify what a man must not be, since in that case he is no man" (p. 24). He reads Norse sex roles as sharply split. Women ruled *innan stokks*, indoors, and carried the keys on their girdles (p. 20).
- **Níð and níðingr.**
  - *Níð* is gross symbolic insult. Its purpose is "to terminate a period of peace ... and isolate an opponent from society by declaring that he is unworthy to be a member" (p. 32).
  - The man attacked must "challenge his adversary to battle, or avenge himself by blood-revenge" (p. 32).
  - *Níðingr* is wider. It covers oath-breakers, traitors, killers of kin or of the defenceless, and cowards. "The purpose of *níð* was always to make a man a *níðingr* by force of symbolism" (p. 32).
- **The pagan and Christian codes differ.** Níð despises "mainly or only the passive role". The Church condemned both partners. The secular laws "do not mention homosexuality" (p. 26). Bishop Þorlákr's penances (1178-93) ran nine or ten years with flogging. Women who "satisfy each other" got the same penance as men (p. 26).

### The law (ch. 2)
- **Gulaþing law: making níð.**
  - "Nobody is to make verbal *níð* about another person, nor 'timber-*níð*' either" (p. 15). This is the distinction between ***tunguníð*** (spoken) and ***tréníð*** (carved) (p. 28).
  - The penalty is outlawry, which "was equivalent to a death sentence. The outlaw was excluded from the general peace of the community, so the offended party was justified in killing him" (p. 16).
  - The same law also outlaws *ýki* ("exaggeration"), slander of what cannot be. One example is to declare that a man "is a woman every ninth night or has born a child" (p. 15).
- **Gulaþing law: *fullréttisorð*** (insults owed full compensation, p. 16):
  - saying a man has given birth;
  - calling him *sannsorðinn*;
  - comparing him to a mare, a bitch, a harlot, or any female animal.
  - The Frostaþing law rates comparison with a *male* animal at half compensation (p. 16).
- **Grágás** (p. 17; Old Norse p. 100):
  - Carving "timber-*níð*" or setting up a ***níðstöng*** carries lesser outlawry, prosecuted "with a panel of twelve".
  - ***Ragr, stroðinn, sorðinn*** "all have full outlawry as the penalty".
  - "A man has the right to kill in retaliation for these three words." The right runs as long as the right to kill over women, "up to the next General Assembly".
  - The speaker "falls with forfeit immunity at the hands of anyone who accompanies the man" he insulted.
  - This is "the least conditional" right of vengeance in Icelandic law. The only other cases are killing and sex with a close kinswoman (p. 17).
  - **The outlaw is the one who says the word, not the one who is called it.**
- **Dress.** Grágás bars a woman from "wearing male clothes, from cutting her hair like a man, bearing arms or in general behaving like a man". There is "a corresponding edict against men appearing in a female role" (p. 22, citing Staðarhólsbók chs. 155, 254). The book does not give the penalty. Other notes give lesser outlawry; verify in Dennis-Foote-Perkins before using.
- **The Swedish Hednalag** (p. 31; Old Norse p. 103).
  - The insult is "You are not like a man, and no man at heart." The reply is "I am as much a man as you", followed by a challenge to single combat.
  - If the insulted man fails to appear, "he will become what he has been called, and he is not qualified to take an oath, nor is he valid as a witness, either for man or woman" (p. 31).
  - In Egils saga the challenge is "you will be a *níðingr* to every man if you do not dare" (p. 31).
- **Verse law** (Grágás "On poetry", p. 70).
  - "No man is to versify either praise or blame about another."
  - One stanza about a man costs three marks; several are punished more heavily.
  - A half-stanza of scorn, "or the kind of praise that he composes in order to insult", carries full outlawry.
  - In Staðarhólsbók, "every word is to stand as it is said, and is not to be construed according to the rules of scaldic diction". An ambiguous word draws only half-compensation. Jónsbók (1281) makes it no offence at all (pp. 70-71).
  - The Law Rock is where níð was "above all other places" forbidden (p. 34).

### Níð as genre (chs. 3-4)
- **Níð need not be verse.** "There seem to be no serious reasons for limiting the term *níð* to versified insults" (p. 30). Verse still carried further and lasted longer.
- **Flyting at the drinking-match.** Örvar-Odds saga's "flyting" is a drinking-match. There the opponent is mocked as lying "between a calf and a slave-woman, while others fought" (p. 25). Sexual and social taboos are stacked in one insult (p. 25).
- **Senna.** "In a *senna* two or more people accuse each other of despicable qualities or actions" (p. 38). Examples:
  - Broddi's senna at the Law Rock in Ölkofra þáttr goes through six chieftains one at a time. Each gets his own slur: a stallion mounting him, turning into a mare, cowardice, incest (pp. 35-38).
  - He overreaches against a kinsman and asks his forgiveness the next day (p. 36).
  - Lokasenna is the mythic parallel.
  - Helgakviða Hundingsbana I has a senna before battle (pp. 38, 53).
- **Two functions.** Níð *humiliates* the proud (Ölkofra) or *challenges* a man so that he proves it false (Króka-Refr) (pp. 38-43).
  - Refr's enemies say "every ninth night he was a woman and needed a man, and this is why he was called Refr the *argr*". They also spread the story that he was paid to leave Iceland "on the grounds that he was *argr*" (p. 41). The same speech glosses *Refr hinn ragi* as "Refr the coward": the translator renders the word both ways on one page.
  - He kills them and ends as the Danish king's lord of twelve farms (pp. 41-42).
- **Dialogue verse (*að botna vísu*, "capping").** One speaker gives half a stanza and the other completes it with a twist (p. 58). "The poet had to save his life by completing a stanza of which a troll or other supernatural being speaks the first half" (p. 58).
- **Weapon puns.** Gísli and Skeggi trade capped couplets in their duel. Each couplet reads as a weapon-boast and also as níð, with the sword as phallus (pp. 58-61).
- **Tréníð in practice.**
  - Skeggi orders a carved effigy of Gísli and Kolbjörn, "one standing behind the other" (pp. 51-52).
  - Bjarnar saga describes such an effigy. It was worse for "the one standing in front" (p. 56).
  - Egill raises a hazel pole with a horse's head (pp. 28-29).
  - In Vatnsdæla saga the challenge is "if you have the spirit of a man rather than a mare". The absent man gets a pole fixed in a slaughtered mare's breast, with a carved head and a runic curse: he is to be "every man's *níðingr* and nowhere be in company with proper men" (p. 29).
  - Níð "always conveys contempt, and its purpose is to expel the person concerned from the social community as unworthy" (p. 29).
  - A graffito c. 1300 on the cloister between the archbishop's house and Niðaróss cathedral names Laurentius Kálfsson "Peter's anus". It is written níð, by "people who could read and write" (p. 81).
- **The Christian turn.** In Þorvalds þáttr, Þorvaldr kills two men for verses saying the bishop bore his children. "I was not going to have them call us *ragir*" (p. 54). Here *ragr* covers both partners, which is the Christian sense.
  - The bishop defuses the verse by rereading *bera* ("bear") as "carry" (p. 54).
  - He also preaches: "a Christian should not himself pursue revenge, ... he must for God's sake endure backbiting and malice" (p. 54).
  - The saga audience "had no hope of meeting the demand" (p. 78).

### Bodies, women, castration (chs. 4-5)
- **Mare slander.** Þormóðr kills four Greenland brothers because "they reckoned that I went together with men in the same way as mares with stallions" (p. 71).
- ***Klámhögg* (the shame-stroke).** A *klámhögg* on the buttocks stands beside castration in Grágás's list of major wounds. Both take away manhood (p. 68).
- **Being struck by a woman.** "It is shameful for a man to be struck or wounded by a woman, because he cannot avenge himself. It was an infamous deed to use force against a woman" (p. 76).
  - Auðr hits Eyjólfr with his own bribe-purse. "You may remember as long as you live ... that a woman has struck you" (p. 76).
  - His man stops him killing her, because that would be a *níðingsverk* (p. 76). In Hárbarðsljóð Þórr is mocked: "you committed *klæki* ... when you thrashed women" (p. 77).
- **Last word.** "A man was a man only as long as he had the strength, courage and virility to be so" (p. 87). Egill, old and blind, is laughed at by women (p. 86).
- **Women in men's roles are praised.** Auðr the Deep-minded heads a settler family as the law allowed (pp. 21-22). The Laxdæla woman puts on breeches and kills her husband with the author's approval (p. 22). A valkyrie can be a warrior "yet at the same time remain a woman in the full sense of the word" (p. 22).
  - The motive "by no means evokes contempt", but "it is a feature to be overcome ... Even for valkyries, marriage is the proper outcome" (p. 23).
  - **Þornbjörg** (Hrólfs saga Gautrekssonar, pp. 69-70) is the mirror case. A princess "who ... once and for all wants to be a man", she rules as "king" and blinds or castrates her suitors. Her father agrees to Hrólfr making war on her. Ketill strikes her with a klámhögg, aimed at "the man's role she has adopted. He despises this role, but not necessarily herself, since she is after all a woman" (p. 69). In the end she "hands over her weapons to her father, and settles down to embroidery" (p. 70).
  - The converse is "rendered impossible": "the mere suggestion of a man in a female role was enough to symbolize unmanliness" (p. 23). In Þrymskviða, Þórr's bridal disguise includes keys "clinking" at his loins (p. 23).
- **Castration in the north is how enemies are unmade.**
  - In 1135 Magnús was blinded, maimed and gelded "in such a way that he could never call himself a king again" (p. 82).
  - In 1228 Sturla Sighvatsson had two priests gelded on Grímsey (p. 82).
  - He later had his cousin Órækja blinded and gelded at Surtshellir, telling the torturer "to think of Arnbjorg", Órækja's wife (pp. 83-84). The book prints the date as 1226; the event is usually dated 1236. Check before use.
  - Mutilating captives also left them "unable to beget sons to act as new claimants and avengers" (p. 81).
  - "Castration of a conquered enemy can be likened to the use of *níð*. In both cases the intention is to make a no-man of a man" (p. 84).
- **The berserk is the counter-figure to the farmer.** Bjorn the pale is a sorcerer whom iron does not bite. He takes farmers' wives and daughters, and "the berserk motive is related to the motive of *níð*" (p. 46).
- **Seiðr.** In Gísla saga, Þorgrímr nef is a *seiðskratti*, "a man who practises *ergi*". He is hired into the plot to kill Vésteinn, and his seiðr later stops the outlaw Gísli from finding help anywhere in Iceland. He works it "with all possible *ergi* and devilry" (*skelmiskapr*), phrasing that recalls Snorri's Óðinn (pp. 63-64). Beyond this and p. 19, the book only cites Strömbäck's *Sejd* (p. 94).

### Answers to the brief's questions, in short
- **Argr, ragr, ergi:** a quality, not an act. It is sexual at root, and cowardice is its most important sense; the two cannot be separated. Of women, *org* means lustful, not unmanned.
- **Níðingr:** the social result of failing to answer níð.
- **Tunguníð and tréníð:** spoken and carved níð are the same offence in law. The carved form carries lesser outlawry. The three words carry full outlawry and a right to kill, and that right belongs to the man insulted.
- **Placement:** the book gives no positive status for a man called *argr* except "no-man". He is not outlawed either: he becomes a *níðingr*, socially dead. Under the Hednalag he is unfit to swear an oath or bear witness (p. 31). Women do have a defined legal and social place: keys, the indoors, and Auðr's headship. Any placement for a trans woman runs through being counted among women, not through a third status.

**Senna duel: proposed structure (row 4).**
- **Who and where.** It opens when someone shouts *ergi* (`tm_seidr.0003`). It can also be challenged at a feast, or forced by a níð-pole. There are two speakers; the court watches, and its opinion of each speaker matters.
- **Rounds.** Three rounds of capping. In each round the aggressor speaks a half-stanza and the defender caps it; the roles alternate.
  - **Plain insult** (*fullréttisorð*). A strong roll on diplomacy plus prowess. If it wins at a thing, the target gets `tm_right_to_kill` against the speaker.
  - **Double-edged verse** (*hálfréttisorð*). A diplomacy plus learning roll. It wins less outright, but it is legally safe, and the hall hears both meanings.
  - **Reread** (the bishop's move, p. 54). A learning roll. It cancels the opponent's round by turning the insult into praise.
  - **Own it.** Bæddel characters only, and only against *ergi*/seiðr insults. An automatic draw that gives piety: "*bera* means bear. I did."
  - **Steel.** Ends the duel at once and starts single combat, as in row 2.
- **Scoring.** Best of three.
  - The winner gains prestige: 50 at a feast, 100 at a thing.
  - The loser carries "what he has been called" (`tm_named_modifier`) until he answers within a year.
- **Escalation.** A loss against kin costs the winner opinion with that kin, as in Broddi's apology (p. 36). Insulting a sister of the faith gives same-faith negative opinion.
