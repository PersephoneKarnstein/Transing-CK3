# So Wai Man, *A Study of Eunuchs in Tang and Northern Song China* (2000): design reading

**Citation (verified from the title pages, PDF pp. 1–5).** SO Wai Man [Chinese given name not printed on the title pages; 蘇偉文 is unconfirmed], *A Study of Eunuchs in Tang and Northern Song China* 唐與北宋宦官研究. Thesis for the degree of **Master of Philosophy** (not a PhD), Department of Chinese, Faculty of Arts, The University of Hong Kong, August 2000. Supervisor Dr HUI Chun Hing 許振興. Two volumes, written in Chinese. HKU Scholars Hub: https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/128656.

**What was read.** Table of contents in full; read in full: ch. 2 §1 (Tang palace offices), ch. 14 (Yizong 859–873), ch. 15 (Xizong 873–888), ch. 16 (Zhaozong 888–904, incl. §1 on eunuchs' fictive sons and the 903 purge), ch. 17 §§1–3 (Song offices and Taizu's controls), ch. 26 (conclusion), the appendix rows and notes for 859–888, and the Tang and Song lineage tables (pp. 171–194, 580–606, skimmed for adopted daughters and wives). **Note on scope.** The thesis is a political narrative, organised reign by reign. It has no chapter on recruitment, castration or the trade; those topics appear only in passing (see §1.1). Its thesis: Tang eunuchs became a disaster and Northern Song eunuchs did not because Tang eunuchs gained **independent military power** (the Shence armies) (abstract, PDF p. 3).

## 1. Argument and evidence (Tang)

### 1.1 Origins and recruitment (thin in this thesis)
- So has **no section on castration, the trade, or "tribute" eunuchs**. The only recruitment case read is Gao Lishi: in 698 he "was presented to Empress Wu by the Lingnan pacification commissioner Li Qianli" (被嶺南討擊使李千里進奉給武太后), was expelled for a small fault, and got back into the palace because the eunuch Gao Yanfu "took him in as a fictive son" (收養為假子) (p. 19). His birth mother, lost when he was a child, was later found by the Lingnan governor and brought to Chang'an (p. 21). So the southern-tribute route is attested, but only through this one famous case.
- Yang Fugong is the plainest case of a boy cut *because* he was adopted into a eunuch house: 「以父，幼為宦者」, "because of his [adoptive] father, he became a eunuch as a child" (*Jiu Tang shu* 184, quoted in appendix n. 27, p. 694). His birth surname was Lin (p. 153).
- Numbers: by 705–706 there were "more than 3,000 eunuchs, over 1,000 given supernumerary posts of rank seven and above" (p. 13); under Xuanzong "eunuchs of yellow robe and above, 3,000; in purple and scarlet, over 1,000" (p. 21). In 903 only "thirty young and weak ones in yellow robes were kept to sweep" (p. 161).
- **Why rulers trusted eunuchs.** So's own explanation: because eunuchs are 「刑餘之人，本身無篡位的可能」, "people left over from punishment, who cannot themselves usurp the throne", rulers found them less threatening than ministers, generals, kin or in-laws. He calls this their quality of "dispelling [the ruler's] suspicion" (釋疑) (p. 19). This is a modern historian's gloss, though it repeats the classical view.

### 1.2 Naming and classification
- So does not discuss "not-man" or "woman" language at all. The thesis has no gender analysis. The hostile vocabulary it quotes is the ordinary vocabulary of the histories:
  - 刑餘之人, "people left over from punishment" (pp. 19, 149);
  - 閹豎, "castrated boys/servants" (Wei Zheng to Taizong, p. 10); 宦豎, 凶豎 (p. 160); 閹人 (Yao Chong's "ten points": "the post of the throat and tongue came out of the mouths of castrates", 喉舌之任出閹人之口, p. 19);
  - 家奴, "household slaves": Wenzong "controlled by household slaves" (受制家奴) (pp. 3, 117);
  - 北司 vs 南司, "the Northern Office" (eunuchs) vs "the Southern Office" (the regular bureaucracy): Meng Zhaotu in 881: 「天下者，高祖、太宗之天下，非北司之天下」, "the realm is the realm of Gaozu and Taizong, not of the Northern Office" (p. 143). He was drowned for it.
  - 內人, "inner persons", used by censors protesting eunuchs as postal commissioners in 863 (p. 139).
  - Li Maozhen in 892 calls Yang Fugong "one boy-servant" (一豎) (p. 157); Zhaozong's officials speak of "the four great evils" (四大惡) of 902 (p. 161).
- Kin terms run the other way too: princes and princesses called Gao Lishi "grandpa" (阿翁), the imperial sons-in-law called him "father" (爺), and the crown prince called him "second brother" (二兄) (p. 21). The boy emperor Xizong called Tian Lingzi "father" (呼田令孜為"父") (p. 142). Yang Fugong signed himself "the elder statesman who settled the succession" (定策國老) and called Zhaozong "an ungrateful protégé" (負心門生) (p. 157).

### 1.3 Adoption: eunuch lineages (ch. 16 §1, pp. 149–155; lineage tables pp. 171–194)
- **Rule of 791.** *Tang huiyao* 65: 「內侍省五品已上，許養一子，仍以同姓者，初養日不得過十歲」, "palace eunuchs of rank five and above may adopt one son, of the same surname, not over ten years old when first adopted" (p. 150). So reads three rules: rank five or higher; same surname; age ten or under.
- **None of the three was enforced** before or after 791 (pp. 150–154):
  - *One son*: dozens of epitaphs list four, five, six fictive sons, some for eunuchs of rank eight or seven (p. 150–153).
  - *Same surname*: adoptees simply took the adoptive surname (冒所養姓). Yang Sixu was born Su; Gao Lishi born Feng; Ju Wenzhen became Liu Zhenliang; Yang Fuguang born Qiao; Yang Fugong born Lin; Tian Lingzi born Chen (p. 153).
  - *Ten or under*: grown soldiers were adopted. Yu Chao'en adopted the general Shang Kegu "for his courage" and renamed him Yu Zhide (p. 153); Yang Fuguang adopted Yang Shouliang, a former bandit "over seven feet tall, the colour of iron" (p. 153); Tian Lingzi adopted Song Wentong, a Shence commander he had meant to kill ("on seeing him, gladly adopted him instead", 既見，乃欣然更養為子) and renamed him Tian Yanbin. This is **Li Maozhen**, later lord of Fengxiang (pp. 153, 194). In 884 Tian adopted Wang Jian, Han Jian, Zhang Zao, Jin Hui and Li Shitai together with their thousands of men as the "Five Imperial-Escort Divisions" (隨駕五都) (p. 154).
- **Motive.** Eunuchs adopted for heirs and old age: an epitaph says 「老而無子曰悲」, "to be old and without a son is grief" (p. 154). But multiple adoption was also a way to build power (p. 154).
- **Eunuch sons and non-eunuch sons in one house.** A eunuch's fictive sons could be eunuchs or not; houses had both. Chen Zhongsheng (d. 754) had eunuch son Chen Xianfu and non-eunuch sons; Yang Zhilian (d. 806) had five eunuch sons and one non-eunuch, Yang Hengding, a soldier; Tian Lingzi had the eunuch Tian Kuangyou and the non-eunuch warlords (p. 155). The non-eunuch sons were the ones who turned on their fathers: Wang Jian killed Tian Lingzi (893); Yang Shouli (Li Shunjie) was set against Yang Fugong (891) (pp. 155–157).
- **Adopted daughters, wives, and eunuch intermarriage.** Tang eunuchs also "adopted daughters and took wives, and could remarry if the wife died, quite like ordinary men; there is a strong sense of 'making a family'" (有濃厚的"成家"意味在內) (p. 154). Eunuch houses married each other through **adopted daughters**: Yang Zhilian's wife was the adopted daughter of the eunuch Liu Shouzhi; Liu Yingrun's wife Yang Ting was the adopted daughter of the eunuch Yang Weiliang; Qiu Wenyi's wife was the adopted daughter of the eunuch Wang Jinxing, and of Qiu Wenyi's own six adopted daughters, five married eunuchs (pp. 154, 185). An epitaph praises the match: 「惟仇氏、惟王氏，家聲赫弈，枝派蟬聯」, "the Qiu and the Wang, houses of shining name, branches linked like cicadas" (p. 155). So marks adopted daughters with # in his tables and lists more (pp. 171–194).
- **Long lineages.** The Yang of Hongnong: Yang Yanzuo → Yang Zhilian (Left Shence commander 801–806) → Yang Qinyi (Commissioner, then commander) → three sons Yang Xuanyi (Commissioner in the 860s), Yang Xuanjia (Left commander c. 863–866/9), Yang Xuanshi (Right commander, 870s) → Yang Fugong (son of Xuanyi) and Yang Fuguang (son of Xuanjia) → dozens of sons named Shou-. "The Yang were a brilliant eunuch house" (p. 154). Liu Honggui → Liu Xingshen (commander, enthroned Xizong in 873) → Liu Zunli (d. 868) (pp. 151, 154). Chen Zhongsheng's father, grandfather and great-grandfather were all eunuchs (p. 149).
- Shared generation characters mark adoptive brothers: the Shou- sons of Yang Fuguang, "dozens, all prefects and generals" (p. 153, 177).
- Yang Fugong 「諸假子皆為節度使、刺史，又養宦官子六百人，皆為監軍」, "all his fictive sons were governors and prefects; he also raised six hundred eunuch sons, all army supervisors"; called "the princelings of the outer residences" (外宅郎君) (p. 152). The minister Kong Wei criticised him for "raising many strong men as fictive sons" (多養壯士為假子) and was robbed for it (p. 157).

### 1.4 Palace and armies, 860–907 (chs. 14–16; appendix table pp. 607–)
- **Two offices.** The Shence Army Commanders (神策軍護軍中尉, Left and Right) held troops; the two Commissioners of Military Affairs (樞密使) held documents and access. So's main argument for this period: the commanders, not the commissioners, chose emperors. Liu Xingshen was "honest and dared not meddle" as a Commissioner under Wuzong yet enthroned Xizong once he held the Shence army; Yang Fugong did nothing in 873 as Commissioner but enthroned Zhaozong in 888 as commander (p. 139).
- Under Yizong the Commissioners sat in on the Yanying audiences with the chief ministers. Minister Du Cong said 「內外之臣，事猶一體，宰相、樞密共參國政」, "inner and outer officials are one body; ministers and Commissioners together take part in government" (p. 138). Minister Yang Shou got his post by claiming kinship with the Left commander Yang Xuanjia (敘宗姓) and was pushed out when he refused his requests (pp. 138–139).
- **Holders in 867** (appendix, p. 623; many marked "uncertain" 疑): Left commander **Yang Xuanjia** (uncertain); Right commander **Ximen Jixuan or Han Wenyue** (uncertain); Commissioners **Yang Xuanyi** and Yang Qing (uncertain). Yang Xuanyi died in 869 and his adoptive son **Yang Fugong** was "recalled from mourning to be Commissioner" (起復為樞密使), succeeding his father in office (appendix n. 27, p. 694). Liu Xingshen was in retirement in 868 and was Left commander by 873 (n. 28, p. 696). Note his adoptive son Liu Zunli died in 868, aged 53. Yizong was gravely ill for about half of 867 (p. 139).
- **Who enthroned whom (860–907):**
  - 873: Left commander Liu Xingshen and Right commander Han Wenyue made Prince of Pu (Li Yan, aged 12) heir; he became Xizong. Later sources say they "killed the elder and set up the younger to profit their power" (殺長立幼以利其權) (pp. 139, 141).
  - 873–885: Tian Lingzi, who had "slept and risen with" Xizong as a prince, became Commissioner, then Shence commander; Xizong "handed government entirely to him" and called him father (p. 142). Tian confiscated merchants' goods into the inner treasury and had protesters beaten to death (p. 142). The remonstrant Hou Changye was made to kill himself in the Palace Domestic Service for criticising him (p. 142).
  - 880–885: Huang Chao takes Chang'an; Tian takes Xizong to Chengdu, where Tian's "fictive elder brother" Chen Jingxuan was governor (p. 142). The eunuch Yang Fuguang, Yang Fugong's cousin by adoption, wins the war by bringing in Li Keyong, and is ranked first in merit (p. 144).
  - 885–886: Tian's quarrel with Wang Chongrong (started by Tian's fictive son Tian Kuangyou) drives Xizong into a second flight; Zhu Mei sets up a puppet emperor Li Yun. Tian hands the Shence command to Yang Fugong and goes to his brother in Sichuan (p. 144).
  - 888: Xizong dying; ministers wanted Prince of Ji (Li Bao), "older and worthy", but Shence commander Yang Fugong alone named Prince of Shou (Li Jie), who became Zhaozong (p. 145).
  - 891–894: Zhaozong turns Yang Fugong's own fictive son Yang Shouli against him (renamed Li Shunjie and given the keys of the six armies); Yang Fugong flees to his adoptive nephew Yang Shouliang at Xingyuan; Li Maozhen uses this to seize Shannan; Yang Fugong is caught and beheaded (pp. 157–158; date 892 or 894, p. 152).
  - 893: Wang Jian, Tian Lingzi's fictive son, takes Chengdu and kills Chen Jingxuan and Tian Lingzi (p. 156).
  - 895–897: Zhaozong raises princely armies outside the Shence; Han Jian (another of Tian's fictive sons) forces him to disband them and kills eleven princes (p. 158).
  - 900: Left commander Liu Jishu and Right commander Wang Zhongxian, with Commissioners Wang Yanfan and Xue Qiwo, depose Zhaozong, lock him in the Shaoyang compound ("sealed the lock with molten iron", 鎔鐵固之), and enthrone the crown prince. Liu Jishu "drew on the ground with a silver staff" listing the emperor's faults (p. 159).
  - 901: Generals Sun Deyi and others kill them and restore Zhaozong. The chief minister Cui Yin asks to give the Shence armies to ministers; the generals refuse: 「未聞書生為軍主」, "we have never heard of a scholar commanding an army" (p. 160). Han Quanhui and Zhang Yanhong become commanders.
  - 901–903: "Southern and Northern Offices each formed parties with the warlords" (南、北司各樹黨結藩鎮) (p. 160): eunuchs with Li Maozhen at Fengxiang, Cui Yin with Zhu Quanzhong. Cui Yin asks Zhaozong "to kill all eunuchs and let palace women run the inner offices" (盡誅宦官，但以宮人掌內諸司事); the eunuchs answer by placing literate beauties around the emperor as informers (pp. 160–161).
- **The purge of 903.** Back in Chang'an (1st month, 903), Cui Yin and Zhu Quanzhong drove several hundred eunuchs into the Palace Domestic Service and killed them all, "the cries of the wronged heard inside and out"; eunuchs abroad were to be arrested and killed where they were; only "thirty young and weak ones in yellow robes" were kept to sweep (p. 161). Zhaozong "wrote an elegy to mourn" the commander Diwu Kefan as innocent (p. 161).
- **Survivors.** Several provincial army-supervisor eunuchs were hidden by the warlords, who "killed other prisoners in their place to satisfy the edict": **Zhang Chengye** (Hedong, with Li Keyong), Zhang Juhan (Youzhou), Cheng Kuangrou (Qinghai), Yu Quanhu (Xichuan, with Wang Jian) and the retired Yan Zunmei (Huainan) (p. 170, ch. 16 n. 109). The Five Dynasties are not studied; the thesis jumps from 907 to 960.
- So's conclusion (from the abstract and ch. 16): the difference between Tang and Song lies in **independent military power**. Once eunuchs commanded armies they could make and unmake emperors; once the Shence armies passed to warlords, the eunuchs could be massacred.

### 1.5 Northern Song controls (ch. 17 §§1, 3; ch. 20 §6; ch. 22 §2; ch. 26)
- **Offices.** Song eunuchs sat in two offices: the Palace Domestic Service 內侍省 ("front office") and the Inner Palace Service 入內內侍省 ("rear office", closer to the family). A new eunuch began as "little yellow gate" (小黃門) and could rise to Chief Superintendent (都都知) (p. 195).
- **Numbers.** *Songshi*'s preface: "When Taizu first settled the realm, the Lateral Courts had no more than fifty attendants" (掖庭給事不過五十人) (p. 200). Quotas: Renzong 1053, Palace Domestic Service ranks from Palace Servitor to Yellow Gate "fixed at 180" (以一百八十人為額) (p. 345); Shenzong kept 180 (p. 427); Zhezong 1087 cut it to 100 (pp. 222–227 notes). In 1059 Renzong ordered that because "the Inner Palace Service has too many eunuchs, for now stop bringing in adopted sons" (其權住進養子入內) (p. 345). That edict shows that in Song the usual way into the palace was **as a eunuch's adopted son**.
- **Adoption law (Taizu).**
  - 966: a eunuch may adopt **one** son only if he is **over thirty** and holds office; if his own adoptive father is alive he may not adopt; existing multiple adoptions are grandfathered. And: 「內外士庶不得將童男養為宦者及規求財利…違者不赦」, "officials and commoners, inside and outside the capital, may not raise boys as eunuchs or seek profit by it… offenders not pardoned" (p. 200).
  - 971: because "eunuchs' adopted sons often quarrel over property and go to law" (內侍養子多爭財起訟), every eunuch must report existing adopted sons "by name and age to the Court of Imperial Ceremonies (宣徽院), to be **entered in a register and kept**" (置籍收係). Future adoptions need a petition to that court and an issued certificate (憑據). Secret adoption: anyone may denounce it, **death** for the offender, and 100,000 cash to the informer from the offender's property (p. 200).
  - So's explanation: Taizu acted because "Tang was not long past, and he took warning from it" (去唐不遠，有所懲也) (p. 200).
- Song eunuchs also had wives and adopted heirs; the Song lineage table lists wives (one posthumously ennobled) and guesses at adopted daughters behind marriage ties (pp. 580–606). In 1085 Empress Dowager Gao had the eunuch Liang Weijian's **wife** secretly sew a yellow robe for a ten-year-old, ready for the boy Zhezong's sudden accession (p. 463).
- **Confiscation came with disgrace, not with death.** In 969 the eunuch Zhang Yu was beaten, exiled with his household, and "his property registered and confiscated" (籍其財) for factional ties (p. 200). In 1097 the adopted sons of Empress Gao's eunuchs Liang Weijian and Chen Yan were struck off and exiled to Hainan, and the two eunuchs' houses, land and money were "all searched out, registered and confiscated" (並根括籍沒入官) (p. 477).
- **Gendered epithets (Song).** Shenzong refused to promote the eunuch Cheng Fang: "attendants wear sable tails; we choose them for their gentleness" (侍中珥貂，取其溫柔) (p. 427). Under Huizong, the eunuch general Tong Guan was nicknamed **"the Old-Woman Chancellor"** (媪相), and Liang Shicheng "the Hidden Chancellor" (隱相) (pp. 544, 578). So reports both nicknames without comment. Outside So, the sources pair 媪相 against Cai Jing's 公相 "Gentleman Chancellor"; check before using.
- **So's conclusion (ch. 26, pp. 577–579).** Song eunuchs meddled too (Wang Ji'en at Taizong's accession in 976, Zhou Huaizheng in 1020, Tong Guan), but they had neither an independent army nor a dedicated eunuch office like the Tang Commissioners, so they rose and fell with their patrons. Song eunuchs adopted heirs, adopted daughters, married and intermarried like Tang ones, but Song never produced lineages "of shining name, branches linked like cicadas". "Adoption needs a mechanism of lasting power to work its strength" (p. 579).

### 1.6 Gaps
- No Five Dynasties chapter (907–960) and nothing on Liao or Jin eunuchs.
- No discussion of castration, the knife, the southern trade, or Fujian/Lingnan boys beyond Gao Lishi.
- No gender analysis. Its "naming" evidence is incidental. Use Rideout, Dale, Tsai or Chiang for that.
- For eunuch marriage and adoption, So cites 雷家驥、黃淑梅〈唐代宦官的婚姻與收養關係〉(parts 1–2) as the specialist study (p. 7 n. 16). That article is worth getting.

## 2. Design implications

### 2.1 Answers to the design questions
- **Palace faction, 867–907.** The source supports a two-seat model in which the **Shence commanders (中尉, Left and Right) decide the succession** and the **two Commissioners (樞密使) control access and documents**. In So's words, the commanders held "the initiative and the decisive power in choosing a new ruler" (p. 139); when commanders and Commissioners disagreed (826–827, 859), "the Commissioners lost and were killed" (p. 579). Successions: 873 Liu Xingshen and Han Wenyue → Xizong (aged 12, a younger son); 888 Yang Fugong → Zhaozong, over the ministers' choice; 900 Liu Jishu and Wang Zhongxian depose Zhaozong; 901 restoration; 903 massacre; 904 Zhu Quanzhong murders Zhaozong; 907 end of Tang.
- **A "eunuch register."** For the Tang, **nothing in So**. The only Tang rule is the unenforced 791 adoption edict. Tang censors (御史) were outer-court officials who *protested* eunuchs taking their duties (863, p. 139), so a Tang censor carrying a eunuch register runs against the record. What *is* attested: (a) the eunuch-run Lateral Courts bureau (掖庭局) kept the **registers of palace women** (宮人簿帳) (p. 9), so palace women and eunuchs were both written down by eunuch clerks; (b) the **Song register of 971**: eunuchs' adopted sons entered by name and age at the Court of Imperial Ceremonies (宣徽院), with secret adoption punishable by death (p. 200); (c) the 966 ban on raising boys as eunuchs for profit (p. 200).
- **How eunuch adoption worked (Tang).** Adoption into a eunuch house meant taking its surname and its generation character. The house could include eunuch sons, non-eunuch soldier sons, adopted daughters married to other eunuch houses, and wives. Office passed down the line, as Yang Xuanyi's Commissionership passed to Yang Fugong in 869. Adults were adopted to bind armed followings. The legal limits (one son, same surname, under ten) were ignored.
- **Song limits.** 966: one adopted son, only for a eunuch over thirty holding office. 971: registration and certificates, secret adoption punishable by death. 1053: a quota of 180; 1059: a freeze on bringing in adopted sons; 1087: a quota of 100. Small numbers under Taizu ("no more than fifty").
- **Named characters for 867–907.** Yang Xuanjia, Yang Xuanyi, Yang Fugong, Yang Fuguang, Liu Xingshen, Han Wenyue, Ximen Jixuan, Tian Lingzi, Tian Kuangyou, Chen Jingxuan (Tian's brother), Liu Jishu, Wang Zhongxian, Han Quanhui, Zhang Yanhong, Diwu Kefan, Zhang Chengye. Tian Lingzi's fictive sons include **Wang Jian** (later ruler of Former Shu), **Han Jian**, and **Li Maozhen** (born Song Wentong, renamed Tian Yanbin).
