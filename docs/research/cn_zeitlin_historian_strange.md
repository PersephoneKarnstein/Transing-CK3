## Corrections and checks

1. **The "perverse Confucian rectification of names" is verified, p. 102.** The reading list marked it *unverified* (via Sommer). It is Zeitlin's phrase for a man in the Qing tale "The Human Prodigy" (*Renyao*) castrating a captured female impersonator and renaming him: "Ma's action can thus be seen as a perverse Confucian 'rectification of names'" (p. 102). Note what it rectifies: in the eyes of Ma and the law, the prodigy's crime "was that outward reality did not conform to the inward reality" (p. 102). The knife makes the body fit the name Ma chooses. That is the move the Rectified Names tenet refuses.
2. **The figure in that tale is not a trans woman, and the narrator must never treat him as one.** Wang Double Joy is a rapist who dresses as a woman to reach women's quarters, a disciple of Sang Chong's gang (pp. 98–99). The tale is the classic "deceiver" plot. Use it only as the hostile story the Way has to answer.
3. **Sang Chong is outside the window.** The case "reportedly came to trial in Beijing during the Chenghua reign period (1465–87)" (pp. 110–11). No in-world character before 1465 may cite it. The in-window form of the same slander is the Southern Song tale of Companion of Joy (see §1).
4. **Confirms the Five Phases note.** *Taiping guangji* 367 has "seventeen items" under Human Prodigies, "only four pertain to male impersonators" (p. 253 n. 11), i.e. women passing as men. This matches `cn_wuxingzhi_sex_change_records.md`.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **What *renyao* meant.** The word first appears in Xunzi, "human prodigies" set against heavenly ones (p. 104). Later it gained "an additional, more specialized usage: an impersonator of a member of the opposite sex" (p. 104). Its first use in that sense is the *Nan shi* verdict on Lou Cheng. From "at least the Northern Song on" the general and the specialised senses "coexist" (p. 253 n. 11). A related word, *yaoren* 妖人 "prodigious humans", "usually refers to rebels and sorcerers" (n. 11). For the game: *renyao* is a learned omen word; *yaoren* is what an official calls a dangerous preacher.
- **The old reading blames yin, whichever way the change runs.** Early interpretations "are always one-sided: sex changes are invariably blamed on an excess of yin, on a disorder in the female principle, regardless of the direction of the change" (p. 108). The Ming–Qing shift (a girl becoming a boy is cause for joy; "men who become women are at best tolerated, if not punished", p. 108) is "a later Ming and Qing development" (p. 107). Inside 867–1453 the old reading rules: the omen falls on the women's side of the world.
- **Motive decides the label.** In the later accounts, "the ethical motivation for a female's change in sex ultimately determines whether she is classified as a human prodigy or as an exemplary woman" (p. 109). In Pu's anecdote of a girl struck by a meteor, the family "did not consider her a prodigy but secretly rejoiced" (p. 106): the family's welcome decides the verdict, not the body.
- **Women in men's dress: historians against, romance for.** Two traditions: early historiography "is hostile to male impersonators, regardless of their motives"; ballads, plays and fiction treat them "as virtuous and even heroic figures" (p. 116). Even in the histories, female impersonation (a man dressed as a woman) is "a capital crime", male impersonation "at worst, a misdemeanor" (p. 116).
- **Temporary is virtue; permanent is prodigy.** Masquerading as a man was admirable "as long as the shift was temporary"; otherwise it revealed "yin's forbidden desire to appropriate yang's privileges" (p. 118). Lou Cheng "sought to alter her status permanently", which is why she was a prodigy (p. 119). The *Nan shi* verdict, in Zeitlin's translation: "Yin's desire to be yang cannot be realized; hence the matter will [always] come to light" (p. 117). Yet the historians also give her a voice: "Isn't it a shame to possess such skills and still be obliged to resume the identity of an old woman?" (p. 117). By the late Ming she is praised as a "hero among women" (pp. 117–19).
- **Tang precedent for leaving by the Way.** Xie Xiao'e, in Li Gongzuo's Tang tale (author c. 770–850), lived disguised as a man for years to avenge her father and husband; "After accomplishing her mission, she shaved her head and became a nun" (p. 118). The *Xin Tang shu* put her among the exemplary women (p. 118). Religion is where a woman goes once her crossing is done.
- **The in-window deceiver tale.** In the 13th-century Southern Song *Luchuang xinhua* 綠窗新話, the maid Companion of Joy (Banxi 伴喜) says: "Though I am a woman, both forms are present in me" (p. 110). She seduces her mistress, is caught, "arrested and exiled". A Yuan retelling (*Zuiweng tanlu*) lets her escape and ends: "they cannot be too careful in examining their maidservants" (p. 110). Both versions read the crossing as "a willful act" of seduction (p. 110). This is the Song–Yuan root of the "man in disguise" slander a daughter would face.
- **Sang Chong (Ming, for the record).** A gang of men "who dressed as women with bound feet, made their living plying female trades such as sewing" and raped women of good families (p. 111). The throne recommended death "by slow slicing for committing a crime akin to the 'ten perversities'" (p. 111). Every account but Pu's ends in "unmasking and public execution", "the conventional resolution" of a dislocation in gender (p. 114).
- **Inspection as verdict.** In the Qing tale, villagers "got a group of village women to probe Double Joy's genitals through his clothing" (p. 100); in Ming fiction an official tests a "hermaphrodite nun" with oil and a dog and has her executed (p. 110). The period answer to a doubtful body is a search of it.
- **Political allegory.** Pu's comment on the castration, "Children like to play with crabs, but they fear the claws, so they break them off" (p. 104), reads gender order as state order. Zeitlin: "the correlation between state and gender hierarchy underlying the resolution" (p. 105). The couple even "illicitly parallel the imperial household in having their own personal eunuch" (p. 105).

## 3. In-world loc lines

- Tenet (faith's voice): "They rectify names with a knife: they cut until the body fits the word they chose. We rectify with a name, and wait for the world to fit it."
- Historian (hostile): "Yin's desire to be yang cannot be realised. It will always come to light." (*Nan shi*, after Zeitlin p. 117; used of any crossing.)
- Prefect (hostile): "This is no prodigy. It is a *yaoren*, and those who follow her are a rising."
- Household (hostile): "Have you not read of Companion of Joy? Look well at your maids."
- Sister (reply): "She hid nothing. She told you her name before witnesses."
- Scholar (hostile): "Mulan went home and put her dress back on. This one does not mean to go back."
- Mother (friendly): "I call it a birth. Write that down, if you must write something."
