## 1. The argument in brief

- **Three interpreters.** Pechilis reads one saint through:
  - her own poetry (c. 550);
  - her 12th-c. biographer Cēkkiḻār;
  - present-day festivals.
- She argues that the biography "very much overshadows" the poetry in public memory (pp. 1–3).
- **Date.** "Most scholars date her to ca. 550 CE" (p. 1). The majority view is 550–600, in the "post-Caṅkam period" (p. 14).
- **Gender.** The poet never names herself a woman. She signs only as "Kāraikkālpēy", "the ghoul from Kāraikkāl", and *pēy* is not gendered (p. 8).
  - The first stanza of her "Decade-1" describes a **female** ghoul (*peṇpēy*) in the third person. Medieval tradition read it as a self-portrait, and it shaped her bronzes (p. 8).
  - "Gender was unambiguously added" to her identity between the late 11th and mid 12th centuries (p. 14).

- **The *Periya Purāṇam*'s first hearing.** Cēkkiḻār wrote it in Chidambaram. It was paraded on an elephant and read aloud over a year-long ceremony (p. 82, from a biography of him written two centuries later).

### 2.2 The transformation (Cēkkiḻār, "Story" vv. 45–58; pp. 202–204)

1. **The husband.**
   - After the miracle mangoes, her husband thinks her a "local goddess" (*aṇaṅku*) and sails away (vv. 31–32).
   - He remarries in the Pāṇṭiya country and names his daughter after her (vv. 35–38).
   - Her kin carry her to him in a palanquin. He, his new wife and the child prostrate themselves at her feet (vv. 41–45).
   - He says she "was no longer human" but "a good and great goddess" (v. 47).
2. **The request** (v. 49). She asks the Lord: "remove this sack of flesh that bears my beauty only for the sake of this man", and give her "the form of the ghouls who venerate your sacred feet".
3. **The grant** (v. 50). She "obtained what she desired". She sheds her beauty and becomes "a body of bones in the form of a ghoul that is venerated by all".
4. **The world's answer** (vv. 51, 54).
   - Flowers fall and heaven drums.
   - Her kin bow and leave in fear.
   - Strangers run, and she answers: "If the leader of the gods recognizes me, why should I explain this form to the throngs of people who are ignorant of the truth?" (v. 54).
5. **Recognition** (vv. 56–58).
   - On Kailāsa, Umā asks "What is this kind of love?"
   - Śiva answers that she "cherishes us like a mother", and calls her "Mother!"
   - Śiva says "she obtained this exalted form just as she desired" (v. 58).
6. **Her boon** (vv. 60–61). That those who love him with undying love are not reborn; and that she may sit at his feet, singing, while he dances at Ālaṅkāṭu (Tiruvālaṅkāṭu).

### 2.3 Pechilis's reading of the transformation (ch. 4)

- **Chosen, not suffered.** "Although she is transformed from a beauty to a ghoul this occurs voluntarily, not violently" (p. 88).
- **Asked in clarity.**
  - She asks in a state of "great knowledge through her singular focus" (v. 48), unlike her anxious prayer for the mango (pp. 83–84).
  - Her request marks "full spiritual maturity" (p. 83).
- **The body as truth.**
  - Her husband had dismissed her speech. The new body makes her truth "visible for all to see" (pp. 101–102).
  - "The 'other' body of a ghoul is her refusal to remain in the contested and commodified body of a beautiful woman" (p. 99).
- **She stays a woman.** Śiva's word "Mother" is "reaffirming her gendered identity in the ghoul embodiment" (p. 99).
  - Her beauty and marriageability are what she gives up, not her womanhood.
  - The bronzes give her stretched earlobes without earrings (renounced ornaments) and shrivelled breasts (pp. 92, 115).
- **One of only two full bodily transformations.** The other is Nantaṉār, the untouchable who becomes a Brahman (p. 100).
  - In both, "the text calls attention to the contradictions between the bodies they are born with and their exemplary spiritual devotion" (p. 100).
  - The devotional heart "is 'the same' no matter the body", but "not all bodies are 'the same' socially" (p. 101).
- **A hard counter-fact.** In the *Periya Purāṇam*, male devotees who cut or burn are restored, but "women are not restored when they are cut" (p. 101).
- **A domesticated ghoul.**
  - Cēkkiḻār removes the cremation ground and the ghouls from her poems.
  - He also keeps her clear of the Nīli story (a vengeful female *pēy* of nearby Paḻaiyaṉūr).
  - He makes Śiva a beautiful father (pp. 85–89, after Monius).
  - The *pēy* is "not a demon" in his telling, but "god-consciousness" (pp. 87, 89).
- **Her own poetry is more open** (pp. 140–141):
  - the poet "did not call attention to gender";
  - the self-portrait reading is a tradition, not her text;
  - her "I" and "we" invite every hearer to become a servant.

### 2.4 Places

- **Tiruvālaṅkāṭu**, the site of her dance, lies in the north of Tamil country, "in the region of" Kanchipuram (pp. 77–78).
  - It is tied to Pallava claims and to the Śiva–Kāḷī dance contest with the leg raised to the sky (*ūrdhvatāṇḍava*) (pp. 77–79).
  - The present temple carries Chola inscriptions of the first half of the 11th c. A copper-plate grant of Rājendra Chola dates from 1018 (p. 112).
- **Kāraikkāl** is the seaport of her birth, a merchant town (vv. 1–2, p. 199).
- **The four hundred.** The Thanjavur list (SII 2 no. 66; `ind_tanjavur_inscription.md`) includes temple women named Tiruvalangadi* (¶54) and Karaikkal* (¶361). The asterisks mark letters Hultzsch restored.

### 2.5 Pechilis and Raj 2013 (Narayanan, ch. 2)

- **"Appointed daughter."** In Vedic times a sonless man could appoint a daughter as a *putrikā*, "a daughter who functions like a son in ritual matters". She, or her son, could perform funeral rites (p. 54). This is support from the textual tradition for a daughter's ritual succession.
- **Gender of the soul.** "In later centuries, some Hindu texts also explicitly speak of the soul as being beyond gender" (p. 54). No texts or dates are given, so it is not citable for the period.
- **Liberation loosens rules.** When the focus is *mokṣa* rather than *dharma*, "a greater freedom is seen in women's participation in public spaces" (p. 65).
- **Dance history.** The renaming of *dāsī āṭṭam* ("dance of the servants") to Bharata Nāṭyam is 20th c. (p. 64). It confirms §15's ban on reading the modern dance back.
- The rest of the chapter is present-day practice and the diaspora. It was not used.

## 3. Corrections

1. **"A body changed at her own request by the god" is Cēkkiḻār's, c. 1135, not the poet's.**
   - The poet (c. 550) only says she has joined the ghouls (p. 85).
   - Cite as "*Periya Purāṇam*, Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār purāṇam, v. 49–50 (Pechilis 2012, p. 202)".
   - It is still in period for the game (from 1135), and widely known after it.
2. **The change is not between sexes.** She goes from a beautiful wife to a female ghoul, still "Mother" (p. 99). What she sheds is beauty bound to a husband, not womanhood.
3. **Āṇṭāḷ's century.** Pechilis puts Āṇṭāḷ in the "tenth century" (pp. 1, 90). The India notes use 9th c. (Venkatesan). Keep 9th c., and note the disagreement.
4. **Nampi's date** varies even within Pechilis (late 11th c. on p. 13; 10th c. at p. 220 n. 7). Prentiss gives Kulōttuṅga I (1070–1122). Use "late 11th c.".
5. **Pechilis and Raj 2013 has no chapter on Tamil Śaivism, Śrīvaiṣṇavism or temple women.** Only the *putrikā* (p. 54) is usable.

## 4. Design implications

### 4.1 Can the faith claim Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār's transformation as precedent, in its own voice?

**Yes, with three conditions.**

- **Why it fits.**
  - It is the one canonical, in-period Tamil story in which a devotee **asks the god to remake her body**, is answered at once, and is then **recognised** by the god under a kin title.
  - The canon treats the new body as the true one and the old one as a social costume: "she obtained this exalted form just as she desired" (v. 58).
  - The structure pairs her with Nantaṉār, whose body is made to match his worth (p. 100). That gives the faith a two-case argument from the *Periya Purāṇam*: **the Lord changes bodies to fit the devotee's truth, and he does so when asked.**

1. **Faith voice only.** The narrator says what Cēkkiḻār wrote and what Pechilis reads. The narrator never says she was trans, or that Tamils read her story as a change of sex. Her change kept her a woman.
2. **The faith claims the *pattern*, not the saint.** She is venerated today, with festivals and a merchant community that claims her (ch. 5).
   - The Garland honours her ("Ammai sits before the Dancer").
   - It never says she was one of its daughters.
   - No character is her descendant.
3. **The faith must answer the hard reading.**
   - Her change took her *away* from a husband and from beauty. A hostile Śaiva can say "she asked to be *less* a wife, not more a woman".
   - The faith answers in her own words: if the Lord knows the form, why explain it to the crowd (v. 54)?

### 4.2 How it plugs into the gate

- **Step 4 (the dream).** Keep "a sign, not the success".
- **New step 4b, "The Request".** After the dream, the seeker must *ask* aloud, in the temple, for the body (the Kāraikkāl pattern). Then the staff step follows. The faith's line: "She asked, and was given the form she asked for."
- **On success, recognition by a kin word.** The god's "Mother" for Ammai suggests the Garland's success line: the Lord calls her "Daughter". This matches the *tēvaṉār makaḷ* title (Orr) and the "daughters of Rudra" (Goodall).

### 4.3 Events, modifiers, names, holy sites

- **Event "The Sack of Flesh"** (after 1135, Śaiva neighbours). A wandering reciter performs the *Periya Purāṇam*.
  - The daughter hears verse 49 and recognises her own request.
  - Options: piety; inspiration toward the vow; or a temple manager's frown.
- **Event "Why Should I Explain This Form?"** Strangers stare or flee.
  - Option A: quote v. 54 (stress down, small prestige loss).
  - Option B: explain (diplomacy check).
- **Event "The Husband Bows".** A former spouse comes with a new family and kneels.
  - Options: accept the homage; refuse it ("not at my feet; at His"; p. 99); or bless the namesake daughter.
- **Modifier "Sits Before the Dancer".** Only Ammai sits in Śiva's presence among the 63 bronzes (pp. 114, 138). Use it as holy-site flavour for a Tiruvālaṅkāṭu or Kanchipuram site, not as a trait.
- **Holy-site text.**
  - c_kanchipuram: "Near the Pallava city, at the banyan forest of Ālaṅkāṭu, the Mother of Kāraikkāl sits and sings while He dances with His foot to the sky."
  - This supports §15's seat at Kanchipuram over Srirangam on the Śaiva side.
- **Names.**
  - *Ammai* ("mother"; a title, not a given name).
  - *Punitavati* ("pure one"; the saint's own name, so use for invented characters with care).
  - Shrine names from the four hundred: Tiruvalangadi, Karaikkal.
  - *Kāraikkālpēy* only as an insult in hostile mouths.
- **Line.** The *putrikā* (Narayanan p. 54) gives a textual precedent for a daughter who performs a parent's rites. Add it to the "Take as Daughter" defence. Narrator's note: "the law books allowed an appointed daughter".

### 4.4 Reaction lines

- **Faith:** "She asked Him to take the flesh that was for a husband, and He gave her the form she asked. Ask, sister."
- **Hostile Śaiva (orthodox):** "Ammai shed her beauty to leave a husband. You put on a woman's to gain one."
- **Merchant kin (after Story v. 47):** "When I saw she was no longer what she had been, I left. What else could a man do?"
- **Vaiṣṇava:** "Your ghoul mother sings in the burning ground. Our bride wore the garland in Śrīvilliputtūr." (Śrīvilliputtūr as Āṇṭāḷ's birthplace; the temple building is out of period, §15.)
- **Never.**
  - No Nīli story: it involves murder and mass suicide (p. 85).
  - No claim that the saint was a man, or that the poet was secretly male. Pechilis's point is only that the poems do not *state* gender (pp. 8, 141).
