## 1. The argument in brief

### Seiðr and its practitioners (ch. 3)
- **Seiðr belongs to women, and the völva is a travelling professional.** "the primary role in the performance of seiðr was played by women" (p. 71). The völur are peripatetic: Heiðr is hailed "at all the houses she came to" (Völuspá 22). Þorbjǫrg spends the winter going from feast to feast, and when she is done she is "sent for from another house" (pp. 40-41, 72-73). They are paid in gifts and rings, and are feasted above a skald's dignity (p. 73).
- **They are feared, bribable, and never fully trusted.** Price calls their social job "ritual reassurance". They can see the future but are pressed to tell only good news (p. 74). Heiðr in *Hrólfs saga kraka* switches sides for Signý's gold ring. Oddbjǫrg in *Víga-Glúms saga* answers coercion with calamity (pp. 73-75). Their graves lie outside the gates of Niflhel, "by the door of the dead" (p. 73).
- **Men who do seiðr are real, organised, and sometimes royal.** Rǫgnvaldr réttilbeini, Haraldr Finehair's son by the Sámi sorceress Snæfríðr, gathers a following of *seiðmenn* in Haðaland. His brother Eiríkr Blóðøx, "later to be king of York", burns him in his hall "with 80 seiðmenn, and for this deed he was much praised" (pp. 80-81). The grandson Eyvindr kelda attacks Óláfr Tryggvason in a longship "crewed entirely by seiðmenn" (p. 81). Other kings keep seiðmenn in their retinues (p. 81). Vitgeirr seiðmaðr's verse says farmers' sons and wives do seiðr because the king's son does (p. 81).
- **The performer has a choir.** A ring of women sings the *varðlokkur* round the platform. Some völur travel with their own following: Heiðr in *Ǫrvar-Odds saga* brings 15 girls and 15 boys (p. 82). Price glosses *varðlokkur* as either "guardian spirit fastenings" or "guardian spirit enticements" (p. 170). Þorbjǫrg says the spirits "before wanted to turn from us and refused to obey us" until they heard the song (p. 40).
- **The trance is the völva's, not the client's.** In every set-piece account the seeress goes up on the *seiðhjallr* and the questioner waits below for the answer. Heiðr yawns the spirits in. The rite gets "difficult" when she is uncomfortable or coerced (pp. 74-75, 172). Price leaves open whether it was soul-flight or a "state of receptiveness" to summoned spirits (pp. 171-172).

### The high seat and the platform (pp. 39-41, 119-125)
- **High seat and seiðr-platform are two different things.** Scholars since Olrik (1909) have equated the *seiðhjallr* with the hall's high seat (*hásæti*). Price says the link "is however hard to understand", because "in the one account when both a high seat and a seiðhjallr are mentioned (Eiríks saga rauða) they are clearly separate things" (p. 120). The platform is *built* for the rite: Kotkell is said to have had his "large seiðhjallr" constructed (p. 120). It can hold four men, or twelve on four posts (p. 120), and stand on a battlefield under a black tent (Skuld, p. 120).
- **The real sequence in *Eiríks saga* (pp. 39-41).**
  1. Þorbjǫrg is given a high seat with a hen-feather cushion for the feast.
  2. She eats goat's-milk porridge and the hearts of every animal on the farm, with a brass spoon and an ivory-handled knife with a broken point.
  3. She sleeps the night there.
  4. "Late the next day" she gets her tools.
  5. Women ring the *seiðhjallr*, "on top of which was Þorbiorg".
  6. One reluctant Christian girl, Guðríðr, sings the *varðlokkur* she learned from her foster-mother.
  7. The prophecy follows, and Guðríðr's own fate is told as a reward ("health to you, my daughter").
- **Chair pendants are the material trace.** Some sixteen silver, bronze and amber miniature chairs survive from women's graves and hoards, late 9th to early 11th century, in south and east Scandinavia: Birka Bj. 632, 844, 968; Fyrkat 4; Hedeby; Lejre; Öland; Gotland; Danish hoards (pp. 120-124). Price reads them as high seats or Hliðskjálf, "as distinct from the seiðr-platforms" (p. 125). In burials they go "exclusively with women" (p. 125) and never appear in Christian contexts. The Lejre figure on a throne between two ravens is of uncertain sex, and Price declines to name it Óðinn or Freyja (p. 123).
- **Sitting out (*útiseta*).** The other ritual space is the night spent alone outdoors, at a crossroads or under the hanged. The Gulaþing law bans "sitting out to wake up a troll" (pp. 126-127).

### Equipment (pp. 126-168)
- **Costume: treat the *Eiríks saga* outfit with care.** The pieces are a blue (or black) cloak with straps "decorated with stones right down to the hem", glass beads, a black lambskin hood lined with white catskin, a tinder-wood belt with a pouch of charms, hairy calfskin shoes with tin-knobbed laces, and catskin gloves white and furry inside. Price: "Many aspects of this description must surely be treated with caution". The stones may be "a medieval invention" (p. 127). The cat fur may point to Freyja's cats (p. 69). Archaeological finds give other details:
  - the Fyrkat woman's toe-rings and full-body veil;
  - a possible nose-ring in Birka Bj. 660;
  - silver-embroidered headbands in Bj. 660 and 845.
- **No Norse drum.** The *vétt* that Óðinn "tapped like the völur" (Lokasenna 24) is probably a tub-lid or shield, not a drum (pp. 129-130).
- **The staff is the völva's defining object.** *Vǫlva* means "staff-bearer" (p. 133). Price lists about 51 staffs in the Viking world: 47 iron and 4 wooden (p. 84). He sorts the named types by use (pp. 132-136):
  - *stafr* and *seiðstafr*: the völva's mark of office;
  - *stafsprota*: struck on an enemy's cheek to fog his memory at a lawsuit;
  - *gandr* and *gǫndull*: send spirits out;
  - *gambanteinn* or *tamsvǫndr* ("taming wand"): carved with the runes *ergi*, *œði*, *óþoli*, drives a victim mad with lust (p. 135).

  Staff amulets on rings are found "associated with women" and never on the same ring as Þórr's hammers (p. 168). The Norwegian law banned keeping a "staff or altar" in the house (p. 132).
- **Narcotics.** Fyrkat grave 4 held several hundred henbane seeds in a pouch, and Oseberg held cannabis seeds in a tiny drawstring bag (pp. 168-169). Henbane is linked to flight sensations and to erotic magic.

### The graves (pp. 84-119)
- **Ten or eleven "völva graves".** Price picks them for intact, recorded staff assemblages: Birka Bj. 660, 760, 834, 845; Klinta (Öland); Aska (Östergötland); Fyrkat 4; Kaupang Ka. 294-296; the Gausel "Queen"; Oseberg; Peel Castle (Isle of Man). The women are high-status, often seated in chairs, buried with imports from far away (Samanid bowls, Arabic jugs), and sometimes with Christian crosses used as objects of power (Bj. 660, p. 88).
- **Fyrkat 4 is the clearest case** (pp. 105-113). A woman was buried in a wagon-body inside Harald Bluetooth's royal ring-fort, c. 980. Her grave goods:
  - a blue gown with gold thread and no brooches, under a transparent full-body veil;
  - silver toe-rings with Far-Eastern parallels;
  - a box-brooch holding white-lead face paint;
  - a phosphorus-white substance in a glass ampule;
  - a chair pendant;
  - henbane;
  - a pig's jaw and old pellets of hair and possibly bone ash;
  - an iron staff, and a slim wooden one beside a meat-spit.

  She was "honoured in death above all others present", in the circle of a king who claimed to have made the Danes Christian (p. 111). A fragment from the same box-brooch turned up at another ring-fort, Borgring, so she may have travelled between royal forts (p. 110).
- **Kaupang Ka. 294-296.** A woman sits in the stern of the boat, probably holding the steering oar, with a staff pinned under a rock, a shield, an axe, and a dog's head in a bronze bowl. She is "probably the most elaborate possible sorceress burial so far known" (pp. 113-114).
- **Birka Bj. 834.** A woman sits on a man's lap in a single chair, with a staff and shield. A spear was thrown over them into the chamber wall: Óðinn's dedication, "There would seem little doubt" (p. 95).
- **Gender-crossed goods in the graves.**
  - **Klinta:** a couple cremated together. The woman got a battle-axe and woodworking tools; the man got an oval brooch, beads and a needle. Price rejects the excavators' "coincidence": "The ambiguous gender statements thus made should not be over-interpreted, but ... they implicate both individuals" (p. 103). He suggests a sorcerous couple, völva and seiðmaðr.
  - **Portway, Andover:** a pagan Anglo-Saxon grave of an osteologically male body "buried in female dress including a full set of jewellery" (p. 177).
  - **Staffs in men's graves:** a few staffs come from graves with weapons, sexed male only by grave goods: Hellebust, Mindre-Sunde, Tuna, Ladby, and a seated "probable man's grave" in Iceland (pp. 151-159). They are a minority, and all are provisional.

### Ergi, níð and gender (pp. 172-183)
- **Snorri's founding line is about priestesses.** Seiðr "brings with it so much ergi that manly men thought it shameful to perform, and so this skill was taught to the priestesses [gyðjur]" (Ynglinga saga 7, p. 172).
- **Women are not shamed by seiðr; men are.** "the performance of seiðr by women was socially acceptable" (p. 175). Noreen's four senses of *argr* for a man are "morally useless", "unmanly" (taking the female role in sex), "one who employs sorcery, and specifically seiðr", and "cowardly" (p. 173). The law codes make "performed sorcery" one of the full-compensation insults, alongside being a woman every ninth night (p. 173). In Iceland's witch trials of 1554-1720, 116 of 125 defendants were men (p. 175).
- **Price is cautious on "drag".** Calling Þórr's bridal disguise "drag" imposes modern codes. Cross-dressing "does not necessarily make transvestism one of the defining characteristics of seiðr as performed by men" (p. 175). Two Gotlandic stones from Lärbro Tängelgårda show bearded figures in women's dress, perhaps *seiðmenn* (p. 175). The níð against Bishop Friðrekr has him bearing nine children, perhaps because of his flowing vestments: "this would also link ergi to the clothes of a particular ritual specialist" (p. 175).
- **A queer Óðinn: yes. A third gender: open.** Solli's queer reading of Óðinn is convincing: "the overall idea of such gender constructions is convincing" (p. 176). But Price will not commit to a fixed third gender: "it is hard to tell whether these took the form of new gender constructions or merely a blurring of existing boundaries" (p. 177). Solli's reading of *seiðberendr* ("seiðr-carrier"; North: "seiðr-womb-bearer") as a third-gender shaman "rests ultimately on the speculative etymology" (p. 177).
- **Seiðr is sexual.** The evidence (pp. 177-183):
  - staffs with phallic names (*gǫndull* = penis in *Bósa saga*; *vǫlr* → Vǫlsi);
  - *Vǫlsa þáttr*: a woman-led cult of a preserved horse phallus, and the same woman asking to be lifted over the door-frame to see the other world (pp. 125, 178-179);
  - the sex acts in Ibn Faḍlān's Rus' funeral;
  - erotic magic as a main purpose of seiðr.

  The völva is "regarded as sexually dangerous" (p. 183).

### The Sámi and "shamanism": Price's cautions (chs 4-5, 8)
- **Not a loan in either direction.** Price rejects both old models: that the Sámi borrowed from the Norse (Fritzner) and that the Norse borrowed seiðr from the Sámi (Strömbäck). He treats seiðr and *noaidevuohta* as "parallel symbolic streams" that exchanged ideas at a shared frontier (pp. 191-193, 228-229). Similarities do not prove borrowing, and neither tradition can be used to fill the other's gaps.
- **The Norse treated Sámi magic as different and stronger.** Price records:
  - *finnvitka*, "to practise magic in a Finn way";
  - "travelling to the Finns to receive a prediction";
  - Gunnhildr, "later to be the wife of Eiríkr blóðøx", sent to the Sámi to learn sorcery and seducing her teachers for more (p. 226).

  In *Vatnsdæla* a Sámi seeress is hired for a seiðr "in the old manner". She "sat on a high seat", and men went up from their benches to ask their fates (p. 227). The Chukchi likewise valued foreign shamans more highly (p. 250).
- **Vivallen grave 9 (Härjedalen).** A Sámi man in his fifties was "buried clothed as a woman from the Nordic society". He wore a Birka-type linen dress, beads, a brooch and a needle-case, with a steppe-style man's belt. Price: possibly a *noaidi* (p. 223). Price lists the identities at play, ending with "men and women, and a possible third gender" (p. 228). In ch. 7 he is "the cross-dressing male-bodied Sámi" (p. 329).
- **Circumpolar gender categories are real but various.** These include the Chukchi "soft men", who were feared as the most powerful shamans and also scorned. Some soft men "preferred death" to the spirits' command to change (pp. 249-250). They also include Inuit shamans as a third gender, and Yukaghir and Sakha shamans who cross-dressed without being seen as a separate category (pp. 250-251).
- **The 2019 retreat from "shamanism".** In 2002 Price called seiðr "a 'shamanic' belief system" (p. 271). In ch. 8 he keeps the comparison but puts "shamanism" in scare quotes. He reviews Tolley (2009), who finds no real shamanism in the texts and reads Þorbjǫrg's high seat as "recalling a bishop's cathedra" and her staff as a crozier (p. 335). He also reviews Dillmann (2006), who denies the völur any trance. Price answers both with the archaeology: staff graves, unusual costume, platforms at cult sites, the chair pendants (p. 335). Design consequence: write the rite in Norse terms (*seiðr*, *varðlokkur*, going out, the spirits refusing). Never call it "shamanic" in game text.

### War (ch. 6) and the closing chapters (chs 7-8)
- **Seiðr's second branch is war.** Its uses (p. 294):
  - fear and confusion, or courage and clarity;
  - weakness, or strength;
  - binding limbs;
  - breaking or hardening weapons;
  - invulnerability;
  - killing;
  - raising the dead to fight;
  - protection from sorcerers, and killing them.

  Gunnhildr of York "used seiðr to ensure that Egill ... would never know peace in Iceland" (p. 273). Skuld builds her *seiðhjallr* on the battlefield (pp. 120, 294).
- **Sorcerers stand behind the battle line.** Price's composite of the battlefield: "Behind the battle, some of the sorcerers ... may be present in person, perhaps sitting on platforms. Volur, seiðkonur and the others are there ... perhaps ringed by a chanting enclosure of assistants". The seiðmenn are given "a wide berth" (p. 327).
- **Valkyries were ugly before they were pretty.** The early valkyries were "far more fearsome" beings of carnage. The tragic shield-maiden is a later literary layer (pp. 274-276). In *Darraðarljóð* twelve women weave war on a loom of men's heads and guts (pp. 276ff, 327). Freyja "chooses half the slain", and her hall may be the afterlife for "worthy females" (p. 69).
- **Women fighting: the non-Norse sources.**
  - Skylitzes: after the Rus' defeat of 971 (Sviatoslav of Kyiv's army) the Byzantines "find the bodies of women in armour" (p. 275).
  - The *Cogadh*'s fleet of the *Inghen Ruaidh*, "the Red Girl" (p. 275).
  - Birka Bj. 581, a female warrior confirmed by genomics (pp. 275-276).

  The Rus' at Dorostolon fought with a howling frenzy that Leo the Deacon compared to animals (p. 306). A fresco in Hagia Sophia, Kyiv (c. 1054) shows a masked spear-fighter (p. 307).
- **Þorgerðr Hǫlgabrúðr: Trondheim's war-patron.** Jarl Hákon of Hlaðir, after sacrificing his son, invokes her and her sister Irpa at Hjǫrungavágr (986). A hailstorm comes out of the north and "an arrow flew from every finger of the ogress" (pp. 288-289). Her idol is hooded. She is a giantess-troll rather than a goddess, of "great sexual power" and fertility, and gives prophecy like a völva (pp. 289-290).
- **The weapon dancer is on a woman's necklace.** The 2019 cover image is the silver weapon-dancer from Birka Bj. 571, a woman's grave (p. iv, pp. 320-323).
- **The staffs are distaffs (Heide).** Price accepts Heide's proof that the "basket" staffs are caged distaffs, "used in the rituals of magic to 'wind back' the travelling soul on its thread" (p. 338). Gustin reads them as symbols of women's textile power (p. 337). Price's synthesis: perhaps the staff-bearer was meant to "push people off-balance, a disconcerting feeling of not knowing exactly who or what she was" (p. 337).
- **Price's own unsexing of the staff graves (2019).** "Can we be sure that the people in the majority of the 'staff graves' ... were gendered women? No, we cannot." There is little osteology and "virtually no genomic data at all". "A transgender reading is also possible", though the term is modern and "problematic (some would say impossible)" for the remote past (p. 341). He looks again at Bj. 660 and 845 and wonders if they hold the "'missing' male sorcerers" (p. 341). Dillmann's view that *ergi* was "not inherent to seiðr" but triggered by it is noted, with Price seeing it as "rather more affirmative" for male practitioners (p. 340).
- **A seer in the York army (910).** The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle lists, among the Northumbrian army's dead at Tettenhall, "Osferð hlytte". Price glosses *hlytte* as possibly "the seer" (OE *hlyta*, diviner), a war-band's ritual specialist, a "regimental chaplain" (pp. 342-343).

### Saxo's Uppsala passage and "effeminate cult personnel": what Price says
- **Saxo's Book 6 Uppsala passage is not discussed anywhere in the book.** That is Starkaðr's disgust at the "effeminate" mimes and the clatter of bells at the Uppsala sacrifices. Searches for Starkaðr/Starcatherus, mimes, bells and *histriones* found nothing. Saxo appears only for Vitolfus, Óðinn as "Wecha" in women's disguise (Book 3), the shield-maidens, and the storm at Hjǫrungavágr.
- **What he uses for Uppsala instead:**
  - Adam of Bremen IV.26: the idol of Fricco *cum ingenti priapo*, "with a huge phallus", and "'shameless' songs and performances" at the festivals (p. 181);
  - recent work suggesting the Gamla Uppsala "temple" was a great feasting hall used for festivals (p. 31);
  - the Fyris Plains battle-spell "outside Uppsala" (p. 294).
- **On effeminate or cross-dressed cult personnel, he offers:**
  - Snorri's *gyðjur*, the priestesses who were taught seiðr (p. 172);
  - the Lärbro Tängelgårda stones (Gotland): bearded figures in women's dress, "conceivable" as a gathering of seiðmenn (p. 175);
  - the níð against Bishop Friðrekr's vestments (p. 175);
  - Vivallen (p. 223);
  - the Klinta and Portway graves.

  He does not cite Tacitus' Naharvali priest *muliebri ornatu*. So he gives no support to "Freyr's priests dressed as women". This confirms `norse_reading_list.md` finding 1. Uppsala text should rest on Adam's phallic Fricco and "shameless" performances, plus Saxo's mimes and bells cited directly from Saxo, not from Price.

## 3. Quotable lines for in-game text (in-world paraphrases)

These are in-world paraphrases for event, trait and holy-site text. Attribute them to speakers in the world, never to Price.

- "Many spirits came that turned from us before, and would not obey us, until they heard her sing." (after Þorbjǫrg, *Eiríks saga*, p. 40) Use it for the trance success toast.
- "You will be no worse a woman for it." (after Þorbjǫrg to Guðríðr, p. 40) The völva's line to a reluctant singer, or to the new daughter.
- "Health to you, my daughter." (p. 40) The völva's closing line in the rite.
- "She went from house to house, and they always sent for her from the next one." (after p. 41 and Völuspá 22) For the seeress's circuit.
- "It brings so much ergi that the men thought it shameful, and so it was given to the priestesses." (after Ynglinga saga 7, p. 172) For the tenet desc.
- "She spins herself out, and her sisters wind her home." (after Heide, p. 338) For the Iron Distaff artifact.
- "When she came in with the staff, no one knew quite who or what she was, and that was the point." (after p. 337) For the staff, or for the Daughter of the High Seat trait.
- "He burned the Eighty in their hall, and they praised him for it. He went on to be king at York." (after *Haralds saga*, p. 81) York lore.
- "His ship was crewed by none but seiðmenn." (after p. 81) For a sorcerer war-band or MAA.
- "An arrow flew from every finger of her, and each one killed a man." (after *Jómsvíkinga saga*, p. 289) Trondheim battle event.
