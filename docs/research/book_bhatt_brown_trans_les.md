# Bhatt, *Brown/Trans/Les* (2026): design reading

**Citation.** Talia Bhatt, *Brown / Trans / Les*. © 2026 Talia Bhatt. The title page gives only the title, the author and "author of 'Trans/Rad/Fem'" (l.2-4). No subtitle, publisher, ISBN or series number appears in the text. "Essays on Transfeminism, Book 2" is **not** on the title page, so this note does not use it. The book calls itself the "follow-up" to *Trans/Rad/Fem* (Intro, l.40) and "my next book on radical transfeminism" (ch. 2, l.178).

**What was read.** The whole plain-text file, all 1030 lines, front and back matter included (`scratchpad/books/bhatt_brown_trans_les.txt`).

**How cited.** Chapter and line of the text file, e.g. "ch. 3, l.244". "Intro" and "Concl." are the unnumbered introduction and conclusion. The file's markers are offset by one: `chapter-001` is the Introduction, `chapter-002` is chapter 1, and so on to `chapter-010`, the Conclusion.

**Quotes.** Verbatim and under 25 words. The copyright page allows "brief quotations in a publication review" (l.7). Everything else is paraphrase. Private people the book names or quotes from social media (two friends under pseudonyms, a doctoral student's posts, an activist's post) are left out on purpose.

**Relation to existing notes.** Book 1 is `book_bhatt_chu.md` (Part I) and `book_bhatt_third_sex.md` ("The Third Sex", "Degendering and Regendering"). This note does not replace either. Where it overlaps with the third-sex note, the third-sex note's design rows still stand and this note adds to them (§5).

## 2. Core arguments

**2.1 Racialized degendering is not transmisogyny (ch. 1).** Under patriarchy, women fall into two bins: "those valued for their gestational capacity, and everyone else" (ch. 1, l.66). Infertile cis women, racialized women, disabled women and trans women all land in the second bin. Racialized women are called "men" not because anyone believes it but to mark them as brutes who may be hurt (l.77, l.80). So "racialized women are thus hardly being 'masculinized'" (l.73). But collapsing this into "cis women of colour experience transmisogyny" erases trans women of colour (l.83). Many cis women of a degraded class will still police the line against trans women to secure their own place (l.89). A warning: "One day, you will be offal too" (l.93).

**2.2 Intersectionality is radical feminism, not its refutation (ch. 2).** Bhatt reads Crenshaw's 1989 paper and her 2010 essay on MacKinnon. The core is "we shouldn't have to choose" (l.127) between race and sex. The popular charge that dominance feminism is "essentialist" is a misuse Crenshaw herself rejected (l.140-152). Bhatt's own evidence: "it is not the White Woman who is campaigning against the outlawing of marital rape in India" (l.170). The general rule: "If a feminism is found to not adequately serve the interests of patriarchy, it is twisted until it does" (l.181). The chapter ends with a pledge: multiply-marginalised women are always asked "to whom do you really belong?" (l.189); her answer is to the woman who calls her sister (l.191-197).

**2.3 Penetrability (ch. 3).** The ideology of sex boils down to one rule: personhood belongs to whoever penetrates (l.217-218). Slurs for gay men (sodomite, fairy, queen) began as names for the penetrable male (l.231). Trans women are accused of keeping the tool of penetration while taking the signs of the penetrable; "the most relevant phallus is the imagined one" (l.244). Hence "Have you had the surgery?" (l.245) and the fetish that treats her as "man-lite" (l.247). Gender clinics demanded hyperfeminine, heterosexual patients: gatekeeping aimed at "producing the perfect, penetrable subject" (l.276). Fighting shame is not enough: "You also have to do the work" (l.284), meaning organise.

**2.4 Natalism, nativism, nationalism (ch. 4).** The Nation needs mothers of the right blood and treats every other woman as disposable (l.312-315). Modesty politics reduces to "Be a womb, or be a hole" (l.319). Queerness is treated as "desertion, as a dereliction of duty" (l.327). Gay assimilation trades conformity for tolerance; trans people cannot make that trade, because transition shows the line can be crossed: they "still need to make the crossing, and so reveal that such a crossing is possible" (l.331). The "Transgender Breeding Program" panic (l.333-340) recycles the hijra child-stealer myth: a trans woman in West Bengal was stoned to death in 2019 as a "child-lifter". "Her name is unknown. Proof of her crimes never surfaced." (l.379). Then: "So much for 'veneration'." (l.380).

**2.5 White people did not invent gender (ch. 4).** Bhatt rejects the view (Jude Doyle, Gill-Peterson, essays in *Feminism Against Cisness*) that patriarchy and binary gender came with Christian colonisers (l.353-364). She cites foot-binding in tenth-century China and the Laws of Manu calling "eunuchs" unclean (l.359). The "non-patriarchal wonderland" turns the non-West into a "preserved, primitive 'living past'" for Western use (l.362). "White people did not invent gender." (l.388). Non-Western regimes now call queer rights Western imperialism, so queer people there are squeezed from both sides (l.384). She adds that the Ambedkar epigraph (1945) shows communal fights over women's bodies predate the Raj (l.292, l.378).

**2.6 Against the decolonial golden age (ch. 5).** Lugones builds "the coloniality of gender" on Oyěwùmí's claim that Yoruba society had no gender before colonisation (l.431-435). Bhatt's objections: the claim is too sweeping for "non-Western society" as a whole (l.440); it treats settler, slaving and extractive colonialism as one thing (l.442-444); and Oyěwùmí's own evidence fails. With Bakare-Yusuf: language is not reality; seniority "is therefore often a ruse for other forms of power" (l.469); a wife enters her husband's lineage at the bottom (l.471); and "We cannot simply use the experience of princesses and privileged women" (l.476). Oyěwùmí's father became a Yoruba king (l.477), she defended FGM and child betrothal against "Westerners" (l.482), and she rejected claims of institutional lesbianism in West Africa (l.486-487). Even if the paradise were real: "So fucking what?" (l.500). Bhatt prefers foremothers who fought without precedent: "I am proud of my foremothers, and I don't understand why more of us aren't." (l.504).

**2.7 Honour culture inside feminism (ch. 6).** "Honor killing" is not a racist Western label; it translates *izzat*, family honour (l.540). Some minority women's groups are attacked by their own conservatives as showing "Westernization" and by leftists for "washing our dirty laundry in public" (l.539). "diaspora feminists have their own honor culture" (l.574): they defend the homeland's image before the Western gaze, and Third World women become props (l.594, l.605-608). She quotes an Iranian feminist group: people can be "trapped between two forms of violence" (l.602), a regime at home and war from abroad. Laws banning the veil are always wrong (l.613); refusing to criticise veiling is also wrong (l.615-616). "Enemy feminisms, after all, thrive outside the West too." (l.623).

**2.8 Political heterosexuality (ch. 7).** Working-class Indian lesbians in Maya Sharma's study are rarely called lesbians. They are called prostitutes or girl-sellers, because a woman with no man to own her is read as a loose, public woman (l.674). Even a women's rights group split up two women in love (l.675-676). Separatism cannot win, since "the point of patriarchy has always been that it's coercive" (l.680). bell hooks blamed lesbian feminists for feminism's bad press (l.701-702) and read the ball-culture film as showing Black gay men who "worship at the throne of whiteness" (l.720); Bhatt answers with Smith's question about the abject: "are they allowed to simply want better for themselves?" (l.748). The turn: Bhatt apologises "for every lesbian who has ever shamed women for loving or sleeping with men" (l.810). Instead, "feminist struggle has to build power and structures outside of the family, outside of the state, and outside of heterosexuality" (l.813). Her priority: property, income, single motherhood and spinsterhood first (l.814).

**2.9 Epistemic vandalism (ch. 8).** A 2025 study of sex workers put trans women's work down to "a sense of fun", though its own data show most began as children for lack of options (l.828-840). Nanda wrote that a hijra saw herself as a not-woman while quoting her saying "I am a lady." (l.841). Raymond was told why clinic patients acted stereotyped (to pass the clinic) and ignored it (l.843-844). The history runs from Krafft-Ebing to Benjamin's scale, which rewarded attraction to men (l.850-856), to autogynephilia studies with absurd thresholds and coerced subjects (l.869-901), to ROGD (l.920-938) and the Cass Review (l.940-945). Trans women learned to pass each other the clinicians' criteria (l.905, citing Stone). The term: epistemic vandalism is refusing any knowledge "insufficiently transphobic" (l.959). The epilogue (l.963-987) is a grief poem; see §7 before using anything from it.

**2.10 Conclusion.** The recurring mistake of every newer feminism is the question "How do you build a robust feminist analysis that absolves men of their role in upholding patriarchy?" (Concl., l.996). Answer: you can't. On categories: "not believing in them doesn't stop others from trying to shove you in anyway" (l.1000). Patriarchy hurts men too, but "Women remain the ones who are hurt first" (l.1011).

## 3. What the book does not cover

Stated so no one fills the gaps from memory. The book has nothing on Byzantium, eunuch courts, Norse or Anglo-Saxon material, medieval Judaism, Tamil devotion, or the Mande world. China appears once (foot-binding, l.359). West Africa appears as Yoruba (ch. 5) and in a Lumumba epigraph (l.611). India appears through hijra, caste, the Mahābhārata's Eklavya (l.412-415, l.491), the Laws of Manu (l.359) and Ambedkar (l.292). Ancient Mesopotamia appears once, in the epilogue (Asu-shu-namir, l.978). "Two-spirit" is never named; the nearest material is Lugones's use of Paula Gunn Allen and of "third gender" in the Andes (l.431, l.436). Trans men appear only as objects of others' regendering (Shrier, l.926-935; the UK Supreme Court ruling, l.953).

## 7. Standing cautions

**No "third gender" romanticisation.** Book 2 doubles down. New ammunition: Hirschfeld's scholarly third sex centred "the penetrable male" (ch. 8, l.851); the UK "intermediate zone" as modern legal third-sexing (l.951-953); Asu-shu-namir as a "third sex" sent to save the world's fertility and then banished (l.978-980). Do **not** use Ishtar's descent or the *assinnu* as a proud precedent. The web notes that mention them (`web_baedelism.md`, `web_third_wave_transfeminism.md`) are now doubly weak.

**No golden past.** Ch. 5 is the sharpest statement yet (l.497-504). See §5 row 2 on E1, H18 and F1. Ch. 4 also says Christianity did not bring patriarchy to anyone (l.388), which bears on the Bædling era arc (row 12).

**Never a living community as a faith.**
- Bhatt notes the likeness of ball-culture houses to "hijra houses and the guru-chela system" (ch. 7, l.713). Do not model Houses or Take as Daughter on the guru-chela system. It is a living hijra institution. The current Houses rest on Raha and van der Drift and monastic forms, which is fine.
- The hijra child-lifter killing (ch. 4, l.379) is a modern event about a living community. Use only its pattern (the rumour), never the event.
- Badenya's contemporary sources (Broqua, Geoffrion, Mackin, Gaudio) describe living people. Bhatt's ch. 7 point applies: do not judge them as too conservative or demand they be subversive (l.722, l.748). The faith already borrows roles, not identities; keep it so.
- The bissu sources in the README include a "decolonizing" framing (Ismoyo 2020) and a "third gender" one (Andaya). Bhatt's ch. 4-5 argument hits both. They feed no built faith; keep it that way.

**Trans men out of scope.** The book's material on trans men (Shrier's regendering, l.926-935; the UK ruling, l.953; Doyle's book, l.351-356) gives nothing to build. Leave it.

**No suicide or self-harm content.** Avoid: Jody Howard's threat in order to get an abortion (ch. 1, l.58); a murder-suicide comparison (ch. 3, l.255); l.907 and l.943; and the whole epilogue "Madwoman" (ch. 8, l.963-987), which is built on longing for death ("beloved of Ereshkigal", l.983). Nothing from the epilogue should reach loc, even paraphrased.

**No antisemitic tropes in Ha-Nehpakhot.** The book has no material on Judaism. Jews appear only as the Nazis' scapegoats (ch. 4, l.296, l.303). Israel appears in lists of nationalisms and in the 2025 war (l.401, l.591, l.599). Two cautions for the design: §5 row 14 (the lord's licence as "Christian protector against Jews") and row 15 (no natalism critique mapped onto the Jewish minority).
