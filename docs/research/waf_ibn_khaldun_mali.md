# Ibn Khaldūn, *Histoire des Berbères* vol. 2, the Mali section (c. 1380s; trans. 1854): design reading

Source: Ibn Khaldoun, *Histoire des Berbères et des dynasties musulmanes de l'Afrique septentrionale*, trans. Baron de Slane, vol. 2 (Algiers: Imprimerie du Gouvernement, 1854), archive.org `ibn-khaldun-histoire-des-berberes-et-des-dynasties-musulmanes-de-l-afrique-septentrionale-v-2`.
Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo. Page numbers are the edition's; the OCR damages several running heads (p. 111 reads "LLL", p. 112 "as", p. 114 "sai", p. 115 "us"), so they were reconstructed from the sequence 110–116.

**What was read.** In full: the chapter on "les rois des peuples nègres", pp. 109–116, from Ibn Saʿīd's list of Sudanic peoples through the Mali king list to Mansa Maghā III (1390). Skipped: the rest of the volume, including the Marinid chapters that describe Abū l-Ḥasan's embassy to Mali ("ainsi que nous le dirons ailleurs", p. 114) and Appendix 1 cited in the notes. Those are worth a later pass for court gifts.

**Relation to existing notes.** `south_reading_list.md` (West Africa, finding 2) quotes the sister's-son sentence from p. 111 and calls Mārī Jāṭa the earliest written Sunjata. This note confirms both, gives the whole king list, and answers the brief's question about Sākūra. It pairs with `waf_ibn_battuta_mali.md`, which is an eyewitness of Sulaymān's court in 1352–53; Ibn Khaldūn's informants are a Ghana muftī (1393–94), a Takrūrī interpreter in Cairo, a Sijilmāsa qāḍī who lived at Gao (met 1374–75), and Ibn Khadīja, who travelled with Mansa Mūsā.

## 1. The argument in brief

Ibn Khaldūn writes Mali's history as a list of kings with short judgments, from informants he names. Ghana weakened as the Almoravids rose, who subjected and converted many of its people; the Sosso then enslaved Ghana; Mali conquered the Sosso and spread to the Atlantic (p. 110). Mali's first convert was "Bermendana" (pp. 110–111). The greatest king, who subdued the Sosso, was **Mārī Jāṭa**: "mari" means an emir of royal descent and "djata" means lion; he reigned 25 years; his genealogy is unknown to Ibn Khaldūn (p. 111). His sons followed. A grandson through a **daughter** was then chosen under the "barbarian" rule that gives the throne to the sister or the sister's son (p. 111). A freed slave usurped and ruled well (p. 111). The throne returned to Mārī Jāṭa's sons, then passed to "un fils de sa sœur", Abū Bakr, whose son was **Mansa Mūsā** (p. 112). Mūsā's pilgrimage, his gold, and the poet-architect al-Sāḥilī follow (pp. 112–114). Then the late kings: Sulaymān, Qāsā, the ruinous Mārī Jāṭa II, Mūsā II under a vizier, and a run of murders to 1390 (pp. 114–116).

## 2. What matters for the design (page refs)

### The king list (pp. 110–116)

1. **Mārī Jāṭa** ("Mari-Djata"): subdued the Sosso, 25 years (p. 111). "Teguen" glossed as "grandson" (p. 111).
2. **Mansa Walī** ("Mença-Ouéli", Walī = ʿAlī), his son; "un de leurs plus grands rois"; made the ḥajj under Baybars (p. 111).
3. **Wātī**, his brother (p. 111).
4. **Khalīfa**, another brother, "faible d'esprit", who shot passers-by with arrows for fun and was killed by the people (p. 111).
5. **Abū Bakr, "fils de la fille de Mari-Djata"**, chosen "d'après le principe des nations barbares qui mettent la sœur [du monarque décédé] ou le fils de sa sœur en possession [du trône]"; his father's line unknown (p. 111).
6. **Sākūra** ("Sakoura", pronounced "Sebkera" per the muftī), "un affranchi de la famille royale", seized power; made the ḥajj under al-Nāṣir; killed at Tājūra on the way home (p. 111). Under him Mali grew greatly, took Gao, and reached from the Atlantic to Takrūr; Maghribi merchants came (p. 111). Gao's capture is also credited to Saghmandja, a general of Mansa Mūsā (p. 112).
7. **Gao** ("Qū"), a son of Mārī Jāṭa, then his son **Muḥammad ibn Gao** (p. 112).
8. "L'autorité passa des enfants de Mari-Djata à un fils de sa sœur, nommé Abou-Bekr" (p. 112).
9. **Mansa Mūsā**, son and successor of that Abū Bakr: powerful, holy, just; ḥajj 724/1324; reigned 25 years (pp. 112–114).
10. **Mansa Maghā** (Maghā = Muḥammad), Mūsā's son, 4 years (p. 114).
11. **Mansa Sulaymān**, son of Abū Bakr and brother of Mūsā, 24 years (p. 114).
12. **Qāsā** ("Cassa", variants "Fenba, Canba"), Sulaymān's **son**, nine months (p. 114).
13. **Mārī Jāṭa II**, son of Maghā, 14 years: tyrant, "ne respectant pas même l'honneur des femmes" (p. 114); sold the great gold nugget of 20 qinṭārs; died of sleeping sickness 775/1373–74 (p. 115).
14. **Mūsā II**, his son: just and generous, but held "en chartre privée" by his vizier, also called Mārī Jāṭa; the vizier campaigned east past Gao and besieged Tagaddā (p. 115). Mūsā II died 789/1387 (p. 116).
15. **Maghā II**, his brother, killed after about a year (p. 116).
16. **Sandakī**, "mari de la mère de Mouça" (Sandakī glossed "vizier"), took the throne and was killed within months by a man of Mārī Jāṭa's family (p. 116).
17. **Maḥmūd**, who claimed descent from Mansa Qū son of Mansa Walī son of Mārī Jāṭa, came from the pagan lands behind Mali and ruled from 792/1390 as **Mansa Maghā** (p. 116).

### Sākūra: the brief's question

- He is "un affranchi de la famille royale" (p. 111): a **freed slave** of the royal household. Ibn Khaldūn says nothing about castration. **There is no eunuch ruler in this text.** Sākūra is a precedent for a non-royal of servile origin ruling well, making the ḥajj, and enlarging the realm, not for a eunuch-king.

### Matrilineal succession

- The sentence on p. 111 is Ibn Khaldūn's explanation of one choice (Abū Bakr, a daughter's son). He calls the principle that of "nations barbares", meaning non-Arab or non-Muslim custom; he does not say it was Mali's rule in every reign.
- **New in this pass:** Mansa Mūsā's own branch is a sister's line. The throne passed "à un fils de sa sœur" (Mārī Jāṭa's sister's son), Abū Bakr, and Mūsā is his son (p. 112). The most famous king of Mali, in Ibn Khaldūn's account, descends from the founder's sister, not from the founder.
- Note the text's ambiguity: the first Abū Bakr is a *daughter's* son (p. 111), the second a *sister's* son (p. 112). The text does not say whether these are one man (de Slane adds no note on it). Treat as two moments of the same principle.
- **Sandakī**, the king's mother's husband, takes the throne (p. 116). Power through the mother's marriage, not the father's line.

### Mansa Mūsā's pilgrimage and court (pp. 112–114)

- He met the Granadan poet Abū Isḥāq al-Sāḥilī ("Toueidjen") at Mecca and brought him home; al-Sāḥilī's sons kept the favour and settled at Walata (p. 112).
- Ibn Khadīja, a rebel Almohad descendant, joined Mūsā's return: "Douze mille jeunes esclaves, revêtues de tuniques de brocart et de soie de Yemen, portaient ses effets" (p. 113). The adjective is feminine in de Slane's French; check the Arabic before reading "young slave women".
- The Takrūrī interpreter: Mūsā came with 80 loads of gold dust of three qinṭārs each; his people carry baggage on slaves' or men's backs, and camels only for the ḥajj (p. 113).
- Al-Sāḥilī built him a square plastered audience hall with a cupola and bright arabesques, "l'architecture était inconnue dans ce pays"; Mūsā paid 12,000 mithqāls (pp. 113–114). This is the cupola Ibn Baṭṭūṭa sat beneath (`waf_ibn_battuta_mali.md`, p. 403).
- Mūsā exchanged gifts with the Marinid Abū l-Ḥasan; the successors kept the friendship (p. 114). Mārī Jāṭa II sent Abū Sālim a giraffe in 762/1360–61 (p. 114).

### Peoples and names

- Ibn Saʿīd's list west of Kanem: Kawkaw (Gao), Nacara, Takrūr, Lemi, Nemnem, Djabi, Kouri, Ankerar (p. 109).
- The Ghana muftī said Takrūr was called "Zeghaï" and Mali "Ankaris" (p. 110). (De Slane does not explain either.)
- The Sosso ("Sousou") enslaved Ghana after the Almoravids (p. 110).
- The Mali capital, per the Sijilmāsa qāḍī: "Beled-Beni…" (damaged), large, well-watered, a caravan halt (p. 116).

## 3. Corrections to the candidate

1. **No eunuch ruler.** Sākūra was a freed slave (p. 111). Drop any "eunuch mansa" idea from the Badenya world. If the design wants a servile-born ruler, Sākūra is the precedent; he is male and nothing in the text suggests gender crossing.
2. **The sister's-son passage is p. 111; the sister's-son line of Mansa Mūsā is p. 112.** The reading list cites only p. 111. Add p. 112: it is the stronger design fact.
3. **Qāsā.** Ibn Khaldūn names "Cassa" as Sulaymān's son and successor (p. 114); Ibn Baṭṭūṭa names Qāsā as Sulaymān's chief wife and says the word means "queen" (p. 417). Either Ibn Khaldūn's informant confused a title, or Sulaymān's heir bore the name. The design should not stake anything on Qāsā being a personal name.
4. **Mārī Jāṭa is not called Sunjata here.** The equation Mārī Jāṭa = Sunjata is modern scholarship (Delafosse onward). Ibn Khaldūn's gloss ("emir of royal descent" + "lion") supports it, but the narrator should say "the king Ibn Khaldūn calls Mārī Jāṭa".
5. **No date for Mārī Jāṭa.** Ibn Khaldūn gives only the reign length (25 years). The c. 1235 date for Kirina is modern reconstruction.

## 4. Design implications

### Line

- **Holy-site or doctrine text (faith voice):** "The founder's sister's son took the throne, and from him came Mūsā." Narrator note: Ibn Khaldūn, *Histoire des Berbères* II, pp. 111–112.
- **Succession law flavour:** a Badenya realm could prefer a sister's son, and fall back on a daughter's son. Both are attested (pp. 111–112). The shared *Sisters Choose* fits.
- **Sandakī event (p. 116):** a king's mother's husband seizes the throne and is killed within months. A cautionary event for a faith whose line runs through mothers: "the mother's husband is not the mother."
- **Khalīfa (p. 111):** the people kill a king who shoots passers-by. A model for a "the people depose a cruel king" event that favours the sister's line.

### World

- **Sākūra the freed slave** as a historical character for a 1290s bookmark or decision: a non-royal regent who enlarges Mali. Keep male, as recorded.
- **Al-Sāḥilī's cupola** (pp. 113–114): a building or a court artefact ("the plastered hall"), available after 1324.
- **The great gold nugget** sold by Mārī Jāṭa II (p. 115): an artefact that can be lost to a spendthrift king.
- **The vizier-regent Mārī Jāṭa** who keeps Mūsā II "en chartre privée" (p. 115): the shape of Mali's politics after 1374, useful for late-game instability.

### Reaction

- Ibn Khaldūn's own phrase for the matrilineal rule, "le principe des nations barbares" (p. 111), is a ready hostile-scholar reaction line, attributed to "a Maghribi historian" in-game, not to Ibn Khaldūn by name unless he is a character.

### Names

- Royal names from this list: Mārī Jāṭa, Walī, Wātī, Khalīfa, Abū Bakr, Sākūra, Qū (Gao), Muḥammad, Mūsā, Maghā, Sulaymān, Qāsā, Sandakī, Maḥmūd. All male. **No woman is named in pp. 109–116**; Mārī Jāṭa's daughter and sister, through whom the throne passed, are unnamed. For women's names see `waf_ibn_battuta_mali.md` §5.

## Correction (2026-10-19, after `waf_conrad_austen_sunjata.md` and `waf_gomez_african_dominion.md`)
"Mansa Mūsā descends from Mārī Jāṭa's sister's son" rests on de Slane's French only. Levtzion and Hopkins's translation, followed by Gomez, Conrad and Wilks, makes Mūsā a descendant of Sunjata's *brother* Abū Bakr. Do not cite Mali's royal line as matrilateral succession. The solid evidence for succession through a sister or daughter is Ghana (Tunka Manīn succeeded his maternal uncle, 1063, al-Bakrī), Walata (Ibn Baṭṭūṭa), and the first Abū Bakr of Mali, a daughter's son. Check the Arabic before any loc line relies on it.
