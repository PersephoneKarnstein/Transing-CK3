### Chapter theses (read in full)

**Ch. 1, Neil, Introduction (pp. 1–10).** Byzantium "recognised not just two but three genders: women, men and eunuchs, the last category being reserved for physically or spiritually 'neutered' beings" (p. 3). Gender rules were fluid ("slippage"), marked externally by facial hair and clothing, and tied to a religious ideology of "gender transcendence" in which the female ascetic who shed her female body-markers "became male" — the sliding scale ran one direction only, toward an archetypal maleness attached to the male body. Neil frames the volume's central contrast with modern thought: for Byzantines bodies were mutable ("becoming") while gender-as-meaning was eternal.

**Ch. 9, Tougher, Bearding Byzantium (pp. 153–166).** From the seventh century the beard became compulsory for adult Byzantine males; Philotheos (899) formally divides court officials into eunuchs and "bearded" (p. 155). Beardlessness, high voice and the ban on marriage left eunuchs in a permanent liminal "youth" in life-course terms, yet they held generalships (Solomon to Andronikos Eonopolites) and were assimilated to beardless angels in dreambooks and thought — evidence for Ringrose's "third gender" thesis, which Tougher accepts with reservations. His closing hypothesis: the seventh-century switch from foreign slave eunuchs to **native-born Byzantine eunuchs** forced non-eunuch men to adopt the beard as the only visible marker of non-eunuch adult maleness (p. 166).

**Ch. 10, Casey, Spiritual Valency of Gender (pp. 167–181).** Byzantine thought inherited Philo's two-stage creation (an incorporeal, sexless first *adam*) and the ideal of transcending gender; but transcendence was always coded male, so women "became male" (Perpetua, Mary of Egypt, Eugenia, Mary/Marinos) at the cost of their specificity. Transvestite nuns were liminal figures who "offered a rite of transition" (p. 177). Eunuchs, initially suspect as having "cheated" the struggle against the flesh, were from the 8th–12th c. re-read via apophatic ("not A") theology and the doctrine of divinisation: "'against nature' can slip into 'beyond nature'" (pp. 179–80); they became the visual type of the angel and the ritual guardians of the "charismatic space around the ruler", which "enabled empresses to transcend their condition as women and occupy the space created for emperors" (p. 180).

**Ch. 3, Garland, 'Till Death Do Us Part?' (pp. 29–56).** Byzantine monasteries were small, "family sized" (8–20 inmates) and functioned as family property trusts, burial sites and retirement homes; female convents in particular housed "a female-only multigenerational family" of mothers, daughters, aunts and granddaughters (p. 54). Five imperial women's typika (Kecharitomene c.1110–16; Lips and Anagyroi 1294–1301; Bebaia Elpis c.1300; Philanthropos Soter c.1310) reserve to the foundress and her female descendants in perpetuity the control of the house, its property and its liturgy, with the convent "completely untrodden by men" and "closed even to eunuchs" (p. 53), while a eunuch or male salaried steward handled external business. Garland stresses that the founders masculinise their own virtues and pay "lip service" to female weakness, yet create institutions where women run the economy, the music and the ritual on their own terms.

**Ch. 7, Neil, Regarding Women on the Throne (pp. 113–131).** Eirene (regent 780–90, 792–7; sole ruler 797–802) signed the acta of Nicaea II before her son, was acclaimed "new Helen", signed novellae as "Eirene pistos basileus" (masculine) while minting coins as *basilissa* with her own image on both faces. Byzantine and papal sources raise essentially no objection to her sex ("There seems to have been little resistance to the fact of her gender in Byzantium or in Rome", p. 129); the Frankish *femineum imperium* line (Annals of Lorsch, Moissac) is iconoclast polemic, and Neil argues *Annales veteres Francorum*'s "finem" (usurpation, Athaliah) is the truer reading. Her reliance on eunuch ministers (Stauracius, Aëtius) is read as dynastic prudence since eunuchs "were disqualified by their physical imperfection" from the throne — though Stauracius apparently reasoned "that a eunuch was no less fit to reign than a woman" (p. 118).

**Ch. 6, James, Ghosts in the Machine (pp. 93–112).** Tetrarchic and Constantinian imperial women (Prisca, Valeria, Theodora, Fausta, Helena, Constantia, Constantina) are "ciphers", named only when dynastically useful: "women were used … to cement political alliances and to bind men to each other", then "discarded or reused when political alliances changed" (p. 98). Diocletian's system doubled adoption of Caesars with marriage to the Augusti's daughters so that "the two Augusti were both fathers of their adopted sons and in-laws by marriage" (p. 98); Valeria herself adopted Galerius' son by a mistress. Stories about these women (Fausta's choice, Fausta's death) are "good stories" told through sex and childbearing, "the language in which women's actions are almost invariably expressed" (p. 111).

**Ch. 8, Wright, The Brides of 1420 (pp. 133–152).** Sophia of Montferrat (John VIII) and Cleofe Malatesta (Theodoros II of Mistra) were both sexually rejected: John for her face and (Wright suggests) her height; Theodoros by a six-year vow of chastity. Cleofe was pressured to convert to Orthodoxy, "anointed with a little oil", and took up the dress of a Greek nun while insisting "I am as free in my heart as I ever was" (p. 141); she then became Theodoros' "fellow-poet" (σύνεργος) and political partner, credited by Mistra intellectuals with "a truly masculine intelligence" (p. 146) and with improving court women's lives; Pepagomenos' monody addresses the dead *basilissa* as intercessor "as you stand immediately beside God" (p. 148). Shows dress, fasting and anointing as the public technology of a woman's change of status.

**Ch. 2, P. Brown, Byzantine *Virtus* in Southern Italy (pp. 11–27).** Against the scholarly commonplace, Lombard and Norman sources of the 8th–11th c. did not stereotype Byzantine men as effeminate; the real topos was "cunning" and "treachery" (*callidus, perfidia*). Only Liudprand of Cremona (968) and Amatus of Montecassino (c.1080) use *muliebris* / "homes comme fames", both for political ends. Remarkably the eunuch general Narses became the Lombards' *pater patriae* and "exemplar of … virtus" through Paul the Deacon (p. 12), and Lombards and Normans copied Byzantine dress, hairstyles and imperial iconography on coins.

**Ch. 4, A. Brown, Psalmody and Socrates (pp. 57–76) — skimmed.** Female literacy from Hypatia to Anna Komnene, concentrated in abbesses and nuns; convents as the chief new venue for women's literacy (Matrona of Perge teaching pagan women letters in Beirut; Sergia abbess of St Olympias writing a convent history in 630; Irene of Chrysobalanton writing to the emperor in her own hand; the ninth-century nun-hymnographers Theodosia, Thekla and Kassia). Anna Komnene "was forced secretly to seek lessons in grammar from a palace eunuch" (p. 72).

**Ch. 5, Gador-Whyte, Changing Conceptions of Mary (pp. 77–92) — skimmed.** Romanos (6th c.) makes Mary Theotokos, military Protector ("the active, martial, masculine character of the images remains", p. 83), Intercessor (a role "God himself gives to her", p. 84) and ordinary weeping Mother. Her God-bearing lets her "escape the curse of her sex" (p. 80); Anna's barrenness and Mary's virginity are parallel paradoxes of divine creative power. Cites Codex Justinianus 6.40.2 on women made by nature to bear children, "and that is their greatest desire" (p. 88).

**Key verdict for the mod:** the volume never says eunuchs were *women*. The attested range is: feminised male / eternal youth / neither / third gender / angel. The closest the text comes to a eunuch-as-woman equation is (a) Claudian's Eutropius becoming "a pander, and then a lady's maid" (Tougher p. 165), (b) Liudprand's *neutros*, and (c) the structural fact that eunuchs shared women's spaces and women's political position (Garland p. 51; Neil p. 118). A mod faith that *pushes* this one step further ("the castrated are counted among the women") is an intelligible heterodox extension of a real Byzantine category, not an invention from nothing.

### 2.2 Legal status, supply and castration

- **Roman ban, Byzantine native supply.** "the Roman empire had outlawed the castration of its own citizens but it is clear that the Byzantine empire could and did depend on a native supply to meet its demand for eunuchs." (Tougher, p. 166, citing his *Eunuch in Byzantine History*, pp. 60–67.) The shift from "foreign slave eunuchs to the use of native Byzantines as eunuchs" is dated to the seventh century (p. 166).
- **Castration "to qualify boys as potential court officials".** "The sexual indeterminacy of eunuchs, often induced artificially to qualify boys as potential court officials, presents yet another facet of gender relations specific (but not unique) to Byzantium." (Neil, p. 5)
- **Castration as career path, not punishment.** Implied throughout; no passage in this volume treats castration of kin or captives explicitly. (Gap: supply this from Tougher 2008 / Ringrose 2003, both cited here.)
- **Marriage ban.** "eunuchs were not allowed to marry" (Tougher, p. 163).
- **Dynastic disqualification.** Eirene relied on eunuchs Stauracius and Aëtius "who posed little dynastic threat, as their physical deficiencies were supposed to make them ineligible to succeed" (Neil, p. 118); Nelson: "eunuchs could make no claims to the throne themselves, being disqualified by their physical imperfection" (p. 118 n. 28). Yet Stauracius "seems to have harboured ambitions for the imperium 'apparently on the theory that a eunuch was no less fit to reign than a woman'" (p. 118, quoting Treadgold), and Aëtius "had hopes of raising his brother Leo to the throne" (p. 119).
- **Castration not eternal youth.** Claudian's aged Eutropius: "It is crystal clear that castration was not necessarily the key to eternal youth or angelic beauty." (Tougher, p. 166)

### 2.3 Eunuchs in office: church, monastery, court, army

- **Church offices and the attitudinal shift.** "Attitudes towards eunuchs changed when they began to hold high church offices – around the eighth through to the twelfth centuries, attitudes towards both eunuchs and sanctity underwent a seismic shift." (Casey, p. 180, citing Ringrose pp. 112–13.) "In Late Antiquity, eunuchs were often viewed with suspicion, basically because they were considered to have cheated in the struggle to overcome the flesh." (p. 180)
- **Monastic steward of a women's house must be a eunuch.** "The steward at the early twelfth-century foundation of Kecharitomene had to be a eunuch" (Garland, p. 45); at Lips "a male salaried steward" (p. 46); at Bebaia Elpis by contrast "the steward had to be a nun" (p. 46).
- **Monastic exclusion alongside boys.** Male typika exclude "children and beardless youths … especially as eunuchs are also mentioned in conjunction with the troubling children and beardless youths" (Tougher, pp. 162–63). Kecharitomene is "'completely untrodden by men' and closed even to eunuchs" (Garland, p. 53).
- **Court hierarchy formally bifurcated.** "in his treatise of 899 on court hierarchy Philotheos uses the group term 'bearded' to designate those officials who were male but not eunuchs." (Tougher, pp. 154–55)
- **Charismatic space around the ruler.** Eunuchs "ritually protected the ruler and his or her aura from contamination by ordinary mortals, in practice, creating a distinctively gendered space around the ruler. This enabled empresses to transcend their condition as women and occupy the space created for emperors." Eunuchs "'received their charge directly from God in a distinctive ceremony'." (Casey, pp. 180–81, quoting Ringrose p. 210)
- **Generals.** "a string of eunuchs holding military commands in the Byzantine empire, from Solomon in the sixth century until Andronikos Eonopolites in the thirteenth century." (Tougher, p. 164) Narses "vanquisher of the Ostrogoths and governor of Italy" (p. 163); Procopius explains his and Solomon's successes by prophecy — "'the Moors recalled the saying of their women, to the effect that their nation would be destroyed by a beardless man'" (p. 163, Wars 4.12.28, Greek: πρὸς ἀνδρὸς ἀγενείου ὀλεῖται). "Thus there is an underlying assumption that the success of these eunuchs needs to be explained" (p. 163).
- **Narses as Lombard *pater patriae*.** Paul the Deacon made the eunuch "the ideal secular lord, for despite his warlike occupation he was pious, orthodox and a benefactor to churches … 'he obtained victory more by the thanksgivings offered to God than by the arms of war'" (P. Brown, p. 13). Leo Marsicanus later calls Norman mercenary leaders "'like another Narses' (veluti … Narsis)" (p. 20).
- **Eunuch as palace tutor to a princess.** Anna Komnene "was forced secretly to seek lessons in grammar from a palace eunuch" (A. Brown, p. 72).
- **Eunuchs as the ordinary male company of elite women.** "the men with whom they associated were frequently eunuchs, a class with which these women would have had a close rapport in their days at home." (Garland, p. 51)

### 2.4 Beards, hair, bodily theory

- Beard became compulsory for adult males from the seventh century; Phokas (602–10) "the first Byzantine emperor (since Julian) 'with a really distinctive beard'" (Tougher, p. 154). Tougher's thesis: "the beard becoming essential as a vital means of distinguishing non-eunuch adult native males from eunuch native males. Eunuchs may not have been able to grow beards themselves but they may have caused non-eunuch men to want to grow beards in order to assert their masculinity." (p. 166)
- Clement of Alexandria, *Paidagogos* 3.3: "'God planned that woman be smooth-skinned … But man He adorned like the lion, with a beard'" (p. 157); "His beard is the badge of a man … It is older than Eve and is the symbol of the stronger nature" (Τοῦτο οὖν τοῦ ἀνδρὸς τὸ σύνθημα, τὸ γένειον …, p. 157); "'it is a sacrilege to trifle with the symbol of manhood'" (p. 157); men who make their bodies smooth: "'unless one were to see such a person naked, one would think he was a woman'" (p. 157).
- Musonius Rufus 21: men who shave and style hair "'can endure being seen as womanish creatures, hermaphrodites' (… ἀνδρόγυνοι καὶ γυναικώδεις …)" (p. 158 n. 26).
- Julian, *Misopogon*: the Antiochenes "'all of you are handsome and tall and smooth-skinned and beardless'" (p. 158 n. 29); "boys who in their beauty emulate women, and men who have not only their jaws shaved smooth but their whole bodies too, so that those who meet them may think them smoother than women" (p. 158 n. 29).
- Synesius, *In Praise of Baldness* (c.396–405): hairlessness denotes "wisdom and divinity"; women are "the sex preoccupied with hair"; he "utilise[s] adulterers and effeminates (among whom he includes the emasculated devotees of Cybele) to help his case as such men were renowned for taking excessive care of their hair" (pp. 160–61). **The galli of Cybele appear here explicitly as the effeminate archetype against which Christian masculinity is defined.**
- Dreambooks: Oneirocriticon of Daniel — losing one's beard "was bitter for anyone"; Manuel II's dreambook — "the beard as a symbol of family and friends and beardlessness as signifying the loss of both" (p. 155). Nicephorus — "seeing a eunuch is altogether auspicious"; Achmet — eunuchs signify angels, good news, victory, "the birth of a male child" (p. 164).
- Hair rites: *De cerimoniis* 2.23 describes "clipping the hair of an imperial child and distributing it to the child's sponsors, creating ties of spiritual or artificial kinship between the boy and these men" (Leo VI under Basil I; p. 162). Constantine IV sent locks of his sons' hair to Pope Benedict II (684–5) "and the clergy and army of Rome" (p. 162). Earinos, Domitian's eunuch favourite, still clipped and dedicated his hair at Pergamum — "'a ritual that marks his move to manhood'" (p. 165).

### 2.5 Eunuchs and women as a political bloc

- Eirene's base: "not in the military, like her husband and son, but among clerics and civil servants – especially eunuchs, such as Stauracius and Aëtius" (Neil, p. 118). Nelson: "'Irene's reliance on eunuchs was part of, rather than in contradiction with, what Garland recognizes as her political skill'" (p. 118 n. 28).
- Theophanes "uniformly negatively characterised" Eirene's eunuchs (p. 118 n. 28).
- Eunuchs "could lead an army into battle while women could not" (p. 118 n. 29).

### 2.6 Polemic and the Latin view

- Claudian, *In Eutropium* (399): Eutropius "cast off by his male lover when he begins to lose his youthful beauty … 'When a woman grows old her children cement the marriage tie and a mother's dignity compensates for the lost charms of a wife … How can I, an old man, please?'" then "becomes a pander, and then a lady's maid"; the grotesque old eunuch is "thrust … from their houses like a troublesome corpse or an ill-omened ghost" (Tougher, pp. 165–66).
- Liudprand (968) as above; Amatus (c.1080) on imperial soldiery as "'men like women' (home comme fames)" (P. Brown, p. 26); but P. Brown's conclusion: "Were Byzantine males regarded as effeminate by the Lombards and other westerners? The bulk of the evidence seems to lead to an answer of no." (p. 26)

**Priesthood, liturgy, sanctuary**

15. **Council of Laodicea c. 11 and 44** | 380 | "'one should not institute in church those called female presbyters [πρεσβύτιδας] or presiders'"; "'women should not enter the sanctuary'" (Casey, p. 179) | Shows that female presbyters existed to be banned — a historically attested "heresy" the mod's faith can claim to continue.
16. **Female deacons ordained in the bema** | 8th-c. rite | "'both were ordained in the bema, that is, within the sanctuary … an area of the church from which the laity – and a fortiori all laywomen – except the emperor were normally barred'"; numbers peak early, "'following iconoclasm, the evidence … becomes increasingly scanty'" (Casey, p. 179) | Attested female ordination with near-identical rite to men's.
17. **Menstrual and seminal pollution** | from mid-3rd c.; Trullo 692 c. 13 | "the prohibition against menstruating women receiving communion, or even entering the church"; nocturnal emissions bar laymen and clergy from communion "(but not from attending church, as with the women in menstruation)"; Trullo requires clerical continence before liturgy (Casey, p. 178); Syrian leaders had earlier "criticized Christians who abstained from the Eucharist during menstruation" as Judaizing (p. 179) | A faith that abolishes menstrual exclusion has an attested early-Syrian precedent.
18. **Emperor as quasi-priest; woman "physically disqualified"** | 7th–9th c. | "Eirene's gender physically disqualified her from the priesthood, the emperor being theoretically if not in practice the chief priest of the church … Apparently, however, this was not raised as an objection" (Neil, p. 116); Maximus at his trial (655) argued "the emperor belonged to the laity not the priesthood" (p. 116 n. 15) | Imperial-priestly ambiguity; a female sovereign nonetheless signed first at Nicaea II.
19. **Eirene signs the acta first; acclaimed new Helen** | 787 | "Eirene was the first to endorse the acta of the ecumenical council with her signature, even before her son"; "Novo Constantino et novae Helenae aeterna memoria!" (Neil, p. 116); *Libri Carolini* contain "an admonishment against women teaching at a synod, as Eirene was said to have done" (p. 121) | A woman presiding over doctrine; Frankish objection is doctrinal/gendered polemic.
20. **Nuns direct their own liturgy and music** | 12th–14th c. typika | "no professional psalm-singers were allowed in these all-female institutions … the music was entirely in the hands of the nuns themselves, who were able to decide on their own liturgical preferences: the services conducted, lighting arrangements, music, ritual and prayers" (Garland, p. 47); the *ecclesiarchissa* "organised everything relating to services in the church, including the direction of the liturgy" (p. 47) | De facto female liturgical leadership short of the altar.
21. **Women hymnographers** | 9th c. | "Theodosia, Thekla and Kassia were all Constantinopolitan nuns of aristocratic origin … the hymns of Kassia successfully entered the Orthodox liturgy" (A. Brown, p. 68); they "were writing for their nuns from a female perspective" (Garland, p. 47) | Women authoring canonical liturgy.
22. **Gendered church decoration** | Byzantine | "when the monastery of Maroules in Constantinople was changed from a convent to a monastery in 1342 the depictions of female saints were replaced by those of males"; female saints positioned "as close to the sanctuary as possible and even within it" by female patrons (Garland, pp. 48–49) | Iconographic programme can be sex-segregated.
23. **Mary as commander and intercessor** | 6th–7th c. | "'Virgin Commander' (ἡ στρατηγὸς Παρθένος)" (George of Pisidia); Christ tells Mary at the Cross "'Have mercy on Adam and take pity on Eve'" — "the intercessory role is so central to Mary's character that God himself gives it to her" (Gador-Whyte, pp. 81 n. 12, 84) | Female divine-adjacent patroness as military protector.
24. **Dead *basilissa* as intercessor** | 1433 | Pepagomenos to Cleofe: "'with your prayers to the divine, as you stand immediately beside God, to alleviate the distress of our ruler'" — "Cleofe in the position of both Zeus and the Panagia" (Wright, p. 148) | Imperial woman assimilated to Theotokos at death.

**Female rule and regency**

25. **Catalogue of ruling women** | 5th–9th c. | Pulcheria regent at 15 (414); Theodora; Sophia; Martina co-regent then "mutilated and sent into exile"; Euphrosyne; Theodora II ends iconoclasm (Neil, pp. 115–16) | Serial precedent for female regency.
26. **Eirene *basileus*/*basilissa*** | 797–802 | "her adoption of the title of basileus for herself in written documents, while issuing coins with her image on both sides, under the title of basilissa … 'Eirene pistos basileus', on two novellae" (Neil, p. 128 and n. 72); "there was no constitutional opposition to it" (Runciman, p. 129 n. 77) | A woman legally styled with the masculine imperial title.
27. **Eirene blinds her son** | 797 | "Nor does he indicate that he thought the act any more heinous because it was perpetrated against her son … blinding was a relatively normal method of disqualifying one's rivals" (Neil, p. 118); she also "had her five brothers-in-law … blinded" (p. 131) | Female sovereign using ordinary dynastic violence.
28. **Frankish *femineum imperium*** | 800–801 | Annals of Lorsch: "'the title of emperor had already by then ceased among the Greeks, and they had a woman ruler there (femineum imperium)'" (Neil, p. 124); Life of Willehad: "'with men of the royal family lacking there … the state being administered by a woman's authority (feminea … dicione)'" (p. 127) | The only in-period source class that treats female rule as voiding the throne — and Neil argues even that is really iconophile/iconoclast polemic.
29. **Marriage proposal Charlemagne–Eirene** | 798/801 | "The presence of papal legates on Charlemagne's embassy to arrange a marriage contract indicates papal recognition of her rule." (Neil, p. 122) | A reigning woman treated as marriageable sovereign.
30. **Imperial women on coins** | 4th c. | Fausta "nimbed" on Trier gold; Helena as Pax/Securitas; Constantia "Soror Constantini Aug"; Theodora posthumously *Augusta* (James, pp. 101–103); Pulcheria "crowned by the right hand of God" (Neil, p. 129) | Visual sovereignty vocabulary for women.

**Kinship: adoption, artificial kinship, matriline, succession**

31. **Tetrarchic adoption + marriage** | 293 | "the policies of adoption, whereby the men of the tetrarchy were kept in the family, were echoed in the policies of marriage … Thus the two Augusti were both fathers of their adopted sons and in-laws by marriage" (James, p. 98) | Adoptive succession is Roman-imperial norm.
32. **Valeria adopts Candidianus** | c.295/6 | "Valeria adopted Candidianus, Galerius' son by an unnamed mistress" (James, p. 94); she later comes "in disguise to the court of Licinius to learn the fate of her adopted son" (p. 105) | A woman as adoptive parent of an heir.
33. **Isaac Komnenos' adopted son** | 12th c. typikon | Isaac "mentions just three other individuals, including his adopted son" (Garland, p. 37 n. 37) | Adoption in aristocratic commemoration.
34. **Hair-clipping sponsorship = artificial kinship** | 9th c. | "creating ties of spiritual or artificial kinship between the boy and these men" (Tougher, p. 162) | Byzantine ritual mechanism for non-blood kinship, usable as model for a *gallae* adoption rite.
35. **Convent succession through daughters in perpetuity** | 1110–16 | Irene Doukaina: "control of the institution was to pass first to her daughter Eudokia … then to her eldest daughter Anna Komnene; following Anna to a third daughter Maria; then to Anna's daughter Irene; and then in sequence to all her daughters-in-law and granddaughters in perpetuity" (Garland, p. 53) | **Documented matrilineal transmission of a landed institution with its own estates, water-rights, church and bathhouses.**
36. **Euphrosyne "mistress and heir … ancestral convent"** | c.1300 | Theodora Synadene: her daughter is "'mistress and heir of this convent and all my property', and Euphrosyne herself refers to the institution as 'her ancestral convent'" (Garland, p. 41); great-granddaughter Eugenia a further "'foundress'" (p. 50) | Four generations of women inheriting one foundation.
37. **Convent as female-only multigenerational family** | Palaiologan | "convents housed a female-only multigenerational family, in which mothers and daughters could inhabit shared quarters and live and work together" (Garland, p. 54); widows "had made a decision which excluded them from any further contact with their male children" (p. 54) | Separatist women's household with property.
38. **Theodora Palaiologina buried with mother not husband** | 1303 | "'for I cannot bear to be separated from her [my mother] even after my death'"; could not be buried with "her 'heretical' husband Michael VIII" (Garland, p. 55) | Mother–daughter bond overriding marital burial.
39. **Convent as mother-substitute** | typika | the superior "'substitute for your father, your mother, your brothers and sisters'"; nuns addressed as "'daughters, mothers and sisters'" (Garland, p. 45); Theodora Synadene "'conceived in the womb of my heart and gave birth to'" her foundation (p. 45) | Fictive maternal kinship language for an institution.
40. **Fosterage of girls in convents** | typika | "two girls being raised in the convent" at Kecharitomene; at Baionaia "girls admitted to the convent with their mothers focused on learning to read until the age of 13" (Garland, pp. 38, 45 n. 73) | Child-rearing by a women's community.
41. **Double monasteries** | Basil → Palaiologan | "forbidden by Justinian, the Second Council of Nicaea in 787 and the ninth- and eleventh-century patriarchs … actually made a come-back in the Palaiologan era"; Irene Choumnaina founded one at 16 "with herself at its head" and both parents joined (Garland, p. 32) | Mixed-sex religious households repeatedly banned and revived.
42. **Whole families taking vows and splitting by sex** | 783, 1316 | Theoktiste sent all six males to Sakkoudion and "transformed the family house into a convent"; nine-year-old Euthymios "was forced on board the boat by his mother" (Garland, p. 34); Palamas family likewise (p. 34) | Kinship reorganised along sex lines by a matriarch.

**Marriage, divorce, remarriage, concubinage, childlessness**

43. **Bishop's wife must enter a convent** | canon law; Balsamon | clergy "could not accept promotion to a bishopric unless their wives 'voluntarily' entered a monastery … at a distant remove"; Balsamon: by marrying a priest "she had signalled her commitment to monastic life" (Garland, p. 35) | Spouse's status changed by husband's rank.
44. **Marriage as *leitourgia*** | Gregory of Nyssa, Gregory Nazianzen | "marriage as a lifestyle that offers the possibility for a virtuous life of service (leitourgia)"; both Cappadocians "acknowledge marriage as a genuine path of deification even if ultimately they consider virginity to be the better path" (Casey, pp. 177–78) | Marriage subordinated to, but not excluded from, the ascetic ideal.
45. **Spouses separating for the monastic life** | 1400 etc. | Ignatios Theologites and Makrina "'chose to adopt the monastic habit, in the belief that that life in this world was a useless tumult'"; "Such separations were encouraged by the church" (Garland, p. 35); husband's approval needed for a wife's entry (p. 36) | Church-sanctioned marital dissolution.
46. **Theodoros' fixed-term chastity vow** | 1420–27, Mistra | "'abia promesso habitare con lei sei anni et non più, et vive in observantia de sua castità et astenentia, non magiando mai carne'" (Wright, p. 140 n. 27) | Marriage with timed celibacy clause.
47. **Codex Justinianus 6.40.2** | 6th c. | "'nature made women for the purpose of producing offspring, and that is their greatest desire'" (Gador-Whyte, p. 88 and n. 33) | Legal motherhood ideology — the thing the mod's doctrine inverts.
48. **Childlessness / child death as path to the cloister** | typika | "the typikon of Lips considers 'the death of children' as one of the 'terrible things in the world' and allows a woman in this circumstance to be tonsured early" (Garland, pp. 34–35); Neilos Damilas requires a year's trial "'to see if she can endure the loss of her children'" (p. 54) | Motherhood ideology intertwined with women's religious life.
49. **Concubinage and discarded partners** | 4th c. | Helena "a discarded partner … who may not even have been a lawful wife"; Minervina "probably Constantine's concubine rather than wife" (James, p. 99, 95–96) | Non-marital reproduction in the imperial house.
50. **Anna's barrenness ≈ Mary's virginity** | Romanos | "Anna's barrenness functions in this kontakion in a similar way to Mary's virginity, emphasising through paradox the creative power of God" (Gador-Whyte, p. 78) | Sacralised non-reproduction.

**Same-sex relations, sumptuary codes, dress**

51. **Eutropius and his male lover** | 399 | "Eutropius is cast off by his male lover when he begins to lose his youthful beauty" (Tougher, p. 165) | Eunuch as male beloved in late-antique polemic.
52. **Julian on not loving a beautiful youth** | 362–3 | *Mis.* 365A "on Julian and his entourage not falling in love with a beautiful youth" (Tougher, p. 158 n. 29) | Pederastic desire assumed as background.
53. **Byzantine vs Italian female dress** | 1420 | "Byzantine dress concealed as much as possible of a woman's hair, neck, and shape, while Italian dress bared the neck, emphasised the bosom, and favoured ornate, up-swept hair"; "'Most Byzantine fabrics are dense and relatively thick'" (Wright, pp. 133, 140) | Sumptuary gendering.
54. **Cleofe's anointing and nun's dress** | Jan 1427 | "'I have not become a nun because I was anointed with a little oil. Be sure that I am as free in my heart as I ever was.'" She "performed the necessary public acts of conversion – a statement of faith and an anointing with oil – and took up the dress of a Greek nun" (Wright, pp. 140–41, 144) | A status-changing rite = profession + anointing + change of dress.
55. **Imperial bride re-dressed on arrival** | Pseudo-Kodinos | "the wives would have dressed her as an empress, with the red shoes" (Wright, p. 137) | Investiture by women of a woman.
56. **Lombards promise Byzantine dress and haircuts** | 787 | Charlemagne demanded shaving "in the Frankish manner"; Lombards "promised to adopt … eastern imperial dress and haircuts once the Franks had returned north" (P. Brown, p. 16); Gisulf II's "'beautiful' (bel)" Byzantine mantle (p. 23) | Hair/dress as political-cultural allegiance marker.
57. **Nikephoros II in "womanly" garment** | 968 | Liudprand: Nikephoros "struts around the capital in his 'womanly' (muliebris) garment" (P. Brown, p. 17); but Norman kings copied Byzantine imperial dress on coins — "evidently in their eyes imperial vestments were not womanly but manly" (p. 25) | Same garment read as feminine or imperial depending on viewer.
58. **Nuns' bathing limits** | typika | baths "once a month at Kecharitomene (58), but only four times a year at Lips (34)" while Irene's family had "two bathhouses" (Garland, pp. 48 n. 86, 53) | Class-differentiated bodily regime inside a women's house.

**Hermaphrodite/androgyne vocabulary**

59. **ἀνδρόγυνοι** | Musonius Rufus 21 | shaved, coiffed men "ἀνδρόγυνοι καὶ γυναικώδεις" = "womanish creatures, hermaphrodites" (Tougher, p. 158 n. 26) | Classical term of abuse available for a self-chosen third category.
60. **Philo's incorporeal androgyne** | see #1 | "its androgyny must be understood spiritually, which means that the creature was really neither male nor female" (Casey, p. 170) | Positive androgyne as divine image.

**Separatism and women's property**

61. **Women's convents as family trusts** | 12th–14th c. | "setting up a family property trust and private retirement home for themselves and members of their family for generations to come" (Garland, p. 36); Lips endowed with villages, farms, mills, "a fishpond and rental properties, with even the number of olive trees itemised" (p. 51) | Women-controlled landed corporations.
62. **Women-only hospital** | 1294–1301 | Lips "12-bed hospital for women which was to be under the control of the convent's superior" with "a priest, three doctors … a laundress" (Garland, p. 38 n. 43) | Female-run welfare institution.
63. **Officials elected by nuns** | Bebaia Elpis | officers "all of whom were chosen by voting, not by direct appointment by the superior" (Garland, p. 46) | Elective governance in a women's house.
64. **Protectress (*ephoros*) and male guardianship** | typika | "'the frail nature of women requires the ephoreia (protection) and guardianship of men'" (Garland, p. 44) — yet the actual protectress of Kecharitomene is Anna Komnene (p. 52) | Nominal male oversight, real female control.
65. **Eunuch Narses as pious warrior-saint-type** | Paul the Deacon | see §2.3 | The mod's warrior-priestess/gallae general has a direct Lombard-Byzantine literary archetype.

## 4. Belief-plausibility matrix

Legend: **S** = strengthens, **W** = weakens. Entry numbers refer to §3; dossier items to §2. Verdict scale: *attested* (the thing itself existed), *extension* (one deliberate step beyond an attested category), *inversion* (reverses an attested value while keeping its structure), *anachronism* (no in-period hook in this volume).

| Belief | Strengthen | Weaken | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(a) Trans women as a distinct sex class** | §2.1: Byzantium "recognised not just two but three genders" (Neil p. 3); Philotheos' bifurcation of officials into eunuchs vs "bearded" (Tougher p. 155); eunuchs legally barred from marriage and succession (§2.2); Liudprand's *neutros*; Artemidorus "neither among men nor among women" | The third class is *eunuchs*, defined by bodily lack, not by identity; no source treats them as women (§2.1 verdict); #8 no male-to-female stories | **Extension.** A castrate class as a juridically distinct third sex is fully attested; naming that class "women" or "trans women" is the mod's step. Strongest of all the beliefs. |
| **(b) Womanhood achieved by rite** | #7 "Changing the body meant changing one's gender"; #11–12 Mary/Marinos: haircut + dress + monastery entry as "a rite of transition"; #54 Cleofe: profession + anointing + dress; #34 hair-clipping creates kinship; #55 bride re-dressed and shod by women | All attested rites move women *toward* manhood or toward the cloister; #8; #13 Garland calls the dress-change motif fiction | **Inversion.** The Byzantine logic (rite → body → gender) is attested; the direction is reversed. |
| **(c) Castration as sacred rebirth** | §2.1 angel-eunuch; Achmet "eunuchs are pure, angel-like and insusceptible to lust"; Casey: "'against nature' can slip into 'beyond nature'" (p. 180); 8th–12th c. "seismic shift" in attitudes to eunuch sanctity (p. 180); Luke 20:36 angels who do not marry (#3) | Castration done for careers (Neil p. 5), not religion; eunuchs "cheated in the struggle to overcome the flesh" (Casey p. 180); Synesius uses "emasculated devotees of Cybele" as negative type (Tougher p. 160); no Byzantine castration liturgy in this volume | **Extension.** Angelic reading of the eunuch is real; a *liturgy* of castration is the mod's addition, with the galli as the explicit pagan foil Byzantines knew. |
| **(d) Castrated men regarded as women** | Eutropius "a pander, and then a lady's maid" (Tougher p. 165); eunuchs the normal male company of elite women (Garland p. 51); eunuchs politically grouped with a female ruler (Neil p. 118); "feminised males" (Tougher p. 160); Clement/Julian: smooth-bodied men "think them smoother than women" | Kecharitomene closed "even to eunuchs" — i.e. a women's house did *not* count them as women (Garland p. 53); eunuchs commanded armies "while women could not" (Neil p. 118 n. 29); dreambooks and Ringrose make them a third thing, not women | **Inversion/extension.** The strongest single counter-text is the Kecharitomene exclusion. Treat as a heterodox doctrine the mod's faith *asserts* against mainstream Byzantine practice. |
| **(e) Women-only / transfem priesthood** | #15 Laodicea bans *presbytides* (so they existed); #16 female deacons ordained in the bema; #20–21 nuns run liturgy and compose hymns; §2.3 eunuch bishops and the 8th–12th c. church offices; #18–19 Eirene presides at Nicaea II | Laodicea and Trullo bar women from sanctuary; #17 menstrual exclusion; "Eirene's gender physically disqualified her from the priesthood" (Neil p. 116); deaconesses dwindle after iconoclasm | **Extension / heresy-coded.** A priesthood of women and eunuchs is the fusion of two attested classes (female deacons + eunuch clergy). Best framed as revival of the banned *presbytides*. |
| **(f) Separatism** | #35–40, #61–64: women's convents as self-governing, property-holding, multigenerational, "completely untrodden by men", electing officers, excluding sons | Nominal male *ephoreia* (#64); husband's consent required for entry (Garland p. 36); convents "far from the remote sites" — i.e. embedded in cities and family estates, not autonomous | **Attested** at institutional scale (convent), extension at polity scale. |
| **(g) Adoptive / matrilineal heirship** | #31–33 Roman adoptive succession; #32 a woman as adoptive parent; #34 artificial kinship by hair-rite; #35–36 convent headship passing mother → daughters → granddaughters → daughters-in-law "in perpetuity"; #38 mother–daughter burial | Only for *religious* property; imperial succession is agnatic; James: women are "ciphers" in dynastic texts; Eirene's rule read as usurpation from her son | **Attested for institutions, extension for titles.** The Kecharitomene succession clause (#35) is the single best citation. |
| **(h) Communal property** | Convents "stressed the importance of the cenobitic life and communal living" (Garland p. 52); nuns' handicraft "sold for the institution's profit" (p. 45); Philanthropos Soter: all nuns including the foundress share manual labour (p. 40) | "Despite the perception that monasteries in Byzantium shared an egalitarian and communal lifestyle this was certainly not true of the five establishments" (p. 41); princesses keep servants, private apartments, better food; Galatariotou's "labour exploitation" of working nuns (p. 41 n. 54) | **Attested as ideal, contradicted in practice.** Good flavour for an "egalitarian" tenet with an elite-hypocrisy event chain. |
| **(i) Polyamory / non-marital kinship** | #49 concubinage; #39 fictive kinship terms; #41 double monasteries; #46 timed celibacy; #45 spousal separation encouraged | Marriage law is monogamous; eunuchs barred from marriage; Codex Justinianus 6.40.2 (#47); Justinian's remarriage laws | **Weak / anachronism** for polyamory; **attested** for non-marital (monastic, adoptive, sponsorial) kinship. Steer the mod toward "kinship by rite" rather than plural marriage. |
| **(j) Bodily modification as sacrament** | #7; #4 desert bodies losing female markers as proof of victory; §2.4 beard as "sacrilege to trifle with"; #34 hair-clipping with distribution of hair to sponsors; #54 anointing | Castration itself never sacralised in text; body changes are *ascetic by-products*, not sought | **Extension.** Hair-cutting and anointing are attested sacramental body-acts; castration is the leap. |
| **(k) Intra-queer hostility** | Eunuch-on-eunuch rivalry Stauracius vs Aëtius, Eirene "excelled at playing off these two rivals" (Neil p. 119); #51 Eutropius discarded by male lover; Skylitzes' *thēlydrias* slur against a eunuch general (P. Brown p. 12 n. 7); eunuchs suspected of "cheating" by ascetics (Casey p. 180) | — | **Attested.** Factional hostility among eunuchs and between eunuchs and ascetics is the natural in-period analogue of baedelist vs transfeminist conflict. |
| **(l) Heresy framing** | #15 Laodicea bans *presbytides*; #41 double monasteries banned four times; #17 menstrual-abstainers called Judaizers; Frankish "femineum imperium" polemic; Theodora buried apart from "'heretical' husband Michael VIII" (#38); iconoclasm as the frame for all 8th–9th c. gender polemic | — | **Attested.** The faith should be heresy-tagged against Orthodox with the specific canons (Laodicea 11/44, Nicaea II on double monasteries, Trullo 13) as its "condemnations". |
| **(m) Female-dominated gender doctrine** | #2 Gregory submits to Macrina; typika written in women's own voices (Garland pp. 37, 41); #21 Kassia in the liturgy; Eirene signs first at Nicaea II; Cleofe advises the imperial council (#14) | "sources … were almost always commissioned and produced by men" (Neil p. 4); women's own virtues "are masculinised" (Garland p. 44); Casey: "The transcendent spirit turns out to be male after all" (p. 172) | **Extension.** Women authored rules for women's houses; a woman authoring *gender doctrine for all* is the step. Macrina–Gregory is the model dialogue. |

**Overall:** the volume supports a "veneer of non-anachronism" best when the mod's faith is presented as (1) a *heterodox Byzantine* movement that (2) fuses two real third-category classes — eunuch clergy and women's monastics — into one, (3) borrows real rites (tonsure/hair-clipping, anointing, re-dressing, profession) for its transition liturgy, (4) runs inheritance on the typikon model of matrilineal institutional succession, and (5) is condemned by real canons. The two places where the sources push back hardest are the Kecharitomene exclusion of eunuchs from a women's house and Casey's "no stories of Byzantine monks pretending to be women".

### Notes for implementers

- The volume gives the mod a ready-made **antagonist frame**: Orthodox Byzantium in its mainstream form (beards, Trullo, Laodicea, Nicaea II's ban on double monasteries) vs the mod faith as the "heretical" fusion of two tolerated Byzantine third categories. Latin Christendom is the second antagonist, using *neutros* / *femineum imperium*.
- For the "castrated men regarded as women" doctrine, the honest lore line is: *mainstream Byzantines did not do this* (Kecharitomene excluded eunuchs), *but they did everything adjacent to it* — angelic third gender, eunuch stewards of women's houses, eunuch-empress political blocs, and a theory that bodily change changes gender. Present the doctrine as the faith's distinctive heresy.
- For matrilineal heirship, cite the Kecharitomene succession clause (Garland p. 53) verbatim in flavour text; it is the cleanest in-period instance of property and office descending through women by charter.
- Gaps this volume does not fill (go to the works it cites): castration procedure and age (Tougher 2008; Ringrose 2003); adelphopoiesis (not mentioned at all here — search Rapp, *Brother-Making*); eunuch saints' Lives; marriage law proper (Beaucamp, Arjava — flagged by Neil p. 2 as "an obvious lacuna").
