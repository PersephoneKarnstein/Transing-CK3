## 1. The argument in brief

- **Why so many Jewish doctors.** From about 1250, Mediterranean society "medicalized": towns, courts and convents wanted doctors. Jews were under 1% of the population (5–8% in big cities) but sometimes 50% or more of practitioners (p. 1). Shatzmiller compares medicine with moneylending: demand grew, the Church held back, and Jews filled the gap (p. 141).
- **Licensing.** By about 1300 a *licentia practicandi* was required almost everywhere. For Provence, Charles of Anjou's regulation is dated July 12, 1296 (p. 15). James I of Aragon said in 1272 that Jews too must be examined (p. 16). Aragon in 1363 had Jews examined "by physicians of their own law" with one Christian assisting (p. 17). Licences could be partial: bones only, eyes only, or "to cure sick women" (pp. 19–20, 110).
- **Training.** There was no university route for most Jews. Training was private, by family or by a paid master (pp. 22–25). Shatzmiller found no evidence that medicine was taught in yeshivot (pp. 22–23). From the 1370s a few Provençal Jews studied at Montpellier, until the 1394 expulsion (pp. 27–31, 35).
- **The Hebrew library.** Jews translated a whole medical library into Hebrew, mostly from Arabic, with Latin translation coming later. Translation was paid work (pp. 36–55).
- **Court careers.** Royal doctors became *familiares* and sometimes envoys or judges of the Jews. They won tax exemptions and exemptions from the badge, and they interceded for their communities (pp. 56–77).
- **Rejection.** Councils and princes legislated against Jewish doctors, with charges of poisoning, sorcery and seduction (pp. 78–99). The laws failed because clergy and towns kept hiring them (pp. 93–96). His summary is "Tolerance thus emerges as the child of necessity" (p. 141).

## 2. What matters for the design

### 2.1 Translators: confirms `tm_hipukh.0030` ("A Book in Arabic")

- **Princes paid Jews to translate.** On March 2, 1296 Vitalis Benvenist de Porta contracted to translate "certain books of medicine" from Arabic for the king of Aragon. He received two shillings a day "and suitable clothing" and paper, and handed over his quires monthly (p. 43). Faraj of Agrigento translated al-Rāzī's *al-Hawi* for Charles of Anjou in 1278–79. He was paid a daily allowance and a gift, and was paid separately for proofreading (pp. 43–44).
- **Kalonymus, from Arles.**
  - In 1305 he "ran away to Barcelona" to improve his Arabic (p. 47).
  - He wrote a letter from Naples in 1314 or 1324 (p. 47).
  - He translated Galen and Averroes (p. 47).
  - He obtained a royal privilege for the Jews of Rome (p. 47).
  - In 1329 "the royal bailiff of Arles" paid "Master 'Calo'" six ounces of gold a year "for the translation of certain books and for other services" (p. 48).
  - Shatzmiller gives his birth year as 1286 (p. 103). Chotzner, used in `jud_kalonymus_even_bohan.md`, gives 1287.
- **Arabic still in Jewish hands.** In 1302 the Lleida (Lérida) faculty's founding decree assumed that the city's Jews owned Arabic books, and ordered them to lend those books to the professor (pp. 39–40). This is the "book in Arabic" of the event.
- **Every named translator is a man.** No woman translator appears anywhere in the book.

### 2.2 Women physicians: grounds a physician variant

The notaries called them *magistra*, *medica*, *fisica* or *chirurgica*. "Dozens of the documents" record them (p. 109). They never held a university title, and when their families are known, a male relative also practised (p. 109).

- **Licences for women.** The Neapolitan licences justify women doctors on grounds of modesty: by law women may practise, since "they are better suited to treat sick women than are men" (pp. 110–111). Shatzmiller suspects that Sara and others also taught women, but this is his guess (p. 110).
- **Eye-healing was a women's field.** Judah, son of Asher ben Yeḥiel, was saved from blindness as a child by "a Jewess, a skilled oculist" (p. 111).

### 2.3 Arles

- In May 1402 Salomon Avigdor was examined at Arles by four doctors, one of them Christian. He answered "in a high and clear voice", set out his problem, and offered to defend it "against any opponent" (p. 37).
- Abraham Avigdor of Arles is the first Provençal Jew known to have studied at Montpellier, before 1379 (p. 29).
- On Jan. 4, 1369 the count of Provence made Bendit Ḥayyim of Arles a *familiaris* (p. 60).
- In 1385 Pernes hired Astrugus de Marnovilla of Arles (p. 115).
- In 1421 Moses de Roquemart was doctor to the **archbishop of Arles** (p. 94).
- In 1306 David of Arles received a licence that forbade him to approach a Christian's sickbed until the patient had confessed and received communion (p. 91).
- Stouff found few doctors listed for Arles: 6 in 1407, 3 physicians and 1 surgeon in 1440 (p. 106).

### 2.4 The community and its lords (bears on `tm_hipukh.0003` and `tm_turning_niddui_effect`)

- **A lord against the community over dress.** At Xàtiva (Játiva), the community passed a sumptuary law, and the king's physician Samuel got himself exempted. The community answered with a new law aimed at dissenters "forbidding them to wear colored clothing", and the infanta intervened for Samuel (chancery document of Feb. 10, 1282; p. 74). This is the closest precedent in the repo for the elders ruling on one member's dress, and for a Christian patron overruling them.
- **A lord against the rabbi in the synagogue.** In 1385 the king allowed Junez Trigo and his lineage to read the Torah whenever they wished, "notwithstanding the opinion of the rabbi or other Jews" (pp. 74–75).
- **A ban had teeth.** At Manosque in 1310 the rabbi's excommunication of a man meant that no one would circumcise his son. A dissenter did it, and the child died (pp. 134–135). Use this only as background to *niddui*, never as an event.
- **Privilege bred resentment.** Communities fought physicians' tax exemptions in court (p. 73). A daughter under a lord's protection would be resented for her exemption, not only for her dress.
- **"The synagogue of women."** At Orange, in 1459, Blanqueta accused a physician "in the synagogue of women", on a Yom Kippur that fell on a Sabbath (pp. 80–81). This is late and from outside Arles, but it is the first Provençal witness in the repo to a women's synagogue. `jud_shatzmiller_jews_of_provence.md` found none.

### 2.5 Kalonymus as satirist of doctors

*Even Boḥan* has a doctor who diagnoses an abscess "from the pulses of the hand and the chamber pot", takes part of his fee in advance and blames the relatives when the patient dies (p. 103, citing Habermann ed., pp. 44–48). The doctor is paid "before [the] legal heirs" (p. 128, Habermann p. 47). If an elder ever quotes the *Touchstone* on physicians, the passage is here.

## 3. Corrections

1. **`judaism_reading_list.md`, Part A, entry 22.**
   - Sara is "wife of Abraham" (*uxore Abrahe*) in the Latin (ch. 6 n. 36). Neither the Latin nor Shatzmiller calls her a widow or calls Abraham a physician.
   - Shatzmiller gives "only six months" of study (p. 25). The Latin runs "until next Easter" (*hinc ad festum Paschatis*).
   - The page is pp. 24–25, with a second account at pp. 109–110. The Wikipedia page reference is now checked.
2. **Women's section.** Add Orange 1459, "the synagogue of women" (pp. 80–81), to the gallery question in `jud_shatzmiller_jews_of_provence.md`. It shows a women's room or synagogue, not necessarily a gallery. The loc "women's gallery" (`tm_hipukh.0010.desc`) stays plausible. "The women's synagogue" would be closer to this witness.
3. **Kalonymus's birth year.** Shatzmiller gives 1286 (p. 103) and Chotzner 1287. The README and loc give no year, so nothing needs to change.
4. **Montpellier holy site.** "The translators' country, where the learning of the Arabs was put into Hebrew" (`holy_site_tm_montpellier_effects`) is fair as a regional description. Shatzmiller places the 12th-century translation school at **Lunel**, under Meshulam ben Jacob (pp. 41–42). Montpellier's own claim is Jacob ben Machir (d. c. 1307), its professors (pp. 27–28), and Jewish students there in 1370–94 (pp. 29–31). He also shows that the legend of Jewish founders of the medical faculty is false (p. 27). Optional rewording: "Where Jacob ben Machir worked beside the masters of the faculty, and where Jewish students came to learn medicine until 1394."

## 4. Design implications

### `tm_hipukh.0030` (built)
- The desc's claim ("as the Jews of Provence have done for princes before") is confirmed by pp. 43–48.
- **Option a.** Optional texture: "paper and suitable clothing" as part of the commission (p. 43). The 1329 Arles payment (p. 48) suits a post-1322 variant of the desc: "as King Robert's bailiff at Arles paid Master Calo".
- **The translator is a woman.** This is the faith's own fiction. The narrator must not claim a historical woman translator. The current desc does not.
- **Choosing the book.** Galen, Avicenna, al-Rāzī, Averroes or Alfarabi are all safe titles. **Never name Bernard of Gordon's *Lilium medicinae*** as the book. It contains the "Jews bleed from the hemorrhoids" passage (`jud_male_menses_myth.md`), and Shatzmiller notes its Hebrew translation (p. 53).
- **`tm_translation_patron_modifier`.** The protection it gives is grounded in the *familiaris* grants (pp. 59–60). Possible follow-up texture: an exemption from the badge for the translator (pp. 73–74), and the community's resentment of it (p. 73).

### Proposed `tm_hipukh.0031` "A Fisica at Court" (physician variant; not built)
- **Trigger.** A daughter of the Turning at a lord's court, with learning above a threshold.
- **Desc sketch.** "[tm_fisica.GetFirstName] learned the art from her mother's brother, and the notaries would write *fisica* after her name if my seneschal gave her a licence. Without one she will be fined, as Mayrona was at Bayons."
- **Options.**
  - **Licence her.** Costs gold and gives her an opinion bonus, and she becomes court physician. Grounding: the licence system, p. 15.
  - **Licence her for women only.** A smaller bonus. Grounding: the Neapolitan formula, pp. 110–111.
  - **Refuse.** Possible reaction: a bishop cites the Council of Avignon of 1337, which forbade Jews "of both sexes" to treat Christians (p. 92). Its 1341 revocation (pp. 93–94) gives a later answer: "Avignon itself took that back."
- **Names.** Mayrona/Mairona, Regina, Blanca and Benvenguda are already in the street list (`tm_give_daughter_name_effect`). This book shows all four as names of medical women. Bellaire, Pla and Viridimura are from Aragon and Sicily, so do not add them to an Arles list. Hava is Hebrew (Manosque) and could join the scroll list.
- **Teaching.** The Sara contract supports a line event: the daughter takes a pupil, as Sara of Saint-Gilles did. It is a teaching contract only, with no gender content.

### Hard limits (add to `faith_design.md` §13)
No text in any faith may voice these, even as a Christian slur. All are documented in ch. 5:
- **poisoning** ("thirsty for Christian blood", Queen Maria of Aragon, 1397, pp. 87–88; Valladolid 1322 and Salamanca 1335, p. 92; the 1349–50 Provençal rule that Jewish surgeons taste the wine, p. 88);
- **sorcery and devil-charms** (Briançon 1433, p. 86);
- **bones of the Christian dead for medicine** (Savoy 1466; Ibn Zabara's report of the charge, p. 86);
- **seduction of female patients** (synod of Avignon 1337; Manosque 1340, pp. 88–90);
- **abortion charges** (Manosque 1298, Crete c. 1419, pp. 82, 84);
- the 1337 phrase that Jewish doctors' "servile status" was "inflated" (p. 99).

Two items need specific attention:
- **The 1340 sentence to "lose his member"** (p. 90) is castration as a judicial punishment of a Jew. Never use it. It would also fold into the knife.
- **No death-of-a-Christian-patient branch for a Jewish fisica.** Shem Tov of Tortosa's 1261 account shows that one death could endanger a whole community (pp. 83–84). The design should not dramatise it.
- **No positive "mystique" of the Jewish doctor either.** Shatzmiller tests the idea and leaves it unproven (pp. 119–120). A Christian lord's line should praise *her* learning, never Jews' aptitude.

### Link to the potion ban
`tm_hipukh.0031` must never let the fisica prepare a sterilising draught for a member. *Sefer ha-Ḥinukh* 291 forbids even women to castrate a male "with a cup of roots" (`jud_rabbinic_primary_texts.md` §12). Her art stays physic and surgery for others.
