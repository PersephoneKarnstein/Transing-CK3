## 0. Framing warning

- **This is a modern history, 1600s to 2010s.** Its medieval paragraph (pp. 235–239) is a summary of Mamluk sources, mostly through modern Arabic studies. **Nothing in it is evidence for 1066.**
- **Its one Fatimid claim is thin.** Prostitution in Cairo "can be traced back to the Fatimid period (969–1171)" (p. 235). The only support is one *Khiṭaṭ* page (n. 3, a 1997 two-volume Cairo edition, ii p. 357). Footnote 10 cites the same page for the Arḍ al-Ṭabbāla, so n. 3 is probably the Ṭabbāla entry as well. In that entry the Fatimid content is a grant of land to a woman singer in 1058. The vice Maqrīzī describes there belongs to his own time, c. 1400 (§1.2). **Do not cite H&B for Fatimid prostitution.**
- **On gender it is silent.** It never uses *khawal*, *mukhannath* or "effeminate", and it mentions no cross-dressing dancers. Its men in the sex trade are enslaved men, "boys", male dancers and pimps (§2).
- **What it is good for:** how a state that both taxes and condemns sex work treats the women in it. Registers, bribes, crackdowns, banishment, and the women's own counter-moves are all documented across four centuries (§3). That is writing guidance, not period evidence.

## 1. Medieval and Mamluk precedents, with their sources checked

### 1.1 The tax-farm of women's music and sex work (*ḍamān al-maghānī*)
- **H&B (p. 237).** Under the Mamluks the *maghani* taxes were "substantial sources of revenue" (Ibn Taghrībirdī, *Nujūm* i p. 47). A **woman tax-farmer**, the *ḍāminat al-maghānī*, bought the right to collect them and "kept records of the prostitutes". H&B, after ʿAbd al-Rāziq (1999, p. 40), read the tax as paid for state protection and as "state recognition of sex workers as a professional group" (p. 237).
- **Checked in al-Maqrīzī (*Khiṭaṭ*, ed. Sayyid, 1:286).** The tax-farm was abolished by al-Ashraf Shaʿbān in **778/1376–77**, the last such abolition Maqrīzī saw. He calls it "a great affliction". The ruling that matters for writing:
  - "If the noblest woman in Egypt wanted to sell sex, she entered her name with the *ḍāmina*, and no grandee could stop her" *(trans., condensed)*.
  - **The *ḍāmina* also levied every women's celebration:** childbirth, a bride's wedding, henna on a woman's hands, any feast with songs. Holding one "without the *ḍāmina*'s permission" brought ruin *(trans.)*.
  - So the farm taxed **women's music and festivity as a whole**, with sex work inside it. The variant matters: Būlāq reads *al-aghānī* ("songs") for Sayyid's *al-maghānī*.
- **H&B's gloss is doubtful.** They call *maghānī* literally "male singers" (p. 236), a gloss we could not confirm. The Būlāq variant points to "songs" or "singing".
- **Other Mamluk entries (Khiṭaṭ, ed. Sayyid):**
  - 749/1348: the *ḍāminat al-maghānī* is assessed **50,000 dirhams** towards a Nile dyke, alongside the khans and the churches (3:559).
  - **666/1268, Baybars:** prostitution is banned, the khans used for it are looted, some people are exiled, and the women are jailed "until they marry". The sum "assessed on prostitutes" is struck from the *dīwān*, and the entourage gets lawful revenue in its place (1:285).

### 1.2 The "taxes of the singing girls" (*ḥuqūq al-qaynāt*)
- **H&B (p. 238)** say the *huquq al-qinat* fell on "every male and female who provided sexual services in taverns". There was also a *huquq al-Sudan* on slave-prostitutes. Sources: *Khiṭaṭ* p. 144; al-Suyūṭī, *Ḥusn al-muḥāḍara* ii p. 218; al-Bayyūmī Ismāʿīl (1998) p. 201.
- **Checked (*Khiṭaṭ*, ed. Sayyid, 1:240–241).** These are items in the list of dues **abolished in al-Nāṣir Muḥammad's land survey (*rawk*) of 715/1315**. H&B run three items together; Maqrīzī keeps them apart:
  1. ***Ḥuqūq al-qaynāt*:** "what is collected from the *fawāḥish* and *munkarāt*" *(trans.)*. It was gathered by the steward of the sultan's ewer-house "from the rabble".
  2. ***Ḥuqūq al-sūdān*:** a separate item.
  3. **A fixed due on every enslaved woman or man** brought down to the khans for sex, "taken from every male and female" *(trans.)*.
  
  The "male" in H&B's sentence is therefore **enslaved men at the khans**.
- **Also abolished in 1315 (1:239): *rusūm al-wilāya*.** The governors collected it from market headmen and "houses of *fawāḥish*" through a tax-farmer with "boys" (*ṣibyān*) under him. Maqrīzī says it meant exposing hidden people and raiding houses *(trans., paraphrase)*. **Policing as extortion is the medieval pattern too.**
- **C&C's phrase is the same Mamluk levy.** Their "taxes of the singing girls" (p. 203) cites *Khiṭaṭ* "vol. 1, p. 89", which Maqrīzī "later explains" as taxes on prostitutes (C&C ch. 6 n. 69). That fits *ḥuqūq al-qaynāt* or *ḍamān al-maghānī*, not a Fatimid tax. The Būlāq page was not checked against Sayyid's.

### 1.3 Quarters, dress and festivals (all Mamluk or later)
- **Quarters and dress (p. 235).** Prostitutes lived in quarters called *ḥārat al-khawāṭī* or *al-zawānī* (Ibn Taghrībirdī; *Khiṭaṭ*). They were on hills away from homes or beside the pleasure-lakes. They wore the *milāya* and *ṭarḥa* "with red anklets" (ʿAbd al-Rāziq p. 39). **Mamluk; not 1066 costume.**
- **Who protested, and when (p. 235).** Protest came "when sex workers lived and openly practised their trade in regular neighbourhoods". It did not come against the quarters themselves.
- **Festivals (p. 235).** Prostitutes were active at saints' days (*mawālid*) and at the Nile flood. H&B call this "a continuation of mediaeval practices", citing Shoshan p. 43. That is Mamluk evidence, as the Shoshan note already says (§1.3 there: Nile promiscuity "Mamluk only").
- **Bans (p. 239).** Qānṣūh al-Ghawrī (1501–16) razed the shacks at Birkat al-Raṭlī. Bans and tolerance alternate throughout.
- **One medieval case of men killing prostitutes (p. 239).** Three men lured sex workers home and murdered them for their clothes, and were executed. H&B cite Shoshan p. 5, which the Shoshan note did not read. **Not for use** (no violence on screen).

### 1.4 The Arḍ al-Ṭabbāla and Nasab the drummer (Fatimid; new detail)
- **H&B (p. 236).** Birkat al-Raṭlī was part of the Arḍ al-Ṭabbāla, "so well known for illicit activities" that men came there "for sex and hashish". Sources: Ibn Iyās v p. 11; *Khiṭaṭ* pp. 357, 750.
- **Checked (*Khiṭaṭ*, ed. Sayyid, 3:415–417).** This is the land al-Mustanṣir gave Nasab the drummer in 450/1058 (Cairo note §3.2). Maqrīzī's entry adds three things that neither the Cairo note nor C&C record:
  - **"A mannish woman" (*imraʾa murajjala*).** She "stood beneath the palace at feasts and walked before the procession, her troupe (*ṭāʾifa*) around her, drumming and singing" *(trans., condensed)* (3:416).
    - **Neither earlier version has the word.** Ibn Muyassar (d. 1278; OpenITI text) and *Ittiʿāẓ* 2:254 tell the grant without *murajjala* and without the troupe.
    - So *murajjala* is Maqrīzī's own word (d. 1442), from an unnamed source. Treat it as **late and unconfirmed**.
    - **The reading is uncertain (checked 2026-10-27).** The text is unvowelled (*imraʾa mrjla*). "Mannish" is one reading, from *rajula*/*mutarajjila*, a woman like a man. But *murajjal* also means "with combed, dressed hair" (*rajjala al-shaʿr*), and al-Qalqashandī uses *murajjala* of a citadel "set on level ground" (*Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā* 4:97). A woman performer "with dressed hair" fits the passage at least as well. **Do not render it "mannish" in loc**, and build nothing on a gender reading of Nasab.
  - **Her name varies.** Sayyid reads *Nashab* and Būlāq *Nasab*. Ibn ʿAbd al-Ẓāhir (d. 1292), quoted at 3:416, calls her "a woman singer, Nashab, or some say Ṭarab, the singer of al-Mustanṣir" *(trans.)*.
  - **What became of the land.** She had a tomb in the Great Qarāfa, the *Turbat Nashab*. The land was let on ground-lease and built up with houses, "among the delights of Cairo" (3:416, Ibn ʿAbd al-Ẓāhir).
- **When it got its bad name.** The Ṭabbāla's ill repute is **post-1403**. By then only a spot called al-Junayna was left, "one of the vilest places", where hashish was sold (3:417). That is Maqrīzī's own day. The Pearl Pavilion, where the caliph lived at Nawrūz, overlooked "all the Arḍ al-Ṭabbāla" (*Ittiʿāẓ* 2, editor's note citing *Khiṭaṭ* 1:467–468).
- **Reading.** In Rasad's own decade, a woman performer with her own troupe was rewarded with land. A later author called her gender-crossing. Her land, not her trade, became a red-light district three centuries on.

## 2. Men in the sex trade, and how the state classified them

| What H&B record | Period | Page |
|---|---|---|
| "Male and female slaves were employed in sex work" in brothels and taverns at Azbakiyya and al-Raṭlī | Ottoman | 235 |
| A brothel in Azbakiyya that "secured boys and women for customers" (from an 18th-c. poet) | 18th c. | 236 |
| A due on "every male and female" in the tavern trade; really enslaved men and women at the khans (§1.2) | Mamluk (abolished 1315) | 238 |
| "Singers, male and female dancers, prostitutes, and beggars" grouped into trade groups, marching at the end of guild processions and "not fully integrated within the guild system" | Ottoman (Evliya Çelebi) | 237 |
| Prostitution associated with "male and female musicians and dancers"; the term *maghānī* | pre-modern | 236 |
| Male pimps; in the 1950s "women dominated pimping", mostly retired sex workers; since the 1970s about 70% of pimps are women | 1950s to 1990 | 257–258 |

**Classification.** Every state H&B describe classifies by **trade and fiscal category**: *maghānī*, *khurda* (lowly trades such as snake charmers and hashish sellers, p. 237), *ḥuqūq al-sūdān*, and the brothel register. None classifies by gender presentation. **No *khawal*, no effeminate category and no cross-dressing dancers** appear anywhere in the chapter, including in the *ghawāzī*/*ʿawālim* passage (pp. 236–237).

**For the mod:**
- The no-*khawal* rule stands; this chapter gives no support for it before 1800 either.
- The male sex labour these sources record is mostly **enslaved**. It must never be written as a free trade or romanticised.
- For a Sister, the medieval fiscal state cared what you earned from, not what you wore. That matches the *Daʿāʾim*'s penalty for acts and not dress (Cairo note §2.1).

## 3. How a taxing and condemning state treats sex workers (writing guidance)

These are early-modern and modern patterns, offered as models for writing Fatimid hostility, which the Cairo note already sources: the *shurṭa* parade, *ḥisba* banishment, confiscation.

- **The register is the weapon.**
  - The Ottoman tax-farmers and officials "had a free hand in placing women's names on the registers and removing them", often depending on "whether a good bribe had been paid" (p. 238).
  - "Casual female labourers were constantly under threat of being registered as prostitutes" (p. 238).
  - Leaving the colonial register took two witnesses and an application, under marriage or repentance (Art. 13, 1885; p. 246).
  - Since 1951 a woman once arrested stays a "registered prostitute", "suspicious and … subject to being arrested" (p. 255).
- **The tax decides tolerance.**
  - Bans come and go (1635–37, 1642–43, 1733, 1834).
  - When Ismāʿīl Agha shut the brothels in 1733 he had to pay off police who had "lost their share of the taxes" (pp. 239–240).
  - In 1866 a tax let farmers assess each dancer by her looks. A dancer's grievance is recorded through Lucie Duff Gordon (p. 241).
- **Banishment produces dependence.** When Muḥammad ʿAlī banned the trade (1834) and banished the women (1836), they lost "much of their autonomy". They were pushed under "pimps and procurers in return for protection from coercive state power" (p. 241).
- **Integration was the norm in practice.** A 1926 Cairo police memorandum found transactional sex "integrated rather than marginalized" in popular neighbourhoods "as long as it did not disturb the public order". Most clandestine workers were seamstresses, washerwomen, servants and shop assistants, many of them married, supplementing family income (p. 248).
- **The women's own counter-moves:**
  - They sued their exploiters as plaintiffs in the consular courts (p. 252).
  - After criminalisation, three-quarters worked autonomously in the 1950s (p. 257).
  - In later networks, pimps (mostly women) pay lawyers, keep the dependants of jailed women, and visit or send colleagues to visit them in prison. That gives "much moral support", above all to women whose families have abandoned them (p. 258).
- **Voices.**
  - H&B note that the postcolonial scholarship rests on sex workers' own narratives, "mostly absent in previous studies" (p. 255). But it was gathered from arrested or imprisoned women for crime-control ends (p. 255), and one scholar posed as a client with police permission (p. 255 n. 86).
  - **The workers' own politics are therefore only glimpsed here.** H&B give no sex-worker organisation, petition or collective statement for any period. The guild-like structures are pimps' (Evliya's three *shaykhs*, p. 237), not the women's.
  - Informants in the case files and ethnographies are described here by role only.
- **The state's cover.** Arrest campaigns against well-known women were used by the Mubarak government to distract from "failing economic policies and political repression" (pp. 259–260).
- **Clients are invisible.** Since 1961 clients are "treated as witnesses", never arrested, and the state keeps no record of them (p. 255).

## 4. Cross-checks

1. **Cairo note §3.2 (Nasab).** The note has her from *Ittiʿāẓ* 2:254 only. *Khiṭaṭ* 3:416 adds *imraʾa murajjala*, her troupe, the procession, the variant names and her tomb (§1.4). This also traces C&C's "with her group" (C&C p. 89, via Lev 2001) to its source.
2. **C&C p. 203 ("taxes of the singing girls").** It is a Mamluk levy, either *ḥuqūq al-qaynāt* (abolished 1315) or *ḍamān al-maghānī* (abolished 1376–77). C&C place it in a Fatimid chapter without dating it. The C&C note repeats it without a date. **Not Fatimid evidence.**
3. **Cairo note §2.6 (*ḥisba*: repentance, then *taʿzīr* and banishment for the singing-woman and the *mukhannath*).** This fits the Mamluk practice H&B summarise. Maqrīzī shows that the state's own tax-farmer **licensed** the same women the *muḥtasib* banished. Treat the treatise and the treasury as two different voices, not one policy.
4. **Cairo note §1.2 (the procurer of 1024).** H&B add nothing Fatimid. The Fatimid record of sex work remains al-Musabbiḥī's police entries.
5. **Cairo note §1.6 and the *khawal* rule.** H&B are silent on *khawal* and on effeminate dancers. Their *ghawāzī*/*ʿawālim* split is drawn from Lane (1825–28) and Van Nieuwkerk (pp. 236–237). This fits the late-18th/19th-century dating in Fortier and Abdelmoez. **Rule stands.**
6. **Noralla's "1961 anti-prostitution law".** H&B give the sequence:
   - Military Decree 76 of 1949 closed the brothels (p. 254);
   - Law 68 of 1951 criminalised the women themselves and defined prostitution as "the practice of vice with others with no distinction" (p. 254);
   - Law 10 of 1961, from the union with Syria, extended this (pp. 254–255).
   
   H&B do not discuss the law's use against men or trans women. Consistent, nothing to change.
7. **Shoshan note.** H&B's Shoshan p. 43 (festival sex) is Mamluk, as the Shoshan note says. Shoshan p. 5 (the murders) was not in the Shoshan note's reading.
8. **H&B's Fatimid sentence (p. 235)** should not be cited (§0).
