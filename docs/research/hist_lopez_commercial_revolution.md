**Scope and bias.** A short synthesis for students. Its thesis is that Italy's merchant-run towns made Europe's first self-made economic take-off, from the 10th c. to the plague. It is Italo-centric: Venice, Genoa and Florence are the model; Byzantium, Islam, Scandinavia and the Jews appear as "early starters who failed to keep up" (p. 117). Africa appears only as the source of gold from "Palola" (Senegal, p. 112). Lopez's pages on the Jews (pp. 60–62, 77–78, 121–122) generalise in ways that are now dated; see §8.

**Relation to other notes.** Read with `byz_sophoulis_banditry.md` (bands, robbery, guards) and `cn_vaissiere_sogdian_traders.md` (a Central Asian merchant diaspora). Sophoulis shows the road from the robber's side. Lopez shows it from the merchant's: tolls, safe-conducts, convoys, privileges. The Hansa and Gotland are treated in more depth in Dollinger (same folder), not read for this note.

**Relevance:** high for the general model of a merchant company (contracts, liability, agents abroad, privileged quarters, relations with rulers) and for the roadmap item "a charter for a trading post held by contract". Moderate for the Bædling (Visby, the Hansa, Skanör) and Adelphai (Italians in Byzantium). Low for women. Nil for eunuchs or gender crossing.

## 1. Partnership forms (the contracts a company is made of)

- **Rogadia ("by prayer").** First in Muslim law and in the earliest Venetian sources. A merchant carried another's goods "as a friendly gesture and without compensation". Lopez says it was especially popular with the North African Jews, whose "togetherness was strengthened by the special dangers of minority status". He also suggests that it might conceal a charge to escape the ban on interest (pp. 73–74). Obsolete early in Venice and unknown elsewhere in the West. It was replaced by the **commission** contract, for a percentage or fee (p. 74).
- **Societas** (partnership). A Roman form: partners pool capital and labour and share profit and risk. Every partner was "jointly and unlimitedly liable" for the others' debts (p. 74).
- **Fraterna.** The earliest Venetian form: brothers jointly managing an inheritance invested in trade, "an adaptation to commerce of a patriarchal institution" (p. 74).
- **Compagnia** (from *cum panis*, "sharer of the same bread"). By the 12th c. It joined brothers, cousins and "sons-in-law chosen among the brightest employees", for a limited sum and term, still with unlimited joint liability (p. 74).
  - It took interest-bearing deposits "above the social capital" (p. 75).
  - Its weakness: sooner or later one partner made a "bad debt", and "his insolvency dragged the others into ruin" (p. 75).
  - No compagnia wrecked a whole town's economy before the mid-13th c. (p. 75).
  - The largest failed in the 14th c.: the Bonsignori of Siena through discord among partners (1298); the Borrino of Piacenza at a partner's death (1300); the Bardi of Florence in 1346, owed over a million florins by the kings of England and Sicily (p. 105). "Princes were notoriously bad payers" (p. 105).
- **Column.** Used at Amalfi, other Italo-Byzantine ports and Ragusa, but not at Venice. Every contribution of capital and labour by captain, sailors and merchants was entered in a column of the ship's log, and profit and risk were shared by value. It fades after the 12th c. but survived in Adriatic fishing (pp. 75–76).
- **Sea loan.** A loan for one voyage, waived if the ship was lost. It fell from favour in the mid-13th c. when the popes ruled the premium was usury (p. 76).
- **Commenda or colleganza** (Lopez's forms are *collegantia*, "colleagueship", and *commenda*, "recommendation"; p. 76).
  - A "stay-at-home party" lent capital to a "traveling party" for one round trip. The lender bore the risk to capital and usually took three-quarters of the profit. The manager bore the risk to labour and kept the rest (pp. 76–77).
  - Third parties dealt only with the manager (p. 77).
  - It was not chiefly rich-and-poor. "The same man could be simultaneously a lender in some contracts and a borrower in others", and a poor man could put a small sum with a great merchant (p. 77).
  - In early contracts the manager had to prove his accounts; later the lender agreed to believe him "without an oath or a witness" (p. 77).
  - Lopez calls it the "closest medieval antecedent" of the joint-stock company, and its origin Islamic or Byzantine, or several independent ones (pp. 76–77).
  - Three commendas of 1156–1158 trebled a Genoese investor's 200 pounds, and the manager earned almost 150 pounds (p. 97).
- **Northern versions.** The German "Easterlings" had partnership and commenda contracts "cruder and hazier" than the Mediterranean ones, "possibly derived from still simpler Scandinavian models". They had no double entry and no insurance (p. 118).

## 2. Guilds, brotherhoods and communes

- **Merchant guilds** appeared in many towns between the 10th and 12th c. They took members "on a foot of equality" but let each pursue unlimited gain. Later they merged into the commune or became cliques (pp. 125–126).
- **Craft guilds** were "a federation of autonomous workshops". Masters decided; journeymen and apprentices expected to become masters. Guilds banned work after dark, limited staff, and standardised quality, size and price. A guild's seal was its advertising (pp. 127–128).
  - They spent dues on "religious ceremonies and assistance to impoverished members", on banquets, and on politics (p. 128).
  - Many had "a long clandestine existence before obtaining official recognition", and some of their activities were illegal (p. 127).
  - In 1294 more than 36,000 of Bologna's almost 50,000 people were guild members or their relatives, including notaries and physicians (p. 126).
  - Guild revolts: Courtrai (1302), where Flemish textile workers beat the French king and the oligarchs; Vilvorde (1306), where the duke of Brabant crushed them (p. 129).
- **"Hansa"** was a common northern word for associations of merchants. Its original meaning "probably was 'armed convoy'" (p. 114). Several hansas came and went along the North Sea before the League, which Lopez dates to 1369 (pp. 114–115).
- **The Visby brotherhood.** The Scandinavian "brotherhood" of merchants at Visby on Gotland was eclipsed by the community of "German visitors of Gotland", which Lopez calls "the first core of the future Hanseatic League" (p. 117). He gives no date.
- **The commune as a company.** The 12th-c. Italian communes were "governments of the merchants, by the merchants, for the merchants" (p. 70). Merchants served in the army "on equal footing with landowners" as early as 750 (pp. 66–67). Townsmen overthrew or bought out their lord (p. 67), and each town drew in its district's nobles until they joined the commune "as the only alternative to total ruin" (p. 69).
- **Princes as co-founders.** In 1120 the duke of Zähringen and twenty-four merchants founded Freiburg im Breisgau, and Bern followed the same pattern (p. 114).

## 3. Factories, quarters and colonies held by privilege

- **Quarters demanded and granted.** Italo-Byzantine merchants, unlike the Jews, "were backed by governments and fleets of their own". They "demanded, and obtained, special quarters" with "facilities for sleeping, warehousing, praying, and washing up" (p. 64).
- **What a quarter was worth.** The colonies mattered not by size "but on the rebates of tariffs granted to them and on the opportunities offered by the surrounding region" (pp. 64–65).
- **Privilege in exchange for help.**
  - **1082:** Alexios Komnenos, pressed by Turks and Normans, bought Venetian naval help by exempting Venice from the duties "still paid by his own subjects" (p. 65). Lopez calls it "a distant premonition" of 1204 (p. 65).
  - **1088:** Pisans, Genoese and Amalfitans sacked Mahdiya and left "only after obtaining commercial privileges" (p. 65).
  - The crusader states granted "fiscal privileges and autonomous quarters" in return for military and economic help (p. 99).
  - In Muslim ports, privileges were won "not by force but by diplomatic means" (p. 100).
- **Enclave against fondaco.** Genoa's revenue from Pera, "a walled enclave under Genoese rule", was less than twice that from Alexandria, where Genoa "merely rented a small cluster of buildings (*fondaco* or *fondoq*) which the Muslims locked up at night". Lopez: "political control was not as essential as free access to markets" (p. 100).
- **Hansa establishments.** The yard at Novgorod, the London steelyard, the Bruges kontor and the Bergen quarter: "the establishments and privileges were permanent, but the population consisted chiefly of temporary callers, and sovereignty rested with the local rulers" (p. 118).
- **Colonies grow roots.** At first colonists were younger sons, junior partners and agents who went home when they could. Later "people of any age settled down in the colony, begat children from local women, and built replicas of their home cities abroad" (p. 108).
- **The sedentary merchant.** As profits fell, partners stayed home and worked through agents, couriers and correspondence; a weekly courier ran to the Champagne fairs (pp. 107–108).
- **Fairs as temporary privilege.** For a fair, lords and towns would "interrupt any war, lower any toll", relax restrictions on aliens, give speedy justice, and renounce *aubaine* (seizing a dead alien's goods), *ius naufragii* (seizing wrecks and sometimes their survivors) and reprisal (seizing the goods of all fellow citizens of a defaulting merchant) (p. 89). Later merchants won the same liberties all year round (p. 90).
- **Staples and control.** English kings herded foreign wool buyers into staple ports; Venice controlled German spice importers (pp. 90–91).

## 4. Merchants and rulers

- **Byzantine contempt for trade.** Emperor Theophilos (from 829) saw a fine merchant ship enter the port and was told it was his wife's. "God made me an emperor, you would make me a ship captain!" He had ship and cargo burned. Byzantine historians praised the "act of justice" for centuries (pp. 65–66). Roman law, taken into a Byzantine code, forbade gentlemen to trade (p. 66). Lopez sets this beside 1082, when Venice's exemption made "the native Byzantine merchants underprivileged in their own land" (p. 65).
- **Western rulers needed merchants.** Shipowners were treated "in the same gingerly, if not quite respectful, way" as people who could lend or fight (p. 80). Philip the Fair entrusted his finances to the Florentine Musciatto Guidi and his navy to the Genoese Benedetto Zaccaria (p. 166).
- **Credit as a weapon.** The great Italian companies "used credit grants as a weapon to pry commercial concessions from foreign governments" (p. 105). The pope was their best friend: he needed them to move tithes and threatened his debtors with church penalties (p. 105).
- **War and trade together.** Pisa and Genoa invested booty from fighting Muslim raiders in more ships (p. 65). Most ships served "interchangeably for commerce and for war" (p. 100). Younger sons of Italian nobles pooled capital in ventures that involved "a fight with pirates, brigands, unfriendly lords, and possibly Infidels" (p. 67). The Teutonic Order combined "ruthless force and business talent" (p. 115).
- **The safe road.** Powers along a route had a self-interest not to impose "exorbitant tolls or letting robbers go unpunished", since merchants would detour (p. 102). Roads after Rome were built and kept mainly by "half-commercial, half-religious organizations" (p. 79).
- **Muleteers stayed small.** "Muleteers and carters, however, did not thrive enough to form powerful companies" (p. 80).
- **Sailors were partners.** Sailors could carry freight of their own and add its profit to their pay. Medieval oarsmen were "proud, free citizens and tough fighters", not galley slaves (p. 81).

## 5. The northern sea and Gotland (for the Bædling)

- **Before 1100.** In the 9th and early 10th c. "only the Frisians" competed with the Scandinavians. Westphalian merchants followed in the late 10th and early 11th c. (p. 115). Northern trade "often" alternated "trade with agriculture, piracy, and war" (p. 20).
- **Lübeck** was refounded in 1143 by Henry the Lion as a German town (p. 115). The German towns of the Baltic looked to Lübeck as "the kingpin of their trade" (p. 115).
- **Visby** (see §2): the Scandinavian brotherhood eclipsed by the German visitors (p. 117).
- **Privileges in the North.** German merchants gained "special privileges at the great fairs of Skanör", in all Swedish ports, and in all Norwegian ports but the far north (p. 117). The Skanör herring fair: the Danish king took the tolls and his subjects unloaded the fish, but Lübeck merchants were the main buyers; 34,000 barrels in 1368 (p. 119).
- **Why the Scandinavians fell behind.** Their sea trade was "not so much the business of free townsmen as that of noblemen and peasants with a knack for navigation", their ships were smaller than the cog, and their techniques were out of date (pp. 117–118). This is Lopez's thesis, in his own developmental terms.
- **1274.** Reval wrote to Lübeck: "Our two towns belong together like the arms of Christ crucified" (p. 117).

## 7. Women, eunuchs, gender crossing, disguise: findings and absences

- **Women in trade.** The only woman in trade in the pages read is **the wife of Emperor Theophilos**, whose merchant ship was burned (pp. 65–66). Lopez does not name her.
- **Women elsewhere:**
  - "a marriage with a woman of property" as one way a craftsman became a merchant entrepreneur (p. 125);
  - noblemen who "married merchants' daughters" (p. 67) and poor nobles marrying a rich farmer's daughter (p. 164);
  - colonists who "begat children from local women" (p. 108);
  - unskilled women washing and spinning wool at the bottom of the cloth industry (p. 137), and women and religious orders spinning and weaving cotton as a side activity (p. 138);
  - housewives grinding grain by hand (p. 43, searched only).
- **Absent:** women as commenda investors or managers, women in guilds (beyond spinners), women in a hansa or colony, women in convoys. Lopez is silent rather than negative.
- **Eunuchs, gender crossing, disguise:** none. Pilgrims' dress served aliens as protection on the road (p. 25), the only "costume" in the book.
- **Design consequence.** Any trans woman's company, guild or quarter is the mod's invention as far as this book goes. The Theophilos story is a strong period anchor for a woman owning a ship at the Byzantine court, and for the court's disapproval.

## 8. Guardrails and risks

- **Ha-Nehpakhot: no merchant proposals from this book.** Lopez's pages on the Jews (pp. 60–62, 73, 77–78, 108, 121–122) are a cluster of exactly the motifs the mod excludes:
  - "ferrying slaves" and tax-collecting (p. 62);
  - moneylending, pawnbroking and a Jewish "money-lender" ancestor of the Pierleoni (pp. 67, 77–78);
  - interest charged to Jews at Nuremberg (p. 97);
  - English dependence on "the Jews" until "squeezed dry and expelled" (pp. 120–121);
  - the claim that they were made "ruthless because of the hate they encountered" (p. 122).
  These are Lopez's 1971 generalisations, not findings, and the slave claim is the motif Phelan 2023 rejects. Even the rogadia (p. 73), which is free help between merchants, is framed by Lopez as possibly hidden usury. **Do not let money, credit, tax farming or trade carry any Ha-Nehpakhot content.** If the Nehpakhot ever touch a merchant company, it must be as protected people on someone else's ship, protection given for service, never a loan.
- **Provence.** Provence was where the Jews were "especially numerous" (p. 62), and Provence and Languedoc fell behind the Italians in the 13th c. (p. 120). Montpellier was bought by the king of France (p. 105). This is context only.
- **No people as backward by nature.** Lopez calls Byzantines, Muslims and Scandinavians "early starters who failed to keep up" (p. 117), and calls Scandinavian tribes "unruly" (p. 117). Do not put this in the narrator's voice for the Bædling or Adelphai. It is one historian's developmental model.
- **Violence.** The massacre of over a hundred Amalfitan merchants in Cairo in 996 (p. 64) is not event content.
- **Crusade framing.** The Mahdiya raid (1088) and the Baltic crusade (p. 115) should not be framed as a faith's victory over "infidels" in the narrator's voice.
- **Slaves as goods.** Lopez lists slaves among Venice's early exports (p. 63) and Europe's exports to Islam (p. 26). No mechanic trades in people.
