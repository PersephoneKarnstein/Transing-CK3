# Hirschberg, *A History of the Jews in North Africa*, vol. 1 (1974): design reading

Source: H. Z. (J. W.) Hirschberg, *A History of the Jews in North Africa*, vol. I: *From Antiquity to the Sixteenth Century*, 2nd revised ed., translated from the Hebrew (Bialik Institute, 1965) by M. Eichelberg (Leiden: E. J. Brill, 1974), xxiv + 518 pp., 4 maps. ISBN 90 04 03820 5.
Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo (`sources_pending/maghreb/hirschberg_north_africa.txt`, gitignored). Page numbers are the book's own, taken from the running heads. Hirschberg's own chapter numbering in the text (Chapter Two "In the Maghreb States", Chapter Three "Jewish Society", Chapter Four "Communal Organization") differs from the garbled table of contents in the extraction; I cite pages only.

**What was read.**
- In full: "In the Maghreb States" pp. 87–139 (the Kahina, Idrisid Fez, Aghlabid and Fatimid Kairouan, the Zirids, the 1057 sack, Almoravids, Almohads, Solomon Cohen's letter of 1148, Tunisia and Tripolitania, the last years of 'Abd al-Mu'min); "Jewish Society" pp. 165–204 (beliefs and customs, education, captives, endowments, slaves, the family and the status of women, proselytes, renegades and forced converts, restrictions and discrimination, the Almohad regulations); "Communal Organization" pp. 205–246 (the negidim of Kairouan, the rabbinical courts, religious functionaries, elders, the Muslim courts).
- In part: pp. 140–145 (distribution of the Jewish population; the "Judaizers"); pp. 327–329 (R. Nissim); pp. 344–361 (Qal'at Hammad, Tahert, Tlemcen, Fez, Alfasi, Marrakesh, Sijilmasa, Dar'a, Ceuta, Ibn 'Aqnin, "Twilight over the Maghreb"); pp. 362–378 (the Almohad successor states, Hafsids, Ziyanids, Merinids, 13th-century evidence).
- Searched but not read through: the whole text for Hafsid, Merinid, 1229, eunuch, hermaphrodite/androgynos/tumtum, Purim, bath, transvestite/dress. Not read: the Greco-Roman chapter, the "Economic Life" chapter (pp. 247–297, left aside on purpose: it is trade and finance), and the appendices.

**Relation to existing notes.** This is the first source in the repo on Maghrebi Jewry. It gives the regime chronology and the shape of community authority (nagid, courts, elders, the ban, the Muslim courts). It says almost nothing about gender variance (see §5). Its companion note is `jud_maghreb_gil_islamic_countries.md`, which corrects it on the nagid and adds Geniza women's names.

**Design reading of the table.** The Maghreb is three different worlds inside the game's span: (a) 867–1057, a confident, learned, court-connected Jewry centred on Kairouan, with Fez largest by numbers; (b) 1057–1146, scattered after the Bedouin sack, but secure under the Almoravids, with physicians at court; (c) 1146–c. 1230, the Almohad forced conversions, when no one could live openly as a Jew from Bougie westward. Anything that celebrates an open Jewish street in Fez or Marrakesh between 1147 and about 1230 is ahistorical unless the faith itself has made the difference by ruling there.

## 2. Community authority

### 2.1 The nagid
- Hirschberg's view: the nagid was **appointed by the Muslim ruler**, "not … by election by his Jewish brethren" or by the geonim (pp. 205–206). He held a court post: secretary, "physician in ordinary", banker (p. 206). His job was to answer to the ruler for the community and to intercede for it.
- **Abraham b. 'Ata** (nagid c. 1010–c. 1020) was physician to Badis and al-Mu'izz and rode with Badis on campaign (pp. 112, 212). He spent two months in the west shielding the threatened communities of Ashir, Mesila and Tahert (p. 113). Hay called him "chief of the elders of the Jews" (p. 212).
- **Jacob b. 'Amram** (c. 1035–1041): not a scholar, a patron who fed and clothed students (pp. 213–214); the exilarch's letter calls him "great in the king's house" (p. 214).
- The office lived and died with the Zirid court. When the Zirids lost their capital, "there was no room for the appointment of a nagid" (pp. 216–217).
- Gil (companion note) disagrees: the title was an honorific from the yeshivot. Both agree the nagid was the Jew closest to the ruler, usually a physician.
- **Not usable as content:** the nagid's tax-collecting role (pp. 206–207) and Jacob ibn Jau's appointment to squeeze money "properly or improperly" out of the communities (p. 107). Both put money at the centre.

### 2.2 The courts and the elders
- **No outside authority appointed Maghrebi judges** (p. 226). Babylon and Palestine gave opinions; they could not enforce them (pp. 218–220). The gaon told Kairouan in the 9th century that its court was not even "qualified" (pp. 219–220).
- Courts are attested at Tripoli, Kairouan, Qabes, Majana, Migzar (elders only), Qal'at Hammad, Sijilmasa and Mahdiya (pp. 218–225). Where there was no court, "claims were brought before the elders" (p. 224).
- **The elders** were heads of the leading families, not elected; recognised by the authorities; they apportioned taxes, sat with the dayyan and helped enforce his rulings (p. 230). They testified before Muslim courts "by public mandate" (p. 231).
- **Hay on bad judges:** dayyanim who pawn the beds of the poor are "dayyanim of Sodom"; the community should "select from amongst you godfearing men … and set them over you" (p. 228). This is a sourced warrant for the street choosing its own judges.

### 2.3 The ban and the ruler's role
- The ban (*niddui*/*herem*) was the courts' main weapon: a man who swears not to appear before the town's dayyan "is to be excommunicated and chastised" (p. 221). Disobedient litigants: excommunicate, then whip after repentance (p. 235).
- **No source here says the ban needed the Muslim ruler's licence.** That is the key difference from Provence, where the Christian lord licensed the ban (Shatzmiller, `jud_shatzmiller_jews_of_provence.md` §4.3). In the Maghreb the ruler's role was **enforcement**, not licensing: the Jewish courts "had no police" (p. 236), so after thirty days under the ban a creditor could go to a Muslim court (p. 237). Gentile courts heard Jewish notables, not ordinary witnesses (p. 238).
- The Muslim state "did not compel" Jews to use its courts (p. 233). Going to the qadi without leave made one an "informer" (p. 232). Alfasi extended the stigma to raising one's voice against a Jew in a Gentile market (p. 246).
- One sign of royal humiliation: a Maliki jurist sent the nagid Abraham b. 'Ata away angrily for not wearing the *ghiyar* (pp. 198–199; Gil tells the same story more fully).

### 3.2 Status, marriage and the courts
- Hirschberg's framing: Berber women had more freedom than Arab women, "no harem and no veil", and Maghrebi Jewish women mixed more freely at gatherings (pp. 172, 184).
- **Kairouan widows** could collect their marriage settlement without taking an oath (p. 185). The marriage settlement was a fixed sum across North Africa, the same for rich and poor (p. 185).
- **Monogamy.** A man may not take a second wife without the first's consent; if he does, she may take her settlement and force a divorce (p. 186). Goitein found one case of bigamy in hundreds of documents (p. 189).
- **A gaon's ruling on a man who pulled out some of his wife's hair** shows "the concern of the rabbis for woman's dignity" (p. 186).
- **Women in court.** In the Geniza papers women "appear in court and make contracts of sale or partnership", sometimes with women (p. 190). One woman took her brother to "the chief judge" for her share of their father's estate; Hirschberg guesses she leaned to Karaism, whose law gives daughters a share (p. 244). Rabbanite–Karaite marriage contracts barred both spouses from Gentile courts (p. 244).
- **Women's quarters / gallery.** Not found. Separate Jewish quarters (*hara*) existed in Kairouan, Fez and Sijilmasa by choice, open to non-Jews (p. 197).
- **Learning.** R. Nissim's only daughter was "devout and well-taught in the Law" (p. 328). Hirschberg also passes on an unkind physical remark from Ibn Daud; do not reuse it.

### 3.3 Weddings, women's music
- Qabes: the bride was led "with words of rejoicing" to a relative's house the day before the wedding (p. 171).
- Kairouan asked Hay about wedding songs. He allowed hymns, banned love songs, and on the women: "that women beat drums and dance—if this is done in the presence of men, then there is nothing more objectionable" (p. 172).
- Kairouan betrothal by ring in the synagogue, the father accepting for his daughter (p. 172).

### 3.4 Slavery and concubinage (context only; not for content)
Bondwomen, concubinage and the punishment of masters are on pp. 181–183 and 188–189. Under the guardrails none of this should become content.

## 4. Stories and texts a court or faith could use
- **R. Nissim's *Fine Book of Comfort*** (Kairouan, 11th c.): stories of poor, faithful spouses and of virtuous women, "the social short story in Jewish literature" (pp. 176–177, 186–188). It also tells of a captive girl ransomed by a butcher (pp. 178–179).
- **The Purim bonfire of Kairouan.** Nissim describes *meshawarta de-pura*, "the stirrup of Purim": jumping over great bonfires on Purim eve in Kairouan (p. 170). This is the only Maghrebi Purim custom in the book and a real hook for the Turning.
- **Pentecost water-pouring** among Moroccan Jews (p. 170), explained by Hay through Nissim: the clear day of the Lawgiving (p. 170).
- **The Name and amulets.** Kairouan scholars asked Hay about wonder-workers who used the Divine Name. Hay called it "idle things", though he accepted amulets (pp. 166–169).
- **Maimon's *Epistle of Consolation*** (Fez, 1160): a forced convert may say "a short, even a very short, prayer, which may even be in the Arabic language" (pp. 193–194).
- **Maimonides' *Epistle on Forced Conversion***: no Jew may accuse another; those who escape "shall be accepted … they shall not be turned away" (pp. 194–195).
- **Ibn 'Aqnin's *Cure of Souls*** (Ceuta or Fez, late 12th c.): a teaching order from reading to astronomy and medicine; the pupil must ask questions, for "the shamefast man cannot learn" (p. 358).
- **Alfasi**: born at Qal'at Hammad c. 1013; taught at Fez; left in 1088 at 75 after a denunciation (pp. 347–349).

## 5. Gender and the body: findings, mostly absence
- **Eunuchs:** only as harem guards in the Arab East, in a sentence on Muslim women's status (p. 184). Nothing on Jewish or Maghrebi eunuchs.
- **Khuntha/androgynos/tumtum, cross-dressing, effeminacy, mukhannathun:** not found by search or in the chapters read.
- **Purim dress:** not found. The Purim custom is the bonfire (p. 170).
- **Same-sex desire:** one footnote cites Ibn Hawqal: "homosexuality, but not prostitution, is common in the Muslim Maghreb" (p. 183 n. 5). It is an outsider's slur about Muslims, not evidence about Jews. Do not use.
- **Dress codes (*ghiyar*):** the Aghlabid donkey patch (p. 198); a hint of black shoes under al-Hakim, "not felt" in Kairouan (p. 198); the Almohad blue robe and cap, then yellow (p. 201); a late-medieval Andalusi *hisba* manual prescribing "bells for women" of the protected peoples (p. 203, after the period).
- **Bathing:** the Fez exiles mentioned "the ritual bathhouses they had had in that city" (p. 106). This is mikveh material: out of bounds.

**Result:** Hirschberg gives no Maghrebi source for gender crossing. The sect's texts stay Provençal. In the Maghreb its content must come from community law, learning, medicine and the Purim fire.

## 6. Rabbanites, Karaites and dissent
- Karaites at Warghlan left their houses at Passover "so as to comply literally" with Exodus (p. 170). Maimonides compared the Jews between Tunis and Alexandria to Karaites in their purity rules. Hirschberg says this came from Ibadi neighbours, not from Karaism (pp. 165–166).
- The Karaite Sahl b. Masliah charged that the rabbis rule "by means of excommunication, ban and appeal to the Gentile authorities" (p. 228). This is a dissenting voice on the street's judges.
- Rabbanite–Karaite mixed marriages, with clauses keeping both out of Gentile courts (p. 244).
- **The Maghreb as a refuge** for those who had left the community or wanted to return to it (p. 191): a responsum says a returning apostate "shall go to a place where he is not known" and be received like any Jew. This describes a dissenting current living at the edges.

## 7. Muslim rulers and Jews at court
- Physicians: under the Aghlabid governor (p. 100); Abraham b. 'Ata under Badis and al-Mu'izz (p. 212); Meir ibn Qamniel and Solomon b. al-Mu'allim under 'Ali b. Yusuf, who titled them "vizier" (pp. 122, 351). Hirschberg suggests the two physicians won fair compensation for Jews whose houses were taken for the Marrakesh mosque (p. 122).
- Tubiya, physician of Tripoli, hid from the summons of a dying "king" of Qabes. He came out only when the city's Muslim elders (the sheikhs of the Banu Matruh) took the Jewish notables hostage (p. 130).
- Dangers: Jacob ibn Jau jailed (p. 107); Alfasi denounced (p. 347); after the period, the Ruqqasa courtiers of the Merinids executed in 1302 (p. 371).

## 8. Guardrail notes
- **Money-led material to avoid:** Ibn Jau's levies (p. 107); the Almoravid *farida* of 100,000 dinars (p. 118); Moses Dar'i's followers borrowing from Muslims before the false Passover (p. 121); the nagid as tax-farmer (pp. 206–207); all of "Economic Life" (pp. 247–297).
- **Do not model:** the Almohad forced conversions as a mechanic or a choice; expulsions (Kairouan closed to Jews in the 13th c., p. 116); the Idrisid emir Yahya's rape of a Jewish woman (pp. 99–100; it ended in a joint Jewish–Muslim crowd killing him, which is still not usable).
- **The Kahina** must not be presented as a Jewish queen. Hirschberg finds the claim unsupported, and Jewish folk memory calls her a persecutor (pp. 93–94).
- **Maimonides on Maghrebis** ("dull and coarse", p. 165) is a learned man's prejudice; do not voice it as fact.

## 10. Open questions
- Menahem Ben-Sasson's Hebrew study of the Qayrawan community (*Tsemihat ha-qehilla*, 1996; title and date from general knowledge) post-dates this book. Gil cites it on the negidim (Gil p. 192 n. 127, pp. 348–374 of Ben-Sasson). It is the main modern study of Kairouan's community authority and is worth reading.
- Corcos (*Zion* 32, 1967) on the Almohads' attitude to the Jews (cited p. 363 n. 1) would firm up 1184–1230.
- Krakowski and Goitein vol. 3 for women's names and marriage; Rustow for Karaites.
- Typo in the book: p. 328 gives Hananel's death as "about 1157"; p. 115 and Gil give 1055/56.
