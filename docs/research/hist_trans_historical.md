# Trans Historical: Gender Plurality Before the Modern — Research Report for "Transbian Maximalism" (CK3 mod)

> **Superseded for design (2026-10-25)** by `v2/trans_historical.md`, which has page cites. Several proposals here break later rules: the galli clamp (Attis imagery), third-sex and "monstrous race" readings, and "hermaphrodite" as a game term. None reached the game files. Matrona of Perge was assigned female and lived as the eunuch "Babylas"; she is not evidence that castrated men were treated as women.

Source: *Trans Historical: Gender Plurality Before the Modern*, eds. Greta LaFleur, Masha Raskolnikov, Anna Kłosowska (Cornell University Press, 2021).
Purpose: harvest medieval/early-modern precedents that lend a veneer of non-anachronism to the mod's faith/culture mechanics (random "transgender" trait; ritual castration à la galli; castrated men regarded as women; adoptive matrilineal heirship; women-/trans-woman-centred priesthood).
Note: all quotations below are verbatim from the plain-text edition; chapter titles are given with each quote. Page numbers are not reliably recoverable from the text file, so quotes are cited by chapter.

### Chapter 1 — Leah DeVun, "Mapping the Borders of Sex"

**P5. The hermaphrodite race of *Marvels of the East* / *Liber monstrorum*** | Old English & Anglo-Latin texts, c. 975–12th c.; "East"/Africa | A whole society of "hermaphrodites" who alternate between fathering and bearing children and between male and female labour. The right breast is male "for performing work," the left female "for nourishing children." | "Hermaphrodites are so called because both sexes appear in them. . . . These, having a male right breast and …" (ch. 1, quoting MS Bodley 614, fol. 50v: "Sunt homines ermafrodite nuncupati eo, quod eis uterque sexus appareat. . . . Hii dexteram mammam virilem sinistram muliebrem habentes. …") — "a right breast like a man for performing work, and a left breast like a woman for nourishing children" (ch. 1, quoting Liber monstrorum). | Medieval imagination of an entire *society* organised around non-binary sex and alternating gender roles — the closest thing in the book to a "gender-variant culture" template. Also a basis for in-game flavour: foreign chroniclers describing the mod's culture as a *gens utriusque sexus*.

**P6. Hereford Mappamundi "gens uterque sexus"** | c. 1300, Hereford, England | The map places a bifurcated male/female figure in Africa with the caption that this is "a race of both sexes, unnatural in many of their customs." | "Gens uterque sexus innaturales multimodis modis." (ch. 1, n. 13) | Direct Latin tag for a hostile chronicler's description of the faith's adherents; also demonstrates that medieval Christians located gender-variant *peoples* at the world's edge (Africa/India), so a "found" faith from the margins fits mappa-mundi logic.

**P7. Westminster Abbey Bestiary bifurcated figure with sword and scissors** | c. 1270–80, England | A half-male/half-female figure holds a sword on the masculine side and scissors on the feminine side. | "the masculine side clutches a weapon while the feminine side prefers a tool of domesticity." (ch. 1) | Iconography for a faith symbol (sword + shears). Note: sex "emerges through the body's encounter with the outside world, through its acts and affiliations" — DeVun's reading that medieval sex was "an open-ended process or activity, and one shored up more by sociality than by any single destiny of the flesh" is usable to justify sex-as-social-role.

**P8. Isidore of Seville, *Etymologiae* on hermaphrodites and *monstrum*** | 7th c. Visigothic Spain | Source for the Marvels text; also for the etymology *monstrum* < *monstrare*, "to show" — monsters "show" divine messages. | "The word for 'monster,' monstrum, was sometimes thought to derive from the Latin word monstrare, meaning 'to show.' Medieval audiences …" (ch. 1) | In-game theology: a trans-affirming faith could gloss its gender-variant members as *monstra* in the positive sense, "shown" by God — a term reclaimed from Isidore.

**P9. Maternal abbots and viragos — accepted gender inversion in orthodox Christianity** | 12th c. Cistercian writing (Bernard of Clairvaux etc.) | DeVun notes mainstream medieval texts "praised at least certain gender-inverting characteristics, whether among maternalistic male abbots or virile female 'viragos,' who were nevertheless accepted as indubitable men or women." (ch. 1; cites Bynum, "Jesus as Mother and Abbot as Mother") | Shows the Church itself used cross-gendered language for religious offices (abbot as mother); a priesthood that feminises its male-born clergy has an orthodox analogue.

### Chapter 2 — Igor H. De Souza, "Elenx de Céspedes: Indeterminate Genders in the Spanish Inquisition"

**P10. Elenx de Céspedes — self-declared hermaphrodite, surgeon, soldier, husband** | b. 1546 Alhama; trial Toledo 1587–89 | Born enslaved to a Morisca mother; married a man at 16 and bore a child; later lived as a man (tailor, soldier in the Alpujarras war 1568–71, licensed surgeon), married María del Caño in a church ceremony (1586). Claimed to be a hermaphrodite in whom "the masculine sex heated up" after widowhood. Convicted of bigamy and sorcery, 200 lashes and ten years' unpaid hospital service, where they "became famous and sought after as a miracle worker." | "In reality I am and was a hermaphrodite. I have and had two natures, one of a man and the other of a woman." — "I married first as a woman to a man, then as a man to a woman, because when I married …" — "hermaphrodites, as I am and have been, are not unnatural" (ch. 2, quoting Elenx's letter to the Inquisition). On naming: "As a teenager they called themself Elena in honor of their former master's wife. . . . Elenx testified that …" (ch. 2, n. 23). Hospital fame: "where they became famous and sought after as a miracle worker" (ch. 2, n. 20); hermaphrodites perceived as "an individual equipped with redemptory and fantastic powers" (ch. 2, n. 52, quoting Cleminson & Vázquez García). | A real early-modern person who *changed legal sex by medical attestation and rite (marriage banns)*; the humoral "heating up" model is a native theory of transition; the figure of the hermaphrodite as miracle-worker/healer gives a historical hook for a gender-variant healing priesthood. Name-change precedent (gender-neutral surname-only naming).

**P11. The vicar's "capon" test — castrated men as a routine legal category at marriage** | Madrid, 1586 | When Elenx applied for a marriage licence, the vicar, "who saw that I was beardless and hairless, asked me if I was a capon [i.e., a 'castrated' man]"; Elenx was examined by "three or four men" who "testified that they'd seen me and that I wasn't a capon." | "The vicar, who saw that I was beardless and hairless, asked me if I was a capon [i.e., a 'castrated' …" (ch. 2, quoting Kagan & Dyer, *Inquisitorial Inquiries*) | Shows that castrated men (*capón*) were a recognised social/legal class whose capacity to marry was questioned by the Church — a basis for CK3 "castrated characters cannot/can marry" rules and for a beardless-eunuch-as-not-quite-man category.

**P12. Hermaphrodites must choose a sex and swear it before a bishop** | early modern Iberia (canon/secular legal doctrine) | Cleminson & Vázquez García, cited by De Souza: those who "displayed characteristics of both sexes in equal measure 'should choose one of these and swear before a bishop that they …" (ch. 2, n. 5) | A documented *rite of sex-election before a prelate* — a ready-made historical template for a CK3 decision "Swear your sex before the High Priestess," and for the belief that gender is fixed by oath/rite rather than birth.

**P13. Galenic one-sex model vs. Aristotelian two-sex model** | antiquity, received in 16th-c. Iberia | "For Galen, male and female genitalia are structurally identical; the only distinction is placement in the body: the penis is …" Followers of Aristotle "considered 'hermaphrodites' to be aberrations of nature, prodigies or monsters," but "prodigies or monsters were considered part of nature." (ch. 2) | Medical-theoretical backbone for in-game "transition is a change of degree, not kind." A faith could adopt Galen explicitly: all bodies are one sex at different heats.

**P14. Pliny: female bodies transform into male through exertion (never the reverse)** | 1st c. CE, Rome; cited in 16th-c. Spain | "In Pliny's view, the female body was essentially unstable and susceptible to transformation into a male body as a result …" (ch. 2) Also n. 35: "the example of a sex change was almost invariably that of a woman becoming a man (that is, growing a …" | WEAKENS plausibility of male-to-female "natural" transition in learned medieval discourse: the classical direction of sex-change is toward the "more perfect" male. The mod's faith would be inverting the Plinian hierarchy — a heretical move, which is itself usable flavour (a faith that reverses the order of perfection).

**P15. Siete Partidas and the 1497 Pragmática — sodomy as crime against nature punished by fire** | Castile, 13th c. and 1497 | "The earliest systematic code of Spanish law, the Siete Partidas (thirteenth century), stands out for its clear declaration of sodomy …" The 1497 Pragmática "introduced capital punishment for sodomy in the form of a public burning (auto-da-fé)." (ch. 2) | Hostile legal environment the faith would face in Catholic realms; sodomy prosecutions "presupposed control over gender expression" — gender policing as the mechanism of persecution events.

**P16. Gender presentation was not itself a crime; sodomy was the lever** | Castile, 1587 | "Elenx could not have been prosecuted merely for their gender expression, as that did not constitute a crime in itself. …" (ch. 2) | Important nuance for CK3 opinion/crime design: cross-living per se is not criminal in Castilian law; it becomes legible to authority only via marriage/sodomy. Also: Elenx was moved "from the men's side of the prison . . . to the women's side" once the tribunal fixed their gender (n. 23) — institutions re-sorted a person by decided sex.

**P17. *Burlador(a)* — trickster as gender deviant** | Golden Age Spain | "Burlar can refer 'specifically to the performance of a gender in defiance of one's biological identity, or a refusal to …" (ch. 2, n. 51, quoting Elizabeth Rhodes) | Spanish-language slur/term for in-game flavour (what Catholic rivals call adherents).

### Chapter 3 — Kathleen Perry Long, "The Case of Marin le Marcis"

**P18. Marin le Marcis — petition for re-baptism as a man; death sentence overturned** | 1601, Montivilliers & Rouen, Normandy | Baptised a girl, worked as a chambermaid, "having realized that he was a man, Marin le Marcis requested to be re-baptized as a Catholic man" in order to marry the widow Jeanne le Febvre. Both were tried for sodomy; Marin sentenced to be burned alive (reduced to hanging), Jeanne to be flogged. On appeal the physician Jacques Duval's clinical exam found "functional male genitalia"; the Parlement of Rouen freed him but ordered him to wear women's clothing until age 25 and "not live with anyone of either sex." Ten years later Duval found him living as a bearded tailor under the name "du Marcis the younger." | "Born a Catholic, baptized a girl, converted to Protestantism, and having realized that he was a man, Marin le Marcis …" (ch. 3) — "this 'womanly man [gunanthrope] is at present in a better manly state than he was before, and that, under the …" (ch. 3, quoting Duval 1612) — "The court of the Parliament of Rouen, unable to decide with certainty whether he was male or female, left Marin …" (ch. 3) | Rite-based gender change (re-baptism) sought through the Church; a court-imposed probationary period in the other gender until majority — template for a CK3 "transition" decision with a timed cooldown; a legal moment where self-determination of sex was permitted.

**P19. Paré: "ancient and modern laws" oblige hermaphrodites to elect a sex on pain of death** | Ambroise Paré, *Des monstres et prodiges* (1573/1585), France | "both the ancient and modern laws have obliged and still oblige these latter to choose which sex organs they wish …" Paré recognises four kinds of hermaphrodite (predominantly male, predominantly female, neuter, double) but "all of these must be assigned a gender." | "les loix anciennes et modernes ont fait et font encore eslire, duquel sexe ils veulent user, avec defense, sur peine de perdre la vie" (ch. 3, n. 31). "And if the hermaphrodite is as much like one as another, he will be called a hermaphrodite, or a 'man-and-woman.'" (ch. 3) | Confirms a continuous European legal tradition (Roman law → medieval → early modern) of *elective sex* fixed by oath — strongest single precedent for "womanhood achieved by rite/legal election." Also supplies a neuter/"man-and-woman" category outside the binary.

**P20. Germain Garnier ("Marie-Germain") — renaming by episcopal authority before an assembly** | c. 1560s, Vitry-le-François, Champagne | A shepherd held to be a girl until 15 leapt a ditch and "the genitalia and the male rod came to be developed in him"; physicians were assembled, the Bishop informed, and "by his authority, an assembly having been called, the shepherd received a man's name: and instead of Marie . . …" | "after having reported to the Bishop . . . and by his authority, an assembly having been called, the shepherd received a man's name" (ch. 3, quoting Paré); Long: Duval's text "summarizes the basic steps required to change one's gender officially in early modern France: physical transformation, change of clothing and …" (ch. 3, n. 39). | A *public ecclesiastical rite of gender re-assignment* (bishop + assembled people + new name + new clothes) — the closest Christian analogue to the mod's transition ceremony. Montaigne also recorded this case (not in the text, do not cite).

**P21. "Nature tends always toward what is most perfect" — no man ever became a woman** | Paré (1585), echoed by Duval (1612) | "we therefore never find in any true story that any man ever became a woman, because Nature tends always toward what is most perfect." Duval repeats: "man is more perfect than woman," but "plays around with this dictum by asking how one might believe that men may become women once more, suggesting that …" | "Nature tend toujours à ce qui est le plus parfaict, et non au contraire faire que ce qui est parfaict, devienne imparfaict." (ch. 3, n. 36) — Duval: "Quelle raison donc nous induira croire, qu'un homme redevienne femme . . . nous remarquons nature s'estre grandement delectee en la varieté." (ch. 3, n. 61) | WEAKENS plausibility of learned acceptance of male-to-female transition; but Duval's 1612 question ("what reason will induce us to believe that a man may become a woman again") shows the possibility was at least articulated. A faith that reveres MtF transition would be consciously inverting the Galenic-Aristotelian hierarchy of perfection — a theologically "heretical" stance that can be a tenet.

**P22. Duval: sex is shaped by environment, diet, freedom and clothing (humoral transition)** | 1612, Rouen | Duval argued to the court that when Marin "was free, slept well in a nice bed, had enough food and exercise, and enjoyed frequent sexual activity, he had …" He "describes a feedback loop between the sexed body and its environment, suggesting that the body itself can be transformed by …" | "Duval suggests that Marin's humoral balance is altered by fear, as well as by lack of freedom of movement. The …" (ch. 3) | A period-authentic theory for how *lifestyle and clothing* transform the sexed body — supports "transition by practice" (dress, diet, seclusion) rather than surgery alone.

**P23. *L'isle des hermaphrodites* (1605) — surgical attempt to make a man a woman; "common gender" language** | France, 1605 satire | A satirical novel describes "an imaginary language that uses what is known as 'common gender,'" and a scene where surgeons try to make a man into a woman: "they were doing all that they could to make him a woman; but . . . he remained neuter in gender." | "En une autre piece, je voyois ce mesme homme estendu tout nud sus une table, et plusieurs à l'entour de …" (ch. 3, n. 24) | Early-modern (post-CK3) but shows that *surgical* MtF transformation was imaginable as satire; "neuter gender" and "common gender" as grammatical/legal categories for in-game flavour.

**P24. *Gunanthrope* — Duval's coinage "womanly man"** | 1612 | "The contradictory signs on and inside Marin's body cause Duval to call him a Gunanthrope, or womanly man; the name …" (ch. 3) | Greek-derived learned term for a dual-gendered person; usable as a title/term in Latin-Christian flavour text.

**P25. Court-ordered cross-dressing as *probation*, not punishment** | Rouen 1601 | The court ordered Marin "to wear feminine attire and avoid sexual relations with members of either sex only until the age of majority," with "very clear prohibitions and interdictions against him living with anyone of one or the other sex, on pain of death" (ch. 3, n. 5, quoting Duval). | Model for an in-game "liminal" state — a character between genders under celibacy vow for a period before confirmation.

### Chapter 4 — M. W. Bychowski, "The Transgender Turn: Eleanor Rykener Speaks Back"

**P26. Eleanor Rykener — self-named trans woman sex worker, taught by women, clients included nuns** | 11 Dec 1394, Cheapside, London (Plea and Memoranda Roll A34, m.2) | Discovered in women's clothing with John Britby in a stall on Soper's Lane; "she named herself as 'Eleanor'"; the scribe calls her John twenty-five times. She "had learned to perform sex work from a woman, Anna," took gifts including a gold ring from men, and had sex "from women, including several nuns ('quampluribus monialibus') and many other women . . . married and otherwise, seemingly without pay." No verdict is recorded; the mayor's court lacked jurisdiction over sodomy. | "Se Elianoram nominans veste muliebri detectus" (Discovered in women's attire, she named herself as "Eleanor") (ch. 4). "Requesting money for [her] labor, Rykener consented" (Qui ab eo argentum pro labore suo petens sibi consentiebat) (ch. 4). "Although she introduces herself into the record as Eleanor ('Elianoram'), the scribe choses to name her as John ('Johannes') twenty-five times." (ch. 4) "Neither transvestism nor buggery were matters that are otherwise documented in English secular courts" (ch. 4, quoting Goldberg). | The single best-attested medieval trans-feminine *individual* in the CK3 period; name-change as self-assertion; a woman-to-woman apprenticeship (Anna teaching Eleanor "women's work"); nuns as clients hints at women's religious houses as a social world where a trans woman could circulate. Also evidence that English secular law had *no* routine category for cross-dressing — gender crossing per se was not a documented secular crime.

**P27. *Nephandum* — "the unspeakable vice"** | 1394 London Latin | "the scribe calls nephandum. **P28. Kadin Henningsen's "gender labor" reading — womanhood acquired through women's work** | 2019 scholarship on 1394 case | The chapter credits Henningsen's essay for emphasising "the role of women's labor as foundational for Eleanor's expressed identity" (ch. 4), i.e. that Rykener became a woman by doing women's work (embroidery, barmaid, sex work taught by women). | Supports belief (b) "womanhood achieved by practice/rite" with a medieval English case: womanhood as something *learned from women*.

### Chapter 5 — Anna Kłosowska, "Wojciech of Poznań and the Trans Archive, Poland, 1550–1561"

**P29. Wojciech of Poznań — publicly assigned female by town council; lived a decade as a woman; three husbands** | c. 1550 Poznań; deposed 10 March 1561, Kazimierz (Kraków); "Burned." | After six months as a monk, "the lords aldermen ordered him to be examined by women at the town hall in front of the men"; the mother was sworn "under the threat of death and infamy to dress/present [nosić] him as a woman"; a maiden's dress was bought and "he dressed in such clothes for ten years." Married three men (Sebastian the brewer, Wawrzyniec the farm worker, John the smith — "handfasted/shook hands in front of good people and were married in church"), learned embroidery, brewed beer, kept a brothel, and had many women lovers.
