## 0. Framing (A0 restated, and binding)

1. **Living, criminalised communities.** Every term below belongs to living people, many of them criminalised or attacked:
   - Senegal applies art. 319-3 (Broqua 2017 ¶33);
   - northern Nigeria has *hisbah* raids (Pierce p. 2);
   - Ghana had media panics in 2006 and 2011 (Geoffrion ¶18);
   - Abidjan has a tabloid press hostile to the Miss Woubi contest (Mackin pp. 19–20).
   - The faith takes none of their names: góor-jigéen, woubi, *qualité*, 'yan daudu, Kodjo-besia, *travesti*, Woubikan. Its daughters are not their ancestors.
2. **Grade.** The earliest witness for any of these figures is 1894 (Broqua 2017 ¶2). Everything here is LATER. Each design use below is an **analogy for tone and social shape**, and the narrator must say so.
3. **Not a third gender.** Broqua (¶40–41) and Geoffrion (¶40) do not support a third category. The surveys show that when "woman" and "transgender" are both offered, more choose "woman" (Larmarange and Broqua 2023 p. 11; §A4).
4. **Who speaks.** Most informants are assigned male and take women's roles. Few describe a woman's identity in their own words. Where they do, it is noted below. Their words are never put into a game character's mouth.

## A. Source by source

### A1. Broqua 2017: the góor-jigéen (Wolof, Senegal)

- **The term.** *Góor-jigéen*, "homme-femme" (man-woman) (¶1).
- **The earliest witness.** The naval doctor Corre, in 1894, was told the Wolof expression "serait de date récente" (would be of recent date) (¶2).
- **Gorer, 1935.**
  - They "do not suffer in any way socially, though the Mohammedans refuse them religious burial" (¶3).
  - They are "sought after as the best conversationalists and the best dancers" (¶3).
- **Béart, 1955.** Some lead "les femmes pour les tam-tam de fanal ou de faux-lion" (the women at the fanal and false-lion drum dances) (¶5).
- **Role, c. 1900–1980.**
  - The góor-jigéen was "agrégé au monde des femmes, principalement celles de la haute société (nommées *diriyanké*)" (attached to the world of women, mainly high-society women, called *diriyanké*) (¶16).
  - He was "maître de cérémonie lors de mariages ou de baptêmes", thanks to cooking skill. The "sauce góor-jigéen" survives (¶16).
  - He was the women's counsellor. One former góor-jigéen called them "carrément des grandes dames" (real grandes dames) and "un peu un faiseur de roi, ou de reine" (something of a king-maker, or queen-maker) (¶16).
- **Patrons.** The patron women were sometimes called "mère[s] de góor-jigéen" (mothers of a góor-jigéen) (¶17, after Niang et al. 2002: 10).
  - Through them the góor-jigéen had a political role. In Saint-Louis they staged Senghor's campaign entry with a coded slogan (¶17, after Niang 2010).
  - Their status was "comparable à celui du griot" (comparable to the griot's) (¶17, after Gning 2013). They organised the *sabar* drum dances (¶17).
- **Kinship and sexuality.** Sexuality was not asked about. "certains pouvaient d'ailleurs se marier et avoir des enfants" (some could marry and have children) (¶18).
- **Seniority.**
  - A former góor-jigéen said: "J'ai fait mes armes chez les góor-jigéen que je considérais plus ou moins comme mes mamans" (I learned the ropes among góor-jigéen I thought of more or less as my mothers). Their houses were meeting places, and they gave "carrément des leçons" (real lessons) (¶34).
  - The networks were "hiérarchisés sur la base de l'ancienneté" (ranked by seniority) and taught the role's skills and rules (¶35).
- **Dependence on patrons.** Another man feared becoming like them. They "venaient chez les gens" (were always at other people's houses) and depended on patrons' generosity (¶36–37).
- **Religion.**
  - The Islamic NGO Jamra campaigned from the early 1980s (¶30).
  - The 2008 "mariage clandestin" (clandestine wedding) photos opened a period of arrests under art. 319-3 (¶33).
- **The argument.** A gender category was "homosexualised". Broqua reports the case against a third category (¶40–41).

### A2. Broqua 2010: Bamako (a Mande-language source; see also A10)

- **Fieldwork.** Interviews in Bamako, conducted in Bambara and then transcribed (p. 39).
- **The pair.** Insiders split partners into *yoss*, insertive and masculine, and *qualités*, receptive "et présentent des caractéristiques féminines" (with feminine characteristics) (p. 39).
- **Understanding without taking part.** Someone who knows of the milieu without taking part is "*fàamu*", "comprendre" (to understand) (p. 44).
- **Insults.** The common insults are the Wolof *góor-jigéen* and the Bambara "*sa*", "serpent" (snake): "en référence au mouvement ondulatoire de l'animal, en ce qu'il évoque une démarche féminine" (for the snake's undulation, which recalls a woman's gait) (p. 46).
  - *Sa* is used more widely for any man thought insufficiently masculine (p. 46).
- **Shame and guilt.** "la honte (*màlo* en langue bambara) constitue un puissant régulateur social" (shame is a powerful social regulator), as a threat to honour (*dànbe*) (p. 47).
  - Islamic guilt adds to shame. Some abstain during Ramadan (pp. 47–48).
- **Marriage.** Most men marry women. Not marrying by about thirty "est perçu comme anormal" (is seen as abnormal) (p. 51).
- **Discretion and secrets.**
  - Men keep *sutura*, "la discrétion", which protects reputation (p. 48).
  - The Bambara *gundo*, "secret", also means "une réalité qui est connue de tous mais dont personne ne doit parler" (a thing everyone knows but no one may speak of) (p. 50).
  - This independently confirms Jansen's account of Kela's *gundow* as the right to speak, not hidden content (`waf_jansen_kangaba_griots.md` §2.1).
- **What is missing.** No institutional role like the góor-jigéen exists here. (A1.5 in `south_more_sources.md` cites "p. 45" for *fàamu*, "p. 47" for *sa* and "p. 48" for *màlo*. The pages are 44, 46 and 47.)

### A3. Broqua and Larmarange 2022: woubi and yossi (Abidjan)

- **The Wolof roots.** The Wolof *ubbi* means "to open"; *yoos* has several meanings, including "small fry" (p. 4). In Mali the pair is *yossi*/*qualité* (p. 4).
- **Woubikan.**
  - Woubikan is the "extremely extensive lexicon known only by those concerned". The name is built on *kan*, "language" in Bambara and Jula (p. 5). It grew alongside Nouchi, a street language invented "to communicate without being understood" (p. 5).
  - Its words for women who love women: *bakary*, feminine *troussou*, and *bakary yossi* for women in men's roles (p. 5).
- **"God made us."** Barbara, of the 1998 film, said in 2001: "Anyway, I think God did it right. If He made the woubis, it is because He also made the yossis" (p. 5).
- **The survey.**
  - 518 respondents. On a man/woman/transgender question: 62% man, 25% woman, 13% transgender (p. 12).
  - In a multiple-choice question 21% chose "transgender" and 29% "travesti" (p. 20).
  - Among woubis, 42% said "female" and 21% "transgender" (p. 14).
- **Religion.** Catholic, evangelical and Muslim respondents all appear. The categories show no difference by religion (p. 12; supplementary table).

### A4. Larmarange and Broqua 2023: survey categories

- **Imported labels.** Quantitative surveys import Northern labels. Local ones such as *ibbi*/*yoos* or *branché* are never used (p. 7).
- **Gender-identity options.** Surveys have offered "woman", "female", "transgender", "both", "neither", "travesti", "a woman in a man's body" and more (p. 10).
- **Key finding.** "lorsque l'enquête propose à la fois une modalité « femme » et une modalité « transgenre », la proportion ayant répondu « femme » est usuellement plus élevée" (when a survey offers both "woman" and "transgender", the share answering "woman" is usually higher) (p. 11).
  - For a trans-women's faith, the design lesson is direct: its daughters are women, not a third kind.

### A5. Geoffrion 2013: Kodjo-besia (Akan, Ghana)

- **The term.** *Kodjo-besia*, "Kodjo is a woman [inside]" (¶16). Kodjo is "a common Ghanaian name which means 'Monday born'" (n. 9).
- **Roles.**
  - Market women, food sellers and beauticians. "They cook jolof rice, carry it on their heads to go and sell" (¶16).
  - "They even join women's clubs!" Some are "very wealthy traders" (¶16).
- **Born so.** "'That is how they were born. It is more natural to them'" (¶16).
  - The term can mean a "female soul" (¶19). They are tolerated "on the basis that they cannot be changed" (¶19).
  - Some students felt "born in the wrong body" (¶21–22). One said "I don't know if God decided to make me a male" (¶22).
- **Possessed priests, second-hand.** Some *akɔmfoɔ* (priests) are called Kodjo-besia. "Both men and women can be possessed by spirits of the 'opposite' gender" (¶17, after Banks 2012). Second-hand and Akan: **do not import.**
- **Mothers.** Mothers quietly support a son's femininity: they buy cooking utensils and teach cooking (¶23–25).
  - One family "had already found a female name" for an unborn child. The town then called the boy "Ama" (¶31). Ama is the Saturday-born girl's day-name, matching his Saturday-born *Kwame* (n. 14).
  - Market women and midwives told him: "You have to be the female for your mom!" (¶31).
- **Festive cross-dressing.** Mothers excuse it as "festive transvestism", done for "fun", which carries no stigma at a football victory or a hall party (¶38).
- **Dual, not third.**
  - "I wished sometimes our genders could be dual" (¶40). His grandmother said there was nothing besides male and female (¶40).
  - The wish is for movement between two, not a third (¶39–40).
- **Shift in meaning.** Since 2006 and 2011, Kodjo-besia has come to mean "gay" (¶18).
- **Church.** Church is where young men switch "to the masculine side" (¶39).

### A6. Mackin 2026: woubis, travestis and *Woubi Chéri* (Abidjan, 1997–2021)

- **Local categories** (p. 14):
  - woubis, men who take "le rôle social de femmes" (the social role of women) in a male couple;
  - yossis;
  - travestis;
  - *toussou bakari*, women who love women.
- **In their own words.** In the film Barbara says: "On m'appelle toujours woubi parce que [...] je me comporte en femme" (I'm still called woubi because I behave as a woman) (p. 15).
  - Mackin: "Il est plus question de rôle, de comportement et de romance" (it is more about role, behaviour and romance), not orientation (p. 15). "il s'agit de « rôles » sociaux, pas sexuels" (these are social roles, not sexual ones) (p. 21).
- **The festival.**
  - The film builds to a "matinée mandingue", a "fête accompagnée d'un orchestre et de griots" (a celebration with an orchestra and griots) (p. 16). It was held at Bingerville on 27 December 1997 and organised by the Association des Travestis de Côte d'Ivoire (p. 17).
  - The film's arc is "une féminité conquérante de l'espace public" (a femininity conquering public space) (p. 17).
- **Hostility.**
  - Press photographs taken without consent wrecked earlier soirées (pp. 17–18).
  - 2020s songs mock travestis as "fausses femmes" (false women) with "faux cils, faux seins" (false lashes, false breasts) (p. 18).
  - Miss Woubi (since 2009) was hit by hostile coverage in 2012 and cancelled in 2013 (pp. 19–20).

### A7. Pierce 2016: 'yan daudu and *karuwai* (Hausa, northern Nigeria)

- **The raid.** The 2007 Bauchi *hisbah* raid on men said to be wearing women's clothes. The press called it a "gay wedding" (p. 2).
- **'Yan daudu** (after Gaudio and fieldwork 1993–2002):
  - they "sometimes wear articles of women's clothing, use female pronouns and otherwise address one another as women would";
  - for example they say *kawa*, a woman's word for a close woman friend (p. 9 and n. 28).
- **Livelihood.** They often live in the houses of *karuwai*, independent women. Many earn a living "selling cooked food" (p. 9).
- **History.** The *karuwai* are evidenced before the colonial period, in the early-19th-c. Sokoto reformers' writings (p. 9).
- **Spirit.** 'Yan daudu are associated with the *bori* spirit 'Dan Galadima. *'Yan* means "children of" or "those who do" (p. 9 n. 29). Not Mande; *bori* is out of scope (reading list C).
- **Page fix.** A1 in `south_more_sources.md` gives "PDF p. 8". The passage is on proof p. 9 (file page 8).

### A8. Bertolt 2020: góor-jigéen, brotherhoods and burial (Senegal)

- **Roles.** Góor-jigéen were "très appréciées des femmes" (much liked by women). At weddings they led the entertainment; they went with women to market and to the fields (pp. 656–657).
  - They "jouaient les rôles de poètes, de musiciens ou d'interprètes" (played the parts of poets, musicians or interpreters) (p. 657, after Niang 2010).
  - They "pouvaient se marier avec un homme" (could marry a man), and these weddings were festive community events (p. 657, after Niang 2010).
- **Religion.** Senegal is 94% Muslim and mostly Sufi, with brotherhoods and marabouts. "Chaque Sénégalais a son marabout" (every Senegalese has his marabout) (p. 660 n. 7).
  - Brotherhood Islam and the reformers converge on rejecting homosexuality (p. 660).
- **Burial.** Burial requires recognition as Muslim. An activist reported that some LGBT people "ont été déterrés" (were dug up) and that cemeteries were guarded against their burial (p. 662).
  - With Gorer's 1935 note (A1), burial is the oldest and sharpest religious exclusion in this material.

### A9. M'Baye 2019: góor-jigéen in colonial Senegal (essay)

- **What it is.** An essay reading two European texts, Corre's *L'ethnographie criminelle* (1894) and Michael Davidson's travelogue "Dakar" (in *Some Boys*, 1970; visits of 1949 and 1958), as evidence of an "Afropolitan" gender-variant subculture in colonial Senegal (pp. 1–2).
- **The term.** *Gôr djiguène*, "man-woman", an "umbrella term" for persons "perceived as being transgender, homosexual, bisexual, or too effeminate" (p. 3). Spellings vary (*goorjigeen*, *goor jiggen*; p. 11).
- **No earlier attestation.** M'Baye's Corre passages concern the *signares* (creole women) and "vices contre nature" in "milieux trop privés" (pp. 4–5), and "pederasty" that Corre blames on Europeans in Algeria and Indochina (p. 5). None names the góor-jigéen. The 1894 witness that the Wolof term existed is Broqua 2017 ¶2 (A1), not this essay.
- **Davidson's dated scenes.**
  - 1958: a "middle-aged" person whom Davidson calls a *tapette* enters a French bar in silk and gold earrings; the patronne serves him as any customer (p. 11; M'Baye cites Davidson p. 167, and "p. 27" for the patronne).
  - French sex workers drive the newcomer out for trespassing on their "beat" (p. 12).
  - A drag party in a *bidonville* (p. 12).
- **Problems.**
  - The "transgender" label is M'Baye's, not his sources'; Davidson writes "tapette" and "queen" (pp. 11–12).
  - Davidson is a European sex tourist writing about boys; M'Baye himself calls his impulses pedophilic (p. 9) and reports that most at the 1958 party were "around fifteen" (p. 12), yet reads them as Afropolitan agents. **Nothing from Davidson may be used.**
  - Citation errors: the same Corre passage is "p. 56" in the text and "p. 34" in the note (pp. 4–5); a Davidson quotation is given as "p. 27" in a chapter that runs pp. 163–172 (p. 11); a novel (Condé's *Segu*) is used as evidence (p. 5).
- **Usable.** *Sutura*, "a Wolofized Arabic concept that can mean discretion, modesty, privacy, or protection" (p. 5, after Mills 2011). The saying *Niit deñu koy sutural*, "you owe your fellow human being discretion" (p. 5, after Coly 2019). Broqua 2010 p. 48 records *sutura* in Bamako too (A2).

### A10. Broqua 2012: social forms of male homosexuality in Bamako

- **The argument.** Bamako today resembles New York before "liberation" (Chauncey): a gender model of the effeminate versus the "normal" man, double lives, tactics of silence (pp. 117–121). But reading Africa as the West's past is "evolutionniste" and wrong. Globalisation diversifies; Bamako combines the gender, age and "egalitarian" models (pp. 121–125).
- **Bambara and borrowed terms** (p. 118):
  - *góor-jigéen* (from Wolof), the most common word;
  - *cεtεmùsotε*, "ni homme, ni femme", and *cεmùsoman*, "un homme comme une femme";
  - *yossi* (from Wolof) for the masculine, insertive partner; *qualité* (French) for the feminine one; a man may call his partner "ma femme";
  - *soungourouba*, "pute" in the sense of an easy girl, for the receptive partner; *kamelemba*, "coureur de jupons", for the insertive one;
  - *să*, "serpent", the main pejorative; it names gender, not sex, and is used for any man thought not virile enough (p. 118).
- **Self-names are veiled.** *Milieu* (the community), *branché* (a member), *contre* (a homophobe), even *truc* (pp. 124, 126–127). The too visible are called *déclarés* and avoided (p. 121); young effeminate men are *petites folles* (p. 131).
- **Tactics** ("jeux du système"):
  - two young men may share a bed without suspicion (p. 120);
  - mock weddings between men are held partly in public, "moyennant un savant mensonge sur le motif des réjouissances" (p. 120);
  - possession cults and trades such as dyer and hairdresser offer acceptance (p. 121);
  - money in sex follows the general rule that "tout service mérite salaire" (p. 126).
- **Silence.** Public disclosure is rarely seen as a necessary step; silence may be "la condition de l'acceptation" (p. 127).
- **Kinship.** Marriage and children are obligatory and "souvent largement consentie[s]"; reproduction is kept apart from pleasure (pp. 127–128). A man whose role in an AIDS association became known was summoned to a family council of "plusieurs dizaines de personnes" and told to step back (p. 136).
- **Law and hostility.** In 2012 Malian law did not mention homosexual practices (p. 122; later changes not checked here). Religious authorities on the national ethics committee tried to stop the 2000s studies (pp. 129–130). A tea circle (*grin*) once assured Broqua that "an official congress of homosexuals" had met in Bamako, a rumour set off by an "homme-femme" insulted in the street (p. 134 n.).
- **No feminine institution.** As in 2010 (A2), Bamako has no role like the Wolof góor-jigéen's ceremonial one.

### A11. Niang et al. 2002: the Horizons report (Dakar; skimmed)

- **The study.** 250 men surveyed and interviewed in Dakar and nearby towns for HIV programming (pp. 1, 7–8). A public-health report; the sample is young and not representative (p. 8).
- **Terms** (p. 9).
  - *Gor jigeen*, "man-woman": "a man who is very close to the world of women, to the point of identifying with them." Men now find it "discriminatory and demeaning": "The term Gor jigeen frightens us."
  - Self-names: *Ibbi* (more feminine, less dominant in sex) and *Yoos* (insertive, who "do not consider themselves to be homosexuals"). The categories have "more to do with identity and status than with sexual practices."
- **Roles** (pp. 9–10).
  - "In ceremonies, the Yoos are served food by the Ibbi," and the Ibbi, "like women", eat with their hands (p. 9).
  - Ibbi advise powerful women "on clothes and make-up, cook for certain special occasions, and help organize marriage and baptism ceremonies" (p. 10).
  - In the 1950s, each "great lady" of a political party had a group of such men "who acted as her publicists", attacking her women rivals, for her support (p. 10).
  - These patrons were "traditionally called 'Gor jigeen mothers'" (p. 10). This confirms Broqua 2017 ¶17's citation of "Niang et al. 2002: 10".
  - An Ibbi may take a young woman as his "sister" (*jigeen*), do her henna, make-up and hair, and be her confidant, "without any possibility of having sexual relations"; she calls him "Camen" (p. 10; unglossed).
- **Names.** Older Ibbi "will give you the surname that you are to call them by" and hide their real names (p. 10).
- **Protection.** In some Lébou neighbourhoods "No one dares insult an Ibbi who is a member of our community; he is protected the way you would protect the insane" (p. 10). Protection as of a dependant, not honour.
- **Religion** (p. 13). A Muslim dignitary: "we cannot accept homosexuals either in our homes or in our mosques"; a cleric says a handshake requires purifying prayers. Men themselves say they will renounce it in old age to be right with Islam.
- **Not used.** The violence figures (p. 12), the testimonies of childhood abuse (p. 11), the recognition signs in the mosque (p. 10) and all clinical material.

## B. What these suggest for a medieval Mande faith (analogy only)

### B1. Roles that recur across four societies

The same social shape recurs in Wolof, Akan, Hausa and Abidjan (Mande-influenced) material:
- a person assigned male lives in the **world of women**;
- **cooking and feeding** (Broqua ¶16; Geoffrion ¶16; Pierce p. 9);
- running **weddings and naming feasts** (Broqua ¶16; Bertolt p. 657);
- **advising** women (Broqua ¶16);
- **sponsored by a powerful woman** called a "mother" (Broqua ¶17);
- learning from **elders ranked by seniority** who are "like mothers" (Broqua ¶34–35);
- sometimes as **poet, musician or interpreter**, of a status "comparable to the griot's" (Broqua ¶17; Bertolt p. 657).

For Badenya this matches, without being borrowed from, three grounded Mande pieces:
- the *mother's house* (Koné; `waf_conrad_austen_sunjata.md`);
- the jeli's word (`waf_ibn_battuta_mali.md`; `waf_jansen_kangaba_griots.md`);
- women performing at ceremonies (`waf_schulz_jelimusow.md` §2.2).

The narrator can say so: "Later West African societies knew people in roles like these; the faith is a reconstruction, not their ancestor."

### B2. Language people use for themselves

- **"Born so", "God made us".** The phrases are "That is how they were born" (Geoffrion ¶16), "I think God did it right" (Broqua and Larmarange p. 5), and Gaudio's title *Allah Made Us*.
  - So the faith's doctrine should treat womanhood as **given and recognised**, not chosen or bought.
- **Behaviour and role.** "je me comporte en femme" (Mackin p. 15).
  - The gate should be about being *recognised* in a woman's role, which is the social shape of the Mande gate.
- **Women's address.** Female pronouns and women's words for friends (*kawa*, Pierce p. 9 n. 28).
  - Design: once claimed, a daughter is addressed by women's kinship and friendship terms. The House's women call her "my mother's child". This is a Mande term (Koné), not borrowed.

### B3. Naming

- **Day-names.** In Akan practice a feminine day-name can be given to a boy, as Ama for Kwame (Geoffrion ¶31, n. 14). Kodjo-besia is itself built on a day-name (n. 9).
  - Mande naming is different: the mother's name is prefixed (`waf_jansen_kangaba_griots.md` §4.2). Do not use Akan day-names for Badenya.
  - The analogy holds only in general: West African naming can recognise a person's gender through the name.
- **Unborn girls' names.** "they had already found a female name for me" (Geoffrion ¶31).
  - Faith voice, as myth: a mother who chose a girl's name before the birth keeps it for her child. Narrator flag.

### B4. Religion

- **Islam is the hostile frame.** It appears in all four countries:
  - refused burial (Broqua ¶3; Bertolt p. 662);
  - Jamra and the *hisbah* (Broqua ¶30; Pierce p. 2);
  - guilt (Broqua 2010 pp. 47–48).
- **Non-Islamic tolerance.** Its only examples are the Akan possession priests (second-hand, Akan) and the Hausa *bori* tie (Pierce). Neither is Mande. **Do not import.**
- **Brotherhood Islam is softer on women** but still hostile (Bertolt p. 660).
- **In-world mapping.** A Maliki jurist at the mansa's court is the hostile voice; a local, conciliating *khaṭīb* is the softer one (`waf_tarikh_es_soudan.md` §2.3). This needs no modern source.

### B5. Words *not* to use and words to use

- **Not as self-names:** góor-jigéen, woubi, *qualité*, *yoss*/*yossi*, travesti, 'yan daudu, Kodjo-besia, *sa*, Woubikan, *kawa*.
  - Added from A9–A11 and `waf_gaudio_otu.md`: *Ibbi*/*Yoos*, *cεtεmùsotε*, *cεmùsoman*, *soungourouba*, *kamelemba*, *branché*, *milieu*, *déclaré*, *masu harka*, sasso/sasofo, *Kwadwo Besia*, *Ntowbea*, *ƙawa*, *uwa*/*'ya* as titles, "Camen".
- **As hostile in-world slurs only** (Mande characters of other faiths):
  - "snake-walker", after *sa* (Broqua 2010 p. 46);
  - "false woman", after the 2020s songs (Mackin p. 18);
  - "man-woman" (Wolof, Broqua 2017 ¶1).
  - Each line is shown as a slur, and the faith's characters answer it.
- **Frame for opinion modifiers:** *màlo* (shame) and *dànbe* (honour) (Broqua 2010 p. 47), with Schulz's *tògò*/*dambe*.
- **A Mande word for the faith's secret:** *gundo*, the thing everyone knows and no one may say (Broqua 2010 p. 50; Jansen 2002 p. 19). The faith's gate can be "known to all, said by one".

## D. Design suggestions for Badenya

### D1. Gate

- **Recognised, not chosen.** Doctrine voice: "The djinn knew what I was before the smith did; the mother knew before the djinn" (after Geoffrion ¶16, 19; Broqua and Larmarange p. 5). The narrator marks it as the faith's theology.
- **A mother's sponsorship as step 2's cost.** A claiming woman of rank sponsors her: gold, a place in her house, and her name prefixed to the daughter's (`waf_jansen_kangaba_griots.md` §4.2).
  - The social shape matches the "mothers" of Broqua ¶17. Say so only in this doc, never in loc.
- **Elders as mothers.** House elders teach the newly named "real lessons" (after Broqua ¶34): a short **apprenticeship** after the gate. The new daughter serves at three feasts before she can sponsor anyone.

### D2. Line

- **Seniority.** The House is ranked by seniority from the washing (`waf_tarikh_es_soudan.md` §4.1), and seniors are called "mother" (Broqua ¶34–35).
- **Marriage and children.** A daughter of Badenya may marry and have, or adopt, children. Some góor-jigéen "pouvaient [...] se marier et avoir des enfants" (could marry and have children) (Broqua ¶18). The Houses' *Take as Daughter* covers the rest.
- **Patronage dependence.** A daughter without her own income depends on her sponsor (Broqua ¶36–37). A House with too many unsponsored daughters frays, which uses the existing Sisters' Houses strain.

### D3. Holy sites

None of these sources gives a Mande site. Keep the sites from the medieval and epic notes: `c_sibi_mali` with Koulikoro, `c_tabon`, `c_niani`, `c_awkar`, `c_walata`. Add one doctrine instead:
- **"The House buries its own."** Refused burial is the oldest religious exclusion in this material (Gorer 1935 in Broqua ¶3; Bertolt p. 662). So the faith's Houses keep **their own burial ground**, and a daughter's funeral is the House's duty.
  - Mechanic: a "Buried by the House" event on a daughter's death, with a stability or piety gain for her House.
  - Never write exhumation into game text.

### D4. Names

- Nothing here for Badenya's name list. Keep the Mande sources and the mother-name prefix (`waf_jansen_kangaba_griots.md` §4.7).
- Do not use any informant's or activist's name (Barbara, Tatiana, Kwesi, Ato, Kwame, etc.).

### D5. Festival

- **The naming feast as the daughter's stage.** Across these sources the recurring public role is running **weddings and naming feasts** (Broqua ¶16; Bertolt p. 657). So the New Roof festival (`waf_jansen_kangaba_griots.md` §4.6) should be paired with a recurring **"Naming Feast"** event:
  - a daughter of the House cooks and leads it for a family at court;
  - the reward is *tògò* (name) and opinion with that family's women.
- **A Manding-style feast with griots.** It was the idiom of celebration chosen by the Abidjan travestis in 1997 (Mackin pp. 16–17). That can inspire the *tone* of the New Roof's last day: dancing, a *jelimuso*, the age-set's name shouted.
  - The narrator never cites the 1997 fête in loc.

### D6. Reaction lines (hostile Mande or Maliki characters; each answered by the faith)

- "Snake-walker." Faith: "The snake goes where it likes and no one owns it."
- "A false woman." Faith: "Ask her mother which of us is false."
- Maliki jurist: "The mosque will not bury her." Faith: "Then her House will."
- Approving Mande matron: "She cooks for the naming like one born to it." Faith: "She was."
- Hostile courtier: "Neither man nor woman." Faith: "A woman. Her mother said so, and the jeli sang it." (Shape from *cεtεmùsotε*, Broqua 2012 p. 118; Koné p. 2.)

### D7. Additions from A9–A11 (analogy only; see also `waf_gaudio_otu.md` §4–5)

- **The great lady's voice.** A daughter attached to a great woman at court can serve as her praise voice and the scourge of her rivals (after Niang et al. 2002 p. 10, the 1950s "publicists"). Mechanic: the daughter adds to her patroness's scheme power or prestige against rival women at court; the rivals' enmity is the cost. It fits the intrigue around queen Qāsā (`waf_ibn_battuta_mali.md`).
- **A sister outside the House.** A daughter may befriend a young woman of court as her "sister", doing her henna and hair: friendship, not courtship (after Niang p. 10). Mechanic: a friend relation and opinion with the young woman's mother.
- **The feast under another name.** At a hostile court the House can hold its Naming Feast as "a sister's wedding" (after Broqua 2012 p. 120; Gaudio 2009 p. 176). Lower risk, lower prestige; discovery feeds the "They say it was a wedding" event (`waf_gaudio_otu.md` §4.7).
- **Owed discretion.** Faith flavour: the House never exposes anyone's secret, even an enemy's (after *sutura*, A9 and A2; *gundo*, B5). Mechanic: refusing to use a known secret against a rival gives opinion with House members.
- **The family council.** On joining the House, a daughter may be summoned before her natal kin in council (after Broqua 2012 p. 136): stay (kin opinion loss) or keep both ties at a cost in gold or prestige.
- **Do not use:** the *Ibbi*/*Yoos* serving order or eating customs, protection "as you would protect the insane", recognition signs, anything from Davidson, any testimony of violence or abuse.
