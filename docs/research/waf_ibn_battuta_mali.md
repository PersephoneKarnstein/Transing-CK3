# Ibn Baṭṭūṭa, *Voyages* vol. 4, the Mali chapter (1352–53; ed. 1858/1874): design reading

Source: *Voyages d'Ibn Batoutah*, Arabic text with French translation by C. Defrémery and B. R. Sanguinetti, vol. 4 (Paris: Imprimerie nationale, 1st ed. 1858; the copy read is the 1874 printing, archive.org `ldpd_5998008_003`). The Arabic pages are interleaved with the French; only the French was read.
Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo (the OCR'd Arabic was filtered out). Page numbers are the edition's, taken from the running heads.

**What was read.** In full: the Sudan journey from Sijilmāsa to Tagaddā, pp. 376–445: Taghāza and the desert (376–385), Walata (385–390), the road to Mali (390–397), Mali (397–424), the return by Mīma, Timbuktu and Gao (425–436), the Bardāma and Tagaddā (437–445). Skipped: everything before p. 376 (the Maghrib, al-Andalus) and after p. 445 (the road to Fez). The Arabic text was not read; where a French gloss matters (e.g. "eunuques", p. 404) it is flagged for checking against the Arabic or Hamdun and King's English.

**Relation to existing notes.** `south_reading_list.md` ("West Africa", finding 1 and 2, candidate A) already cites pp. 388–389, 412–414, 417–418, 423–424 from a first skim. This note checks those pages, corrects several, and adds what the skim missed: the bird above the throne, the cousins' nakedness, Qāsā's plot, the Bardāma women's protection, and Tagaddā's sister's-sons. ## 1. The argument in brief

Ibn Baṭṭūṭa spent about eight months in the Mali capital (he dates his arrival 14 Jumādā I 753 and his departure 22 Muḥarram 754, p. 424), and he disliked most of it. His report is a hostile Maghribi jurist's: the court is mean (Sulaymān's "vil cadeau", pp. 399–400), the customs shocking (pp. 423–424). That hostility is useful. It means the things he records against his will (the women's freedom at Walata, the masked bards, the queen who shares the throne, the women's nakedness at court) were not invented to flatter Mali. It also means every judgment is his, not the Malians'. He was told the bard rite was pre-Islamic (p. 414); he did not see that for himself.

What the chapter gives the design: a court calendar (Friday, Ramaḍān's 27th night, the two feasts); a throne (the *penpi*) under a golden bird; an interpreter-bard (Dūghā) who is paid in gold dust before the whole court; masked bards licensed to tell the king to do good; a queen who is "the associate" in government and can fall; a desert town where descent runs through the mother's brother and women keep men friends; and a Berber sultan whose heirs are his sister's sons.

Nothing in it concerns a man living as a woman.

## 2. What matters for the design

### Walata (Iwālātan): the line and women's freedom (pp. 385–390)

- The town's governor is the *farbā* (vice-roy), p. 385; he speaks to merchants only through an interpreter "par suite de son mépris pour eux" (p. 385). Model for an insult at a Malian court.
- The guest-meal is millet with honey and sour milk in a calabash; it is the best hospitality they know (p. 386). Ibn Baṭṭūṭa sneers; a faith event can serve it as honour.
- Women: "elles ont plus de mérite et sont plus considérées que les hommes" (p. 387).
- The line: no man is named for his father; "chacun rattache sa généalogie à son oncle maternel", and the heirs are the sister's sons, not the man's own children (p. 388). He has seen this elsewhere only in Malabar (p. 388). They are Muslims, pray, study law and learn the Qurʾān (p. 388). **The matrilineal custom is Muslim practice here, not a pagan survival.**
- Women go unveiled, pray punctually, and will not travel with a husband; the family forbids it (p. 388). Women have men friends who are not kin, and men have women friends (pp. 388–389).
- Two scenes: the qāḍī of Walata with his "amie", whom he wished to take on the ḥajj; the sultan refused (p. 389). Abū Muḥammad Yandakān with his wife and her male friend on a canopied bed: "Nos femmes, d'ailleurs, ne sont point comme celles de vos pays" (p. 389). Ibn Baṭṭūṭa never visits him again (p. 389).
- The caravan quarrel that loses Ibn Zīrī is with "le fils de son oncle maternel" (p. 380): the mother's brother's line structures who owes whom.

### The road and the capital (pp. 390–399)

- Hollow baobabs used as cisterns, beehives, and once a weaver's workshop (p. 391).
- Travellers carry salt, glass beads and spices, not coin; village women come out to sell millet, sour milk, chickens (p. 394). Salt is money (p. 378); a camel-load is 8–10 mithqāls at Walata and 20–40 at Mali (p. 378). Cowries at Gao and Mali (p. 435).
- Zāghari has Ibāḍī "white" merchants, the Saghanaghu, beside Maliki "Turi" (p. 394). Zāgha is Muslim "depuis très-longtemps" (p. 395).
- Entry to the capital needs permission (p. 397). There is a white merchants' quarter (p. 397). The qāḍī ʿAbd al-Raḥmān is black, a pilgrim; the interpreter Dūghā sends an ox; Ibn al-Faqīh is married to the daughter of the sultan's paternal uncle, who feeds the guests (pp. 397–398; the running heads for these pages are lost in the OCR).
- "Mensa signifie sultan" (p. 399). Sulaymān is "avare" (p. 399); the people dislike him for it (p. 419).

### The audience (pp. 403–409)

- The cupola: windows plated with silver and gold; a silk cord with an Egyptian striped kerchief is let out to signal the king's sitting; drums and horns (p. 403). About 300 armed slaves come out (p. 403). Two saddled horses and two rams "contre le mauvais œil" (p. 404).
- Dūghā the interpreter stands at the door in silk, with a gold-sheathed sword, boots and spurs ("personne, excepté lui, ne porte de bottes ce jour-là"), and two short lances, one silver, one gold (p. 404).
- "Les militaires, les gouverneurs, les pages ou eunuques, les Messoûfites" sit outside (p. 404). **Check the Arabic.** "Pages ou eunuques" is the translators' double gloss of one word, very likely *al-fityān* ("youths, pages"). It is not evidence of a eunuch corps at Mali.
- Speech is relayed: petitioner to Dūghā, Dūghā to a man standing under the windows, that man to the king (p. 405). No one speaks to the mansa directly.
- **The *penpi*:** a three-step dais under a tree, covered with silk and cushions, under a silk parasol "au sommet duquel se voit un oiseau d'or, grand comme un épervier" (p. 406). The king wears a gold skullcap and a red velvet tunic of European cloth, carries a bow and quiver, and walks very slowly (p. 406). Singers go before him with gold and silver *kanābir* (p. 406).
- Oaths: the people swear by the king's name, "Mensa Soleïmân kî" (p. 407).
- Prostration: the summoned man changes into worn clothes and a dirty cap, beats the ground with his elbows, and throws dust on his head and back after the king answers (pp. 407–408).
- **Bowstring testimony:** a man recites his deeds ("tel jour j'ai tué un tel homme") and witnesses confirm by drawing and releasing a bowstring (p. 408).
- Ibn Juzayy adds that Mali's envoy to Fez brought a basket of dust to throw on himself whenever the Marinid sultan spoke kindly (p. 409).
- Sanctuary: an accused person takes refuge in the mosque, or failing that in the preacher's house (p. 419).

### The feasts, Dūghā and the masked bards (pp. 409–414)

- Ibn Baṭṭūṭa was in Mali for both ʿĪd al-Fiṭr and ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā (p. 409). At the prayer the preacher's sermon is explained "dans son langage" by a man with a lance, and it urges obedience to the king (p. 410).
- On both feast afternoons the king sits on the *penpi*. Four emirs stand beside him driving off flies (p. 411).
- **Dūghā's performance.** He comes with his four wives and about a hundred slave women in fine robes with gold and silver headbands. He sits on a raised seat and plays a reed instrument with bells beneath, singing the king's wars (pp. 411–412). "Ses épouses et ses femmes esclaves chantent avec lui et jouent avec des arcs" (p. 412). Thirty slave boys in red drum; his disciples tumble and fence (p. 412).
- His pay: a purse of 200 mithqāls of gold dust, its contents announced to everyone; the commanders draw their bows in thanks, and next day each gives Dūghā a gift (pp. 412–413). He repeats the ceremony every Friday after the afternoon prayer (p. 413).
- **The bards.** After Dūghā, "les poètes arrivent, et ils sont nommés djoulâ, mot dont le singulier est djâli" (p. 413). Each is inside a feather figure like a *shiqshāq* (a bird) with a wooden head and red beak (p. 413). Their verses, he was told, are an admonition: kings who sat on this *penpi* did good; "fais à ton tour beaucoup de bien, afin qu'il soit rappelé après ta mort" (pp. 413–414). The chief poet climbs the steps and lays his head in the king's lap, then on his right and left shoulders (p. 414). He was assured it is "une habitude très-ancienne, antérieure à l'introduction de l'islamisme" (p. 414).
- Among Mali's faults: "une sorte de bouffonnerie quand les poètes récitent leurs vers au sultan" (p. 424).

**Note the separation.** Dūghā (interpreter, praise-singer, sword-player, paid in gold, with women who sing and play with bows) and the masked *djâli* (admonishers, unpaid in the text) are two different acts. The reading list's "the jeli must be paid in gold dust, as Dugha was" merges them.

### Women at court (pp. 397–398, 411–412, 417–419, 423–424)

- **Qāsā.** Sulaymān quarrels with "son épouse principale, la fille de son oncle paternel, qui était appelée Kâçâ; le sens de ce mot, chez les nègres, est reine" (p. 417). She is "dans le gouvernement l'associée du souverain, d'après l'usage de ce peuple", and her name is said from the pulpit with the king's (p. 417). He confines her with a commander and gives power to another wife, **Bandjū** ("Bendjoû"), "qui n'était pas au nombre des filles de rois" (p. 417). People disapprove (p. 417).
- **The cousins' verdict.** The king's paternal cousins go to congratulate Bandjū with ashes on their arms but no dust on their heads; when Qāsā is freed they cover head and body with dust for her (p. 417). Bandjū complains; the cousins take refuge in the mosque; pardoned, they come before the king naked, as custom requires, morning and evening for seven days (pp. 417–418).
- **Qāsā rides daily** to the audience place with her slaves of both sexes, dust on their heads, she veiled (p. 418).
- **The plot.** The king has a slave woman of Qāsā's brought in fettered; she says Qāsā sent her to Jāṭal, a paternal cousin of the king in exile at "Canborni", urging him to take the kingdom: "Moi et tous les militaires, nous te sommes entièrement dévoués" (pp. 418–419). The commanders say she deserves death; she takes refuge in the preacher's house (p. 419). The story ends there.
- **Nakedness.** Slave women, servants and young girls appear naked before men, even serving the commanders' Ramaḍān meals in the palace (p. 423). All women who go in to the sovereign are naked and unveiled, "ses filles aussi"; on the 27th night of Ramaḍān about a hundred slave women came out of the palace with food, naked, with two of the king's daughters (pp. 423–424). Ibn Baṭṭūṭa lists it as the first fault. Combined with the cousins' seven days (p. 418), **nakedness before the mansa is a sign of submission and pardon**, not sexual display.
- A slave woman exposes the qāḍī who faked a theft of Mansa Mūsā's gift (pp. 427–428): slave women as witnesses who bring down men.

### Kings and the past (pp. 419–421)

- Before Sulaymān reigned Mansa Maghā, and before him Mansa Mūsā, "un prince généreux et vertueux" (p. 419). Mūsā's grandfather (*aïeul*) "Sârek Djâthah" became Muslim through the grandfather of the jurist Mudrik (p. 420).
- Mūsā seated a man who had given him 7⅓ mithqāls as a boy beside him on the *penpi*, asked the commanders what such a kindness deserved, and gave 700 (p. 420). A model "debt repaid" event.
- Farbā Maghā of Mīma made the ḥajj with Mansa Mūsā (p. 427).

### The return: the "cannibals", the Bardāma, Tagaddā (pp. 428–445)

- Gold-country "anthropophages" visiting Sulaymān (pp. 428–429). **Do not use.** It is a hostile trope about a neighbouring people; it has no place in a faith's world.
- **The Bardāma (Berber nomads):** caravans travel safely only under their protection, "et celle de la femme est plus efficace encore que celle de l'homme" (p. 437). A man who marries one of their women may not take her beyond Gao or Walata (pp. 437–438).
- **Tagaddā:** educated slave women are rarely sold and fetch a high price (p. 439). The copper is smelted in houses by slaves of both sexes (p. 441). Bornu exports "les belles esclaves, les eunuques et les étoffes teintes" (pp. 441–442). This is the region's only eunuch source in the chapter, and it is Kanem-Bornu, not Mali.
- **The sultan of Tagaddā, Izār:** "Les fils de sa sœur l'accompagnaient, et ce sont eux qui hériteront de son royaume" (p. 443). His mother and sister come to greet the guests; his mother sends milk (p. 443).
- The return caravan carries about 600 slave girls (p. 445).

## 3. Corrections to the candidate (`south_reading_list.md`, West Africa)

1. **Walata pages.** The women's standing is p. 387, the maternal-uncle line p. 388, the friends pp. 388–389, the two scenes p. 389. The reading list's "pp. 388–389" is close; use 387–389.
2. **Dūghā's women** are on pp. 411–412 (introduced p. 411, bows p. 412). They are his four wives and about a hundred slave women, not "royal wives".
3. **Dūghā is not the masked bard.** Dūghā is paid 200 mithqāls (p. 412) and gifts next day (p. 413). The *djâli* in feather masks (p. 413) are a separate group, and no payment is recorded for them. If the gate requires gold, it pays the singer-interpreter, or the faith invents the masked bard's fee. Say which.
4. **Qāsā is pp. 417–419**, not 417–418. The plot with Jāṭal (pp. 418–419) is the stronger event: a queen who conspires with an exiled cousin and the army.
5. **The "bird".** The reading list stresses the bird-masked bards. The text also puts a gold bird the size of a hawk on top of the throne's parasol (p. 406). The bard dressed as a bird faces a throne crowned by a bird. That is a better visual link than anything in the gate proposal so far.
6. **"Licence to admonish"** is the informants' account relayed to Ibn Baṭṭūṭa ("On m'a informé", p. 413); the pre-Islamic antiquity is hearsay too ("On m'a assuré", p. 414). Narrator should say "reported".
7. **"Pages ou eunuques"** (p. 404) is not support for eunuchs at Mali. Do not cite it as such without the Arabic.
8. **Sister's-son succession is attested twice in this chapter**: Walata's Massūfa (p. 388) and Tagaddā's Berber sultan (p. 443). Both are Berber (Ṣanhāja) groups, not Mande. For Mali's own royal house the matrilineal evidence is Ibn Khaldūn (`waf_ibn_khaldun_mali.md`), and Qāsā herself is a *paternal* cousin (p. 417).

## 4. Design implications

### Gate (Be Sung Before the Throne)

- **Setting the final step.** Use the feast afternoon on the *penpi* (pp. 411–414). Order: the mansa sits; Dūghā's praise-song with his women playing with bows; the bird-masked bards; the chief bard's head in the king's lap and on each shoulder. The daughter's new name is sung in the bards' turn.
- **Two people to win, not one.** The interpreter (Dūghā's office: the court's voice, paid in gold) must carry the petition (p. 405); the masked bard sings it. That fits the brief's "social gate" better than a single Court Poet. Fallback without Royal Court: a councillor or court chaplain-equivalent as "the interpreter", plus any character with high diplomacy/learning as "the bard".
- **Bowstring witness** (p. 408): when her name is sung, witnesses who know her confirm it by drawing and releasing a bowstring. A clean mechanic: each friend, sister or lover at court adds weight. Women can be witnesses too: Dūghā's women "play with bows" (p. 412).
- **Dust and nakedness** (pp. 407–408, 417–418): the petitioner throws dust on her head when the king answers. Do not make her disrobe; the period meaning is penance and pardon, and that is the wrong register for a daughter's naming. Use dust only.
- Calendar: Friday (Dūghā's weekly repeat, p. 413), the 27th night of Ramaḍān (gifts, p. 402; food procession, p. 424), and the two ʿĪds (p. 409).

### Line

- **Walata as holy site** (`c_walata`): "chacun rattache sa généalogie à son oncle maternel" (p. 388). Holy-site text can say, in the faith's voice, that the town keeps the mother's line under Islam.
- The women's-friends custom (pp. 388–389) is a better world-flavour than a doctrine: a modifier such as "Friend of the House" for a non-kin companion, with Ibn Baṭṭūṭa-style Maliki reaction lines against it.
- **Qāsā as a model for the faith's queen**: "the associate of the sovereign", her name said with his in the sermon (p. 417). An event chain can rebuild her fall: a lesser wife raised, the king's female cousins choosing whom to honour with dust (p. 417), a slave woman's testimony (p. 418), sanctuary in the preacher's house (p. 419).

### World and reaction

- Hostile Maliki jurist reaction lines can borrow his targets: the bards' "bouffonnerie" (p. 424), the unveiled women and their friends (p. 388), the women's nakedness (p. 423). Keep his voice clearly a stranger's: he never stays to understand.
- Faith-friendly line, in a Walata husband's voice: "Our women are not like the women of your country" (after p. 389).
- Mansa Mūsā's repaid kindness (p. 420) fits a generic "a debt from childhood is repaid on the *penpi*" event.
- Bardāma: "the woman's protection is stronger than the man's" (p. 437). Good flavour for a desert-escort event, Berber not Mande.
- Avoid: the "cannibal" visitors (pp. 428–429); the 600 slave girls (p. 445) except as grim background; anything that makes Mali's women's nakedness erotic.

### Names and titles from this chapter

- Titles: *mansa* ("sultan", p. 399), *farbā* (governor/lieutenant, pp. 385, 427, 430, 432), *qāsā* ("queen", p. 417), *djâli*/pl. *djoulâ* (bard, p. 413), *penpi* (throne dais, pp. 405, 411, 413).
- People: Qāsā, Bandjū (women); Dūghā (interpreter); Kandjā Mūsā (the king's lieutenant, p. 404); Jāṭal (the exiled cousin, p. 419); Sāraq Jāṭa (Mūsā's grandfather, p. 420); Mūsā, Maghā, Sulaymān.
