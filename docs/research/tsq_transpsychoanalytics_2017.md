## 1. Essays read in full: gist and what is usable

### Cavanagh, introduction (pp. 326–357)

**Gist.** Psychoanalysis long read trans people as psychotic. Catherine Millot's *Horsexe* (1983) tied trans femininity to psychosis, and many Lacanians still lean on it. A newer, trans-affirming body of work (Prosser, Salamon, Elliot, Carlson, Gherovici, Gozlan) rejects that. Gherovici's motto heads the issue: "Psychoanalysis needs a sex change" (p. 326). Cavanagh notes that all gender identities are, for psychoanalysis, defences against difference, cis as much as trans. In a note she reports, with disappointment, a 2016 talk in which Julia Kristeva echoed Millot's view (p. 353 n. 1). The rest is a literature review and summaries of the issue.

**Usable.** Two background points only. (1) The clinic's history is a history of gatekeepers who presumed sickness; that backs A4, A5 and Bhatt's "epistemic vandalism". (2) Kristeva herself spoke against "recomposed" genders; any Kristevan framing (Stewart, below) must be used with that in mind.

### Elliot and Lyons, "Transphobia as Symptom" (pp. 358–383)

**Gist.** A psychoanalytic "symptomatic reading" of Sheila Jeffreys's *Gender Hurts* (2014). Jeffreys calls trans women "male-bodied transgenders" (p. 361) and lists harms they supposedly do. The authors do not argue with the facts; they ask what fear drives the text. Their answer: the trans woman becomes an "unwoman", an uncanny double (like the undead or the "un-American") that threatens a dream of a pure, united women's community. The fear works through two scenes. In the **bath** scene, a trans woman's imagined male gaze shames women in toilets, showers and festival grounds. In the **bed** scene, her imagined male voice is said to "guilt-trip lesbians into allowing penises into their bodies" (p. 371, quoting Jeffreys). They borrow Lacan's list of the harms a paranoid subject imputes to its enemy: poison, evil spells, influence, physical intrusion, abuse, theft of secrets, profanation, injury at law, spying, defamation, damages (pp. 374–375). The text calls itself a victim while it persecutes. Lacan's "passion for ignorance" (p. 359) names the will not to know. They conclude that "the unwoman does not exist and is a symptom" (p. 379) of that politics.

**Translated into game terms.**
- *Symptom and phobic object → scapegoat pressure.* The claim is that a community's hostility to its "inside outsider" rises when its own unity is shaky. A concrete rule: hostile events against daughters fire more often when the hostile faith is itself under strain (low fervor, a heresy spreading, a schism). CK3 has faith fervor, so this is buildable. It is a stretch from Lacan, but the rule stands on its own.
- *Lacan's list of imputed harms → a rumour table.* Each item maps onto a feature the faiths really have, which is what makes a rumour stick: **poison** (the mare's draught, B2/H14), **spells** (seiðr; accepted witchcraft, B4), **influence** (the priestess who "cracks" courtiers, H15), **intrusion** (into convents, baths and women's quarters), **theft of secrets** (eunuch chamberlains in the palace), **profanation** (Purim, the temple garland), **defamation** (the House's counter-record). Use it to widen Bhatt note row 8 (*The Child-Lifter Rumour*) into a general *Whispers* event with one rumour drawn per faith.
- *The bath scene → a bathhouse event.* Byzantine public baths and the Islamic hammam had set hours for women. A keeper or a matron bars a daughter from the women's hour. Options: go at another hour, bring sisters, appeal to the House Protectress, or petition the lord. See §2 row 3 for what not to build.
- *The bed scene → a spouse event.* See §2 row 4.
- *"Passion for ignorance".* **Not usable** as a mechanic. Bhatt's "epistemic vandalism" already covers it more concretely.
- *Their proposed cure* (the transphobe should "identify with the symptom" and accept the symbolic father). **Not usable.**

**Bhatt pressure.** See §5. The essay explains transphobia as private anxiety. Bhatt explains it as politics: male supremacy, the reproductive Nation, penetrability. The mod writes hostility as the doctrine of vanilla faiths, not as anyone's neurosis.

### Hansbury, "Unthinkable Anxieties" (pp. 384–404)

**Gist.** A trans analyst reads a century of analysts' writing about trans patients and finds four recurring fears in the cis analyst: being tricked into homosexuality; "I will become ungendered or slip outside of gender" (p. 387); the body breaking and losing parts; going mad. Each fear has a defence: insisting on the binary, calling surgery "mutilation" and the patient a monster, diagnosing psychosis or hunting for a cause, and misnaming. Examples: Freud on Schreber; Greenson, whose pronoun for a trans woman switched to "she" only when he described her vagina, then switched back (p. 390); Socarides, who called surgery "spare-parts, 'tinker toy' type of surgery" (p. 391); Colette Chiland, for whom "a man to me is ultimately someone capable of penetrating me virtually" (p. 394) and who told patients "I shall not speak to them in their new gender" (p. 394). The better path asks "how trans?", not "why trans?", and holds the patient's mind in mind. "Unthinkable anxieties must be thought" (p. 395).

**Translated into game terms.**
- *Four fears and four defences → four voices for hostile physicians and scholars.* The tricked man ("he means to lure men"); the man afraid of his own manhood ("if he can, any man can"); the mutilation voice ("the knife makes monsters"); the madness voice ("a demon has her", or a jinn, or a fox spirit, by faith). Each gets a matching act: insist, condemn, diagnose, misname. These become the text variants of Bhatt note row 6 (the physician's record and the counter-record).
- *Greenson and Chiland → penetrability, confirmed.* Recognition switches on and off with the imagined genitals. This is Bhatt's "the most relevant phallus is the imagined one" in a clinician's own words. No new mechanic; it confirms Bhatt note row 7 (aim hostile lines at the imagined body).
- *"How trans?", not "why trans?"* Confirms A4 (transness needs no cause).
- *Holding another's mind in mind (mentalization).* **A stretch, but concrete:** it is what H29 (the sisterhood's school) and the Houses' "let them tend you" event already do. No change.

### Breslow, "There Is Nothing Missing in the Real" (pp. 431–450)

**Gist.** A US school district barred a six-year-old trans girl from the girls' toilets because of the male genitals it imagined she would have as an adult. Breslow shows that the exclusion ran on a fantasy of her future body and of childhood as ignorant of sex. Trans-affirming stories of trans childhood use the same fantasy in reverse. The title quotes Jacqueline Rose: "there is nothing missing in the real" (p. 437). The essay quotes reports of very young children trying to harm themselves; those passages are excluded.

**Usable.** One rule only (§2 row 11): no hostile text about a Marked child's future body, and no child-body content of any kind. The imagined-future-phallus argument confirms Bhatt's penetrability chapter but must not be staged with child characters. Everything else: **not usable**.

### Osserman, "Is the Phallus Uncut?" (pp. 497–517)

**Gist.** Does the penis matter in Lacan's theory of the phallus? Osserman says yes, but not as destiny: "Anatomy is shown to play a fundamental but nondeterministic role" (p. 497). The organ matters because it can be imagined lost and because others desire it. He reads the David Reimer case, foreskin-restoration men and trans narratives. Against Millot, he notes that surgery often works as a "rite of passage" (p. 511, citing Bolin) that brings a person into a community and a set of meanings. "Sometimes one must lose a penis to gain a relation to the phallus" (p. 514). An analyst's case of a trans woman shows disappointment after surgery worked through by mourning old photographs, after which she lives more freely. The Reimer case ends in suicide; that is excluded.

**Translated into game terms.**
- *Surgery as a rite into a community.* This is already the knife's design: Day of Blood, then the Hilaria with sponsor, question and name. Osserman supports one emphasis: the knife's success depends on the sisters and the naming, not on the cut alone. No change.
- *Mourning the old likeness.* A possible after-rite event, *The Old Likeness*: an image of her former self is found. She may keep it sealed in the House book or burn it; both are honoured (pairs with `tsq_keywords_2014.md` row 7). **No regret, no reversal, no "mistake" framing**; Cavanagh notes regret after surgery is very low (p. 354 n. 6).
- *Lacan's imaginary and symbolic phallus.* **Not usable.**
- *Circumcision and foreskin restoration.* **Not usable**, and a hard caution for Ha-Nehpakhot (§5).

### Gherovici, "Depathologizing Trans: From Symptom to Sinthome" (pp. 534–555)

**Gist.** Gherovici argues against Millot that Lacan himself distinguished a delusion of becoming a woman from a real request for sex change. She retells two of Lacan's cases. Henri, raised a girl and abruptly ordered by his father at puberty to "make a choice" and live as a boy, never got to choose. Primeau, a man who felt he was turning into a woman, Lacan judged psychotic, but without the moralising that Millot later claimed. Millot reported Lacan calling such a patient "mon pauvre vieux" ("my poor old man") (p. 545). Gherovici reads transition as a *sinthome*: a creative knot that makes life livable, like Joyce's writing, often expressed by making a new name, a signature. Her analysands sum up transition as "I exist" (p. 552). Her central framing is transition as a matter of life and death, built on patients' statements about suicide; that framing is excluded.

**Translated into game terms.**
- *Henri: a sex imposed and then re-imposed by a father.* Confirms A9 (gender is consensual) and the Serano revision that Declare Before Witnesses is the daughter's own act. Vanilla parents and lords who "decide" a child's sex are the hostile side.
- *The name as signature.* A concrete, small proposal (§2 row 7): at naming, let the daughter choose a name that carries something of her own, a craft, a mother's name or a saint's, not only a feminised birth name. The faiths already have the machinery (`tm_feminise_name_effect`, `tm_give_daughter_name_effect`, Badenya's mother-name prefix).
- *"I exist."* Usable as a daughter's line at her naming (§3.2).
- *Sinthome, jouissance, Borromean knots.* **Not usable.**
- *Life and death.* **Not usable**, by the no-suicide rule.

### Stewart, "Transgender Subjectivity in Revolt" (pp. 577–607)

**Gist.** Stewart reads trans lives through Kristeva's "intimate revolt": a movement of return (questioning the past), rupture (breaking with an imposed sign) and rebirth (renewal), set against "precarity", the politically induced exposure of trans people to violence. The opening scene: a trans punk singer burns her birth certificate on stage and shouts "Goodbye, gender!" (p. 578). The Kristeva epigraph speaks of the time and space "to break off, remember, refashion" (p. 577). Stewart ends by praising liminality, being "not x and not y".

**Translated into game terms.**
- *Return, rupture, rebirth → a writing guide for gates.* Each faith's gate already has three beats; use these names as a check. Return: a look back at the old life (Badenya's claiming mother; the Garland's waking of the friends). Rupture: one object that is broken or given up. Rebirth: the name. Give each faith a **rupture object** of its own, in period: the father's name in the patronymic (Bædling; already `tm_feminise_name_effect`), the eunuch-register entry the Yutai Dao refuses, the worn garland (Garland), a burned or buried name-slip (generic). Do not use a birth certificate or any modern document.
- *Precarity.* Already in the mod as era decay, suppression and the Houses' strain. No change.
- *Liminality, "not x and not y".* **Not usable.** It is the third-thing framing Bhatt rejects (§5).
- *Intimate revolt over politics.* **Not usable** as doctrine. Bhatt: shame-fighting is not enough, "you also have to do the work". The faiths organise.

### Salah, "What Does Tiresias Want?" (pp. 632–638)

**Gist.** Tiresias changed sex twice. Asked by Zeus and Hera who has more pleasure, she said women, nine shares to one. Hera blinded her and Zeus, unable to undo it, gave her prophecy. Salah notes: "And people are always asking her about the other sex" (p. 632). The Tiresian is both a cis fantasy of transsexuality and a figure trans writers reclaim. Trans writing can talk back to psychoanalysis. In a note she adds that clinicians long distrusted trans patients for knowing the clinic's theories too well (p. 636 n. 6).

**Translated into game terms.**
- *The Tiresias question → an event.* At a court where a re-embodied daughter lives, a courtier asks her, as one who "has been both", which sex has the greater pleasure. Options: refuse ("I am no one's oracle"); answer like Tiresias (amuses the court, offends a powerful woman); turn it back on him. The faith's line reads the myth Bhatt's way: the god's gift of prophecy was payment for a blinding. "Veneration" is the price of an injury (§3.2).
- *Clinicians distrusting coached answers.* Gives Bhatt note row 5 (*Teach Her the Answers*) a counterweight: an outside examiner may suspect she was taught. A small risk on a successful tutoring, not a reason to drop it.
- **Verify before use.** Tiresias in Byzantine Greek sources (Apollodorus; Phlegon; the 12th-century scholars Tzetzes and Eustathius) before Adelphai Dorleyaiou uses it. Ovid is Latin and little read in Byzantium. Tiresias returns to manhood; like Ilā for the Garland, she is the "both and neither" figure the faiths do not take as a model.

## 4. What psychoanalysis cannot give the mod

Stated plainly, so no one builds it:
- The phallus, castration, jouissance, sexuation formulas, the real, the *sinthome*, the Borromean knot, the semiotic and the symbolic, the drives of gaze and voice as theory. None of these maps onto a CK3 mechanic. Only the concrete scenes and lists that the authors use (the bath, the bed, Lacan's list of imputed harms, Hansbury's four fears, the Tiresias myth) were translated above.
- "Transphobia as symptom" as an in-game diagnosis of any faith. Hostility is written as doctrine, not neurosis.
- Any therapy or "cure", for daughters or for their enemies.

## 5. Cautions

**Where essays conflict with Bhatt (the mod follows Bhatt).**
- *Elliot and Lyons.* They treat "radical lesbian feminism" as a block and explain its transphobia as anxiety. Bhatt is herself a radical lesbian feminist who rejects trans exclusion on political grounds, and she reads transmisogyny as structural. So: the mod never makes lesbian separatists, as a class, its hostile voice. Hostile voices are vanilla clergy, physicians and courts. The Houses are trans-lesbian-centred and that stays.
- *Stewart.* Praises liminality ("not x and not y") and an inner revolt over organising. Bhatt rejects the third thing and insists on doing the work. Use only the three-beat structure.
- *Dalla Torre on Mieli.* "The woman within every man" makes trans womanhood a hidden potential of all men, close to what Bhatt attacks as regendering and male fantasy. Mieli also celebrates being penetrated as liberation, where Bhatt reads penetrability as the axis of degradation. Not used.
- *The Lacanians in general* (Carlson, Gherovici, Gozlan). Their "sexual difference" is a universal structure of lack, not a social hierarchy. Bhatt's sex is material and hierarchical. Use their clinical stories, not their theory.
- *Cavanagh's note on Kristeva* (p. 353 n. 1). Kristeva spoke against "recomposed" genders. Never present a Kristevan idea as friendly authority in loc.

**Antisemitism.**
- Elliot and Lyons borrow Žižek's reading of anti-Semitism ("the Jew" as fantasised enemy) as a model for transphobia (p. 360). Do not import that analogy into game text. With a Jewish faith in the mod, it would set Jews and daughters up as interchangeable phantoms.
- Osserman's circumcision material must never touch Ha-Nehpakhot. Medieval Christian polemic linked circumcision to castration and feminisation; that belongs with the "Jewish male menses" tropes the mod already bans (`jud_male_menses_myth.md`).
- Row 3: no mikveh scene without a source.

**No suicide or self-harm content.** Excluded: Gherovici's life-and-death framing and her patients' statements; Henri's history (Gherovici, p. 543); the end of the Reimer case (Osserman, pp. 498, 508); the children's self-harm reports (Breslow, pp. 442–443); the Al-Kassim statement; the Wiggins piece; "The Danish Girl's Death Drive". Nothing from these reaches loc, even paraphrased.

**Trans men out of scope.** Not used: Offman's patient Sam; Gherovici's analysand Jay and her mastectomy case; Osserman's Mark Rees, David Reimer and Suchet's patient; Hansbury's cases of trans men. Hansbury and Salah are used only for their general arguments.

**Children.** Only the rule in row 11.

**Living people.** The issue names living analysts, writers and performers. None is used as a precedent or a speaker in loc. The trans singer in Stewart's opening scene is not named in loc.

**Period checks before use.** Tiresias in Byzantine sources (row 9); the Byzantine and Islamic bath customs for women (row 3).
