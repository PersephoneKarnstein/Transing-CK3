# Shaun Marmon, *Eunuchs and Sacred Boundaries in Islamic Society* (OUP 1995) — research dossier

Source file: `scratchpad/books/marmon_eunuchs_islamic.txt` (full text read, 6,836 lines: preface, 5 chapters, 321 endnotes, index). Page numbers below are the printed pages of the OUP edition as recovered from running heads in the text. Notes are cited as `n.NN (p. X)`.

## 0. Headline findings for the mod (read this first)

1. **Al-Jahiz is NOT in this book.** Neither "Jahiz", the "realm of male characteristics to that of females" statement, the *mukhannathun*, nor Islamic jurists' debates on hermaphrodites (*khuntha*) appear anywhere in Marmon's text. Marmon mentions "the hermaphrodite" only twice, in passing (p. 63; n.256 p. 145), each time deferring to Paula Sanders, "Gendering the Ungendered Body: Hermaphrodites in Medieval Islamic Law" (1991). The al-Jahiz passage must be chased through the works Marmon cites: **David Ayalon, "On the Eunuchs in Islam," *Jerusalem Studies in Arabic and Islam* 1 (1979): 67–124** (Marmon's n.3, n.109, n.255 — she quotes from it the proverb "the eunuch does not procreate [*khadim la yakun lahu walad*]", p. 87 / n.255 p. 145) and Ayalon's "The Eunuchs in the Mamluk Sultanate" (1977). For *mukhannathun*: **Everett Rowson, "The Categorization of Gender and Sexual Irregularity in Medieval Arabic Vice Lists," in *Body Guards* (1991), pp. 50–79** (Marmon n.20, p. 119). So the second-hand al-Jahiz anchor remains second-hand after this book.
2. What Marmon **does** give that is directly usable: (a) a 14th-c. jurist (Taj al-Din al-Subki, d. 1370) who classes eunuchs as a distinct *ta'ifa* whose "office is inscribed on their flesh", who are "like women" in reason, and who imagine themselves "a man" among women and "a woman" among men (pp. 62–63); (b) a 12th–20th-c. **eunuch priesthood** at the Prophet's tomb in Medina with its own rules (*qawanin*), customs, ranks, costume, quarter, novitiate, elected/appointed chief, endowed lifetime posts and enormous waqf wealth (pp. 14, 52–53, 60–61, 105, 110); (c) the explicit **non-biological succession** of eunuch offices and households: tomb-guardian posts pass "to eunuchs", not to sons (pp. 87–88), eunuchs purchase and manumit boys who inherit their posts (pp. 71–72, 105), and novices live "with their elder brethren ... a few years and then set up their own establishments" (p. 105); (d) the eunuchs' entry into the holy service described as a *hijra*, a rebirth that "turn[s] away from the honor of the service of kings" (p. 60); (e) a Hanafi position that even *employing* eunuchs is reprehensible because it "abets castration, which is prohibited" (p. 63); (f) a case where a non-eunuch may hold a eunuch's post only if he lives celibate and becomes a "functional eunuch" (p. 26).
3. Nothing in the book supports penal castration (belief n) — Islamic law forbids castration outright and Marmon never mentions castration as punishment. The sponsor/novitiate system (belief o) is, by contrast, well attested (pp. 71–72, 105).

## 1. Book map

**Preface (pp. ix–x).** States the thesis: eunuchs are "a category of nongendered individuals who both defined and crossed highly charged boundaries of moral and physical space in the world of the living and in the world of the dead" (p. ix). The subject is "the representation of eunuchs in the context of the sacred", centred on Cairo and Medina; the eunuchs themselves "maintain a dignified silence" (p. x). As of 1990, 17 eunuchs served at Medina and 14 at Mecca (p. ix).

**Ch. 1, "Cairo: Eunuchs and Sacred Boundaries" (pp. 3–30).** Uses al-Ghuzuli's literary "house" (eunuchs placed in the vestibule, *dihliz*) to argue that eunuchs are stationed at thresholds. Describes the two eunuch officers of the Mamluk household — the *zimam* over the women and the *muqaddam al-mamalik* over adolescent military cadets — and the Cairo Citadel as a sanctuary (*haram*) with the sultan as its "shrine". Then surveys Mamluk waqf deeds endowing eunuch guardians in royal and elite tombs, reading tombs as replicas of the household and of the Prophet's tomb at Medina.

**Ch. 2, "Madina: Sultan and Prophet" (pp. 31–53).** Reconstructs the contested origins of the "eunuchs of the Prophet" (founded by Nur al-Din or Salah al-Din, 12th c.; 24 then 40 endowed posts funded by the Egyptian villages Naqqada and Sindbis). Traces the architectural multiplication of barriers around the Prophet's tomb (the 'A'isha chamber, 'Umar ibn 'Abd al-'Aziz's hexagon, Baybars' *maqsura* of 1269, closure of the doors in 1477) and argues the eunuchs became "a kind of eunuch priesthood" controlling the interior corridor, the *kiswa* and the lamps.

**Ch. 3, "More Exalted Than the Service of Kings" (pp. 55–77).** The "row of the eunuchs" formed every Friday between the Sunni preacher and the stone-throwing Shi'i congregation (late 13th c.). The hagiographic image of the eunuchs in Ibn Farhun, al-Firuzabadi and al-Sakhawi: dreadful (*hayba*), ascetic, lavishly charitable, "like a kind father and a compassionate mother". Contains the book's key legal passage: Taj al-Din al-Subki's chapter on eunuchs (three anatomical kinds, gaze rules, gender indeterminacy, Abu Hanifa's disapproval). Ends with the Cairo–Medina "eunuch network" of waqf endowments for "unemployed" (exiled) eunuchs and for eunuch freedmen.

**Ch. 4, "Eunuchs, Children, and Time" (pp. 79–92).** The eunuch society is an admitted innovation never called *bid'a*; it is legitimated by spatial association with the Companions. Eunuchs are "outside time": they never pass through *bulugh* (puberty), are *rashid* (competent) but never *kamil* (complete), do not procreate, and so their offices pass "to eunuchs" rather than to sons. Compares eunuchs and prepubescent children as neutral intermediaries; describes the blindfolded changing of the tomb's *kiswa* and a layman's temporary robing as a eunuch.

**Ch. 5, "The Longue Durée of the Eunuchs of the Prophet" (pp. 93–112).** European travellers (Wild 1607, Pitts c.1680, Niebuhr, Burckhardt 1811–14, Burton 1853, Keane 1881, Snouck Hurgronje, Rutter 1925) and their explanations of eunuch guardians; the hand-kissing, horses, title "Your Highness"; the novitiate houses of Mecca and Medina; the Wahhabi expulsions of 1804 and the 1925 desacralisation; a 1990 Saudi interview reducing the eunuchs to "sexual police" who lack *shahwa*.

**Notes (pp. 113–150).** Rich: n.16 (al-Suyuti's eunuch made a bean-seller as an insult to his *jins*), n.24 (eunuch Kafur called "more of a man" than an amir), n.37 (1583 Ottoman firman that Hebron tomb posts go to eunuchs "as has been customary since olden times"), n.48 & n.103 (eunuchs were the one slave group not defined by skin colour), n.118 (a non-eunuch chief eunuch praised as "the most like them in his ways"), n.256 (Artemis/Atalanta comparison; eunuch vs hermaphrodite vs voluntary celibate), n.275 (clinical effects of prepubertal castration), n.306 (boys castrated outside the Dar al-Islam, "a provision that, with few exceptions, seems to have been observed").

## 2. Gender and boundary dossier

### 2.1 Marmon's thesis: eunuchs as makers and crossers of sacred boundaries

- "eunuchs, a category of nongendered individuals who both defined and crossed highly charged boundaries of moral and physical space in the world of the living and in the world of the dead." (Preface, p. ix)
- Yahya ibn Khalid on the vestibule: "It is the place where the eunuchs sit at ease, and it is the furthest limit of the boundary for one who asks permission [to enter]." (p. 4, quoting al-Ghuzuli)
- "Such eunuchs were not only the guardians of the harim, themselves physical symbols, like the veil, of the ideals of domestic seclusion; they were also neutral emissaries in a moral universe highly charged with sexual tension, a universe in which the forces of fitna ... were seen as an omnipresent threat to the social and moral order." (pp. 5–6)
- The *harim* root: "A religious sanctuary, where no blood can be shed, such as the holy cities of Mecca and Madina, is a haram. ... the adult male, other than a husband, who is allowed to see a woman unveiled, to cross the boundaries of the harim, is one who is paradoxically referred to as mahram, 'forbidden,' because he is, by virtue of consanguinity, 'forbidden' sexually and thus 'permitted' morally and spatially." (p. 6)
- Marmon notes the word *harim* "as an architectural term was never gendered by Mamluk lexicographers as 'women's quarters' but was defined inclusively as 'the harim of a dwelling: the interior [of the dwelling] once its door has been closed.'" (p. 6; cf. n.13 pp. 116–17 — she found no Mamluk term for gendered quarters like Ottoman *haremlik/selamlik*).
- "Despite their frequent presentation as guardians of social and sexual order, they were paradoxically in their very persons the embodiment of social and sexual ambiguity. It is not surprising that the ambiguous, even morally perilous task of violating the vestibule, in the case of confiscations, should fall to eunuchs." (p. 8)
- The Citadel as sanctuary: the eunuch *zimamdar* Kafur refused rebel amirs entry "on the grounds that 'the harim of the sultan is present in the Citadel'" and "had, as it were, pushed the dihliz to the gates of the Citadel" (pp. 9–10). "The sultan was, in and of himself, a kind of sanctuary, and the eunuchs who surrounded him not only controlled access to his person and his family but also served to emphasize the sacred power of rulership." (pp. 12–13)
- The two-family model: the *zimamdar* over the women's Noble Dwellings behind "the Gate of the Veil (Bab al-Sitara)" and the *muqaddam al-mamalik* over cadets living "in a condition of seclusion which, in theory, was not unlike that of a harim" (p. 11). "Just as the zimamdar must guard against sexual transgressions by or against the women of the household, the muqaddam has similar responsibilities for adolescent boys, who, like women, are portrayed as being potential sources of fitna, or sexual temptation and disorder." (p. 12) Both must have "hamiyya, an ardent, almost fanatical passion in the pursuit of their duties as well as zeal (ghayra) in safeguarding their charges" (p. 12).
- Concentric sanctuary model (inner shrine / outer haram) applied to the Prophet's tomb, the Citadel, and Mamluk tomb complexes (pp. 13, 16).
- "the power of the eunuchs both to establish sacred boundaries and to cross those boundaries safely extended beyond the world of the living into the world of the dead." (pp. 14–15)
- On the tomb: "Not only did they seem to be able to cross over with relative ease into this holy space, which is so highly charged with baraka that entry was potentially lethal for most humans, but they could on occasion provide a kind of safe conduct for ordinary Muslims." (p. 47)
- Al-Sakhawi: the eunuchs' right to spend the night (*mabit*) inside the sanctuary "was the original reason for their [the eunuchs'] creation" (p. 43). Marmon: "it was also appropriate that the people who would spend the night adjacent to the Prophet ... would belong to that category of beings who were physically constructed to act as mediators of boundaries." (p. 43)
- "The eunuchs, who as a category (ta'ifa) embodied both ambiguity and mediation, thus forced the antagonists into a common moral construct that went far deeper than their political and doctrinal differences." (p. 60)
- "it was the quality of being a eunuch—of belonging to the ta'ifa of ambiguity—which best enabled them to fulfill their role as intercessors before the ultimate Intercessor, the Prophet." (p. 85)
- "Just as the eunuch moved without danger between the world of men and the world of women, the very young child, as yet not 'complete' (baligh) in his or her sexuality, could move between different spheres of physical and moral space" (p. 90).

### 2.2 How jurists and writers gendered eunuchs

**Taj al-Din 'Abd al-Wahhab al-Subki (727/1327–771/1370), *Kitab mu'id al-ni'am wa-mubid al-niqam*, pp. 54–56 — the book's central legal text (Marmon pp. 61–63):**
- Al-Subki divides society into offices (*waza'if*) and categories (*tawa'if*). "Since his concern is with individual responsibility and piety within these social categories, women, whom al-Subki characterizes as 'lacking in reason and religion,' and young children are by definition excluded." (p. 61)
- "Eunuchs, with the clear implication that 'eunuchness' is itself an office, are included in a separate chapter in the section of the book that deals with palace offices. The eunuchs, however, are themselves divided into subgroups according to office: the zimam, who is concerned with women, and the muqaddam al-mamalik, 'who has authority over adolescent boys.' Both the zimam and the muqaddam are linked together as one ta'ifa, that of the eunuchs (tawashiya)." (p. 62)
- **Three anatomical kinds:** "Al-Subki gives us three possible embodiments of the eunuch: the mamsuh, whose penis and testicles have been removed; the khasi, whose testicles alone have been removed; and the majbub, whose penis has been removed but whose testicles have been left intact." (p. 62)
- **Gaze rules:** "Al-Subki 'and most of our colleagues,' he tells us, agree that this [looking at *ajnabiyyat*, 'strange women'] is permitted for the mamsuh, the 'smooth' eunuch, but not for the khasi or the majbub. For looking on a woman who owns him, or whose husband owns him, any eunuch, al-Subki argues, is more acceptable than an uncastrated slave, rebuking those jurists who argue that the gaze of an uncastrated slave upon his mistress is permitted: 'How can it be permitted for handsome mamluks who inspire fitna with their beauty to look upon their mistresses when women are "lacking in reason and religion"!' The gaze of the women themselves is addressed in one line in which al-Subki cites the opinion of Malik ibn Anas, the founder of the Maliki school of jurisprudence, that a woman should look on a eunuch only if he is owned by her or her husband." (p. 62)
- **Flesh as office:** "What is striking about al-Subki's discussion of the eunuchs is that they are the only category of human beings in his social scheme whose office (wazifa) is inscribed on their flesh. His own doubts about how to gender the eunuchs, given the indeterminate nature of their bodies, are reflected in his discussion of their moral qualities." (pp. 62–63)
- **Indeterminate self-gendering (the closest thing in the book to the al-Jahiz idea):** "Al-Subki tells us that the devoted obedience of the eunuch to the one he serves is well known. But this same quality has its negative side, for it arises from the eunuch's lack of rational ability (like that of women) and their general similarity to women. But the eunuch's own identity is indeterminate, for 'when he mixes with women, he tells himself that he is a man, when he is with men, he tells himself that he is a woman.' The final list of qualities that al-Subki reports is a combination of both 'male' and 'female' attributes: 'It is said that eunuchs are the harshest people in their jealousy and the most distinguished in both the kindness and the firm leadership which they display to the one who is under their authority, whether a woman or a mamluk.'" (p. 63) Marmon adds that al-Subki is "quoting a proverb that appears in normative collections of prophetic traditions and in the works of Arabic belletrists" (p. 64) — i.e., the "man among women, woman among men" saying circulated as a hadith-collection proverb and in adab (this is probably where al-Jahiz would be found).
- **Made not born; castration forbidden:** "The eunuch, after all, unlike the hermaphrodite, is made, not born. This 'making,' the act of castration, was and is forbidden under Islamic law. Slave traders, at least in theory, castrated young boys on the borders of the Islamic realm before importing them. This legal fiction, however, was still troubling to some pious scholars. Al-Subki ends his chapter with the statement that one can find in the books of the Hanafi legal school that its founder, Abu Hanifa, declared the taking of eunuchs into one's service reprehensible (yukrah), not forbidden (yuhram), because making use of eunuchs was 'an abetment of castration, which is [a] prohibited [act].'" (p. 63)

**Al-Qalqashandi (d. 1418), *Subh al-a'sha* 12:260–62:** "It has been the custom [qad jara al-'ada] that this office should go to a eunuch [khadim] from among the castrated males [min al-khisyan] who are referred to as tawashiya, meaning by that 'those from the doors of the sultan.'" (p. 42). The diploma of investiture for the chief eunuch calls him "one of those who are brought close" (*min al-muqarrabin*) (p. 84) and places him among the Companions, *muhajirun* and *ansar* (pp. 80–81).

**Hagiographers (Ibn Farhun d. 1372, al-Firuzabadi, al-Sakhawi):**
- "Within the sacred space of the haram of Madina, the eunuch is also given both male and female qualities. He is 'like a kind father and a compassionate mother to the [Sunni] mujawirun.' In the context of the Sunni-Shi'i struggle, the eunuch is also a figure of power, endowed with a very 'masculine' ability to inspire fear." (p. 64)
- "Neither man nor woman, the eunuch of the Prophet unites the tender qualities of both the male and the female parent since he behaves 'like a fond mother and a compassionate father' to the Sunni immigrants." (p. 67)
- Eunuchs described as *jamil*, *wasim*, *ahsan*, *akmal*; "The eunuch Shafi' 'was a [true] eunuch in his appearance and height. He was the greatest of his race in his ability to inspire dread and his authority.'" (p. 66)
- "Eunuchs generally were considered to be frail creatures, but this frailty did not preclude their use of physical violence. ... their neutrality endows them with power and with an immunity from physical threats. In their role as the ceremonial guardians of the tomb, the eunuchs often struck individuals whom they considered to have offended the sacred honor of the Prophet, and who, for the most part, dared not return the blows." (p. 40)
- Ibn Taghri Birdi on the eunuch Kafur who held the Citadel gate: he was "'more of a man' (arjal) than Arghun, the commander of the royal stables" who abandoned his post (n.24, p. 119).
- A non-eunuch who held the chief eunuch's post, Shahin al-Jamali, was praised by al-Sakhawi: "He was the most like them [the eunuchs] in his ways" (n.118, p. 135). The appointment of an uncastrated man (*fahl*) to the office in 1435 was "a violation of sunna" and "a great misfortune and a trial" (al-Maqrizi, pp. 42–43).

**The life-cycle argument (pp. 86–87):** "Islamic law divides the life cycle ... The most important moment of the cycle by far is puberty, or bulugh (lit. 'readiness,' 'ripeness'), the physical transformation of the child into a 'complete man' (rajul kamil) or a 'complete woman' (mar'a kamila), that is, a person with a set sexual identity ... The eunuch, castrated before adolescence, would never go through the change of bulugh. Because Islamic law ... also recognized another standard of competence, intellectual maturity (rushd), the free adult eunuch was not condemned to a perpetual state of legal infancy but possessed full legal capacity in most areas of the law. In strictly legal terms a eunuch could thus be rashid (mentally competent), but, on a more profound level, he could never be kamil (complete)." Then: "The eunuchs are outside this process, for 'the eunuch does not procreate [khadim la yakun lahu walad].' Eunuchs are thus, in a sense, outside of time itself." (p. 87)

**Eunuch vs hermaphrodite vs celibate (n.256, p. 145):** "Unlike Atalanta, or any human child, the eunuchs, by virtue of their castration, were removed from the normal path that leads across the boundaries between childhood and gendered adulthood. And unlike the hermaphrodite (see Sanders, 'Gendering the Ungendered Body'), whose very existence confuses those boundaries, or the voluntary celibate, who defies them, the eunuch did not violate the natural process of maturation since he was completely outside of it."

**Eunuchs transcend skin-colour categories (n.48, p. 126; n.103, p. 133):** "The eunuchs were in fact the only group of slaves in Mamluk Egypt whose careers were not determined by categories of color and who seemed, by virtue of being eunuchs, to transcend this boundary." "Unlike fuhul (uncastrated male) slaves, eunuchs were defined not primarily in terms of (perceived) ethnicity and/or skin color but in terms of their eunuchness." Eunuchs regarded themselves as a *jins* (kind): al-Suyuti's making his freed eunuch a bean-seller was "an act the other eunuchs see clearly as an intended insult to their kind (jins)" (n.16, p. 117).

**European/modern gendering:** Rutter (1925): eunuchs police women in the Meccan sanctuary because "they are not really men in the full sense of the word" (p. 95) — Marmon notes this sexual explanation "does not appear in Arabic descriptions of the eunuch societies of Mecca and Madina until the late 1970s" (p. 95). Pitts c.1660: "They usually grow to a great stature, have an effeminate voice and never have any hair grow on their faces." (p. 95). Burton: "this high-shouldered, spindle-shanked, beardless bit of neutrality" (p. 100); "Disconnected with humanity, he is cruel, fierce, brave, and capable of any villainy" (p. 100). 1990 Saudi official Salim Farid: "God has deprived them of sensual pleasure in the world but he has enriched them with material possessions and, before that, with the honor of serving the Sacred House"; their defining trait is "their lack of shahwa, or sexual desire" (p. 111). Marmon's verdict: "Their modern representation narrowly defines their role as that of sexual police—neutered guardians of morality." (p. 112)

### 2.3 Eunuchs and the women's quarters; eunuchs as angels / liminal

- Al-Subki on the *zimam*: "Among the eunuchs is the zimam. He is the one who is concerned with women. It is his duty and his right to cast his eyes upon their affairs and to advise the master of the house [concerning them]. He must inform him [the master] of any suspicion which he himself is unable to clear, and he must prevent agents of debauchery such as old women and others from gaining access to the women [of the household]." (p. 5)
- The dead *khawand* Tatar al-Hijaziyya's chief eunuch beat a Qur'an reader for allegedly entering her tomb without under-trousers: "You dare to enter the presence of a khawand without sarawil!" (p. 22). Marmon: "Tatar in death still participated in the same social and sexual mores that had maintained her honor and prestige in her lifetime." (p. 23)
- The Garden (*rawda*) of the Prophet's mosque: "by day a place of prayer and study, at night the special provenance of the eunuchs and hosts of invisible angels." (p. 46) In Keane's version of the tomb legend, the child who enters the tomb finds "the Prophet with the Koran on his knees, and an angel on each side of him reading to him. The angel on the right of the Prophet rose as he entered and introduced himself as Gabriel" (pp. 88–89).
- Eunuch/child as neutral intermediary: "Like the magical 'drei Knaben' of Mozart's Magic Flute, who act as neutral messengers between the dangerous and disorderly female world of the Queen of the Night and the sunlit, rational male world of Sarastro, the eunuch/child is an intermediate being, safe in both worlds and belonging to neither." (p. 90)
- Snouck Hurgronje on sinless prepubescent boys: "he may be adorned with gold and silver, the use of which for this purpose is forbidden to men, and the festivities on certain occasions in the life of boys are very like those in the life of women." (p. 89) Small boys in 19th-c. Mecca "might be adorned with jewelry, even dressed in 'female' clothes" against the evil eye (p. 90).
- Ottoman palace eunuchs and children: eunuchs "always nourished a great love and adoration for them ... whom they would call bizim kilar! (One belonging to us!) Musbah never quite knew whether they meant belonging to the African race or to the odd community of eunuchs!" (n.264, p. 146)

### 2.4 Prayer, veiling and purity rules touching eunuchs

- Veiling in general: Ibn Taymiyya: women must be "both protected and cordoned off" and "veiling with clothing and houses [al-istitar bi'l-libas wa'l-buyut]" imposed because "the appearance of women [in public] is a cause of fitna and men are set over them" (p. 8). Women past childbearing "no longer desired for marriage" were exempt from some veiling but still suspect as "agents of debauchery" (pp. 8–9; n.21 p. 119).
- Women were unveiled only at funerals and at the tombs; at the Qarafa they "would uncover their faces ... join together in singing and dancing ... hire their own female preacher (wa'iza), laugh out loud and flirt with young boys" (p. 24).
- 'A'isha "would not appear unveiled even before the dead 'Umar, since he was neither her husband nor her father" and walled off the graves (p. 46).
- Purity before entering the tomb: the Sufi 'Umar al-Nasa'i (1153) at first refused "on the grounds that he suffered from an illness that kept him in an almost constant state of ritual impurity", fasted a week and was miraculously cured for the duration (p. 47). Marmon: the preparations "have the tone of compensatory rites for the violation of a boundary. Within a few years the eunuchs had become the guardians of that boundary." (p. 47)
- **Prayer inside the enclosure:** after Baybars' *maqsura* (1269) "prayer was apparently forbidden inside the maqsura to everyone—except the eunuchs." (p. 52) After 1477 "the doors are not opened at all except at the time of the lighting of lamps and then only some of the eunuchs and the janitors enter or someone of high rank with the permission of the chief eunuch." (al-Samhudi, p. 53)
- **Eunuch costume and robing of outsiders:** Sulayman Faydi (1920) was clothed in "the voluminous wide-sleeved white ceremonial robe of the eunuchs of the Prophet", told to remove his *tarbush* and wear a white turban brought by a child under ten, and "clad in the eunuch's robe and the child's turban, silently followed the eunuchs into the corridor of the hujra" (pp. 89–90). The Khedive 'Abbas Hilmi II (1909) likewise "reverently don[ned] the costume of the eunuchs of the Prophet to participate in the evening lighting of the lamps" (p. 103).
- **Blindfolded kiswa ritual:** "Only the very old ones from among them enter, and they blindfold their eyes lest they see anything, and they hang up the new curtain. ... he who is wealthy among those who go in ... manumits three or four slaves—and some manumit even more—in gratitude" (Katib 'Ali Efendi, p. 91). Burton was told anyone who looked on the tomb "would be blinded by the supernatural light" (p. 94).
- Nightly rite: "the lighting of lamps (tasrij) and the evening circumambulation (tawaf) of the mosque by the aghas to expel all ordinary believers from the sanctuary had constituted the central ritual of the eunuch society of Madina for almost eight hundred years" (p. 93).

### 2.5 Marriage and eunuchs

Marmon has almost nothing on eunuchs marrying. Relevant data: cadets leaving the Citadel barracks "were often married to young women who had been raised and educated as slaves within the sultan's harim" (p. 12) — eunuchs arranged, rather than entered, marriages. Eunuchs maintained "large extended households of slaves, freedmen, freedwomen, and clients" (p. 61) and manumitted "male and female" slaves (p. 68), but no spouse is ever mentioned. A tomb-guardian post could be held by a non-eunuch only "with no residence of women" (p. 26). The Shi'i judge's monopoly on confirming Sunni marriage contracts (p. 57) is about sectarian control, not eunuchs.

## 3. Castration and supply dossier

### 3.1 The prohibition and the border fiction
- "This 'making,' the act of castration, was and is forbidden under Islamic law. Slave traders, at least in theory, castrated young boys on the borders of the Islamic realm before importing them. This legal fiction, however, was still troubling to some pious scholars." (p. 63) Abu Hanifa held using eunuchs *yukrah* (reprehensible) as "an abetment of castration, which is [a] prohibited [act]." (p. 63)
- n.306 (p. 150): "It would also have been contrary to Islamic law for the boys to be castrated within the Dar al-Islam—a provision that, with few exceptions, seems to have been observed."
- Rutter (1925): African boys "were castrated before being exported to the Hijaz 'as owing to the hazardous nature of the mutilation, the chief of the Aghas will not purchase them until they have safely undergone it.'" (p. 105)
- Snouck Hurgronje: castration was "this great abuse condemned even by Islam but still maintained. ... All are imported already castrated, but the demand for this article for the mosque makes Mekka an accomplice in this evil. Among the aghas ... are found Nubians, negroes, Abyssinians, often strongly built, but seldom amiable people." (pp. 98–99)
- Blame-shifting to outsiders: Lady Evelyn Cobbold (1934) blamed "the villainous Umayyads and ... the Christian Greeks, 'who practised the horrible trade of mutilation and trafficked in the victims of their greed'" (p. 107); Rafi' (1979/81) claimed castration was "inherited from foreigners" and that "the Ottomans learned [it] from the Greeks when they conquered Constantinople" (p. 110); Salim Farid (1990) blamed "Italian soldiers in Mussolini's army who made a habit of castrating Ethiopian captives" (p. 111). Marmon does **not** discuss Verdun, Prague, or Upper Egyptian monasteries; the supply geography must come from Ayalon.

### 3.2 Sources of eunuchs (ethnicity as perceived by the sources)
- Ibn Jubayr (1184): "The guardians are Ethiopian and Slavic eunuchs. They present an elegant appearance and are meticulous in their clothing and bearing." (p. 33; n.81 p. 130 notes *saqaliba* is ambiguous and that North Africans used *fityan*, "young men", as a euphemism for eunuchs.)
- Ibn Battuta (1326): "eunuchs from among the Ethiopians and such like" (p. 34).
- Al-Sakhawi (1490s): "forty or more eunuchs ... from several ethnic groups: Hindi (i.e., from the Indian subcontinent); Takruri (West African); Rumi (Greek or Balkan, possibly from the non-Muslim frontiers of Anatolia); and Habashi (Ethiopian or East African). The Hindi, he says, make up the majority in his own time." (p. 39)
- Ilyas al-Madani's (d. 1721) version of the founding: "They were to be Habashis, and if there were no Habashis then Rumis, then Takruris, then Hindis." (p. 44)
- Zayn al-Din Yahya's deed (1460) ranks "the most righteous of the eunuchs of the white race in particular", then "the Ethiopians and the blacks", then white uncastrated freedmen (pp. 25–26). Mamluk names: Muqbil al-Rusi ("the Russian", p. 69), Khushqadam al-Rumi, Aytimur al-Rumi, Iyas al-Ashrafi ("a white eunuch", n.118), 'Abd al-Latif al-Qiftjaqi (Qipchaq), Shahin & Sunqur al-Jamali (*rumi al-jins*, "enslaved together as children and sold along with their mother", n.118 p. 135), Rashid al-Hindi, Hasan Agha al-Bukhari ("an oblate sent to the mosque by one of the amirs of Bukhara", p. 103), a "Takruri (West African) eunuch" given to al-Suyuti by grateful West African pilgrims (p. 7; n.16).
- Ottoman period: after 1586 the *kizlaragasi* (chief black eunuch of Topkapi) controlled the Haramayn endowments and "endowed positions in the eunuch societies of the holy cities were increasingly used as retirement posts and/or honorable exile for black eunuchs from the Ottoman harem" (n.103, p. 133). Burckhardt 1814: "most of the eunuchs, or Towashye, are negroes; a few were copper-colored Indians." (n.103)

### 3.3 Age, technique, mortality, physique
- Castration followed enslavement in childhood: "For the eunuch, however, the 'death' of enslavement suffered as a child was succeeded almost immediately by castration. If he survived the physical trauma of the operation, he would enter the Islamic world as a member of an unusual category, removed from the normal cycle of human maturation and deprived of a biological future." (p. 86)
- "If he survived the painful urinary tract infections from which eunuchs often suffered, he would grow old as all men grow old. ... His oddly distorted body, warped by hormonal deprivation, would not age like the bodies of uncastrated men but would only become more wasted and cadaverous with time." (p. 87)
- Clinical note (n.275, p. 147, quoting Money & Ehrhardt): "The eunuch retains a soprano voice and grows no beard. His arms and legs grow disproportionately long, relative to the trunk. Pubic and axillary hair grows ... but, along with body hair, is feminine in pattern. There is no masculine balding. Subcutaneous fat deposits are feminine in distribution."
- Three anatomical categories (*mamsuh* / *khasi* / *majbub*), p. 62; Johann Wild (1607) saw eunuchs who were "entirely castrated [die sind ganz verschnitten] and have no shame [of their condition]" (p. 96).
- Burckhardt (1811): "The black eunuchs, unlike those of Europe, become emaciated; their features are extremely coarse, nothing but the bones being distinguishable; their hands are those of a skeleton" (p. 95). Mamluk biographers, by contrast, call eunuchs *jamil*, *wasim*, *akmal* (p. 66).

### 3.4 Prices and incomes
- Khushqadam al-Rumi (d. 1435) set aside "forty gold florins (ifluriyat) to purchase a eunuch for the service of the mosque" and forty florins a year for his salary (p. 72).
- Tomb eunuchs: six at Qalawun's tomb at "fifty dirhams each" per month (n.43, p. 125); three at Barquq's tomb at "ten dirhams ... a month in addition to what they receive from their appointment as Sufis" (p. 23); Shahin al-Hasani's tomb eunuch "one hundred dirhams a month" (p. 24); 'Abd al-Latif min Anisbay 875 dirhams a year to an unemployed eunuch in Medina (p. 70); Khushqadam 12,000 dirhams a year to "the working and the battalin" (p. 71); 'Anbar 2,000 dirhams a year (pp. 71–72).
- Wealth: Dinar al-Mu'izzi lost 25,000 dinars to a mismanaging friend and forgave it (p. 68); the 1804 Wahhabi fine on the chief eunuch was "the value of three thousand francs" (p. 106); Sawwab's feasts had dishes "the likes of which are only found on the tables of great kings" (p. 68).

### 3.5 Recruitment and training of the tomb corps
- Mamluk era: "most of the eunuchs came to 'the noble service' of the Prophet in their mature years after a long tenure in the Cairo Citadel, 'at the doors of the sultan.'" They were "learned and devout individuals, reciters of the Qur'an, students of jurisprudence and prophetic tradition (hadith)" and "most certainly multilingual" (p. 59). Yaqut al-Khazindari "had served in the Cairo Citadel for over thirty years before his appointment as chief eunuch of Madina" (p. 65).
- Conditions of appointment (Ilyas al-Madani): "that they be reciters of the Book of God and people of ritual piety" (p. 44). Diploma: the chief eunuch is "to enjoin every individual from among the ta'ifa of the eunuchs to that which will bring him closer to God in service ... to guide the one who departs from the laws of the service (qawanin al-khidma) to the right path and to be an example to them by the decorum (adab) of his [own] path" (p. 60).
- **Novitiate (sponsor/mentor system):** Burckhardt (1811, Mecca): "some twenty boys who 'live together in a house, till they are sufficiently instructed to be given in charge to their elder brethren, with whom they remain a few years and then set up their own establishments.'" (p. 105). Rutter (1925, Medina): "they had a number of little black boys in their houses who must have been recently purchased by or presented to them. These youths spent their time in study and in serving the elders, preparing to enter the Mosque service when they should arrive at years of discretion." (p. 105). Mecca: "the young boys live together in a large house, where they are instructed by the elders in religious matters and in their proper duties" in the quarter of al-Hajla (p. 105).
- Oblates: Khushqadam's purchased eunuch "was a true oblate. He 'affiliated to the Prophet's threshold' without knowing any other ties of clientage." (p. 72) 'Izz al-Din Dinar: "from among his thirty freed people, male and female, 'seven of his eunuchs tended the lamps in the hujra.'" (p. 71) 'Abd al-Latif al-Qiftjaqi funded his eunuch freedman 'Anbar on condition he "stay a resident in Madina" (pp. 71–72).
- Tomb guardians in Cairo: "the initial cohort of eunuchs was to be drawn from the freed people of the tomb's founder and were to be buried in it" (pp. 21–22); Sultan Hasan's deed: recruit from his, his children's or his father's freedmen; "If no eunuchs in these categories could be found, the administrator of the endowment was to buy eunuch slaves and manumit them in the name of Sultan Hasan." (p. 22)
- Chief eunuch: at Medina appointed from Cairo/Istanbul, and until the mid-19th c. also *shaykh al-haram*, "one of the most powerful in Madina, rivaling even that of the amir of the city" (p. 42); at Mecca "the agha al-tawashiya was elected to his post by his fellow eunuchs" (p. 101). Mecca's eunuchs had "a shaykh from among themselves and a deputy [naqib] and ranks in the service" (Rafi', p. 110); they divided into two lamp-lighting "teams", "Naqqada" and "Sindbis" (p. 39).

### 3.6 Manumission, property, households, "families" of eunuchs
- "By the time the eunuchs moved to Madina, they were themselves freedmen, often wealthy older individuals with a long career in the Cairo Citadel. Beyond their household of origin (the household of their manumitter), they could potentially command other networks: royal women, Mamluk officers whom they had trained in the barracks, their own freed slaves and clients in Cairo, and, most important, other eunuchs. The holy ta'ifa to which they now belonged ... was still a part of the broader ta'ifa of all eunuchs." (p. 61)
- "In Madina the eunuchs lived in their own quarter and maintained large extended households of slaves, freedmen, freedwomen, and clients." (p. 61) The *farrashun* (non-eunuch servitors) included "freed slaves of the eunuchs" (p. 28).
- Dinar supported widows and orphans "so that they were numbered among the members of his household" (p. 67); eunuchs "purchase, raise, and manumit male and female slaves; and take on dependents beyond their own freed people" (p. 68). Dinar al-Murshidi manumitted "thirty slaves, 'male and female, black and white.'" (p. 68)
- Quasi-paternity: Kafur al-Hariri had "raised" (*tarbiya*) the two most powerful amirs of the empire, who treated him "with devoted friendship [muwala]"; "to whom, as young boys, he had stood in the role of father and protector" (pp. 75–76).
- Waqf as eunuch-to-eunuch inheritance: Marmon reads the Cairo deeds as revealing "a loyalty defined by membership in the peculiar category of eunuch, a loyalty that implies a shared sense of difference which coexists with and sometimes transcends other loyalties" (p. 69). Deeds direct money "to the lords, the eunuchs dwelling in Noble Madina" (Muqbil al-Rusi, p. 71), to "the amirs, the poor exiled eunuchs" (Sawwab al-Khawarazmi, p. 71), and if a named eunuch dies or leaves, "to two eunuchs ... who have no stipend" (p. 72).
- Offices pass to eunuchs not sons: "In most endowment deeds for tomb complexes ... the positions are generally assigned to specific individuals with the provision that on their death the offices are to pass to their male children. In the case of eunuch guardians, however, there is a dramatic break. The initial guardians are generally designated, but, after their deaths, the offices simply go to 'eunuchs.'" (pp. 87–88)
- Niebuhr's theory (eunuchs guard treasure because childless) rebutted: "The absence of offspring did not ... guarantee fiscal honesty, especially in a society in which the household and family were not defined by biological ties alone." (p. 97)
- Ottoman royal eunuchs in 1924 "pooled their funds ... to buy a house in Istanbul—a kind of eunuch pension where they lived out their remaining years in communal obscurity" (p. 109).
- Rafi' (1981): "many have also bought property and put it into waqf for them so that they have come to have an abundance of wealth beyond that which is assigned to them by way of salary from the government" (p. 110). Medina's eunuchs in 1990 "control their own funds and have no separate official in charge of their affairs" (p. 111).

## 6. Terminology and names

### 6.1 Arabic/Turkish terms (with Marmon's glosses)
- **khadim / khuddam / khadam** — lit. "servant"; "a common euphemism for 'eunuch,' the word khasi (castrated one) being rarely used, and was current as early as the ninth century. By the Mamluk period it was the most common term for 'eunuch,' followed only by tawashi." (p. 41). German travellers' "Hadim" (p. 96).
- **khasi / khisyan** — "castrated one"; in al-Subki specifically the eunuch "whose testicles alone have been removed" (p. 62).
- **mamsuh** — "the 'smooth' eunuch", penis and testicles removed (p. 62).
- **majbub** — penis removed, testicles intact (p. 62).
- **fahl / fuhul** — uncastrated male(s) (pp. 26, 42, 100); Ibn Qasim "the first of the fuhul" to be chief eunuch (n.116).
- **tawashi / tawashiya** — "the more formal title for eunuchs" (n.3); for al-Qalqashandi "those from the doors of the sultan"; for al-Maqrizi "royal eunuchs (al-khuddam al-mulukiyya)" (p. 42); Piloti's "tavassi ... chastres" (n.30).
- **agha / aghawat** — Ottoman-period term; "from the respectful title agha (lord)" (p. 41); Rafi' wrongly derived it from Greek "guardian of the bed" (p. 110). **tawashi al-nabi** — "eunuch of the Prophet", also used by Mecca's eunuchs (p. 94).
- **zimam / zimamdar** — eunuch supervisor of a household's women; also *zimam al-qubba*, "zimam of the tomb" (pp. 5, 18–19; n.52 p. 127 on the Persian *zanan-dar* vs Arabic *zimam* "reins" etymologies). **azimma** — plural, "supervisors, people of authority" (p. 18).
- **muqaddam al-mamalik al-sultaniyya** — eunuch commander of the sultan's cadets; **muqaddam al-tabaqa** — eunuch barracks chief (pp. 11–12).
- **shaykh al-khuddam / shaykh al-haram** — chief eunuch of Medina (p. 41); **mustaslim** — later title, "the keys to the pure hujra are in his keeping" (n.112); **agha al-tawashiya** — chief eunuch of Mecca, elected (p. 101); **naqib** — deputy (p. 110); **kabir al-tawashiya** — chief eunuch of a tomb (p. 22); **kizlaragasi** — "agha of the girls", Ottoman chief harem eunuch (p. 101).
- **khazindar** — treasurer (eunuch), of the sultan or of the sanctuary (n.17, n.107, p. 104); **ra'is nawbat al-jamdariyya** — chief of the Masters of the Robe (p. 18); **khuddam al-hawsh** — "eunuchs of the courtyard" with access to the secluded sultan (pp. 12, 53).
- **sadana / sadin** — shrine custodians; "that which makes a veil/barrier (hijaba) is a sadin" (p. 33; n.85).
- **farrashun** — non-eunuch servitors/janitors of the sanctuary (pp. 28, 101).
- **al-khidma al-sharifa** — "the noble service" of the Prophet (p. 41); **khadim al-haramayn** — "servant of the Two Sanctuaries", sultan's title (p. 35).
- **ta'ifa / tawa'if** — "category"; "the ta'ifa of ambiguity" (pp. 60, 85). **jins** — "kind", eunuchs' own corporate identity (n.16). **jins al-bid** — "the white race" (p. 18).
- **qawanin al-khidma** — "laws of the service" (p. 60); **nizam** — the eunuchs' own "order" (p. 107); **'ada / sunna** — custom.
- **battalin / khuddam battalin** — "unemployed" eunuchs, i.e. exiled Citadel eunuchs living in Medina (pp. 70–71).
- **mabit** — right to spend the night in the sanctuary (p. 43); **nawba** — a eunuch's turn at night-vigil (p. 68); **tasrij** — evening lamp-lighting (p. 93); **tawaf** — evening circumambulation expelling believers (p. 93); **dikka / dikkat al-aghawat** — eunuchs' raised platform (pp. 80, 110); **kiswa** — the tomb's brocade raiment (pp. 47, 91); **maqsura** — enclosure (p. 51); **hujra** — the Prophet's chamber/tomb (p. 13); **rawda** — the Garden (p. 46); **kavuk** — high white Ottoman ceremonial turban (p. 102).
- **hayba / mahaba** — "a respect bordering on dread" (p. 40), "salutary 'awe' or 'dread'" (p. 64); **sawla 'azima** — "arbitrary power" (p. 65); **sarama** — boldness; **shihama** — astuteness (p. 67); **sadhaja** — guilelessness (p. 67); **hamiyya / ghayra** — ardent zeal (p. 12); **muwala** — devoted friendship; **dalliya** — affectionate familiarity (p. 75); **tarbiya** — upbringing (pp. 12, 75).
- **harim / haram / hurma / mahram / ihtiram / muhtaram / harami** — the h-r-m root cluster (p. 6); **masuna** — "the well-guarded one", title of respect for Mamluk women (p. 19); **khawand** — royal woman (p. 22; n.64); **fitna** — temptation/discord (p. 6); **fasad** — corruption (p. 18); **bid'a** — innovation; **bid'a hasana** — good innovation (p. 79); **ibtikar / huduth** — creation/introduction (p. 80); **ta'zim** — glorification (p. 27); **baraka**; **qurb / muqarrabin** — closeness, "those who are brought close" (p. 84); **jiwar / mujawirun** — pious residence / "neighbours" of the Prophet (pp. 28–29); **ziyara**; **hijra**.
- **bulugh / baligh(a)** — puberty; **rajul kamil / mar'a kamila** — "complete man / complete woman"; **rushd / rashid** — competence; **amrad** — beardless boy (pp. 86–87).
- **sarawil** — under-trousers (p. 22); **shahwa** — sexual desire (p. 111).
- Insults / hostile usages: *ahl al-sharr* "people of evil", *al-mubtadi'un* "innovators", *al-rawafid* "refusers" (for Shi'is, n.160); "bestial people" for castrators (p. 111); Burton's "beardless bit of neutrality", "non-men" (pp. 100, 103); Lady Cobbold's "horrible trade of mutilation" (p. 107); "jester drinking companion of the sultan" (p. 42).
