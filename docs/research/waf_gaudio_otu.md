## 0. Framing (binding)

1. **Living, criminalised people.** 'Yan daudu live under Shari'a codes in twelve northern Nigerian states since 1999–2001 and under *hisba* patrols (Gaudio pp. 23–24). Sasso live under Ghana's "unnatural carnal knowledge" law and an organised anti-LGBT+ coalition; an LGBT+ office in Accra was raided and closed in February 2021 (Otu, Introduction). Neither community is the faith, its ancestor or its model. No term of theirs becomes a faith term.
2. **Not trans women, in their own words.** Gaudio is explicit, and the design must follow him:
   - 'yan daudu are "men who are said to talk and act 'like women'" (p. 3);
   - all those he knew "saw themselves as 'real' men" (p. 10), even those called by women's names;
   - he never met one "who tried to pass as a woman socially" (p. 10), and none mentioned surgery as something wanted (p. 27 n. 18);
   - in daily speech they "prefer to be addressed, with masculine forms" (p. 92); calling a dan daudu "she" outside intimate joking "would probably be interpreted as disrespectful" (p. 74).
   - Gaudio still concedes that "coming out in the open" among 'yan daudu concerns gender, not sexuality, and that "in this sense" the category is "comparable to" transgender (p. 66). Record both halves; do not cite only the second.
   - Otu's subjects are "self-identified effeminate men" (Introduction; ch. 1). Sasso is also a word for same-sex desire in anyone, women included (ch. 1, "Sasso as Homoerotic Act").
   - So these books describe **men in a women's world**. Badenya's daughters are **women**. Anything borrowed is a social *role* or *shape*, never an identity, and the narrator says so.
3. **Grade.** Everything here is LATER: the earliest 'yan daudu memory is Baba of Karo's childhood, c. 1890 (Gaudio pp. 40–42); the earliest written use of the term is early 20th c. (p. 31). Sasso's history before the 2000s is undocumented (Otu ch. 1 n. 5–6).
4. **Persons.** Gaudio gives pseudonyms for everyone except public figures (p. 26); Otu uses pseudonyms for most interlocutors but names one deceased friend in the acknowledgments. This note names no interlocutor, pseudonymous or not, and records no health status. Sexual-violence and child-abuse testimony in both books is not summarised and must never be used.

## 1. The argument in brief

### 1.1 Gaudio

- **Thesis.** 'Yan daudu use language, bodies and media to "play" (*wasa*) with "the boundaries of what it means to be male and female" (p. 7). Their talk and feasts are claims to **cultural citizenship** inside Hausa Muslim society, against reformists who say Islam or "African culture" has no room for them, and against Western activists who assume the gay model is universal (p. 8).
- **Not a pre-Islamic survival.** The literature's *bori* link is weaker than claimed. Besmer saw no dan daudu fall into trance (p. 33). The name likely comes, through the spirit 'Dan Galadima, from a Kanuri noble title, so daudu should be sought "not in the supposedly pre-Islamic traditions of Hausaland" but in Hausa contacts with the Muslim world (p. 34).
- **Decline.** Until the 1980s 'yan daudu were invited to dance at weddings and naming feasts. Reform movements (Izala, the Muslim Brothers) made them unwelcome, and Shari'a after 2000 sharpened it (pp. 54–55, 23–24).
- **Faithful irreverence.** They joke about God and still pray, fast, go on hajj and name their sons with the Qur'an (pp. 117–124). Most "do see" a contradiction between their lives and Islam and hope that discretion (*kunya*) and faith will win God's mercy (p. 193).

### 1.2 Otu

- **Thesis.** Sasso are "amphibious": they move between the Ghanaian state's "obligatory heterosexuality" and LGBT+ NGOs' "obligatory gayness", using each when it helps (Introduction). The term comes from Kwame Gyekye's reading of the Akan Adinkra sign of two-headed crocodiles sharing one stomach, the person between self and community (Introduction).
- **Rituals.** Outdoorings (naming feasts), weddings, funerals and birthdays are where sasso are needed, visible and loved, and also where the heteronormative order is renewed (ch. 4).
- **Hostility.** Christian (especially Pentecostal and Charismatic) bodies lead the hostility. Otu traces it to 1960s campaigns for monogamy by the Christian Council of Ghana with Christian Aid (ch. 5).

## 2. What matters for the design

### 2.1 Terms, and what people call themselves

| Term | Gloss and source | Use in the mod |
|---|---|---|
| *'yan daudu* (sg. *dan daudu*) | "sons of / those who do" daudu, "the practice of men acting 'like women'" (Gaudio p. 9, glossary p. 207) | never |
| *'yan daudun riga* | "shirted" 'yan daudu, feminine only in private (p. 9) | never |
| *masu harka* | "people who do the deed", in-group self-name of men who have sex with men (p. 9) | never |
| *uwa*, *bábà*; *'ya*; *ƙawa* | "mother"; "daughter"; "girlfriend", the bond-friend word of women and girls (pp. 68, 72; glossary) | the *shape* only: see §4.4 |
| *karuwa* | "prostitute", earlier a "profligate person" of either sex; independent women prefer *mata masu zaman kansu*, "women who live on their own" (pp. 5, 43–44) | never |
| *biki* | feast, "especially in celebration of a wedding or naming" (glossary p. 207) | the shape only: §4.1 |
| *sasso* (Ga), *saso*, *sasofo* (Twi) | "coequal", "to occupy the same status"; Banks's informants derive it from *mi saso*, "my mate" (Otu ch. 1, "Sasso and Sasofo", "Sasso Vernaculars") | never |
| *Kwadwo Besia*, *Ntowbea* | both "man-woman"; Kwadwo is the Monday-born boy's name, *besia* is Fante for "female" (Otu Introduction and n. 15) | never; see §3 |
| "sister", "auntie" | what sasso call each other (Otu ch. 1, "Being and Doing Sasso") | the shape only |

- **Self-descriptions.** People say what they *do* more than what they *are*. Few 'yan daudu said "I came out"; the phrase is used of others and carries a judgment (Gaudio pp. 66–67). Sasso speak of "doing sasso" and "being sasso" in one breath (Otu ch. 1).
- **Feminine self-reference exists, as stance.** One dan daudu recalls being his friend's girlfriend "since I was a girl" (Gaudio p. 80). Gaudio reads gendered pronouns as a "strategic" stance in a context, not a stable identity (p. 92).

### 2.2 The "mothers" network

- **Mother and daughter.** A newcomer who leaves home depends on an older dan daudu or an independent woman, who becomes his "mother", gives him a room, and teaches him "the art and business of cooking" (Gaudio pp. 62–63). Kinship terms index "this intimate dependence" (p. 68).
- **Referral.** A newcomer who arrives "without a referral from anyone close to them" is received "cordially, but with suspicion" (p. 69).
- **The mother as patron.** A wealthy elder housed his "daughters", taught them to dress and to meet patrons, and paid for their first pilgrimages (pp. 134–135).
- **Girlfriends.** *Ƙawa* carries "mutual responsibilities of obligation and trust" (p. 72). The cardinal rule: girlfriends should not "steal" each other's boyfriends (p. 79). A saying closes a quarrel: "'We fought, now we can stay together'" (p. 110).
- **Fellowship.** *Zumuncin bariki*, "bariki fellowship", is the solidarity of the network (p. 72); at its best "the friend of a friend might find kinship, a home, a livelihood" (p. 86).
- **Sasso.** Otu's interlocutors call each other "sister" and "auntie"; effeminacy "allows us to know our 'sisters', even if they are not yet in the collective" (ch. 1, "Being and Doing Sasso"). Market women protect one sasso, and some say they "wished that I were their daughter" (ibid.).
- **The mothers' house as a bar.** In Jamestown a bar and an older sasso's shop serve as safe houses; even there an elder may abuse them, and they keep the peace out of respect for the owner (Otu ch. 4, "Insiders and Outsiders").

### 2.3 Roles

- **Cooking and selling food.** The visible trade of 'yan daudu (Gaudio pp. 4–5, 62–63). Cooking is women's work, but they turn it into a *sana'a*, a recognised craft (pp. 64–65). Sasso trades: "food vending, interior design, tailoring and dressmaking" (Otu ch. 1).
- **Go-betweens.** *Kawalai*, "intermediaries" who arrange matches between men and independent women (Gaudio p. 6, glossary p. 208). Gaudio rejects "pimp" as a translation (p. 55). **Not usable** (see §5).
- **Feast-makers.** At an outdooring the sasso organiser assigns other sasso to count guests, serve food and receive gifts; sasso are wanted for "efficient organizational skills" and for the festive colour they bring (Otu ch. 4, "Clubhouse as a Ritual Space").
- **The voice of the feast.** The master of ceremonies at that outdooring was a sasso, "chosen specifically for his unrivaled oratorical gifts"; in a Ga outdooring this is the *otsiame*, "linguist" (Otu ch. 4, "The Contours of an Outdooring"). Otu compares him to a griot (ch. 4, "The Liminal Process").
- **Wit.** 'Yan daudu are known for proverbs and *habaici* (innuendo), a genre linked to co-wives (Gaudio pp. 90–91, 97–100). Their restaurant slogan: "We don't like fighting, just playing and laughing" (p. 93).
- **Bori, second-hand.** Baba of Karo, born c. 1880, recalls that the Emir of Zarewa appointed one dan daudu and three independent women as the town's "keepers" of *bori* (Gaudio p. 42, after Smith 1981: 224). A pre-colonial office granted by a Muslim ruler, but Hausa and *bori*: **do not import**.

### 2.4 Feasts: the biki and the outdooring

- **The biki.** Bikis hosted by 'yan daudu and independent women are "modeled after the parties organized by married Hausa women to celebrate weddings and the naming of babies" (Gaudio p. 19). "Hausa men are not supposed to have bikis of their own" (p. 163), so the feast is itself a feminine claim.
- **Its officers.** Printed invitations list the host, the singer, "Parents", "Male Friends", "Female Friends" and "Players"; the glossary has *uwar biki*, "mother of the biki", and *uban biki*, "father of the biki" (pp. 164–165, 209). The "parents" help plan and pay (p. 165).
- **Its shape.** The play space is permitted by the ward head; dancing, gifts and possession happen in no fixed order; prayers break the music at prayer times; the formal gift-giving, *ajo*, is always on the last day (p. 165). The glossary also has a word for the final festivities glossed "wipe up the house" (p. 207; the extraction's spelling is unreliable).
- **Age and silence.** Younger 'yan daudu dance, wearing a woman's wrapper or head-tie; older ones "sit silently in expensive masculine attire" as patrons (p. 145). "Silence is an index of his (aspired-to) social status", while hired praise-shouters speak for him (pp. 168–169). Gaudio sets the Hausa praise-shouter beside the "Mande *jali*" as castes of wordsmiths (p. 168).
- **Money.** Donors place bills one by one on the forehead of the person honoured (p. 169). The guests' gifts give the host capital: one host used his to grow his business (p. 63).
- **The outdooring.** Ga *kpodjiemo*, "coming out of the door": on the eighth day the baby goes to the father's family house and is named by an elder of good name, "because it is believed that the baby takes on the character of the person who names it" (Otu ch. 4, quoting Fayorsey 1992). At the outdooring Otu saw, the oldest surviving male of the paternal family named the child (ibid.).
- **"How would these events look without us?"** One sasso: "You can't be a sasso and not be interested in an outdooring, a wedding, or a funeral" (Otu ch. 4, epigraph).

### 2.5 Religion

- **"God made us."** Asked why 'yan daudu excel at women's speech, one replied: "Because we, God made us" (Gaudio p. 121). Non-'yan daudu also say "Haka Allah ya yi shi", "That's how God made him" (p. 122). The same speaker at once voices the opponents' answer: that he "put it on himself" out of unbelief (p. 121). The phrase is **contested**, not a consensus.
- **Sasso and the Christian God.** A sasso jeered on the street says he is "not in control of how God created me" and was "born that way" (Otu ch. 1). An Anglican chorister whose priest knows he is sasso keeps singing in the choir (Otu ch. 2, "Don't Do unto Others").
- **Practice.** 'Yan daudu pray, fast and go on hajj (Gaudio pp. 1–3, 133–136); a dan daudu names his son with a Muslim name, Qur'an recitation and a slaughtered goat (p. 119).
- **Patience and the covered secret.** At a sickbed the prayers are "May God heal", "May God increase your patience", "May God keep a secret" (*Allah ya rufe asiri*, "May God cover a secret") (pp. 198–199). Gaudio reads the last as a gift: some things are "best kept covered", so "God alone is left to judge" (p. 199).
- **Discretion as reputation.** When a drunk friend made a scene, a host cried "My secret's been exposed!"; his "secret" was his name for minding his own business, not a hidden fact (pp. 111–112).
- **Friendly clergy, at the margin.** Some less-educated *malamai* count 'yan daudu among their clients and make charms for them (pp. 140–141).
- **Hostility.**
  - A 1957 letter claims the Prophet expelled 'yan daudu; a hadith on "men who try to resemble women" is cited (pp. 46–47).
  - A 1976 song-poem says the dan daudu "has ruined the name of men" and that the singer would rather his son died (pp. 49–51).
  - Another poem: "a man can't become a woman, you hear, / Just as a woman can't become a man" (p. 53).
  - Sufi orders condemn them too (p. 124).
  - In Ghana: a coalition of churches and Muslim bodies campaigns against "the LGBT+ problem"; Pentecostal leaders call sexuality education "satanic" (Otu ch. 5, opening and n. 1).

### 2.6 Kinship, marriage, money

- **Marriage.** Most 'yan daudu marry women and have children; Hausa people "do not see marriage as a choice" (Gaudio p. 10). Wealth earned in daudu pays for a wedding (pp. 63, 145). Under Shari'a the 'yan daudu kept their food businesses "as men with an Islamically legitimate, if unconventional, occupation", while independent women lost theirs (p. 203).
- **Natal family.** A son who sends gifts home is tolerated (pp. 135–136). One sasso's mother told him to leave home after rumours (Otu ch. 2, "Are You a Man or a Woman?"). One dan daudu's tolerant father protected him from a press exposé (Gaudio p. 177).
- **Inheritance.** When a wealthy dan daudu died, his "daughters" said he meant them to have a house; his children won in court (p. 200). When another died, his family claimed his house and business from his assistant (p. 204).
- **Day-names.** Kwadwo/Kojo is the Monday boy's name; at the outdooring Otu saw, the child was named Kwadwo for the day of birth and given an ancestral name (Otu ch. 4). Akan, not Mande.

### 2.7 Death and burial

- **The name at the grave.** After a dan daudu died, a friend referred to him as "she" while telling a story. A masculine man "chastised him for using the feminine pronoun now that Madhuri was dead", and the others defended it by the love between "girlfriends" (Gaudio p. 192; Madhuri is a pseudonym, p. 193 n. 2).
- **Proverb.** "Whatever happens, happens, [says] the dan daudu at the grave" (p. 117): the stereotype of indifference to judgment.
- **Funerals as gatherings.** Sasso meet each other, and their lovers, at weddings and funerals (Otu ch. 1; ch. 4). Adinkra cloth is traditionally mourning cloth, "to say goodbye" to the dead (Otu Introduction, after Dzobo). Akan.
- **Silence about cause of death.** Deaths were rarely named as AIDS; one friend insisted "It's illness, that's all" (Gaudio p. 196). **Not usable.**

### 2.8 Hostile scripts

- **The rumoured wedding.** A pattern across the material:
  - a 1994 Hausa weekly headlined "Man marries man in Kano" over a biki whose invitation said "Sister Wedding Ceremony", and printed the guests' names (Gaudio pp. 175–176);
  - the Bauchi raid of 2007 (pp. 13, 178);
  - violence in Jamestown after rumours that sasso had organised a wedding (Otu ch. 1).
- **Exposure.** Fear of being exposed shapes every space (Gaudio pp. 111–114); "Whoever has a room, that's who will benefit" from Shari'a (p. 115).
- **Internal policing.** A discreet sasso tells a flamboyant one to "manage his effeminacy" in public (Otu ch. 1). Older 'yan daudu say only "children" wear head-ties (Gaudio p. 158).

## 3. Corrections to the candidate and to existing notes

1. **Kodjo-besia.** `waf_contemporary_gender.md` A5 and the brief use Geoffrion's *Kodjo-besia*, "Kodjo is a woman [inside]". Otu spells it *Kwadwo Besia*, adds *Ntowbea* (*ntow* "male" + *bea* "female"), glosses both "man-woman", and calls the first "a derogatory category" now "synonymous with homosexual" (Introduction n. 15; ch. 1, "Introducing Kwadwo Besia"). Sasso use it among themselves only "jocularly". Otu's own etymology (*dwo*, "cool, calm, patient") is his speculation. Keep it on the do-not-use list; it is also a slur.
2. **"God made us."** A0/B2 of the existing note treats "God made us" as the phrase people use. It is used, by 'yan daudu and by others (Gaudio pp. 121–122), but in the same breath the speaker voices the charge that they choose it out of unbelief. The faith may hold the doctrine; the narrator must not present it as what West Africans believe.
3. **'Yan daudu and *bori*.** A7 (after Pierce) ties 'yan daudu to the spirit 'Dan Galadima. Gaudio: Besmer saw none possessed (p. 33); the 1990s pattern of men possessed by female spirits being 'yan daudu "seems not to have been the case in earlier historical periods" (p. 59 n. 24); the name likely derives from an Islamic-era Kanuri title (p. 34). This confirms `south_reading_list.md` finding 5: no pre-Islamic or pre-1453 'yan daudu.
4. **Earliest witness.** 'Yan daudu: Baba of Karo's childhood memories, c. 1888–90, told in 1949–50 (Gaudio pp. 40–42). Smith's English edition misspells the word as *'yandauda*, "sons of dirt" (p. 42); Baba herself called them "unhealthy", i.e. impotent, without moral blame (p. 43).
5. **Sasso etymology.** "Coequal" (Ga) is Otu's gloss; Banks's informants give *mi saso*, "my mate"; most sasso "had very little to say" about the source (Otu ch. 1, n. 6). Do not cite "coequal" as settled.
6. **Gaudio on the jali.** Gaudio's comparison of Hausa praise-shouters to the "Mande *jali*" (p. 168) is comparative remark, not evidence about Mali.
7. **Otu has no page numbers here.** Every Otu reference in the repo should be checked against a paginated copy before it is quoted outside design notes.

## 4. Design implications

All of this is **analogy for tone and social shape**. The narrator marks it; loc never cites these books or names these communities.

### 4.1 The Naming Feast, revised (events)

`waf_contemporary_gender.md` D5 proposes a recurring Naming Feast that a daughter cooks and leads. Gaudio and Otu give it a fuller shape:
- **Officers.** Each feast has a *feast mother* and a *feast father* who sponsor it and sit in the seats of honour (after Gaudio pp. 164–165, 209). In Badenya the feast mother is the House's senior woman. The feast father is any male ally at court.
- **The daughter's two ages.**
  - Young daughters **serve**: cook, count guests, carry gifts, dance (after Otu ch. 4; Gaudio p. 166).
  - Senior daughters **sit and are spoken for**: a jeli speaks their praise while they keep silent, because "silence is an index of status" (after Gaudio pp. 168–169). This joins the Naming Feast to Badenya's gate step 3, where the jeli speaks a name before the mansa.
  - Mechanic: a daughter's first feasts give *serving* events; after N feasts or on reaching House seniority she switches to *hosting* events. Matches the "three feasts before she can sponsor" apprenticeship in D1.
- **The gift-count on the last day.** Hosting costs gold up front; on the last day guests' gifts return gold scaled by the host's opinion with attendees and the number of House members present (after the last-day gift ritual, Gaudio p. 165, and the capital it gives, p. 63). Gold dust laid on the forehead is the visual (after p. 169; Mande gold, not Nigerian notes).
- **The ward head's leave.** The feast needs the county holder's permission. A hostile holder can refuse (event branch), a friendly one gains opinion (after p. 164).
- **Prayer pauses.** At a Muslim court the music stops at prayer times (after p. 165): a small line that makes the feast Malian, not a pagan rite against Islam.

### 4.2 The House as host to strangers (events)

- **"Who sent you?"** A newcomer seeking the House arrives with or without a mother's word. With a word, the House takes her in. Without one, an event: test her (a season of service) or turn her away (after Gaudio p. 69).
- **The roof and the craft.** The claiming mother gives a room and teaches a craft: cooking, dyeing, dressmaking, hair (after Gaudio pp. 62–63; Otu ch. 1; Broqua 2012 p. 121 lists dyers and hairdressers). Mechanic: the claimed daughter gets a *craft* modifier (stewardship or diplomacy flavour) after a year in the mother's house.
- **The mother pays the road.** A rich mother may pay a daughter's first long journey (pilgrimage to Mecca for a Muslim-court House; trade to Walata otherwise) (after Gaudio pp. 134–135). Mechanic: a decision "Send a daughter on the road" with a gold cost, a long absence and a prestige or learning return.

### 4.3 Religion texture

- **Doctrine, faith voice only:** "She was made a daughter before she was named one." The narrator adds that later West Africans who took women's roles said God made them so, while others answered that they chose it (Gaudio pp. 121–122), and that the faith's daughters are imagined as women, as those men mostly were not.
- **The covering prayer.** At a sickbed the House prays for healing, for patience, and that what should be covered stay covered (after Gaudio pp. 198–199). This joins the Mande *gundo*, the thing everyone knows and no one may say (`waf_contemporary_gender.md` B5). Mechanic: a House sickbed event with a stress loss for the sick character.
- **Patience as a virtue.** Badenya can prize patience over boldness: a "hot" seeker cooled by the word is already in the gate (Jansen; Schulz). Gaudio's *haƙuri* is the same shape (p. 198). Use Mande vocabulary only after a speaker's check.
- **The friendly marabout.** A low-status local cleric who writes amulets for the House and visits its feasts, against the court's Maliki jurist (after Gaudio pp. 140–141). Grade the amulet-writer as BACK for 14th-c. Mali.
- **Laughter.** Joking and laughter are a daughter's register (after Gaudio p. 93; Otu ch. 4). A trait-flavour line: "The House laughs loudest at the jurist's feast."

### 4.4 Kinship terms and the network (names, modifiers)

- **Mother, daughter, sister.** Badenya already uses "mother" and "my mother's child". Add the peer bond: two daughters claimed by the same House in the same year are **sisters of the roof** (tie to the New Roof age-set, `waf_jansen_kangaba_griots.md` §4.6). Shape from *ƙawa* (Gaudio p. 72) and sasso "sister" (Otu ch. 1); the name is invented and English.
- **Obligations.** Sisters of the roof do not court each other's suitors. If one does, an event offers a quarrel and then a reconciliation that leaves both with a small bond modifier: the House saying is "a quarrel settled binds tighter" (after p. 79 and the saying on p. 110, paraphrased, never quoted).
- **Protection by neighbours.** A county that hosts a House long enough gains a modifier, "Her street keeps her", that blocks the mob event there; outsiders in a hostile event come "from another quarter" (after Otu ch. 1, the market women; Niang et al. 2002 p. 10, the Lébou neighbourhoods, see `waf_contemporary_gender.md` A11).

### 4.5 Names

- **Named by the mothers.** The claiming mother names the daughter; the jeli makes the name public (Badenya gate step 3). In the sources, elders give in-group names to newcomers (Niang et al. 2002 p. 10) and 'yan daudu keep a "man's name" beside a "dan daudu name" (Gaudio p. 202). The faith inverts this: its name is **one name, said aloud**, not a second, hidden one. Narrator note.
- **Character from the namer.** In a Ga outdooring the child "takes on the character of the person who names it" (Otu ch. 4, after Fayorsey). Mechanic: the daughter inherits one personality-flavour line from her claiming mother. Mark as Ga analogy.
- **No day-names, no Hausa feminine names, no informant names.** Keep the Mande lists in `waf_ibn_battuta_mali.md` §5 and the mother-name prefix (`waf_jansen_kangaba_griots.md` §4.2).

### 4.6 Burial (event chain for "The House buries its own")

- **The name at the grave.** On a daughter's death her natal kin may want her buried under her birth name. The House insists on her name and "she"; the jeli sings it at the grave (after Gaudio p. 192). Branches: kin yield (House prestige), kin refuse (House buries her in its own ground; opinion loss with kin), or a compromise in which the kin mourn privately.
- **The estate.** Her goods: to her House or her blood kin (after Gaudio pp. 200, 204)? Mechanic: on death, if she held wealth, an event lets the House claim it (gold to the House, opinion loss with kin) or yield it (kin opinion gain, House stability loss). Never a law that always favours the House: the sources show kin winning.
- **The funeral as gathering.** A daughter's funeral counts as a House gathering: members attending gain a small bond; rivals in the House may reconcile there (after Otu ch. 4 and ch. 1).
- **Never:** exhumation, guarded cemeteries, disease or "what did she die of" lines.

### 4.7 Hostile events

- **"They say it was a wedding."** A Naming Feast is reported to the jurist as a marriage between men. Branches: the host's kin swear it was a sister's feast (after Gaudio p. 176); the jurist's men break it up; or the mansa's favour quashes it. Narrator note that later rumours of this kind preceded raids and mobs. Never name real places or events in loc.
- **The family council.** The daughter's natal kin summon her before a council of dozens and demand she leave the House (after Broqua 2012 p. 136, `waf_contemporary_gender.md` A10).
- **"Too declared."** House elders ask a young daughter to be quieter in the market; she can comply (safety) or refuse (prestige, risk) (after Otu ch. 1; Broqua 2012 p. 121).

### 4.8 Reaction lines (hostile, each answered by the faith)

- Maliki jurist: "God made men men; she has refused the gift." Faith: "Then let God refuse her. You may not." (Shape from the 1976 poem, Gaudio pp. 49–51; never quote it.)
- Courtier: "Neither bird nor beast." Faith: "The House knows exactly what she is." (After p. 50, inverted.)
- Kinsman at a grave: "Say 'he'; she is dead now." Faith: "She is dead. She is not unmade." (After p. 192.)

## 5. Lines Badenya must not cross

1. **No community mapped.** No 'yan daudu, *masu harka*, *'yan daudun riga*, sasso, sasofo, Kwadwo Besia, Ntowbea, *karuwai* or *kawalai* in loc, in faith names, titles or holy-site text. No line saying the faith's daughters were their ancestors or that they are "what became of" the faith.
2. **Not "men who act like women".** Never describe a daughter as a man in women's work, as feminine, effeminate or "like a woman". Gaudio's people mostly called themselves men; Badenya's daughters are women.
3. **No sex economy.** No go-between, brothel, "women's house", patron-for-sex or "gentor/log" structure. The feast and the craft carry the economy, not sex.
4. **No possession.** No *bori*, no men possessed by female spirits, no 'Dan Galadima, no Akan possessed priests.
5. **No informants' words in characters' mouths.** "Allah made us", "We fought, now we can stay together", the heron proverb, the grave proverb and the breast joke stay in this note. Concepts may be borrowed; sentences may not.
6. **No disease, no violence from testimony.** Nothing from the HIV material, the healers, the sexual-violence and child-abuse testimony, or the misrecognition attacks in Otu ch. 2.
7. **No exposure as spectacle.** Rumour, raids and councils may exist as persecution, never as comedy or a scene where a daughter is "found out".
8. **No Akan or Ga borrowings for Mande.** No day-names, no Adinkra, no outdooring vocabulary, no *otsiame*.
9. **No modern places or dates in loc.** Kano, Katsina, Sabon Gari, Jamestown, bar names, 1994, 2007.
10. **The narrator marks every back-projection** as reconstruction ("Later West African societies knew men who…; the faith is imagined, not their ancestor").
