## 1. The argument in brief

- **Thesis.** Medieval trans and intersex studies is "the most important development" in the history of medieval sexuality (p. 933), and the first real challenge to the field's consensus (p. 940).
- **The old paradigm (pp. 934–940).** Queer medieval history asked three questions: identity, community and repression. By the 2010s two claims had hardened into "widely accepted dogmas": that medieval Europe was "pre-heteronormative", and that it had no stable concept of sexual identity (p. 940).
  - Wingard sides with the minority that sees a *de facto* hetero/homosexual binary in practice (p. 936).
  - On repression she sets Lochrie and Burgwinkle's "queer utopia" (p. 938) against R. I. Moore's "persecuting society" (pp. 938–939). In Moore's model queer people were one of the "out-groups" with heretics, Jews, lepers and sex workers (p. 939).
- **The 2020–21 "transgender turn" (pp. 940–942).** Four books: Betancourt, *Byzantine Intersectionality* (2020); DeVun, *The Shape of Sex* (2021); LaFleur, Raskolnikov and Kłosowska (eds.), *Trans Historical* (2021); Spencer-Hall and Gutt (eds.), *Trans and Genderqueer Subjects in Medieval Hagiography* (2021). Also Maillet, *Les genres fluides* (2020), which got less attention in English (p. 940 n. 26).
- **DeVun's argument as Wingard gives it (p. 941).** Intersex was "broadly accepted as a morally neutral, if unusual and potentially marvellous" difference in late antiquity and the early Middle Ages. After twelfth- and thirteenth-century scholasticism it was reframed "as unnatural", with "new forms of corrective surgery". Elsewhere: from a "more fluid" Hippocratic/Galenic model to a "more binary" Aristotelian one (pp. 944–945, citing DeVun pp. 103–133).
- **Three axioms of trans history (pp. 943–946).**
  1. Gender is socially constructed.
  2. So is sex: the criteria for a normatively sexed body vary (beard, frame, genitals) (p. 944).
  3. People whose gender does not match their assignment "have always existed", and lived "authentically" through names, clothing, occupations, behaviour and sometimes bodily modification (p. 945). Trans historians take "a middle path" between essentialism and pure construction (p. 945).
- **What the turn adds (pp. 946–950).**
  - *Identity:* re-reading cases (Eleanor Rykener; Joseph of Schönau, FtM) and recovering new ones (Wojciech of Poznań, 1550s) (pp. 946–948).
  - *Agency:* people argued for themselves before courts (Eleno de Céspedes, Marin le Marcis, Rykener, Wojciech) (p. 948).
  - *Community:* Eleanor's network of women who gave her "both clothing and a name" (p. 949, quoting Henningsen); a possible "gender-creative" subculture in Poznań (p. 949).
  - *Repression:* anxieties about sodomy were "fundamentally grounded in transphobia" (p. 950). Betancourt treats transmisogyny as "a distinct form of othering" in Byzantium (p. 950).
  - *Race:* Christian writers tied racial and religious difference to transness. DeVun argues that Christian discourse identifying "Jewish 'inconstancy' and Muslim foreignness with intersexuality" fed antisemitism and Islamophobia (pp. 950–951, citing DeVun pp. 40–101). De Souza links the Spanish Inquisition's policing of sex to its hunt for insincere converts (p. 950).

## 3. Corrections to current docs

- **`comparanda_reading_list.md`, Latin section, item 7.** The entry is right that the Rykener reading is at pp. 946–947. Add p. 949 (community) and p. 950 (repression and race). It is a "Reflections" essay rather than a "review", and its stated date is August 2023, published online January 2024.
- **Item 5 (DeVun).** The summary "morally neutral" to "unnatural", with corrective surgery, matches Wingard p. 941. No change.
- **Date of the Rykener case.** Wingard says Eleanor was "brought before the court of the Mayor of London in 1395" (p. 946). Our files say 1394. Both are defensible: the examination was in December 1394 and the roll is dated 1395 (Henningsen note). Karras and Linkinen give the arrest as 1394 (`cmp_karras_linkinen_rykener.md`). Keep 1394 for the event, and the roll's 1395 for the tooltip.
- **Karras and Linkinen.** Wingard gives pp. 111–121 (p. 947 n. 57). The reading list's 111–122 is wrong (see `cmp_karras_linkinen_rykener.md`).
- **Risk for the Jewish sect.** Point 5 is the risk the Judaism section has not yet named. The sect's whole vocabulary is "turning" (*h-p-k*). A hostile Christian reaction that says "the Jews change their sex as they change their faith" would be historically plausible and would repeat the exact trope DeVun traces. **Rule:** Christian reaction to *Ha-Nehpakhot* should target the sect's doctrine (Purim licence, Kalonymus as satire, "one does not cite miracles") and never Jews as a people. Hostile loc must not pair "inconstancy", "conversion" or "doubleness" with Jewishness. A Christian bishop's line should be about the dress (Aquinas's idolatry rationale), as for the other faiths.

## 4. Design implications

- **Catholic opinion curve (proposal).** A Catholic-faith opinion malus toward daughters that starts small and steps up once around 1200 (the scholastic turn). Script it as an era check (`current_date >= 1200.1.1`) on the existing opinion modifier, with loc: "The schoolmen have decided that what nature mixes is a monster." Before 1200: "The old books call it a marvel." Both lines are in-world paraphrases of DeVun as Wingard reports her, not quotes.
- **Out-group tooltip.** Where a Catholic ruler persecutes a House, add a tooltip naming the company the court keeps: heretics, sodomites, harlots. Do **not** list Jews in loc, even though Moore does, because the line is player-facing and would read as the mod's own grouping.
- **Method line for the README.** Wingard's three axioms are a fair statement of the mod's own stance (people of this kind "have always existed", sex is read through beard and frame). The README's method paragraph can cite her, p. 945, for the "middle path" between essentialism and pure construction.
- **No loc quotes from Wingard.** She is a modern historian; nothing in-world should paraphrase her.
