## 1. Argument map

**Thesis and structure.** Rowson argues that pre-Islamic and early Islamic Arabia had "a form of publicly recognized and institutionalized effeminacy or transvestism among males", whose bearers, the *mukhannathūn*, "were permitted to associate freely with women, on the assumption that they had no sexual interest in them, and often acted as marriage brokers, or, less legitimately, as go-betweens" (p. 671, abstract). They "played an important role in the development of Arabic music in Umayyad Mecca and, especially, Medina", and although "subject to periodic persecution by the state, such measures were not based on any conclusions about their own sexual status—they were not assumed to be homosexual, although a few were—but on their activities as musicians and go-betweens, which were seen as corrupting the morals of society and especially of women. A particularly severe repression under the caliph Sulaymān put an end to the mukhannathūn's prominence in music and society, although not to their existence" (p. 671). The article proceeds in order: (i) the social setting of post-692 Medina as a rich, politically impotent, pleasure-seeking city (p. 671); (ii) the lexicographers' etymology of *mukhannath* (pp. 672-673); (iii) the seven canonical hadith on mukhannathūn and their commentary tradition (pp. 673-677); (iv) the biography of Tuways (pp. 677-681); (v) the other Medinan and Meccan mukhannath musicians, especially al-Dalāl, with Jamīla's concert as a group portrait (pp. 681-685); (vi) a terminological excursus on *takhannuth* versus passive homosexuality (*ubna*, *ḥulāq*, *bighā'*) (pp. 685-687); (vii) government persecution culminating in the mass castration under Sulaymān (pp. 687-692); (viii) the Abbasid aftermath and the change in the figure of the mukhannath (pp. 692-693). Rowson explicitly frames the inquiry against anthropological "berdache" institutions and warns that "An automatic link between the mukhannathūn of the Hijaz and homosexuality can therefore by no means be assumed" (p. 672). His method is cautious: the anecdotes are "too riddled with variants and chronological improbabilities to warrant belief in the historicity of any single account" (p. 680), but the general picture, and especially the post-Sulaymān silence, can be trusted (pp. 681, 692).

**Definition and semantic shift.** The root: "the verb khanatha in the first form means to fold back the mouth of a waterskin for drinking. Derived terms develop the basic idea of bending or folding in the direction of pliability, suppleness, languidness, tenderness, delicacy" (p. 672). Abū 'Ubayd derives the name from "languidness (*takassur*, elsewhere usually paired with *tathannī*, suppleness)" while "a languid woman was called *khunuth*" (pp. 672-673). The *Kitāb al-'Ayn* "derives mukhannath rather from *khunthā*, hermaphrodite, on the basis of parallel gender ambiguity" (p. 673). Later lexicographers "define the mukhannath as a man who resembles or imitates a woman in the languidness of his limbs or the softness (*līn*) of his voice" (p. 673). Rowson's conclusion from the lexica: "mukhannath has the general meaning 'effeminate,' without distinction between involuntary and voluntary behavior, and does not indicate transvestism" (p. 673). The hadith shift this: they show "an identifiable group of men who publicly adopted feminine adornment, at least with regard to the use of henna, and probably in clothing and jewelry as well" (p. 675). Commentators then introduce a moral distinction: al-Kirmānī "distinguishes between constitutional (*khilqī*) and affected (*takallufī*) effeminacy, only the latter being blameworthy" (p. 675), while al-'Aynī and Ibn Ḥajar add that even the constitutionally effeminate man "must make efforts (*takalluf*) to stop being so; if he does not do so, he becomes blameworthy, 'especially if he seems to take pleasure in (his effeminacy)'" (p. 675). The decisive diachronic shift: al-'Aynī (d. 1451) "adds that 'in our time' mukhannath means simply the passive partner in homosexual intercourse", and tries to split the variants so "that the first [mukhannath] signifies 'effeminate' and the second [mukhannith] 'catamite'" (p. 675). Rowson's periodisation: in the first/seventh century "Nowhere in the early material ... is it implied that these mukhannathūn were sexually interested in males" (p. 676); Ibn Ḥabīb (9th c.) and al-Ṭabarānī (10th c.) make the distinction explicitly, "thereby suggesting that by their own time assumptions had changed and mukhannathūn were expected to be homosexually inclined" (p. 676); and "For the 'Abbasid and later periods, the answer is clear: mukhannathūn were assumed to be *baghghā'ūn*, while continuing to display many of the distinctive traits for which they were known in the Umayyad period, such as wit and flippancy, association with music ... and activity as go-betweens, as well as cross-dressing" (p. 686). The closing diagnosis: with Abū Nuwās and the new fashion for homoerotic verse, "'effeminacy,' while continuing to be distinguished from (passive) homosexual activity or desire, was no longer seen as independent from it; and the stigma attached to the latter seems correspondingly to have been directed at the former as well, so that the mukhannathūn were never again to enjoy the status attained by their predecessors in Umayyad Medina" (p. 693).

So: **effeminacy** (lexica, all periods) → **transvestism/adornment plus access to women** (hadith and Umayyad anecdote) → **passive homosexuality** (Abbasid and later). Rowson insists the Umayyad Medinan mukhannath is defined by gender presentation and social role, not by sexual object-choice.

## 2. Dossier on the Medinan mukhannathūn

### 2.1 Who they were

Rowson's roll-call, from the hadith, the *Kitāb al-Aghānī*, the proverb books and Ibn Khurradādhbih:

- **Hīt** (or Hinb; some commentators identify him with **Māti'/Māni'**): the mukhannath of the Prophet's day, "a person usually identified as Hīt" (p. 674), proverbial as "more effeminate than Hīt (*akhnath min Hīt*)" (p. 677). Al-'Aynī and Ibn Ḥajar "give altogether five different names of mukhannathūn banished by Muhammad, of which Hīt (or Hinb) is the one most often mentioned ... Besides Hīt, Māti', and Annah, al-'Aynī mentions al-H.d.m and al-Ḥurr; the former appears as Harim in Ḥamza al-Iṣfahānī" (p. 677 and n. 38). "A total of six different places of banishment are mentioned as well" (p. 677).
- **Annah**: in a non-canonical variant, 'Ā'isha "asks a mukhannath named Annah to direct her to (*tadullunā 'alā*, the standard verb for matchmaking) a suitable wife for her brother 'Abd al-Raḥmān" (p. 676).
- **Tuways** ("little peacock"), Abū 'Abd al-Mun'im 'Īsā b. 'Abdallāh (or Ṭāwūs, or 'Abd al-Malik), born 10/632, d. 92/711 (p. 678). "Tuways, the older of the two, was celebrated not only for his music and his *takhannuth*, but also as a jinx" (p. 678). Client (*mawlā*) of the Banū Makhzūm; "*mawlā* status seems in fact to have been usual among mukhannathūn both earlier and later" (p. 678). "the first of the mukhannathūn to sing 'art music' (? *ghinā' mutqan*), and the first person to compose in the 'lighter' rhythms of *hazaj* and *ramal* in Islam", also "the first person to sing in Arabic in Medina, and also the first to flaunt publicly his effeminacy (? *alqā l-khanath*) there" (p. 678). Rowson accepts him "as the most prominent example, and perhaps in some sense leader, of a group of male professional musicians who publicly adopted women's fashions" (p. 681). "Tuways is reported to have married and fathered children" (p. 681).
- **al-Dalāl** ("coquetry"), real name Nāfidh, kunya Abū Yazīd, a *mawlā* "like his master Tuways"; "The laqab al-Dalāl ('coquetry') is explained as referring to his physical beauty and the charm of his manner; but the wit which constituted much of the latter was often crude" (p. 683). "the third mukhannath, after Hīt and Tuways, whom the Medinese included among the sophisticates (*zurafā'*) and wits (*aṣḥāb nawādir*) to whom they pointed with pride" (p. 683).
- **The eight of Jamīla's concert** (second day, "Tuways and his companions"): "Hīt, Tuways, al-Dalāl, Bard al-Fu'ād ('coolness/contentment of the heart'), Nawmat al-Ḍuḥā ('morning nap'), Qand ('candy'), Raḥma, and Hibatallāh" (p. 681). Qand is probably to be emended to **Find** (p. 681 n. 74; p. 687).
- **Find/Qand**: "a participant in Jamīla's concert and a close friend of the poet Ibn Qays al-Ruqayyāt, many of whose verses he set to music" (p. 687); go-between who "provided a space in his house for lovers' trysts" (p. 687).
- **Ḥabīb Nawmat al-Ḍuḥā**, castration victim and host to the surgeon (p. 692).
- **Nasīm al-Saḥar** ("Breeze of the Dawn"), **Zill al-Shajar** ("Shade Under the Trees"): castration victims known only from their quips (p. 691).
- **Ṭarīf / Ṭarīfa (or Ṭarīqa) b. al-Shūtarī / Ibn al-Shūnīzī**: castration victim; Rowson suspects his name was displaced by Tuways's in Ḥamza's list since Tuways was already dead (p. 691 n. 152; p. 688 n. 126).
- **Ibn Nughāsh / al-Nughāshī**: executed under Yaḥyā b. al-Ḥakam (p. 680).
- **al-Walīd**: "an otherwise unknown mukhannath" at a wedding with Tuways and al-Dalāl (p. 682).
- **Abū l-Ḥurr al-Madīnī** (= Abū l-Khazz?), "referred to as a Medinan mukhannath and marriage broker by Ibn 'Abd Rabbih" (p. 688 n. 126).
- **Ṣāliḥ b. Zuhayr al-Khuzā'ī**, Medinan mukhannath, credited with a famous song (p. 681 n. 68).
- **Sajiyya, Shabīb, Sa'tar, and the Meccan Madār** — names from Ibn Khurradādhbih not otherwise attested (p. 688 n. 126).
- **Ibn Surayj** and his pupil **al-Gharīḍ**: Meccan singers "apparently also" mukhannathūn, who "began as professional lamenters (*nā'iḥ*), an activity traditionally restricted to women" (pp. 682-683). Al-Gharīḍ fled to Yemen under Nāfi' b. 'Alqama (p. 688).
- **Mukhkha** of Mecca, who asked al-Dalāl for an introduction (p. 689).
- **Khālid al-Khirrīt**, a Medinan mukhannath identified by malicious reports with the later governor Khālid al-Qasrī (p. 693).
- **'Ajjāja** of Basra, "the most famous of the mukhannathūn there" (p. 693); **'Abbāda**, court jester at al-Ma'mūn's court (p. 693).
- The poet **al-Aḥwaṣ**, not a mukhannath musician but called "your mukhannath and brother" by 'Abd al-Malik and repeatedly accused of *ubna* (pp. 686-687).

### 2.2 Roles

**Musicians and singers.** The Medinan group is defined by the square tambourine (*duff*) and the light rhythms: Tuways "relied exclusively on the *duff*, a square tambourine, and sang 'lighter' songs, both characteristic of later mukhannathūn as well" (pp. 678-679). "Tuways' preference for the *duff* and for the 'lighter' rhythms of *hazaj* and *ramal* was shared by al-Dalāl, in particular, as well as his other mukhannathūn pupils" (p. 682). A recognisable "mukhannath" sound: when praised for a setting al-Dalāl replied, "'And there is in it something yet greater than that!' and when asked what, replied, 'Reputation (*sum'a*)! Anyone who hears this will know that it is by a mukhannath in truth!'" (p. 682). "A full generation later, in the early 'Abbasid period, mukhannathūn were still associated with *hazaj* and with the *duff*" (p. 682); as late as the 11th c. al-'Utbī "describes captives after a defeat being met in Bukhara by *makhānīth* bearing *dufūf* and spindles" (p. 682 n. 82). Rowson also notes the saying "the best male singers are those who imitate (*tashabbaha bi-*) women, and the best female singers are those who imitate men" and a later vocal technique called *takhnīth*, translated by Neubauer as "Kopfstimme" (p. 683 n. 83).

**Lamenters.** Ibn Surayj and al-Gharīḍ "began as professional lamenters (*nā'iḥ*), an activity traditionally restricted to women" (p. 683) — a direct instance of a female ritual office held by feminine men.

**Matchmakers and go-betweens.** "because of their admission to the women's quarters (which continued despite the Prophet's reported disapproval), the mukhannathūn played a significant role as matchmakers for eligible bachelors with secluded women" (p. 676). Al-Dalāl brokered "the marriage of 'Abdallāh b. Ja'far's daughter to the governor al-Ḥajjāj b. Yūsuf" (p. 683). Find "provided a space in his house for lovers' trysts" (p. 687). Al-Gharīḍ is "depicted conveying verses between Meccan poets and aristocrats, a kind of 'go-between' activity not directed toward marriage, but probably only possible because of his mukhannath status" (p. 689). Khālid al-Khirrīt "used to convey messages between the poet 'Umar b. Abī Rabī'a and various aristocratic ladies in the city" (p. 693). Rowson notes the possible vocalisation of al-Dalāl's name as "*al-Dallāl*, 'marriage broker'" is "ruled out" by the sources (p. 683 n. 89) — but the pun was evidently available.

**Wedding entertainers.** Tuways, al-Dalāl and al-Walīd "are said to have been together at a wedding when 'Abd al-Raḥmān arrived. Seeing them, the latter said, 'I will not sit in company that includes these.' But when Tuways acknowledged his earlier offense, and al-Dalāl lightened the atmosphere with a song in *hazaj*, accompanied by all three mukhannathūn on their tambourines, 'Abd al-Raḥmān agreed to stay" (p. 682). Tuways to the governor Abān: "I was trailing at the heels of the women of my people who accompanied your blessed mother's wedding procession to your good father" (p. 680). Rowson does not say "hairdressers"; the closest is Ibn Nughāsh, who "had dressed his hair" (p. 680) — his own, not others'.

**Wits, buffoons, party guests.** "He could make a bereaved mother laugh!" (of Tuways, p. 679). Find and "at least five other Medinan mukhannathūn, are included in a list in the *Fihrist* of nineteen buffoons (*baṭṭālūn*) about whom monographs had been written" (p. 688). Young Qurashī men "sometimes invited Tuways to entertain them at pleasure parties in the 'parks' (*muntazahāt*) outside Medina" (p. 679).

### 2.3 Access to women and the Prophet's hadith

Qur'an 24:31 lists among those before whom women may unveil "male retainers who lack desire (*al-tābi'īn ghayr ulī l-irba min al-rijāl*), and children" (p. 675). Al-'Aynī glosses the phrase as "'impotent' (*'innīn*) as well as 'insensitive to women's charms'" (p. 675); al-Ṭabarī "gives a total of five interpretations of the phrase, including the two cited by al-'Aynī as well as 'eunuch' (*khaṣī majbūb*), 'old man,' and 'young slave'" (p. 675 n. 34). The Ḥanafī al-Sarakhsī "offers three interpretations—*majbūb*, *mukhannath*, and *ablah*" and distinguishes the mukhannath "in evil acts", who is barred like other sinners, from "the one whose limbs are languid and whose tongue has a lisp (*takassur*) by way of gentle natural constitution, and who has no desire for women and is not mukhannath in evil acts, [to whom] some of our shaykhs would grant such a person license (*rakhkhaṣa*) to be with women" (pp. 675-676 n. 34). So in the legal imagination eunuch and natural mukhannath sit in the same Qur'anic class of men harmless to women.

The hadith of 'Ā'isha: "There was a mukhannath who used to be admitted to the presence of the Prophet's wives. He was considered one of those lacking interest in women (*min ghayr ulī l-irba*). One day the Prophet entered when this mukhannath was with one of his wives; he was describing a woman, and said 'When she comes forward, it is with four, and when she goes away, it is with eight.' The Prophet said, 'Oho! I think this one knows what goes on here! Do not admit him into your (fem. pl.) presence!' So he was kept out (*ḥajabūhu*)" (p. 674). The Umm Salama / Hīt version has Hīt tell her brother "If God grants that you take al-Ṭā'if tomorrow, go after Ghaylān's daughter; for she comes forward with four and goes away with eight!" (p. 674). The "four" and "eight" "are explained by the commentators, at great length, as referring to the woman's belly wrinkles (*'ukan*), four in front, whose ends can be seen wrapping around on the two sides of her back when she walks away, thus appearing to be eight" (p. 674). In Ibn al-Kalbī's version the Prophet replies "'You have taken too good a look, enemy of God!' and banish[es] him from Medina to al-Ḥimā" (p. 677).

Rowson's reading: "the mukhannath is not simply expressing his own appreciation of a woman's body, but describing it for the benefit of another man" (p. 676); al-Muhallab says the Prophet "only barred (the mukhannath) from the women's quarters when he heard him describe the woman in this way (i.e., her belly-wrinkles) which excites the hearts of men; he forbade him (to enter) in order that he not describe (prospective) mates to people and thus nullify the point of secluding women (*al-ḥijāb*)" (p. 676). Hence "it is not entirely clear, then, to what extent the mukhannathūn were punished for their breaking of gender rules in itself, and to what extent such measures were taken rather because of the perceived damage to social institutions from their activities as matchmakers and their corresponding access to women" (p. 676). And: "None of our sources in fact state that Muhammad actually banished more than two mukhannathūn, and there is considerable evidence that such men continued to have access to women's quarters and to describe women to other men" (p. 677).

### 2.4 Dress, henna, speech, jewellery

- Hadith of Abū Hurayra: "A mukhannath, who had dyed his hands and feet with henna, was brought to the Prophet. The Prophet asked, 'What is the matter with this one?' He was told, 'O Apostle of God, he imitates women.' He ordered him banished to al-Naqī'. They said, 'O Apostle of God, shall we not kill him?' He replied, 'I have been forbidden to kill those who pray'" (pp. 673-674).
- Al-Ṭabarānī: "in the days of the Prophet the mukhannathūn spoke languidly, and dyed their hands and feet (with henna), but were not accused of immoral acts (*fāḥisha*)—although they sometimes played hobbyhorse (*kurraj*), a frowned-upon frivolous activity" (p. 675).
- Al-'Aynī "speaks specifically of imitation of women in dress and adornment (listing veils and several types of ornament as examples) and in acts, 'such as languidness of body and feminine modes of speeching and walking'" (p. 675).
- Ibn Nughāsh: "The man had the appearance of a woman: he was wearing fine dyed garments, and had dressed his hair and applied henna to his hands" (p. 680).
- Tuways: "I swore to God that if I saw you become amir I would dye my hands and arms with henna up to the elbows and strut with my tambourine" (p. 680).
- Ibn Surayj "is described as wearing dyed clothing and playing with a locust which he had on a string; when someone chided him for this, he retorted, 'What harm does it to do people if I color my garments and play with my locust?'" (pp. 688-689).
- Al-Aḥwaṣ "entering a mosque wearing two polished, saffron-dyed garments, bedaubed with saffron perfume and with a bundle of basil behind his ear" — which Rowson says is of unclear bearing on *takhannuth* (pp. 686-687).
- Speech: Mukhkha addresses the praying police chief "in the feminine" — "*Ajjilī bi-ṣalātiki lā ṣallā Allāh 'alayki*" (p. 689 and n. 138), and Rowson notes "I have seen no other examples of this use of the feminine among the mukhannathūn in the pre-'Abbasid period, although later it became not uncommon" (p. 689 n. 138). Rowson "would be tempting to speculate that the mukhannathūn sang in falsetto, but I have found no evidence for this" (p. 683 n. 83).
- Rowson's honest caveat: "Our sources offer very little information on the outward manifestations of this *takhannuth*" (p. 680).

### 2.5 Women, men, or a third?

Evidence that they were classed apart from men and nearer to women:
- Jamīla's three-day concert ordered men / "Tuways and his companions" / women: "Their placement between the other men and the women is certainly a reflection of their ambiguous gender status" (p. 682). "the gratuitous and anachronistic inclusion of Hīt, who is nowhere else associated with music and even here does not sing, confirms that *takhannuth* is essential to the identity of these musicians as a distinct group" (p. 682).
- "the mukhannathūn, like the women, are known by nicknames (*alqāb*), in contrast to all but one of the other men, suggesting that the mukhannathūn shared with the women the kind of inferior status which permitted relative familiarity in address and general social intercourse" (p. 682).
- Qur'anic class *ghayr ulī l-irba*, shared with eunuchs (above).
- The castration quips: "With castration I have become a mukhannath in truth!" / "Or rather we have become women in truth!" (p. 691), which Rowson says "identify the essential psychological motivation behind *takhannuth* as gender identification with women" (p. 692).
- The Omani *khanīth* and Cairene *khawal* comparanda flagged in the last note (p. 693 n. 165), with Wikan's title "Man Becomes Woman".

Evidence that they remained men:
- The hadith curses "*al-mukhannathīn min al-rijāl*", "effeminate men" (p. 673) — grammatically men.
- Tuways insisted on male equality: "he refused to listen to anyone who granted him less than equal respect" (p. 679); on his protest at being relegated to the second day, "Tuways seems here to be insisting, against widespread prejudice, on his equality of status with other men" (p. 682 n. 76). He married and fathered children (p. 681).
- Al-Dalāl is addressed as "beautiful man" (*ayyuhā l-rajul al-jamīl*) (p. 684).
- Rowson's own word is "ambiguous gender status" (p. 682), never "third gender".

Net: Rowson presents a socially recognised intermediate status — male in law and grammar, feminine in dress, voice, company and nickname-register, and grouped with women and eunuchs for purposes of access to the harem.

### 2.6 Sexuality as Rowson reconstructs it

- Early period: "Nowhere in the early material, however, is it implied that these mukhannathūn were sexually interested in males" (p. 676). Tuways: "References to a role as matchmaker are lacking in the case of Tuways, as are any references to homosexuality, or indeed to sex at all" (p. 681), and "we can, of course, say nothing about Tuways' actual sex life. What is in question here is the public image of the mukhannathūn" (p. 686 n. 112).
- Al-Dalāl: "adored women and loved to be with them; but any demands (by them for his sexual favors) were in vain" (p. 684); his boast "By God, I have never been guilty of fornication, nor of submitting to a fornicator! I have no desire for what the men and women of your city lust after!" (pp. 683-684). Rowson weighs three readings — chastity, lack of desire altogether, or exclusively homosexual orientation — and finds the third "supported by another story" (p. 684). "al-Dalāl is presented by at least some sources as an unabashed *ma'būn*, that is, someone who sought the passive role in homosexual relations" (p. 685); in Ḥamza's version of the sugared-stones joke the Devil's favour was that he "made me like *ubna*" (p. 685).
- "Al-Gharīḍ and al-Dalāl are the only two mukhannathūn from the pre-'Abbasid period for whom we have explicit anecdotal evidence of homosexual activity" (p. 689).
- Terminology of passive/active: *liwāṭ*, *lūṭī*, *mu'ājir*, *mubādil*, *ma'būn*; "The word *ma'būn* carries strong connotations of pathology, and *ubna* is in fact frequently called a 'disease' (*dā'*)" (p. 685); Umayyad synonym *ḥulāq* / *ḥalaqī*, Abbasid *baghghā'* / *bighā'* (p. 686).
- The Mukhkha anecdote implies sex between mukhannathūn, "a situation I have not encountered anywhere else in the literature" (p. 689).

### 2.7 Religious attitudes

- Curse: "The Prophet cursed effeminate men (*al-mukhannathīn min al-rijāl*) and mannish women (*al-mutarajjilāt min al-nisā'*)" (p. 673); variant "men who imitate women"; some add "Cast them out from your houses!" and "that he and the caliph 'Umar each banished one" (p. 673).
- Slander: "twenty lashes for falsely calling someone a mukhannath", paired in Ibn Māja with falsely calling someone a *lūṭī* and in al-Tirmidhī with falsely calling someone a Jew (p. 673) — "there can be no question about the low social status of the mukhannath" (p. 677).
- Life protected: "I have been forbidden to kill those who pray" (p. 674). Ibn Ḥajar reports "a legal argument for the necessity of distinguishing the mukhannath from the passive homosexual offender, based on the fact that the penalty for the latter, stoning, would obviate the penalty of exile" (p. 676 n. 35).
- Prayer: al-Zuhrī's opinion "that one should pray behind a mukhannath only in cases of necessity" (p. 675).
- Eschatological threat to 'Amr b. Murra: "Any of these rebels (*'uṣāh*) who dies without repenting will be gathered by God on the Day of Resurrection just as he was in this world—mukhannath, naked, without a fringe to cover him before people, unable to stand without falling!" (p. 675), preceded by the penalty list "I will give you a painful beating, shave your head as an example, banish you from your people, and declare plunder of your property permissible to the youth of Medina!" (p. 675).
- Social: "He is under the wrath of God, a mukhannath whom it is shameful to know" ('Abd al-Raḥmān b. Ḥassān on Tuways, p. 679). Governor Abān: "They claim you are an unbeliever." Tuways "replied with the confession of faith and the assertion that he observed the five prayers, the fast of Ramadan, and the pilgrimage" (p. 680).

Rowson: "the execution of Ibn Nughāsh and the bounty put on the heads of other mukhannathūn was, according to the extant reports, based on a perceived connection between cross-dressing and a lack of proper religious commitment" (p. 687).

### 2.8 The castration episode

**Who and when.** "the caliph Sulaymān (reigned 96-99/715-17)" (p. 672); "the identity of the caliph and the governor, the former appearing sometimes as al-Walīd, 'Umar II, or Hishām, and the latter as 'Uthmān b. Ḥayyān, although Sulaymān and Ibn Ḥazm are by far the most frequently named" (p. 691). The governor: "his governor in Medina, Ibn Ḥazm al-Anṣārī" (p. 690). Rowson calls it "this most widely reported of all the mukhannathūn anecdotes" which "appears in a great variety of versions, which differ not only on the nature and scope of the persecution, but also on its occasion and rationale" (p. 690).

**Version A (Aghānī, preferred).** Sulaymān, "in camp in the desert one night, enjoying the company of a slave girl", notices her distracted by a man's singing; he traces it to "one Samīr al-Aylī", and pronounces: "[t]he he-camel brays, and the she-camel comes running; the male goat cries, and the female goat submits herself (?); the male pigeon coos, and the female struts; a man sings, and a woman swoons (*ṭaribat*)," and had the singer castrated. When he then asked him where this business of singing originated, he replied, "In Medina, among the mukhannathūn; they are the best and most highly skilled at it." The caliph then sent an order to his governor in Medina, Ibn Ḥazm al-Anṣārī, to castrate (*akhṣi*) all the mukhannathūn singers there, which he did" (p. 690). Rowson: "In this version, the mukhannathūn were punished simply because they were musicians. The grotesque choice of punishment, meted out equally to the non-mukhannath Samīr, is a response, if not an entirely clear one, to the nature of the offense: music rouses women's passions and is thus a moral threat to society" (p. 690). Note n. 143: "In no version of the story is he ever identified as a mukhannath" (p. 690).

**Version B (Aghānī, jealousy).** The song's verses ("Secluded, she heard my voice, and it kept her awake ... Her neck veiled by two swathes of saffron, / With green ornaments on her breast ...") "matched the slave girl; Sulaymān, furious with jealousy, imprisoned the singer and threatened the girl with her life" (p. 690). Both protest innocence; "Sulaymān was finally convinced. He was unwilling to let Samīr go free, however (*lam taṭib nafsuhu bi-takhliyatihi sawiyyan*), so he had him castrated and ordered the same for the mukhannathūn" (pp. 690-691).

**Version C (Ḥamza; Aghānī/al-Walīd).** Only the mukhannathūn are castrated, "because 'they had become many in Medina, and were ruining the women for the men (*afsadū l-nisā' 'alā l-rijāl*)'"; the al-Walīd version "says that he took action when informed that the women of Quraysh were visited by the mukhannathūn in Medina, despite the Prophet's explicit prohibition" (p. 691).

**Version D (Muṣ'ab al-Zubayrī, lurid).** "After arranging a marriage, according to Muṣ'ab, al-Dalāl would convince the bride that her sexual excitement at the prospect of the wedding night was excessive and would only disgust her husband, and then he would offer to calm her down by having sexual intercourse with her first. He would then go to the groom, make the same point, and offer himself, passively, to cool him down as well. The outraged Sulaymān, here again called 'jealous,' *ghayūr*, but in a general sense, wrote to have all the mukhannathūn castrated, saying, 'They are admitted to the women of Quraysh and corrupt them'" (p. 691). Rowson: "Here, even with explicit testimony to al-Dalāl's homosexual behavior, it is the morals of the women which are of concern" (p. 691).

**Version E (the *taṣḥīf* / scribal-dot version).** "One fairly common addition to the story ... absolves Sulaymān of responsibility for the castration by claiming that what he actually wrote to the governor was 'make a register (*aḥṣi*) of the mukhannathūn'; but the spluttering pen of the amanuensis added a dot to the *ḥā'* so that it read '*ikhṣi*,' 'castrate.' Some of these sources let the governor off the hook as well, reporting that he questioned the reading but was assured that the dot 'looked like a date,' or 'was as big as the star Canopus.' These stories perhaps imply that Sulaymān's action was viewed by some as unexpectedly brutal" (p. 691).

**Scope.** Aghānī 4:61 "claims that altogether nine mukhannathūn were castrated, including al-Dalāl, Ṭarīf, and Ḥabīb Nawmat al-Ḍuḥā" (p. 691 n. 152). "al-Dalāl, who is almost always included" (p. 691). Rowson argues Tuways (d. 92/711) was dead "three years before this event" and that his name "has replaced that of the less well-known Ṭarīfa/Ṭarīqa" (p. 691 n. 152).

**The jokes (Ḥamza's list, p. 691):**
> Tuways: "This is simply a circumcision which we must undergo again."
> al-Dalāl: "Or rather the Greater Circumcision!"
> Nasīm al-Saḥar ("Breeze of the Dawn"): "With castration I have become a mukhannath in truth!"
> Nawmat al-Ḍuḥā: "Or rather we have become women in truth!"
> Bard al-Fu'ād: "We have been spared the trouble of carrying around a spout for urine."
> Zill al-Shajar ("Shade Under the Trees"): "What would we do with an unused weapon, anyway?"

Plus: "According to the Aghānī, one of the victims simply enunciated the benediction, appropriate to a circumcision, '*salima l-khātin wa-l-makhtūn*'" ["may the circumciser and the circumcised be safe"] (p. 691 n. 152).

**Rowson's reading of the jokes.** "The last two statements imply that what the mukhannathūn underwent was *jibāb*, the more drastic form of castration in which the penis was truncated. They serve to stress the mukhannathūn's lack of sexual interest in women, while the two preceding statements identify the essential psychological motivation behind *takhannuth* as gender identification with women. The flippancy of tone in these quips is of course characteristic of the mukhannath persona, and also points to the singular inappropriateness of the punishment, despite its savagery; significantly, there is no positive reference to sexual orientation, as opposed to gender identity" (p. 692).

**Aftermath.** Ibn Abī 'Atīq, hearing of al-Dalāl's castration in the midst of his prayers, insists "that (whatever one might say against him) he had done a fine rendition of some verses by al-Aḥwaṣ" (p. 692). "both Badraqus, the physician who performed the castration, and his assistant were part of a group who set out from Mecca at some later date and were offered hospitality en route by Ḥabīb Nawmat al-Ḍuḥā. When the assistant asked Ḥabīb his identity, he replied, 'Do you not recognize me after having "circumcized" me?' Taken aback, the assistant avoided the food offered by Ḥabīb for fear of poisoning" (p. 692); "A year or two after the operation, the narrator adds, Ḥabīb's beard began to fall out" (p. 692 n. 155). In the *taṣḥīf*-dependent sequel, "the caliph Sulaymān was grieved by the accidental castration of the charming al-Dalāl, and had him secretly brought to his court. When the caliph asked him how he was, al-Dalāl replied, 'Now that you've truncated (*jababta*) me in front, do you want to truncate me in back?' Sulaymān laughed, and ordered him to sing. Unable to decide whether he was more charmed by his wit or his singing, the caliph kept him with him a month, rewarded him richly, and sent him back to the Hijaz" (p. 692). Rowson's conclusion: "What is more striking than these few stories is the general silence in our sources on the Medinese mukhannathūn after this event ... this silence supports the assumption that they did suffer a major blow sometime around the caliphate of Sulaymān" (p. 692). "none of the next generation of singers ... are ever referred to as mukhannathūn" (p. 692). He also suggests "Sulaymān's punishment of al-Aḥwaṣ would seem to represent a similar move on the caliph's part" (p. 692 n. 157).

### 2.9 Later Abbasid courtly mukhannathūn

"Reports about mukhannathūn begin to appear again with any frequency in our sources only in the high 'Abbasid period, and then primarily in Baghdad. But by then their situation had changed rather radically. While we do hear occasionally of mukhannath musicians at court, none achieved sufficient celebrity even to have their names preserved. They continued to play the *duff*, but became associated also with a particular kind of drum and with the *ṭunbūr*, a long-necked lute. More than their music, however, it was their wit that now defined their persona, as illustrated most clearly by the career of 'Abbāda, the son of a cook at the court of al-Ma'mūn (198-218/813-33), who served as a kind of court jester, with some interruptions, for over forty years. In no way a musician, 'Abbāda was also less a wit than a buffoon, whose stock in trade was savage mockery, extravagant burlesque, and low sexual humor, much of the latter turning on his flaunting of his passive homosexuality" (p. 693). Hakam al-Wādī's son: "In your old age, will you take to singing in the style of the mukhannathūn?" — answered: "I sang in the heavy (*thaqīl*) style for sixty years, and never made more than my daily bread; but in the last few years I have sung songs in *hazaj* and made more money than you'd ever seen before!" (p. 692). The combination "of their flippancy, effeminacy, and *bighā'* earned them their own subsection in some of the later joke collections" — al-Ābī's *Nathr al-durr* has chapters *Nawādir al-mukhannathīn*, *Nawādir al-lāṭa*, *Nawādir al-baghghā'īn* (p. 686 and n. 110). Nineteenth-century echo: Lane's "transvestite dancers of Cairo called *khawals* and *ginks*", and Wikan's *khanīth* of Oman (p. 693 n. 165).

## 4. Belief-plausibility matrix

Format: proposition — evidence (entry #) — STRENGTHEN / WEAKEN — verdict for the mod's "veneer of non-anachronism".

**(a) A distinct sex class.** STRENGTHEN: #21 (concert order men / mukhannathūn / women; "ambiguous gender status"), #22 (nickname register shared with women), #7 (Qur'anic *ghayr ulī l-irba* class), #1 (root shared with *khunthā*). WEAKEN: #2 (hadith grammar: "effeminate *men*"), #13/#15 (Tuways insists on equality with men; marries), Rowson never says "third gender". **Verdict: plausible as a recognised social-legal class of "men who do not count as men for harem purposes"; the mod may harden this into a sex class, with the Qur'anic category as its neighbour-world analogue.**

**(b) Womanhood by rite.** STRENGTHEN: #37 ("Or rather we have become women in truth!"; castration called "the Greater Circumcision", with the circumcision benediction — i.e. the victims themselves cast it as a rite of passage), #13 (Ṭāwūs renamed Tuways "when he became mukhannath" — a naming rite), #14 (peers rename his kunya). WEAKEN: the rite here is imposed, not chosen; no source describes a voluntary ceremony. **Verdict: the vocabulary of rite ("circumcision we must undergo again") is attested in the victims' own mouths; a faith that formalises what the Medinans joked is a defensible fictional step.**

**(c) Castration as sacred rebirth.** STRENGTHEN: only the circumcision idiom of #37 and the benediction "*salima l-khātin wa-l-makhtūn*". WEAKEN: #36 (punishment, "grotesque", "savagery"), #38 (even contemporaries thought it a brutal error), #39 (beard loss, fear of poison — trauma, not rebirth). **Verdict: weak in Rowson; the galli/Byzantine material must carry this. Use the Medinan material only as the victims' ironic reframing that the faith later takes literally.**

**(d) Castrated men regarded as women.** STRENGTHEN: #37 (Nawmat al-Ḍuḥā and Nasīm al-Saḥar; Rowson: the quips "identify the essential psychological motivation behind *takhannuth* as gender identification with women", p. 692), #7 (eunuch and mukhannath interchangeable glosses of the Qur'anic class), #34 (feminine grammatical address), #39 (beard falls out). WEAKEN: Rowson stresses the state's logic was removing a sexual threat to women, not reclassifying anyone (#36); eunuchs are not called women anywhere in the article. **Verdict: STRONG as a self-understanding and as a folk logic ("in truth"); the mod can cite the Medinan quips as the seed of a doctrine.**

**(e) Women-plus-castrates priesthood.** STRENGTHEN: #24 (feminine men hold a women's ritual office, lamentation), #20 (Tuways recites creed, keeps the pillars), #4 (they are "those who pray"). WEAKEN: #9 (pray behind a mukhannath only in necessity), #19 (Qur'an test fails; executed), #18/#28 (irreligion as persona), #2 (cursed). **Verdict: WEAK in the Islamic neighbour world — which is useful: the mod's priesthood is precisely what the Medinan mukhannathūn were denied; make their exclusion a grievance in the faith's founding story.**

**(f) Separatism.** STRENGTHEN: #6/#12 (exile to desert, al-Naqī', Khākh, al-Ḥimā; weekly visits to beg), #13 (Tuways's self-exile at al-Suwaydā' in one version, p. 681), #24 (al-Gharīḍ to Yemen), #32 (three days to leave town). WEAKEN: all involuntary; the Medinans wanted in, not out (#15; p. 682 n. 76). **Verdict: plausible as *forced* separatism that a faith re-narrates as chosen homeland; the "desert outside Medina, begging twice a week" image is a ready-made origin myth of a community at the city's edge.**

**(g) Adoptive / matrilineal heirship.** STRENGTHEN: #13/#25 (near-universal *mawlā* status — affiliation by clientage, not blood; Tuways "a great partisan of his patrons, the Banū Makhzūm", p. 679), #17 (effeminacy inherited by association from Hīt), #14 (peers confer names). WEAKEN: Tuways married and had children (#13); al-Dalāl's heir is nowhere mentioned; no women-line inheritance anywhere. **Verdict: the clientage model gives a non-anachronistic hook for non-blood succession among castrates; matrilineality must come from elsewhere.**

**(i) Non-marital kinship.** STRENGTHEN: #21 ("Tuways and his companions"), #25 ("his master Tuways"), #42 (song learned "from another mukhannath"), #34 (introduction network between Mecca and Medina), #14 (collective renaming). WEAKEN: Rowson is cautious ("perhaps in some sense leader", p. 681); no formal household. **Verdict: moderately strong; a master/companion household is well within the evidence.**

**(j) Bodily modification.** STRENGTHEN: #4, #19, #20 (henna on hands, feet, forearms to the elbow; dyed garments; dressed hair; saffron), #36/#39 (castration; *jibāb*; beard loss). WEAKEN: Rowson: "very little information on the outward manifestations" (p. 680). **Verdict: STRONG for henna/dye as a visible marker; strong for castration as a bodily event.**

**(k) Intra-queer hostility.** STRENGTHEN: #24 (Ibn Surayj's "You, you mukhannath"), #34 (al-Dalāl sets Mukhkha on the police chief), #15 (Tuways "would try to trip us up"), #35 (*kiyād mukhannath* cliché). **Verdict: STRONG; rivalry and prank-betrayal are part of the persona.**

**(l) Heresy / outlaw framing.** STRENGTHEN: #2, #8, #9, #16 ("under the wrath of God"), #19 (bounty, execution for irreverence), #20 ("They claim you are an unbeliever"), #30, #32, #33, #36. **Verdict: STRONG — the sources habitually fuse gender-crossing with irreligion and sedition; a faith in the mod can be read by neighbours exactly this way.**

**(n) Penal castration of men judged deviant or dangerous.** STRENGTHEN: #36 (mass castration ordered by the caliph; applied also to the non-mukhannath Samīr for the crime of a song), #37 (victims survive and joke), #38 (contemporaries see it as excessive), #40 (victim later rewarded at court), #41 (community effectively ended). Also the broader penal repertoire: exile (#4, #6, #12, #30), flogging and parade (#28, #30, #34), head-shaving and plunder (#8), bounty and execution (#19). WEAKEN: Rowson calls the choice "grotesque" and "singular[ly] inappropriate" — it was exceptional, not a standing law; no source gives it a juridical basis. **Verdict: STRONGEST item in the article. An 8th-century caliph castrating a class of men because their access to women was judged dangerous is the exact precedent the mod needs for a penal/rehabilitative castration path — and the "register/castrate" dot gives a built-in ambiguity between bureaucratic enrolment and bodily sentence.**

**(o) A mentor/sponsor structure among the mukhannathūn.** STRENGTHEN: #17 (Hīt → Tuways), #25 (Tuways "master" of al-Dalāl; "his other mukhannathūn pupils", p. 682), #24 (Ibn Surayj → al-Gharīḍ), #21 ("Tuways and his companions"), #14 (peers rename), #34 (cross-city introduction), #35 (apprenticeship in "their *kiyād* and ... their speech"), #42 (song transmission). WEAKEN: Rowson hedges on "leader"; the master/pupil language is musical rather than initiatory. **Verdict: STRONG; a sponsor who confers a laqab, teaches the duff and the manner, and vouches for access to women's houses is fully supported.**

### Event chain: "The Dot as Big as Canopus"

- **Trigger:** A ruler of an Islamic (or other hostile) faith with a county containing mukhannath-style musicians/trait-bearers, after a "woman of the court distracted by a singer" incident (adapted from #36 version A/B: the slave girl fails to pour the water).
- **Step 1 — the letter.** The ruler dictates an order about the "effeminates" of the city. Options: *"Make a register of them"* (aḥṣi → they are enrolled as a tolerated class, modifier "Registered Effeminates", small tax/entertainment bonus); *"Castrate them all"* (akhṣi → mass penal castration; the characters gain the castrate trait and, per the mod, womanhood in the eyes of the new faith; large piety with zealots, large tyranny/opinion malus with "unexpectedly brutal" framing, #38).
- **Step 2 — the scribe.** Random chance the scribe's pen splutters: the governor reads *ikhṣi*; he queries it and is told the dot "looked like a date" / "was as big as the star Canopus". Ruler may later disclaim: "I wrote *register*" (small stress relief, no reversal).
- **Step 3 — the jokes.** Each victim fires a line from #37 in a sequence of flavour tooltips: "This is simply a circumcision which we must undergo again." / "Or rather the Greater Circumcision!" / "With castration I have become a mukhannath in truth!" / "Or rather we have become women in truth!" / "We have been spared the trouble of carrying around a spout for urine." / "What would we do with an unused weapon, anyway?" Victims gain the "Laughed at the Knife" modifier: stress loss, prestige, and the rite-counted status in the mod faith.
- **Step 4 — the surgeon's dinner.** Years later the physician's assistant is a guest of one victim ("Do you not recognize me after having 'circumcized' me?"); the victim may poison, forgive, or merely frighten him (#39).
- **Step 5 — the court.** The ruler, "grieved", secretly summons the wittiest victim; "Now that you've truncated me in front, do you want to truncate me in back?" — ruler laughs, grants a month's favour and gold, and the victim may be offered the **Peacock of the Court** position (#40).
- **Step 6 — the silence.** If no victim converts the episode into a founding story (i.e. no faith/culture adopts "The Greater Circumcision"), the community's modifier decays and no new trait-bearers spawn in the county for a generation (#41). If one does, a holy-site or enclave is seeded at the exile's edge (#6, #12).

### Notes on reliability for the mod team

- Rowson treats every anecdote as literary; what he trusts is the pattern and the post-717 silence. Use the names, the quips and the penal repertoire freely; do not present any single episode as "what happened".
- The castration is explicitly not a religious act in the sources; the religious frame is the *victims'* joke. That inversion — the punished adopting the punishment as their rite — is precisely the kind of plausible fiction the project wants.
- The eunuch–mukhannath equivalence in Qur'anic exegesis (#7) is the most useful under-exploited item: it gives the neighbouring Islamic world a native reason to treat the faith's castrates as "women's men" rather than as men.
