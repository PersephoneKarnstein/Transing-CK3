## 1. Puff, "After the History of (Male) Homosexuality" (pp. 17–30)

### The argument in brief
- A historiographical essay. The field has made Foucault's "the sodomite had been a temporary aberration; the homosexual was now a species" into a "road map" (p. 18). The resulting split between premodern acts and modern identities has produced a "proliferation of threshold narratives" (p. 19).
- Puff asks for "multiple chronologies" instead of single breaks (p. 21). He wants more conversation across regions (Ottoman, Arab, Chinese, Iranian work) (pp. 21–22), and a history of homosexuality that is joined to gender history (pp. 26–27).
- He commends Alan Bray's *The Friend* (2003) for putting friendship and its religious rituals, not sex, at the centre of same-sex history from the High Middle Ages on (p. 26).

### The only medieval-range data
- **German and Swiss trial records.** Civic notaries in "late medieval and early modern Switzerland did not regularly record the age" of people prosecuted for *sodomitereyen*. Often "the sexual activity in question did not make it into the written documentation" (p. 21). Few of his records fit the expected model of age- and role-asymmetry.
- **Three settings** in which same-sex behaviour was noted, quoting Puff 2003, p. 92: "it was practiced among adolescents; it existed in the milieu of workers or laborers with negligible age or social distinctions; and it flourished in cross-class and intergenerational relationships of masters with their dependents" (p. 21).
- These records date from 1400 or later. Their topic is male-male sex, not gender-crossing.

## 2. Krass, "Saying It with Flowers" (pp. 63–75)

### The argument in brief
- **The song.** Walther von der Vogelweide's "Under der linden" ("Lindenlied") is "the most famous medieval German love song" (p. 64). A woman tells of meeting her lover (*friedel*) on the heath, on a bed of flowers, with a nightingale as the only witness. The refrain is "tandaradei" (p. 65; text and translation from Cormeau's edition and Paddock).
- **Two taboos played with.**
  - The **clerical** taboo. The song borrows the speech of confession and three biblical scenes: the Song of Songs, the Fall, the Annunciation. In the song, "God is not presented as a judge, but as an accomplice" (pp. 66–67). A confessors' handbook of c. 1330 tells the priest to ask exactly what the sinner did and with whom (p. 66; Guido de Monte Rocherii, *Manipulus curatorum*, n. 11).
  - The **courtly** taboo. In classic Minnesang the lady never yields, and chaperones (*huote*) guard her name. Walther borrows instead from the older **Danubian Minnesang**, which "gave voice to the lady's desire", especially Dietmar von Aist's linden songs (pp. 67–69).
- **"Travesty: The Knight as Lady"** (pp. 70–72).
  - The singer was a man performing a woman's voice. "See how red my mouth is" works as a stage direction: "the real body of the male singer and the imaginary body of the female role coincide" (pp. 70–71).
  - The key phrase is *hêre frowe* (2.3) in the Codex Manesse (MS C). The Weingartner manuscript (MS B) reads ***herre frowe***, "lord lady", and may be the original. Read as a rank, it makes the speaker "at once knight and lady" (p. 71).
  - If the speaker's gender is left open, the song can be read as a knight remembering a tryst with a male *friedel*. Then the open secret (*fornicatio simplex*) hides a second, unspeakable one (*peccatum contra naturam*) (pp. 71–72).
- **Gottfried's joke.** In *Tristan*, Gottfried von Strassburg calls the Minnesänger nightingales and Walther their "leading lady" (*leitevrouwe*), "she of the bird-pasture" (*diu von der Vogelweide*; Krass cites ll. 4802–4803). Krass suggests Gottfried is "perhaps" playing with the singer's ambiguous gender (p. 72).
- **The pastourelle.** The Carmina Burana song *Ich was ein chint sô wolgetân* (CB 185) is a rape under a linden. Walther's song turns that genre into a meeting of willing lovers (p. 70).

## 3. Design implications (both chapters)

- **German lands, reaction.** Neither chapter gives a medieval German case of a trans woman or of gender-crossing before a court. The German reaction layer still rests on:
  - DeVun (`cmp_devun_shape_of_sex.md` §2.2): Cologne physicians, Otto of Freising, the Colmar annals, the Constance alchemist.

  - Puff 2003, which is still needed.
