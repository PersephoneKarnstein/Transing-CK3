**What the book is.** It uses the Geniza to show that in the Fatimid realm (Egypt, Palestine, Syria, with ties to Ifriqiya) Rabbanites and Karaites lived as two parties of one community. They married each other, used each other's courts and needed each other's courtiers. "Heresy" was declared only when it served a political fight and someone had the power to make it stick. For Ha-Nehpakhot, a small dissenting current that must be judged by "the street", this is the most useful book of the three.

## 1. Dates and places for the Maghreb

- **Karaites in North Africa.** By the 10th c. Karaites lived across Syria, Egypt and Ifriqiya. In northern Africa they are attested at "Tripoli, Qayrawān, and Warjlān" (Ouargla) (ch. 1, "The Qaraites").
- **Warjlan as the last refuge (a polemical claim).** Ibn Dawud (1160–61) claimed the Karaites had ceased to exist "except in one city in the Maghrib, in the desert, called Warjlān", with a handful in Egypt and Palestine. Rustow calls the demographic argument "decidedly mendacious" (ch. 8, "Ibn Dāwūd's Depiction").
- **A Karaite from Fez.** David b. Avraham al-Fasi (late 10th c.), the Karaite lexicographer, is among the Karaites who "appeared friendly toward the government", thanking the Muslim conquerors for access to Jerusalem (ch. 4, "Qaraites and the Kingdom of Ishmael").
- **"The man from Fez."** Shelomo b. Yehuda al-Fasi was gaon of the Jerusalem yeshiva 1025–51 (appendix table of geonim). A letter of the 1038–42 schism calls him "the man from Fez" (ch. 11). He is the gaon who refused to ban the Karaites in 1029 (§4 below).
- **Fez and Sijilmasa in the network.** Hayya Gaon wrote in 1007 to "two brothers in Fez who corresponded regularly with the geʾonim of Baghdad" (ch. 6, n. 36). Ester, "granddaughter of the chief rabbinic judge of Sijilmāsa", married Sahlan b. Avraham, head of the Iraqi congregation of Fustat, in 1037 (ch. 6, n. 36).
- **Zirid Ifriqiya and the Fatimid court.** About 1040 the Jerusalem gaon's party asked the nagid of Qayrawan, Ya'aqov b. 'Amram, to ask the Zirid amir al-Mu'izz b. Badis (r. 1016–62) to write to the Fatimid vizier for a rescript confirming the gaon. This is "the latest evidence known of friendly relations between the Zirid amīrs of Ifrīqiya (972–1148) and their Fatimid benefactors" (ch. 11, "The Qayrawān Plan" and n. 44).
- **Hilalians.** "In the 1050s, Ifrīqiya was invaded by Hilālīs from Egypt and in 1061, the Normans" began their conquest of Sicily (ch. 11).
- **Western zeal against dissent.** The Almoravids (1085–1147) and Almohads (1147–1269) appear only as the pressure that hardened Iberian Rabbanite courtiers against Karaites (Epilogue). Ibn Dawud credits "the Almoravids" with having "wiped out every remnant of the Jews from Tangiers to al-Mahdiyya" (Epilogue). That is his attribution, and chronologically it should be the Almohads (*my inference, unverified in Rustow*).

## 2. A Maghrebi Karaite at a rabbinical court (al-Mahdiyya, 1073–74)

Abu Sa'd Yishaq b. Khalaf ibn 'Allun (d. 1073–74) was a Karaite *katib* who had been Fatimid tax minister in Jerusalem until c. 1060. In 1073 he had a **rabbinical court in al-Mahdiyya** draw up a power of attorney. After his death his widow, **Najiya b. Sulayman b. Hiba**, had "the same rabbinical court in al-Mahdiyya" draw up a second one, naming "a certain Abū Sahl Menashshe b. Mūsā the Qaraite" as agent. The Karaite proxy "felt himself bound by the Rabbanite contract" (ch. 10, "Qaraites at Rabbinical Courts").

This is the one Maghrebi case of the dissenting current using the majority's court. Najiya is also the only Maghrebi Jewish woman named in the parts read.

## 3. The limits of communal autonomy

- **The paradox.** Jewish leaders could turn the state on their enemies "as a normal part of Jewish politics", because "the harshest punishment at the Jews' disposal was excommunication, while Jewish leaders depended on the state to administer sanctions such as imprisonment …" (ch. 3, "The Limits of Autonomy"). Offenders "could always flee to another town or straight into the bosom of Islam".
- **The inverse rule.** "the more Jewish leaders tried to force their followers to observe rabbinic law by having state authorities flog or imprison offenders, the …" (ch. 3).
- **A network, not a pyramid.** There was no single point of contact between ruler and Jews. A "wider network of Jewish leaders ... offered access to government support and protection" (ch. 3, introduction). Leadership ran on patronage: "protection from above was exchanged for loyalty from below", individual and binding (ch. 3, "Geʾonim, Merchants, and Courtiers").
- **Titles name power already held.** The first person titled *nagid* was "Abū Isḥāq Avraham b. ʿAṭā of Qayrawān, a physician in attendance at the court of the Zirid governors of Ifrīqiya". The title "did not establish Ibn ʿAṭā's power"; it "acknowledged and reinforced" what he had through the rulers (quoting M. R. Cohen; ch. 3, "Titles among the Jews"). The same word could be a formal title from a gaon or a mere honorific from below (ch. 3).
- **Investiture from the ruler.** Shemu'el b. Hofni, gaon of Sura (998–1013): "installation in an office is incomplete unless it is done by the spiritual leader of any given period". Without such a leader, "each community is at liberty to make its own choice" (ch. 3, "Gaonic Investiture"). The Jerusalem geonim sought documents of investiture from the Fatimid caliphs (ch. 3).

## 4. The ban: who could pronounce it, and who could stop it

From ch. 8, "The Affair of the Ban of Excommunication in 1029":

- **What happened.** In autumn 1029, at the Hoshana Rabba pilgrimage on the Mount of Olives, a Rabbanite crowd tried to have the Karaites banned en masse "against the eaters of meat with milk". "The attempt failed because the gaʾon opposed it and the elders of the yeshiva blocked it by calling on the local Fatimid governors."
- **Who held the ban.** "it was the gaʾon's exclusive prerogative to decree bans". When the crowd would not listen, the gaon Shelomo b. Yehuda "stood on the [cantors'] chair and said, 'It is your choice. Do as you wish'": Rustow reads this as "a statement of despair", not a grant of authority. Three young members of the yeshiva, his son among them, appeared to say the formula, in a way meant to be legally empty.
- **The state's response.** The Fatimid governor of Palestine sent soldiers. Two of the three were jailed in Damascus for months. They were offered release only if they swore never again to bear the title *haver* or serve the community in Palestine. That condition came from an edict of the **Karaite** military governor 'Adaya ibn al-Qazzaz. The Karaites added terms of their own: no ban ever again on the Mount, their own butcher's shop, no Rabbanite authority over them.
- **The caliph's decree (1030).** Answering a Rabbanite petition, al-Zahir ordered "that each group [ṭāʾifa] of the two Jewish groups, the Rabbanites and the Qaraites, should not interfere with one another", each "according to the customs taught in their religious schools", with freedom to trade or not on their own feast days (ch. 8; the same decree is Stillman p. 198). A Karaite counter-decree of 3 April 1034 ordered protection for both groups (ch. 8, "Echoes into the Future").
- **1030: the ruler forbids the ban.** The next year the governor ordered that "anyone who uttered the excommunication should be flogged and sent to prison", and the governor of Jerusalem came up the Mount "with lashes and chains in hand". The ban was not declared. The gaon raised no protest. Rustow takes his silence to mean that "inviting state intervention was a normal part of Jewish affairs under the Fatimids" (ch. 8).
- **The gaon's own argument against banning.** "Should we excommunicate everyone who desecrates God's sabbaths?! But the majority desecrate [the sabbath]!!" And: "[the Qaraites] say that we are the ones who desecrate [it]!" Rustow calls it "a remarkable moment of religious pluralism" (ch. 8). He adds that "many people ... go to Islamic courts" and "many—both men and women" practise magic: the ban could not reach them all.
- **What the ban was for.** In Geniza practice bans hit those who took a second wife without leave, litigants in Islamic courts, debtors and tax-withholders. Rustow: "used principally as a means of bolstering rabbinic authority itself". A ban "was only as good as the community's will to enforce it". "Bans in fact tended to proliferate when rabbinic authority was weakest" (ch. 8, "The Double-Edged Sword"). Bans were proclaimed before an open Torah scroll, sometimes "in the cemetery" (ch. 8, "We Gathered in the Cemetery").
- **Rustow's verdict.** "Excommunicating the Qaraites had itself become a religious error punishable by the state" (Epilogue).
- **A Maghrebi channel.** After the affair the gaon thanked "a certain negid ha-gola—most likely Avraham b. ʿAṭā of Qayrawān" for interceding with the government. This suggests a route to Cairo "via the Zirid governors of Ifrāqiya" (ch. 8, end of "Echoes into the Future"). The route was used again in the schism of 1038–42 (ch. 11). In 1038 the ban against the Karaites *was* pronounced once, by the gaon's son during the schism, and it cost the gaon Karaite help (ch. 11, "The Qayrawān Plan").

**Contrast with Provence.** In Provence the community could not ban without the lord's consent (Shatzmiller 2023, p. 86; Ha-N doc). In the Fatimid world the ban was the gaon's own, but the ruler could **forbid a particular ban** when one party petitioned, and he enforced the prohibition with the lash. The lord's power is the same; it works from the other side.

## 5. How the dissenting current lived beside the majority

- **Marriage across the line.** The Geniza has 13 Rabbanite–Karaite marriages, 1009–1135, from every social stratum. There were also court formularies for them from Ramla (1009) and Fustat (1036), "frequent enough to warrant codification" (ch. 9). "Jurists on both sides sanctioned the marriages" (ch. 9). Contracts "nearly always followed the bride's madhhab" (ch. 9). In 1082 the son of a gaon agreed in his marriage contract to let his Karaite wife keep her own feasts and fasts (ch. 9).
- **Stipulations.** The negotiated clauses concerned Sabbath intercourse, **light on Friday evening** (Karaites: no fire burning; Rabbanites: a lamp lit beforehand is allowed), the calendar and meat (ch. 9, "Marriage Strategies"). In the Fustat formulary, if a Rabbanite husband infringes his Karaite wife's observances, "she has grounds to request and be granted a divorce ... in any place she desires" (ch. 9, "Formularies").
- **Women and betrothal.** "Betrothal decisions usually belonged to the women of the family" (after Goitein). A girl over twelve could not be betrothed without her consent. Widows and divorcées contracted their own marriages (ch. 9). In a 14th-c. Cairo case, the father of the Karaite woman Rivqa ruled that "the choice of suitors belonged to his daughter" (ch. 10).
- **Courts.** Karaites had contracts drawn up in rabbinical courts, and a rabbinical court in Syria devised a legal fiction to let a Karaite cheese merchant sell to Rabbanites (ch. 10).
- **Courtiers as kingmakers.** In the 1020s–40s the Karaite courtiers in Cairo (David b. Yishaq, the Tustaris) were the Rabbanites' main route to the Fatimid court. Rabbanites addressed them as *ha-sar ha-addir*, "the mighty prince" (ch. 3; ch. 7). The ban of 1029 was "a response to Qaraite power rather than powerlessness" (ch. 8).
- **Heresy is declared by power.** "beliefs do not become heretical unless people with the power to make them so exercise that power" (Epilogue). In each case Rustow studied, "the label did not adhere to its targets because they held enough power to repel it" (Epilogue).

## 6. The western warning: where the majority held the state

In al-Andalus and Christian Iberia, Rabbanite courtiers used the ruler's power against Karaites. Shemu'el ibn Naghrilla, vizier of Zirid Granada, boasted that "Our ancestors flogged some of them". Around 1090 Yosef ibn Ferrizuel, physician to Alfonso VI, "drove [the Qaraites] out of all the strongholds of Castile except for one". Under Alfonso VII, Yehuda b. 'Ezra had the king "forbid the heretics to open their mouths" (Epilogue). Maimonides, writing in exile **in Fez in 1160–61**, said the laws for executing heretics "are being applied in all western lands" (Epilogue). Rustow's lesson: in the east "both sides could enlist the state", so dissent survived; where one party monopolised the courtiers, it did not (Epilogue).

## 7. Gender and the body

- **Has:** Najiya of al-Mahdiyya (§2). Women's betrothal power, consent and divorce stipulations (§5). Women among those who practise "magic" in the gaon's list (§4). The Sabbath-light clause (§5). A Rabbanite ketubba requiring the bride to immerse, and Maimonides on Karaite influence on women's immersion (ch. 10). *Guardrail: no mikveh content, so do not use this.*
- **Purim.** In 1030 a rumour said the Karaites had burnt three Rabbanites in effigy at Purim, "a carnivalesque holiday involving ... the inversion of social roles". It was a libel, and the gaon banned the libellers (ch. 8, "The Second Rabbanite Rumor"). *Guardrail: effigies are excluded in the Ha-N doc. Note only that the Purim of inversion was known in the Fatimid world; do not stage it.*
