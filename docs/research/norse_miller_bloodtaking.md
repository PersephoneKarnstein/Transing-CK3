## 1. The argument in brief

- **Feud is a relationship, not a war.** Miller's working list: feud is hostile relations between groups recruited by kinship, household, neighbourhood or clientage; violence is small and controlled; liability is collective; turns alternate; and "people keep score" (pp. 180-81). Icelandic has no word for "feud". People spoke of "dealings", "cases" and "coldness" (p. 181).
- **The native model is balanced exchange.** "It was a model of balance and reciprocity" (p. 182). The vocabulary of gift-exchange is inverted, so a spear thrown or an insult given is a "gift" that makes the receiver a debtor. Bergthora goads her sons: "Gifts have been given to you, to father as well as sons, and your manhood will suffer unless you repay them" (*Njála* 44, p. 182).
- **The model escalates and funds itself.** In the stylised Bergthora-Hallgerd feud each killing is one rank higher than the last (slave, freedman, kin). Compensation does not close it: "the compensation received is available to finance the return blow" (p. 184). Njal pays for Kol with the same purse he received for Svart. The feud went on because the husbands did not include "the real parties of interest ... the women" in the settlement (p. 184).
- **Balance is social, not arithmetic.** Worth is negotiated. A loyal freedman can be "balanced" against a free scoundrel, and every side overvalues its own dead (pp. 184, 200). The model explains after the fact. It cannot predict two things: *who* the target will be and *when* the blow comes (p. 185).
- **Feud only happens between equals.** "Feuding relations were congruent with the boundaries of the field of honor" (p. 185). There is no feud across strata. A slave who kills the master who castrated him is tortured and executed, and "that terminated the affair" (p. 185). On honour generally: "A free man did not generally compete for honor with a slave or servant, nor a man with a woman" (p. 32). A man of rank who is wronged by a lesser person disciplines the offender rather than feud with him (pp. 32-33).
- **Honour is scarce and zero-sum.** "Honor was a precious commodity in very short supply" (p. 30). It is socially judged: the "uninvolved" are the audience and decide who gained and who lost (p. 188). It has inertia, so the honoured can survive dishonourable acts (p. 31).
- **Timing is power.** Whoever holds the next move can make the other side fortify, enlarge its household and sleep in locked bed-closets (p. 193). But delay turns into shame: "Only a slave avenges himself immediately, but a coward never does" (*Grettis saga* 15, p. 193). Over-caution is also mocked (p. 194).
- **The target need not be the wrongdoer.** The law limits revenge to the liable. Custom lets it fall on brothers or cousins, and often on the other side's *best* man (Hrafnkel kills Eyvind, "greater than chieftains", pp. 198-201). Choosing the target is political. Limits: children, women and old men are not proper targets ("There is no renown when only women are at home", p. 207). Women did still die in burnings, and Miller suspects rape (pp. 207-9).
- **Inciting is a dependant's weapon, not a woman's nature.** "What was common to all inciters was not their sex but their dependence on the men they incited" (p. 212). Old men, servants and thingmen goaded too. The **bloody token**: Thorgerd digs up her husband's head and carries it under her cloak to shame her uncle into taking the case (pp. 211-12). *Not* goading could be a public political act. Jorunn's known silence let her husband deny that a killing was revenge for her father, which avoided open feud with a powerful patron (pp. 213-14).
- **Legitimacy has to be won.** Avengers manufactured grounds: planted goods, provoked insults, and "banked" claims to transfer (pp. 215-17). Old slights could be revived when the kin group was strong enough (pp. 217-18). "Legitimacy was something people had to struggle to acquire" (p. 219).
- **Arbitration beat law three to one.** In the saga record arbitrated outcomes outnumber adjudicated ones "by more than three to one" (p. 262). Compensation was available only through arbitration, not at law (p. 263). Arbitration never imposed full outlawry (p. 275). Settlements "balanced corpse against corpse, insult against injury" so that little wealth changed hands, "but if wealth was not always changing hands, honor was" (p. 277). *Mannjafnaðr*, the "evening of men", is used both for balancing corpses and for the competitive verbal duel (pp. 277, 301).
- **Peacemakers are a third force.** At one confiscation court, the two sides of 400 and 100 men faced 200 local "men of good will" gathered "for the peacemaking" (p. 267). Bystanders were expected to separate fighters, women included, by throwing clothes over weapons (p. 260). People caught between both sides make peace. Hence the aim of crosscutting ties through marriage and fostering: "Most felt that peace stood a better chance when allegiances were confused and intertwined" (p. 266).
- **Self-judgment** (*sjálfdæmi*) is a grant of the right to set your own award. The grant is itself paid "in units of prestige and honor", so the award should be moderate (p. 287). Thorgils accepts self-judgment from a kinsman, sets a large sum, then remits it all: generosity as humiliation (p. 287). Snorri goði sets high damages instead of exile because exile sentences "are often disregarded": "I wish to award money damages so high that no man will have been dearer than Hoskuld" (*Njála* 123, pp. 275-76).
- **The truce-breaker.** The *griðníðingr*, "the betrayer of a truce, one of the lowest of the low" (p. 280). Breaking a settlement is worse than leaving your father unavenged (p. 280).
- **Fostering.** Children of farm households were "so frequently sent out" that being raised at home was noteworthy (p. 122). There were three types: placed with a lesser household (patronage), placed between equals "to heal breaches ... or to confirm and buttress settlements", and poor kin pushed on richer kin by law (p. 123). "He who fosters another's child is always considered the lesser man" (*Laxdæla* 27, p. 172). Njal offers to foster the son of a man his sons killed: kindness, and also "a little like a hostage" (p. 172). The foster bond runs "as deep as, if not deeper than, that between parent and child" (p. 171). Foster-children avenge foster-parents, and the law gives the right to kill for sexual assault on a foster-daughter or foster-mother (p. 171).
- **Sworn brotherhood.** *Fóstbróðir* means both foster-brother and blood-brother, so both carry the duty to avenge. The rite is passing under a raised strip of turf and mingling blood (*Gísla saga* 6, p. 173). It vanished after the Saga Age. In a Christian-era pact, the parties swap blood for "self-judgment or outlawry and compensation" as "better suited to Christian men" (*Bjarnar saga* 29, p. 173).
- **Kin groups are not corporate.** Kindreds are bilateral and ego-focused, and are recruited case by case (pp. 140-42). There is no formal way to expel a kinsman: "the collectivity ... from which formal expulsion was the ultimate sanction, was the society itself", that is, outlawry (p. 175). Kin repudiate a troublemaker by not avenging him (pp. 175-77). Inversion: c. 996 kin were to prosecute Christian kinsmen because "Christianity was then considered a shame to one's kin" (*Kristni saga*, p. 177).
- **Outlawry in practice.** Full outlawry means loss of all legal standing and property, and the prosecutor is obliged to kill the outlaw (p. 234). The confiscation court (*féránsdómr*) is a third armed confrontation, and an outlawed chieftain with 400 men can stop it (p. 235). "An outlawry judgment isolated the vengeance target and eroded his support" (p. 238). Aiding outlaws was really prosecuted (p. 238). Outlawry narrows liability, so the outlaw's kin are relieved and tend to drop him (pp. 238-39). Killing three outlaws earns an outlaw a reprieve (p. 239). The outlaw's dependants became a charge on the Quarter (p. 152). Outlaws sought "service in households far removed from their enemies" (p. 237).
- **Christianity** created clerical peacemakers and "a politics of forgiveness" that cost less honour than before (p. 268). Late in the Commonwealth, feud grew into war between big men, paid for by small farmers (p. 218).
- **Honour includes peace.** The plural of *sœmð* ("honour") means "compensation payment" (p. 303). But "honor is the ability to make others believe that you will indeed be tough the next time" (p. 303).
