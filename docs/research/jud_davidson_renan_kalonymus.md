## 1. The arguments in brief

- **Davidson.** Hebrew parody became "a distinct branch" of the literature "between 1319 and 1332" (p. 19), when three works appeared:
  - Kalonymus's *Massekhet Purim*;
  - Levi ben Gershon's *Sefer Habakbuk ha-Navi*;
  - Levi's *Megillat Setarim*.

  All three are Purim parodies of the Talmud, licensed by the day and not meant to disparage. Parody then decayed for nearly three centuries (p. 29). The exception is a small 15th-c. Provençal group about the Purim King (pp. 30–31).
- **Renan–Neubauer.** Kalonymus was "the most active translator of Provence" (p. 71 [417]). They build his career from the colophons of his translations, and keep the pessimistic moralist of *Even Boḥan* apart from the cheerful Roman parodist of *Massekhet Purim* (pp. 102–108 [448–454]).

### 2.2 *Even Boḥan* (Renan–Neubauer no. III, pp. 101–106 [447–452])

- **Date.** The text ends "in the tenth month" (Tevet) of the year 83 of the sixth millennium, i.e. 5083 (p. 101 [447]). The "83" is the year, not his age. Tevet 5083 falls in **December 1322 to January 1323**. Renan says 1322. Chotzner's "about 1324" and the 83→38 emendation are both superseded. **Use "1322" (or "winter 1322–23").**
- **Place.** He finished it in Catalonia (p. 76 [422]; p. 105 [451]). It is dedicated to ten men he met on his travels, at Besalú, Girona, Barcelona and Tarragona. Catalonia is "the country Calonymos had chosen to settle in, or at least always thought of" (p. 105 [451], paraphrased). One dedicatee is "Maestro Bendig", perhaps Meïr Bendig *of Arles* (p. 105 [451]).
- **His age and state.** He calls himself young (§3). §17 suggests he was unmarried: "you mastered me in the love of women" (p. 103 [449], Renan's French).
- **Structure,** in the Sulzbach paragraphing (p. 102 [448]):
  - §§1–22: introduction and address to the heart;
  - §§22–33: past piety against present conduct, including "woe to him who has boys", since a girl is free of the 613 commandments (p. 103 [449]);
  - **§§33–42: "the feasts and half-feasts, which in his time were not kept with full rigour"** (p. 103 [449]). This is the section that holds the Purim revel (Lemberg pp. 24–31);
  - §§42–56: the vanities of learning, among them physicians who "prescribe only to earn money" and astrologers;
  - §§56–60: a prayer;
  - §§90–99: a debate between soul and body;
  - end: the sufferings of Israel (pp. 103–104 [449–450]).
- **The persecutions named:** the Shepherds (1320), the lepers (1321), and the Talmud burned at Toulouse by Bernard Gui on 29 Dec. 1319 (p. 104 [450]).
- **Renan says nothing of the turning prayer or of the Purim dress.** His summary of §§22–33 stops at the girl's freedom from commandments. Renan is no witness for either. The witnesses are the text itself (Lemberg pp. 17–18, 29) and Davidson (§2.4).
- **Editions:** Naples 1489; Venice 1546; Cremona 1558; Sulzbach 1705 (with a Yiddish version); Fürth, n.d.; Lemberg 1865; Meisel's German, Budapest 1878 (p. 106 [452]).

### 2.3 *Massekhet Purim* (Renan–Neubauer no. IV, pp. 106–109 [452–455]; Davidson pp. 19–23, 131–134)

- **Place: Rome.** "Several allusions ... leave no room for doubt" (Renan p. 106–107 [452–453]). Zunz once proposed Ancona, because of a "Yeḥiel of Ancona" in the text (p. 108 [454]).
- **Date.**
  - Zunz, Steinschneider and Gross give 1319–1322.
  - Renan "keeps the same reserve" as Graetz, who gives no date (p. 107 [453]).
  - If the Rome stay came after 1322 (Renan), then *Massekhet Purim* is **later than** *Even Boḥan*.
  - Davidson follows Zunz: Rome, "sometime between 1319 and 1322" (p. 133).
  - **Use "Rome, 1320s".**
- **Content** (Davidson pp. 19–23):
  - It parodies tractate Megillah and Talmudic style.
  - It ends by saying it was "written in mere fun, to amuse people on Purim" (p. 19).
  - Children are "taught to fight" on Purim (p. 20).
  - It mocks the drunkard, the glutton, the miser, the idler and the professional beggar. But "nowhere is the state of society attacked in as wholesale a manner as in the Touch Stone" (p. 20).
  - Roman customs: riding through the streets on horseback with pine branches; merrymaking round a raised **Haman puppet** with shouts of vengeance and trumpets, called *ira* (pp. 20–21); Purim gifts to children "just then introduced" in Rome; "dances and games, chiefly the game of chess"; twenty-seven dishes, macaroni among them (p. 22).
- **Same-sex dancing.** Chotzner's report that the rabbis allowed dancing only "with partners of their own sex" (`jud_kalonymus_even_bohan.md` §4) is **not** in Davidson's or Renan's summaries. It rests on Chotzner alone, and is Roman in any case.
- **Reception.** "The more conservative element were indignant" (Davidson p. 20). Most of the first edition (Pesaro, 1507–1520) "was destroyed by the Jews themselves" (Renan p. 108 [454]); the second edition was Venice 1552.

### 2.4 Davidson's version of the prayer (p. 28)

- Davidson gives a verse version of the turning prayer from *Even Boḥan* (citing Cremona f. 8a; Lemberg p. 17). **Unlike Chotzner's, it keeps the turnings list, Dinah included** (with a footnote to Berakhot 60a), and the petition ("O, that Thou wouldst refashion me a lass").
- In n. 67 he notes that "the greater part" of Chotzner's 1900 rendering "is not found in" the original. This independently confirms `jud_kalonymus_even_bohan.md` §4.
- He frames the passage as "that humorous part of the satire" (p. 28).
- **Avoid:** his verse includes "Until I have run my race", his rendering of עד אגוע. That falls under the no-suicide rule, as in the Kalonymus note. Do not quote Davidson's English in loc.

### 2.5 Purim parody in Provence after Kalonymus

- **Levi ben Gershon,** called Leon de Bagnols, of Orange and Avignon. He wrote *Sefer Habakbuk* before 1332 and *Megillat Setarim* in 1332 (Davidson p. 133). It opens with "the Drunkard's Chain of Tradition" (p. 23). Renan–Neubauer: a Vatican MS owned by Vidal Bonafoux lists a "Scroll of Mysteries for Purim, by the learned Maestro Léon de Bañolas" (p. 254 [600]). The same parody is in an Avignon-provenance Bodleian MS, with **four sentences in Provençal** (p. 255 [601]). Renan thought Levi might have cut Kalonymus's Roman names "to adapt it to the use of the communities of Provence" (p. 255 [601]); Davidson rejects this (p. 24). Either way, **Provence had its own Talmud-parody for Purim by 1332**, from its most respected scholar.
- **The Purim King** (Davidson pp. xv, 30–31):
  - Three MSS from the first half of the 15th century hold parodies by a Provençal author, "certain" from his Provençal words (p. 30).
  - One is a set of 32 by-laws of the Purim King, "ruler over the Vineyard Community", who makes all other kings swear to the laws of "the Prophet of the Bottle" and levies fines for his own benefit (pp. 30–31).
  - An anonymous *Massekhet Purim*, "perhaps written in Provence during the fifteenth century", has every town elect a Purim King a month before Purim, "with full power over the lives and property of his subjects" (p. 30, paraphrased). It also gives a mock biblical origin for dice, and mentions cards, chess and backgammon (p. 31).
  - Davidson's introduction links the Provençal Purim to "the Feast of Fools" (p. xv).
- **Israel Caslari** (Maestre Crescas de Caslar), a physician at Avignon, wrote a poem on Esther and Mordecai **in the vernacular first, then in Hebrew**. Renan says it was the custom to write such poems in both tongues, the vernacular "being meant for the women and children" (p. 303 [649]). The date is c. 1327 if Zunz's second identification holds, c. 1490 on his first. A fragment of a Provençal poem attributed to Crescas was printed in *Romania* (1892).

### 2.6 Arles and the region (Renan–Neubauer)

- Arabic "was still cultivated at Arles, at Tarascon, at Perpignan", and Robert of Anjou "was the instigator of all this work". Many Hebrew authors dedicated works to him (p. 6 [352]).
- In the Caspi article Renan calls Arles "the great city of the time in the eyes of the Jews" (p. 160 [506]).
- Montpellier had Jewish students by the mid-14th century (p. 5 [351]; *unverified* page, read across the page break).

## 3. Davidson's "sixteenth century" and *Even Boḥan* p. 29: the reconciliation

- Davidson dates "the custom of masquerading on Purim" among European Jews "as early as the sixteenth century" (p. xiv). His footnote 5 cites **Orach Ḥayyim 696** (Isserles's gloss) and an article in *MGWJ* 46, pp. 181–182. **He dated the custom from its first codification, not its first description.**
- He knew *Even Boḥan* and translated its prayer (p. 28), but he did not use the festival satire (Lemberg p. 29) as evidence.
- His word is "masquerading", i.e. masks and costume. Isserles names two things: "masks" (פרצופים) and men in women's dress. *Even Boḥan* p. 29 has the second (a woman's dress, anklets, necklaces) and a basket for a hat, but **no masks**.
- **Conclusion.**
  - **Masks** on Purim: unattested in these sources before the 16th century. Keep masks out of the medieval Revel.
  - **Men in women's dress** on Purim: attested c. 1322 in one satirical text (*Even Boḥan* p. 29), written or finished in Catalonia by a Provençal author.
  - Before that, a Purim drag in a mock-punishment parade is documented at Manosque in 1306 (Horowitz pp. 265–268; see the companion note).
  - Its **halakhic permission** is 16th-century (Minz d. 1508, Isserles), after the game ends.

## 4. Is the built Revel accurate, for c. 1322 and before?

Built text (`tm_hipukh.0011.desc`): the young men of the street riot in women's anklets and necklaces, one wears a basket for a hat, one a woman's dress, "for the joke". Then: "The rabbis allow it, for merriment only."

| | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **c. 1322 onward** | The dress, the anklets, the necklaces, the basket-hat and the all-male riot are **accurate to *Even Boḥan* p. 29**. That is a single witness, satirical, and its place is uncertain: Kalonymus was Arlesian, but finished the book in Catalonia. Davidson and Renan add nothing to the scene, and nothing against it. **"The rabbis allow it" is not accurate**: no source in either book (nor Horowitz) has a rabbi permitting it in the period. Kalonymus himself mocks it. |
| **867 to c. 1322** | **Not attested.** The Purim these sources give for the early centuries is the Megillah, wine (Megillah 7b), the feast, gifts, noise at Haman's name, and, in Babylonia, Haman burned in effigy (Davidson p. xv; p. 21 n. 33). In Provence and Italy around 1300 the custom was to draw Haman's name or image on wood or stone and strike it until it was effaced. Davidson cites Aaron ha-Kohen of Lunel's *Orḥot Ḥayyim* (p. 21 n. 33, Hebrew garbled; date of the work c. 1300–1330, *unverified*). |

## 5. Corrections to current docs

1. **"Arles is his birthplace only"** (`jud_kalonymus_even_bohan.md` §1; `faith_design.md` §13 revision 2; the README). This is too strong. Arles was where he **worked** from 1307 to 1317, made all but one of his Hebrew translations, and was back by 1328 (Renan p. 74 [420], p. 77 [423]). Revised wording: "born at Arles and a translator there; *Even Boḥan* was finished in Catalonia." This strengthens the Arles seat.
2. **"At Avignon from about 1314"** (Jewish Encyclopedia, reading list Part B finding 5). Renan's colophon list puts one translation at Avignon and the rest at Arles. Prefer Renan.
3. **"Salonica"** is Salon (Renan p. 73 [419]). This confirms the reading list's guess.
4. **Date of *Even Boḥan*:** Tevet 5083, winter 1322–23 (Renan p. 101 [447]). "c. 1322–24" can tighten to "1322". The in-game 1322.1.1 gate is about a year early; it is harmless, since the gate is for flavour.
5. **The embassy to the pope** (Chotzner; Jewish Encyclopedia "disputed"). Renan rejects it as Kalonymus's (pp. 75–77 [421–423]). **Do not** have the sect claim Kalonymus saved the Jews of Rome.
6. **Robert of Naples:** Kalonymus made Latin translations for him, probably in Rome, probably **after** 1322 (Renan). The `tm_hipukh.0030` premise (a translator from Arabic serving a prince) stands. Note that Kalonymus's own service for Robert was into Latin.
7. ***Massekhet Purim*'s same-sex dancing rule** rests on Chotzner alone (§2.3). Keep it out of the sect unless verified in the Hebrew.

## 6. Design implications

- **The kahal head's title.** Kalonymus's father was *Nasi* of Arles (Chotzner), and a "R. Samuel, bearing the title Nasi, probably living at Arles" reclaimed him from Rome (Renan p. 74 [420]). `scope:tm_kahal_head` in Arles can be styled "the Nasi" in `tm_hipukh.0003` flavour: "The Nasi of the street has called me before the worthies." Elsewhere use "the worthies".
- **Scroll in the common tongue (`tm_hipukh.0010`).** This is now grounded twice in the region: Caslari's vernacular Esther, made "for the women and children" (Renan p. 303 [649]), and the Provençal sentences in the Purim parody MSS. The elder explaining the scroll in the women's gallery in the common tongue fits. A sect line could be: "The scroll was put into our tongue for the women. I am one of those it was put there for."
- **Purim King** (post-1400, optional). A yearly flavour event in a county with `tm_kahal`: the street elects a Purim King a month before Adar. If a daughter of the Turning is elected "king" for the day, the joke turns on the title. The sources' Purim King is a man's mock office, so this is the sect's inversion, not a claim about the period. Gate it at ≥1400 (Davidson pp. 30–31). The *Massekhet Purim* games (dice, chess, cards, backgammon) can flavour the feast.
- **Levi ben Gershon** (Orange and Avignon) as a reaction voice. He is a Provençal Purim parodist and a serious philosopher-astronomer (Renan p. 6 [352]). A scholarly character modelled on him (not named as him) can give the "it was a jest" line in the elders' event: "The great men of Provence write Purim tractates. They do not keep the dress."
- **Narrator.** Keep both readings of Kalonymus open. Davidson calls the prayer "humorous" (p. 28). Renan sees *Even Boḥan* as the work of a sad young man who "spares not himself" (p. 102 [448], paraphrased), written in the bitterest year for Provençal Jews.
- **Bibliography.** Add both works to the README, with Renan–Neubauer as the source for the life.
