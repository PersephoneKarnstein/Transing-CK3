# Lumbley, "Imperatrix, Domina, Rex" (2019): design reading

Source: Coral Lumbley, "Imperatrix, Domina, Rex: Conceptualizing the Female King in Twelfth-Century England," *Medieval Feminist Forum: A Journal of Gender and Sexuality* 55, no. 1 (2019): 64–99. DOI: [10.17077/1536-8742.2149](https://doi.org/10.17077/1536-8742.2149). Volume, issue and pages are verified from the PDF's footer. The DOI comes from the journal's issue page (scholarworks.wmich.edu/mff/vol55/iss1/4) because the PDF does not print one.

Read in full. Quotes are verbatim. The subject is **Empress Matilda** (1102–1167), not Urraca, though Urraca of León-Castile and Melisende of Jerusalem appear as comparanda (pp. 70–71). Primary sources: the *Gesta Stephani* (c. 1148 and after 1153, pro-Stephen, perhaps by Robert of Bath), Matilda's charters, and her round royal seal (Society of Antiquaries cast, 63 mm). Framing is in `medtrans_kim_bychowski_visions.md`.

**Scope note.** Matilda is a woman claiming a man's office: female masculinity and the "female king". She is not a trans woman, and Lumbley herself uses "gender status" rather than "gender identity" because "we lack the evidence" for how Matilda identified (p. 66 n. 6). For this mod the article is **high-relevance for succession, titles and hostile chronicle rhetoric**, and **low-relevance as a trans-feminine precedent**.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **"Matilda was neither a king, nor a queen"** (p. 68). Queenship studies centre the *consors regni*. Female rulers fall "through the cracks of history" (Martin on Urraca, p. 70). Following Martin, Lumbley argues that the twelfth century "was the last moment when the model of a queen as king was possible" (p. 71).
- **Historians double-bind her.** Tolhurst: "she is always wrong: she is both too feminine in her weaknesses and too masculine in her aggressive exercise of power" (p. 71).
- **The hostility targets comportment more than female rule.** Henry I named her heir and had the barons swear to her twice (pp. 72, 79–80). The *Gesta* never says that a woman cannot rule. It attacks her gait, speech and face once she takes London: "every trace of a woman's gentleness removed from her face" (pp. 86–87). Lumbley's thesis: detractors "may not have hated the theoretical political idea of a crowned woman nearly as much as the reality of a masculine woman on the throne" (p. 85).
- **Misnaming is the weapon.** The *Gesta* calls her *comitissa Andegauensis*, Countess of Anjou, a wife's title she rejected. She styled herself *imperatrix*, "daughter of King Henry and Lady of the English" (*domina*), and avoided *regina* in charters (pp. 79, 88–89).
- **The acceptable "third gender" is the virago who serves men.** The *Gesta* praises Stephen's queen Maude, who "bore herself with the valor of a man", but only in service of her husband and son. It "acknowledges with pleasure the idea of a third gender, under very specific conditions" (pp. 81–82).
- **Her male supporters are called effeminate.** Her adherents, the Earl of Warwick and Robert d'Oilli, are *uiri molles*, and Miles de Beauchamp is *laxus et effeminatus* (p. 84). The *Gesta* links her bid for London to the penetration of a feminine city, a reading in which "homoeroticism [is] an innately trans-ing phenomenon" (p. 76).
- **Supporters also had to feminise her.** Gilbert Foliot grounds her right in obedience to her father. Robert of Gloucester is persuaded by the Book of Numbers, where daughters inherit from fathers (p. 81).
- **The seal is a transgender object.** Of twelfth-century Anglo-Norman noblewomen's seals, 87 percent were vesica-shaped, with a standing woman (p. 91). Matilda's seal is **round, with a seated, crowned, sceptred figure** in her father's pose, knees apart, but in a woman's robes (pp. 92, 97). The legend reads "MATHILDIS DEI GRATIA ROMANORUM REGINA" (p. 95). On English seals after 1100 a seated woman "almost invariably represented ... the Virgin Mary" (p. 97). Seals were "extensible embodiment": men pressed teeth or "actual hairs plucked from his beard" into the wax (Bedos-Rezak, p. 93).
- **Coda.** The *Gesta* scorns her for refusing to rise for kneeling magnates, and her seal shows her "obstinately sitting" (pp. 98–99).

### Historical characters and events
- **Empress Matilda** and **Queen Maude (Matilda of Boulogne)** are in vanilla history for the Anarchy (1135–1154). Do **not** make either a sister. Possible ambient event: a Sisters' ruler at peace with England in 1139–1148 receives "a letter sealed in round wax", and the House reads Matilda's seal as a sign that a woman may sit. Flavour only.
- **Urraca of León-Castile** (r. 1109–1126) and **Melisende of Jerusalem** (r. 1131–1153) are the other twelfth-century queens regnant named (pp. 70–71). They are useful only as cited precedents in loc.
- **No trans-feminine primary case** in this article.

### Methodological cautions
- **Gender status, not identity** (p. 66 n. 6). When the mod's text invokes historical figures, it should say what they did and how they were named, not what they "were".
- **Chronicles are propaganda.** The *Gesta*'s portrait is "strategically calculated" (p. 74), and William of Malmesbury's account of the same retreat differs (p. 87). Hostile in-game chronicles should read as partisan.
- **"Transcended her sex" compliments reproduce the hierarchy.** Lumbley warns against praising women for rising above their gender, because it keeps Man as Up (p. 86). The faith's own text should never praise a daughter for being "more than a woman".
