The rest of the casebook (officials, taxes, schools, crime) was listed from the contents and not read.

- Introduction (pp. 1–36): the text, its authors, Song law and the family.

- Part 2, Taxes and Services.

**No nuns.** The translated half has no case about a Buddhist nun or a female Daoist. The only clergy are monks, abbots and an unordained "temple supplicant". Claims about nüguan property law cannot be sourced to this book.

## 1. The argument and the law the cases cite

McKnight's frame: Song judges were not reluctant to rule on civil matters. The state "had issued a large number of rules governing" marriage, divorce, adoption and inheritance (p. 145), and the newly found full text shows "dozens of laws on civil matters" unknown before (p. 146). Many of these rules are quoted inside rulings and survive nowhere else.

### 1a. Adoption

- **The base rule** is same-lineage adoption of the right generation: "All those who are without sons or grandsons are permitted to adopt someone of the same patrilineal group who is of appropriate age and generation" (p. 216; again p. 276).
- **The foundling exception.** "All infants under three years old who are of different surnames may be adopted ... Legally they have the same status as natal sons and grandsons" (p. 243). The child takes the adopter's surname, and the adoption stands even if it was never registered, "if an official investigation verifies the facts" (p. 243; also pp. 280, 249 note).
- **Two different acts.** "To adopt a child during one's lifetime and to set up an heir to avoid extinction of the family line are two entirely different matters" (p. 273). A lifetime adoptee is a son; a posthumous heir (立繼) is appointed by others and gets less (see 1b).
- **Women choose heirs.** "If the wife survives her husband, her wish is decisive in designating the heir" (p. 276). "Installing an heir should follow the orders of the grandparents and parents" (p. 235). A widow named the household head picks the heir over the lineage's candidate (p. 272). A matriarch blocks a cousin's suit and has him beaten (pp. 234–237).
- **Cross-surname adoption was common and contested.** McKnight: the casebook shows "adoptions from outside the surname group were a common phenomenon during the Sung" (p. 160). The rigid judges answer with the old maxim that "the spirits of the dead do not enjoy the sacrifices of those who are not of their kindred" (p. 249) and with the *Chunqiu* line that Zeng was "extinguished" when it took an heir of another surname (pp. 249, 275).
- **Proof of sonship is ritual.** Judges ask who wore the three years' mourning (pp. 216, 241), and whether the heir "waited upon his father in life and buried him" (p. 241). Long years without challenge also count.
- **Expulsion.** An adoptee who "squanders family property, does not take care of the adopting elders, or commits obvious offenses" may be sent back after an inquiry with the elders' testimony (p. 253; also pp. 284–285).
- **Fostered is not adopted.** A foster son (義子), such as a stepson who came with a remarried mother, inherits nothing and must "return to his natal descent group" (pp. 269–271, 282–284). Fan Zhongyan is the model: he took his stepfather's surname, then "wept and left" when he learned his birth family (pp. 282–283).
- **Lots before incense.** When two cousins both want the rich uncle's line and not the poor one, the judge has the boys "burn incense and draw lots, letting Heaven decide" (p. 221; also pp. 222–225, 287).

### 1b. Extinct households (戶絕 *hujue*): who gets the goods

This is the section the design needs. The rulings quote a full schedule.

- **Daughters at home come first.** "When a family line comes to an end, its property is wholly given to the girls who are still at home, with those who have returned to the home being given half shares" (p. 244).
- **A posthumous heir gets a fraction** (Fan Xitang, pp. 297; schedule quoted from the regulations on extinct households):
  - with unmarried daughters: heir ¼, daughters the rest;
  - with unmarried and returned daughters: heir ⅕, daughters ⅘;
  - with returned daughters only: they take their legal share plus half the remainder, "with the other half being confiscated by the authorities";
  - with married daughters only: heir and married daughters split ⅔, and ⅓ "shall be confiscated by the government";
  - with no daughters at all: heir ⅓, capped at 3,000 strings (plus 2,000 if the estate reaches 20,000). McKnight's note: the balance "presumably was confiscated by the state" (p. 532 n. 182).
- **Where there is nobody**, the baseline is total confiscation. One judge says that by statute "the whole estate should go to the government" and a posthumous heir gets a quarter (p. 153).
- **Judges kept waiving it.** In the Xiong case the court "does not wish to follow the usual precedent of taking over the estate" and splits it three ways by lot, "not in accord with the legislative intention. However, the government prefers to be kind" (p. 153). Hu Shipi gives a daughter half and refuses the state's half: "I have an aversion to such a measure, because it might seem that the government had done it for profit" (p. 291).
- **Confiscation as a racket.** A district declared a household extinct although the widow had an adopted son under three, sold the land, and kept the money. The judicial commissioner reverses it: the officials were "tempted by the trifling benefit" and both they and the informer "were after the profits" (p. 281). A buyer of confiscated land informs in the hope of buying cheap (pp. 279–282, 294).
- **A remarried widow keeps the estate.** A widow with no heirs who takes a second husband may keep the usufruct of the first husband's estate up to 5,000 strings; "the law on extinct households applies only when the woman willingly wishes to join the family of her second husband or when she dies" (p. 281).
- **Married-in sons-in-law** get three-tenths of an extinct household if they increased it (p. 243). Another judge gives uxorilocal sons-in-law half, "because the married daughters are the family's own blood" (p. 225).

### 1c. Women's property

- Daughters take half a son's share: "the sons and daughters divide the property, with the daughters receiving shares half as large as those of their brothers" (p. 286).
- Dowry is the wife's: "Property acquired from the wife's family does not belong to the husband's family for its division" (p. 169), though the husband manages it.
- Limits on widows: "A widow without an heir or whose heir is less than sixteen years of age is not permitted to sell land" (p. 171). A widow may make a will only where there is no heir, and only to relatives of three months' mourning or closer (p. 172).
- A concubine is made manager of her late master's estate for her two daughters, and the cash goes to the girls "when they come of age, not when they marry" (McKnight's headnote, p. 259).
- A daughter and son-in-law who nursed and buried the parents inherit over an estranged son (pp. 156–157): care outranks descent.

### 1d. The adopted daughter's estate (the event the brief asked for)

**Hsieh Ch'i-ku 解七姑, "Disposition of the Landed Property Inheritance of Female Orphans"** (Fan Xitang, CMC 8/287–89; pp. 294–299).
- Hsieh Ju-lin and his wife died in a northern invasion near Xiangyang (probably the 1220s Jin attacks, p. 532 n. 179). His household went into captivity.
- Two girls came back years later: his **adopted daughter** Ch'i-ku, born surname Zheng, now 25 and unmarried, and his granddaughter Hsiu-niang. An officer at Xiangyang had raised Hsiu-niang for eight years after finding her on the battlefield.
- A cousin managed the land, set up a boy of another surname as heir, and kept the girls unmarried. An outsider sued for ten years to have the household declared extinct and confiscated. Meanwhile the prefecture had already taken 1,000 strings of rent.
- The ruling: "Since the two girls are here, by law they should inherit. It is not right for the government to collect the rental income" (p. 295). On the adopted daughter: "Ch'i-ku, although originally surnamed Cheng, had been adopted by Hsieh Ju-lin himself as his daughter. Her status is the same as that of a natural daughter" (p. 297).
- The heir gets ¼. Ch'i-ku and Hsiu-niang each get ⅜, each by "certificate ... kept as the record of her property", and the shares are drawn by lot (pp. 298–299).

### 1e. Clergy and temple property

- **Pushing a child into the clergy for his share is a crime.** Inducing or compelling "a family member ... to become a novice attendant, a Buddhist monk, or a Taoist monk, as a way of getting control of property" earns 100 blows (p. 166). Ho Te-mao, sent to a temple at 14, came back to lay life and got half the estate. A laicized cleric takes a share of undivided ancestral property (p. 167).
- **Temple property reverts to the community, not the state** (K'ai-fu Temple, P'eng Ts'ang-fang, pp. 393–397). The temple had 23 halls, and eight had died out. If a hall "came to an end then its property reverted to its permanent residents" (常住) of the main temple (p. 395). Four guest monks tried to split a dead hall off, and the district gave its land to a school. The ruling returned it, citing a 1165 edict that temple land seized by "powerful and wealthy families ... was all to be returned to the temples" (pp. 395–396). The temple was an imperial-birthday ritual site, and that weighed heavily.
- **The reverse case.** A cloister sued for land after a century. The judge notes that the land had been confiscated and sold as an extinct household's property, and "if the religious community had not come to an end, it would certainly not have sold the property" (p. 158). A temple that dies loses its estate.
- **The state appoints and moves abbots** (pp. 391–393).
- **Unregistered religion is the crime.** The edicts quoted against a Lotus Hall congregation (pp. 476–477):
  - those who "practice vegetarianism, worship demons, gather at night and disperse at dawn" to transmit a "devilish faith" are strangled;
  - night chanting "in mixed company" earns three years;
  - "Those who are neither Buddhists nor Taoists but who gather as a scripture group" get 100 blows each.
  The leader is tattooed and exiled. The followers are pardoned. The three halls are turned into rain-prayer shrines with Buddhist monks put in as watchmen (p. 478).
- **Licentious temples** (淫祠) were torn down for building timber (p. 479). A human-sacrifice cult's family property was "confiscated for use as a fund for paying rewards" (p. 482). Here confiscation follows a crime, not the lack of an heir.
- **Clan estates.** McKnight's introduction: Fan Zhongyan founded the first endowed clan estate (義莊), revenues for "shared ritual feasts, schools for the children, and relief for poorer members" (p. 10). It is the elite, Confucian twin of a temple society.

**Usable events and characters (all Southern Song, 1220s–1250s, so the Song years of the CK3 era):**
- **Hsieh Ch'i-ku**: an adopted daughter, born Zheng, back from northern captivity, 25 and kept unmarried by a cousin who holds her land. The court rules she inherits like a natural daughter (pp. 294–299). Her niece Hsiu-niang, raised by a frontier officer, is in the same case. This is the brief's "lawsuit over an adopted daughter's estate".
- **The woman Kan** (two different women of that surname): a widow with a second husband whose estate a district wrongly confiscated (pp. 279–282).
- **Mistress Mao**: a widow at 23 who adopted an outsider and held the estate for 18 years against her husband's nephew (pp. 246–251).
- **The K'ai-fu Temple's dead halls** (pp. 393–397): a template for a Temple Society defending a dead sister-house's land against outsiders and a district that wants it for a school.

## 3. In-world loc lines (attribute in-world)

- Temple Societies desc, revised: "The magistrate takes what a house with no daughter leaves. So every sister of the Way has a daughter, and what no daughter holds, the society holds for all of them."
- Magistrate (hostile): "This household is cut off. The land goes to the state."
- Answer (Way): "She has a daughter. The Code says a daughter taken in is the same as one born."
- Commissioner reversing the confiscation: "Your clerks were tempted by a trifling profit. Return the land to the widow."
- The lot: "Light the incense. Let Heaven choose the Elder Sister."
- Hostile edict (Lotus Hall voice): "They eat no meat, gather at night and scatter at dawn, men and women together. They are neither Buddhist nor Daoist."
- Way's answer: "We are Daoist. Our nüguan are ordained and our House is registered. Count us."
