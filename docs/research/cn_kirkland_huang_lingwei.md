## 1. What the sources say

**Sources for her life.** No dynastic history has a biography of Huang. Everything comes from two inscriptions that Yan Zhenqing 顏真卿 wrote as prefect of Fuzhou 撫州 in 769:
- 《撫州臨川縣井山華姑仙壇碑銘》 (*Quan Tang wen* 340.1a–3b);
- the stele for Lady Wei's altar, 〈晉紫虛元君領上真司命南嶽夫人仙壇碑銘〉 (*QTW* 340.17a–22b).
- Both Chinese titles above are our rendering of Kirkland's romanisations; check them against *QTW* before publishing.
- Du Guangting later reworked them in the *Yongcheng jixian lu* (*Yunji qiqian* 115.9b–12a). Kirkland: "ignored by the official historians" (entry, p. 1).

**Life.**
- **Origins.** Nothing is known of her family. Yan names no husband or children. She was "ordained as a *daoshi*" at **twelve**, which Kirkland calls plausible.
- **The search for the shrine.** Then come nearly forty silent years. At about fifty she began searching for the lost shrine of **Lady Wei Huacun** 魏華存 (251–334), the Celestial Master libationer of the Shangqing revelations, styled Lady of the Southern Marchmount (Nanyue furen, 南嶽夫人). She found it late in **693**, with directions from a theurgist, Hu Huichao. She excavated religious artifacts, and **"the empress Wu heard of Huang's discovery and confiscated all the artifacts"** (Section II).
- **The second shrine.** "Undaunted, Huang went on to locate and restore a second shrine at the nearby Mount of the Well (Ching-shan)". She kept the *zhai* (fasts) there for nearly thirty years.
- **Death.** In 721/22 she told her disciples not to nail her coffin shut, only to cover it with crimson gauze. Lightning tore the gauze and the roof. The coffin held "no body, but only the shroud and Huang's 'screed'": deliverance from the corpse (*shijie*) (Section II).
- **Disciples and transmission.**
  - One disciple is named: a woman, **Li Qiongxian** (characters not given by Kirkland). "Li and other women apparently maintained the shrines at Lin-ch'uan for some years", while male *daoshi* performed the *zhai* and *jiao*. The shrines then fell into disuse.
  - Yan learned of the story only because people in 769 were talking of a female *daoshi*, **Zeng Miaoxing**, who had met Li and had a vision of Huang (Section II).
- **State sanction, in Yan's second text.**
  - Between 710 and 712 Ruizong sent a *daoshi* to restore the rites, "then had a monastery built and **seven female tao-shih ordained there**".
  - Xuanzong had *jiao* and *zhai* performed there, had her yearly "winds and clouds" entered in a lost *Houxian zhuan*, and in 741 sent "a tao-shih bearing the imperial insignia" for a *jiao*. The prefect raised a stele.
- **How Yan portrays her.** He casts her as humble, pious and brave, and is "quite comfortable in eliciting readers' approval of a woman who passed beyond the 'traditional' norms of female behavior".
- **882.** A rescript of Xizong, quoted by Du Guangting, names Huang "an immortal who had descended from heaven". Kirkland says there is "little evidence that anyone in the eighth century really thought of her as a divine being", but by 882 the court academicians did (Section II).
- **What is not known.** She "apparently wrote nothing, and we know nothing more of her beliefs or practices" (entry, p. 2).

**What this shows about Tang nüguan (only what these two short texts show).**
- **Ordination.** Girls were ordained young. The throne decided both the number of ordinations and the place (seven women at one shrine).
- **Authority.** A priestess acted on her own initiative, finding and rebuilding holy sites, and gathered female disciples. Women held a shrine across at least two generations (Huang → Li → Zeng's memory).
- **Ritual.** The great public rites (*jiao*) were still done by male *daoshi* or court envoys.
- **Property.** The throne could seize the holy goods a priestess dug up. Kirkland's texts say nothing on how the land itself was held. For that, see Kohn's *Fengdao kejie* (reading list, §2 background).
- **Sites.** Her sites are the Huagu altar at Jingshan and Lady Wei's altar, both in Linchuan county, Fuzhou (Jiangxi).

**Could she be a character?** No. She died in 721/22, more than a century before the 867 start. She works as **remembered lore and an event chain**, with one date inside the game: **Xizong's rescript of 882**. Nothing in the sources bears on gender beyond her being a woman ordained young who never married. The faith may claim her as a foremother in its own voice; the narrator may not.
