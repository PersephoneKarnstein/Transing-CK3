# Balkwill, "The *Sūtra on Transforming the Female Form*" (2016), design reading

**Citation (verified against the PDF's title page and running heads).** Stephanie Balkwill, "The *Sūtra on Transforming the Female Form*: Unpacking an Early Medieval Chinese Buddhist Text," *Journal of Chinese Religions* 44.2 (November 2016): 127–148. Society for the Study of Chinese Religions / Taylor & Francis. DOI 10.1080/0737769X.2016.1157428 (published online 13 Oct 2016). ISSN 0737-769X.
**Read:** the whole article, pp. 127–148, including the synopsis table (pp. 136–138), notes and bibliography. Chinese quoted from the article's own Taishō citations (T564 = *Zhuan nüshen jing* 轉女身經); translations are Balkwill's unless marked "ours".

## 1. The argument in brief

- **What the text is.** T564, attributed to Dharmamitra, is "a composite work" stitched from T562/563 (the girl who speaks from the womb), T565/566 (the bodhisattva who transforms her female body) and T625 (*Questions of King Great Tree of the Kiṃnaras*), and "likely written in early medieval China" (p. 127). It is absent from Sengyou's catalogue (c. 515) and present in Fajing's (594), so it was made within "a window of some eighty years during the sixth century" (p. 134). (Balkwill's sentence there swaps "former" and "latter"; the window is c. 515–594. On p. 132 she also says it "entered the Chinese Buddhist textual milieu in the fifth century"; the 6th-century dating is her argued one.)
- **The story.** A girl, Taintless Light (無垢光女), listens to the Buddha from the womb, is born from her mother's right side "like a buddha", argues Śāriputra into knots on emptiness, then presses the Buddha himself. He gives ten reasons why a woman should become a man; she vows to; 500 nuns wish to become men; 75 householder wives become men "only after they and their husbands all 'leave home'" (p. 130). At the end she changes sex herself.
- **How the change happens: by vow and an act of truth, not by bodily work.** Her words: 「一切諸法無男，無女」，此言若實，令我女身化成男子！ — "'In all dharmas there is neither male nor female!' And with these true words, I command my woman's body to transform into a male one!" (p. 138, T564: 921b). "Her female form then melts away." The change is (1) the fruit of a prior vow, (2) performed by her own supernatural power, (3) sealed by a statement of truth. For the 75 wives it follows renunciation (*chujia* 出家). Merit sits behind all of it (the girl is already near buddhahood), but nothing is gestated, cultivated or cut.
- **She is right about emptiness and changes anyway.** Before Śāriputra she says "I have not now taken a male's shape and not taken a female's shape" (p. 130). The Buddha concedes "she is correct in her understanding of the emptiness of physical forms" yet argues she "must turn her form into that of a male one" (p. 130).
- **The new Chinese argument: the female body is a servant's body.** The accretion unique to T564 grounds the change "not in its inherent impurity but in the fact that women are not self-sovereign" (p. 143). Key phrase: 不得自在, "cannot obtain self-sovereignty" (p. 142). "The female body resembles that of a maidservant" (猶如婢使, p. 142).
- **The palace passage.** 雖生在王宮，必當屬他 — "a woman who is born inside the imperial palace ... necessarily belongs to another person"; she is "beaten by different kinds of swords and staves" and "defiled by every evil word" (p. 142). T563 has a parallel: "even a princess will also fear others" (n. 35). Balkwill reads this as a hint that the audience was "Buddhist women of both means and power, especially in the north of China" (p. 143).
- **The Buddha as advocate, with a catch.** "The Buddha ... has also become an advocate for women's rights, offering women a somewhat counter-intuitive escape" (p. 143): leave home, become a nun, be sovereign of one's fate, then become a man. The T625 emptiness argument is "blatantly misogynistic" because "this argument is never applied to a male body" (p. 141).
- **Ritual life.** The *Sūtra of the Buddha's Names* (T441), recited at repentance rites (*zhai* 齋) 6th–11th c., lists it and names a "Transforming the Female" buddha (轉女佛) and a "Transforming the Female Genitals" bodhisattva (轉女根菩薩) (n. 13). In Japan it was read at women's funerals and in elite women's vows (Faure, p. 131); in the 13th c. Sōji gave it to the Hokkeji nuns (p. 132).
- **Filial accretion.** The 75 wives were the girl's parents in past lives; she vowed to save them, and she brings her mother to the Path (p. 138). Balkwill reads this as Chinese filial piety written into a renunciation text.
- **What it does not say.** Nothing on Empress Wu, Dunhuang caves, or the Lotus girl beyond comparison. It is a 6th-century text; its reach into 867–1453 is through the *Buddha's Names* rites (to the 11th c.) and Korean/Japanese reception.

**Contradictions with current design:** none directly. It reinforces reading-list finding 1: medieval Chinese Buddhist sex change always ran woman to man.

**Usable as events/characters:** Taintless Light (in-world scripture, not a historical person); the 75 householder wives and husbands who leave home together; the *Buddha's Names* repentance rite naming the buddha "Transforming the Female" (a Buddhist neighbour's rite the Way can attend and contest).

## 3. In-world loc lines (faith voice or Buddhist characters; never narrator)
- "In all dharmas there is neither male nor female. The girl said so, and the Buddha did not deny it. Then why must the true words always end in a man?"
- "The sutra says a woman born in the palace belongs to another. So does a eunuch. The register is the same register."
- "She cannot be sovereign of herself, says the sutra, so she must leave her body. We say: leave the household, keep the body, and be sovereign in the society." (A nüguan answering a nun.)
- Buddhist nun, sympathetic: "I have read the sutra on turning the female form. You are walking the road in the wrong direction, sister, and I will still bring you rice."
- Buddhist monk, hostile: "Taintless Light shed that body with a sentence. You put on its ten defilements for ten months."
