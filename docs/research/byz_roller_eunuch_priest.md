# Roller, "The Ideology of the Eunuch Priest" (1997): design reading

**Citation (verified from the PDF).** Lynn E. Roller, "The Ideology of the Eunuch Priest," *Gender & History* 9.3 (November 1997): 542–559. Blackwell. DOI 10.1111/1468-0424.00075. **Read:** the whole article, including all 39 notes.

**Scope warning.** Roller states at the start that she studies how outsiders *perceived* the galli, not how the galli lived: "In no case does the voice of a eunuch survive" (p. 543). Every gendered description below is a hostile Greek or Roman male gaze. That is exactly what the "remembers it kinder than it was" framing needs, but in a doubled form: the record is harsher than the reality *and* the Sisters' memory is kinder than it.

## 1. The argument

- **Who.** The eunuch priest was "a free man who, as a mature male, castrated himself voluntarily by removing his testicles with a sharp object" (p. 542). Voluntary, adult and testicular are three facts the Rite of the Knife already uses. Note 1 cites Lucretius, Lucian *DDS* 51, Pliny *NH* 35.165 and Juvenal 6.514. Pliny's "Samian potsherd" is the knife of tradition.
- **The homeland is quiet.** No Phrygian-language text mentions eunuch attendants, and "the eunuch deity Attis ... does not exist in Phrygian iconography and cult practice" (pp. 543–4). Before the 3rd century BCE the only possible evidence is a handful of beardless statuettes, which might simply show youths (p. 544).
- **Pessinus was not a eunuch house.** A 1st-century CE Greek inscription shows "the office of chief priest of the Mother at Pessinous was hereditary, passing from father to son" (p. 544). Note 8 names him: Tiberios Claudios Heras was chief priest nine times with the *title* "Attis", and his son Tiberios Claudios Deiotarus held it after him. Note 17 says "Attis" is the priestly title at Pessinus (Polybios 21.37). **This is the single most important pressure on the design. At Pessinus the senior priesthood was a dynastic office held by men with sons, and the title was "Attis", not "gallus".**
- **In Asia Minor the galli had dignity.** In 190 BCE galli talked a Roman army out of sacking Sestos. In 189 BCE the Mother's priests came out from Pessinus to meet the army and promised it victory (Livy 38.18.9). In 102 BCE a Pessinuntine priest called the **Battakes** addressed Rome, and the historians record his costume but nothing of his sex (n. 26). In the homeland the priest was "a figure of dignity who enjoyed the respect accorded to one chosen to be the representative of his community" (p. 544).
- **Soterides of Kyzikos (46 BCE).** The relief shows him "in woman's clothing, a long gown and a veil, implying that within Asia Minor the eunuch priest identified himself as a woman in his physical appearance" (p. 544). Kyzikos is `b_kyzikos`, inside the mod's homeland.
- **The Greek metragyrt** (4th century BCE) was a begging priest whom the Greeks despised, but he "is always described as masculine" (p. 546). Contempt for the Mother's priests is older than contempt for their gender.
- **The gender-mockery starts in the 2nd century BCE**, in the "Gallus and the lion" epigrams (*Anth. Pal.* 6.217–20, 234, 237). These place the gallus at Pessinus or near Sardis, give one of them the name Atys, and call him "a half-woman". Yet "the eunuch is quite clearly conceptualized as male, as the Greek masculine pronoun ... make[s] clear" (p. 547).
- **The first feminine grammar is an epitaph.** *Anth. Pal.* 7.233 is "the first time a Gallus [is] described in feminine grammatical construct": Aristion (a masculine name), "she who used to toss her hair among the pines in honor of Cybele" (pp. 550–1). Another is Trygonion, "the Little Dove", "the flower of the eunuch band in their clubhouse" (*Anth. Pal.* 7.222). Note 30 places that clubhouse on the Palatine.
- **Roman law made them non-persons.** In 101 BCE a slave who castrated himself was exiled. In 77 BCE Genucius was denied a legacy as "neither man nor woman", and he was barred from pleading because of his "obscene presence and corrupt voice" (p. 549; Valerius Maximus 7.7.6). Rome made the gallus "not a woman but a non-person in the eyes of the law" (p. 549).
- **The Megalesia** procession had the galli marching "in full armor and flashing weapons" (Lucretius) (pp. 549–50). Dionysios of Halikarnassos speaks of the priests as "both men and women", so the cult also had **female** clergy (p. 550).
- **Catullus 63.** Once Attis has cut himself the poem shifts to the feminine. Even so, Attis is "only a counterfeit woman, a *notha mulier*" (p. 551) who ends as the goddess's slave. Roller keeps the masculine pronoun on purpose (n. 34).
- **Virgil** turns the gallus into the image of the effeminate Phrygian enemy: "you who are really Phrygian women, not Phrygian men" (*Aen.* 9.614–20) (p. 553).
- **Her conclusion directly contradicts the faith's Aristotelian reading, and supports Bhatt.** "Nowhere in the literature of Mediterranean antiquity is there any intimation that the eunuch priest represented a third gender, a holy state to be respected" (p. 555). The ancient view was "neither man nor woman". That was not a sacred third; it was the absence of gender, and so the absence of personhood.
- **Late antiquity is left out.** Note 27 says only that late-antique sources put the eunuch inside "the debate between pagan and Christian". For the late material, see the Latham and Roller 1999 notes.

## 3. In-world loc lines

- *Elder of the Dorylaion house:* "At Pessinus the priest who wore the name Attis had a son who wore it after him. We do not sing that verse."
- *Event, the Kyzikos stone:* "The stone shows her veiled, and a long gown. The letters under it say 'he'. The mason charged by the letter."
- *Hostile jurist (Genucius precedent):* "Roman law knew no such heir: neither man nor woman, and so no one at all. The praetor would not hear her speak."
- *Galenic splinter, conceding:* "'Neither man nor woman' was the Senate's sentence before it was our doctrine. We have only taken the sting out of it."
- *Dorleyaian answer:* "They called Aristion 'she' once, in a poem about drinking. We have kept it ever since."
