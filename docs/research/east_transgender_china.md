# Chinese gender variance and eunuch material: Howard Chiang (ed.), *Transgender China* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2012)

Research pass for the Adelphai Dorleyaiou (Transbian Maximalism) faith — neighbour-faith text when the faith reaches China, a possible later Chinese branch, and in-period (Tang/Song/Yuan) precedents. Source file: `books/east_01.txt` (~112k words). Page numbers are the book's printed pages, taken from the running headers in the text file. All quotations are verbatim from the book; square-bracketed notes are mine.

**Scope caveat up front.** This volume is a cultural-studies anthology, not an institutional history. Only two chapters (2, Chiang on eunuchs; 3, Burton-Rose on Buddhism/Classicism/Daoism) carry pre-modern evidence in bulk; chapter 4 (Zhou) is late Ming/early Qing literature with some Han–Song anchors; chapter 5 (Wong) is a Ming tale with a Song setting plus modern adaptations. Chapters 6–11 are twentieth- and twenty-first-century and were skimmed only for retrospective claims. The eunuch chapter is, by its own design, about **how late-Qing/Republican sources manufactured our knowledge of castration**; its in-period claims about Tang/Song/Yuan are few and mostly borrowed (Jay 1993, Tsai 1996, Taylor 2000). Cross-reference `research/east_tang_ming_goryeo.md` (Hoeckelmann on Tang, Tsai on Ming, Kim on Goryeo) for institutional detail; this file does **not** repeat that material. Topics the brief asked for that this book does **not** cover at all: the preserved genitals (*bao*), eunuch burial customs, eunuch temple patronage, eunuch Buddhism/Daoism as practice, "stone maiden" (*shinü* 石女) as a term, sodomy statutes, Mulan beyond a one-line mention. These are flagged as absent in §2 rather than filled from memory.

### Chapter theses (chapters read in full)

**Ch. 1, Chiang, "Imagining Transgender China" (pp. 3–19).** A field-history of transgender studies (Anglo-American, institutionalised only in the 1990s) and a programme for a "Chinese transgender studies" that proceeds along three routes: identitarian (concrete trans figures), androgyny/gender-ambiguity, and the "most radical approach … leaving behind Western-derived meanings of gender altogether" (p. 11). Chiang names the dan actors of Peking opera as "the area of Chinese culture in which cross-gender behavior has made the most prominent presence" (p. 8) and frames his own eunuch chapter as "a cautionary tale of the tendency to universalize transgenderism as a category of experience" (p. 11). For the mod the useful point is methodological: the book itself warns against reading eunuchs as a "third sex".

**Ch. 2, Chiang, "Castrated Civilization" (pp. 23–66).** The trope of China as a "castrated civilization" (被閹割的文明) and of eunuchs as a "third sex" (第三性) is a product of the 1870s–1930s, built from three "voices" — Western spectators (Stent 1877, Coltman 1893–94, Matignon 1896), eunuchs' own late self-narrations, and the last imperial family (Puyi's 1923 abolition). Before that, "the absence of a Chinese word for sex until the 1910s" (p. 24) means gender, not sex, is the right lens: castration historically targeted the scrotum (fertility and status), not the penis (pleasure), and eunuchs "retained an overtly 'male' gender in aspiring to a traditional Confucian lifestyle" (p. 44). Castration "also reproduces eunuchs socially and culturally" (p. 43) — a ritual death-and-rebirth that eunuchs themselves administered. Nothing here is Tang/Song/Yuan-specific beyond generalities, but the conceptual frame (scrotum-regime vs penis-regime; castration as social reproduction; eunuchs as the "daily experts" of castration) is directly usable.

**Ch. 3, Burton-Rose, "Gendered Androgyny" (pp. 67–95).** Surveys "nearly 25 hundred years" of Buddhist, Classicist and Daoist material on bodies and identities "considered 'neither male nor female'" (p. 68). Buddhism: the *paṇḍaka* / "Five Types of Non-males" and "Non-females", vinaya exclusion of the sexually ambiguous, Pure Land requirement that women be reborn men, the Lotus Sutra dragon girl's sex change (used by Wu Zetian), the Chan abbess of Moshan, Guanyin's Tang-period sex change. Classicism: *Shijing* "father and mother of the people"; Gan Bao's *Soushenji* (d. 336) recording human sex changes and hermaphrodites as cosmic portents of dynastic ruin. Daoism: women's near-equal leadership in Celestial Masters Daoism, Quanzhen as the first celibate order, Inner Alchemy's male "pregnancy" with a transcendent embryo and the female adept's "decapitating the red dragon" to become "like that of a man's". Thesis: Chinese traditions hold "a rich storehouse of gender integrating symbolism, but the wealth of the storehouse indicates just how difficult life was for the people who did not fit" (p. 87); Daoism is "the 'good cop' to Classicism's 'bad cop'" (p. 80). This is the chapter that supplies most Tang/Song precedents.

**Ch. 4, Zhou, "Androgynous Ideal in Scholar-Beauty Romances" (pp. 97–125).** The late-Ming/early-Qing genre makes both scholar (*caizi*) and beauty (*jiaren*) androgynous — cross-dressing heroines, feminised heroes — and Zhou traces this not to late-Ming gender fluidity alone but to literati "recalcitrant impulses to assert their latent masculinity as institutionalized yin subjects" (p. 121), since Dong Zhongshu had cast the subject as yin to the ruler's yang. Eunuch tyranny (Liu Jin, Wei Zhongxian) intensified officials' self-image as "deserted wives" (p. 104). Contains one fictional self-castrating hero (*Kuaishi zhuan*, p. 108), the Hua Mulan archetype (p. 105), Song-dynasty remonstrance against eunuchs (Cheng Yi, p. 109), and a candid admission that female cross-dressing "can find little support from historical records" outside wartime survival (p. 120). Period is mostly post-1453, so use as late colour or as "later Chinese branch" material only.

**Ch. 5, Wong, "Transgenderism as a Heuristic Device" (pp. 127–158).** Follows the *renyao* 人妖 ("human prodigy") trope from Feng Menglong's 1624 White Snake tale (set under the Southern Song emperor Gaozong) through Pu Songling's "Renyao" (a cross-dressing seducer castrated by the cuckolded husband and kept as his concubine), Republican sexology, and 1980s–90s Hong Kong/Taiwan rewritings. For the mod: a Song-period *Taiping guangji* (978) snake-demon precedent, Nüwa's snake body, the Ming *lijiao* "Three Bonds" cosmology that polices feminine transgression, and the Sang Chong case (a man raised as a girl with bound feet). Mostly post-1453 but the Song setting gives neighbour-faith texture.

### Retrospective claims found in the modern chapters (skim)

- Ch. 7, p. 183 and p. 189: the martial-arts villain Dongfang Bubai "castrates himself to practice a lethal form of martial arts to dominate the world and, in the process, turns into a formidable villain and beautiful woman" — a modern (Jin Yong, 1960s; film 1992) trope, but it shows how Chinese popular culture already pairs **self-castration + feminisation + occult power**, which is exactly the hostile-rumour shape a Confucian official would give the Sisters.
- Ch. 7, p. 191: a film character who is "a gay Daoist priest who conducts funeral rituals by day and performs in drag." Modern, but note the pairing of Daoist ritual office with gender crossing.
- Ch. 8, pp. 214–215: Chen Sen's 1849 novel *Pinhua baojian* 品花寶鑒 about literati patrons and *dan* 旦 actors; "Chinese opera traditionally included only male actors, who were responsible for performing both masculine and feminine roles." Qing; out of window.
- Ch. 9, pp. 249–250: Taiwanese male cross-dressers read via Lacan as "symbolic castration"; the author notes performers who publicly denied homosexuality to escape "contempt for their symbolic castration." Modern.
- Ch. 2, n. 107, p. 65: Chiang notes "a growing trend within the contemporary transgender community in the West to advocate/normalize castration ideation (e.g., 'male-to-eunuch' instead of 'male-to-female')" — a modern footnote, but it is the book's one explicit link between castration and a non-male, non-female identity.

## 2. Pre-modern dossier

### 2A. Eunuchs' gender in Chinese thought (ch. 2 unless noted)

**Terminology and the "third sex" as a modern artefact.**
- "Starting in the early Republican period, cultural commentators often labeled late imperial China as a 'castrated civilization' (被閹割的文明, beiyange de wenming)" (p. 23).
- "In an age of China's metaphoric portrayal as a castrated civilization, the perception of eunuchs as demasculinized 'third sex' (第三性, disanxing) figures became increasingly common. However, historical standards of masculinity and femininity … shifted across time and place" (p. 24).
- "The absence of a Chinese word for sex until the 1910s suggests that the popular depiction of eunuchs as third sex people tells us more about our modern conceptual preoccupations than the historical experience of eunuchs themselves. Before the emergence of the concept of sex, gender might be the more adequate category of analysis for understanding the meaning and practice of castration" (p. 24).
- Chiang defines "eunuchism (the bodily state of castrated men)" (p. 23) and insists it is "a category of experience that needs to be historicized rather than foundational" (p. 23).

**Scrotum-regime vs penis-regime (the body theory actually argued).**
- "If the ultimate purpose of castration is to impair a man's fertility, it is not necessary to destroy the penis but only the testes. In fact, the earliest extant medical description of the operation, by the seventh-century Byzantine Greek physician Paul of Aegina, makes it clear that only the testicles, not the penis, were targeted by the techniques of contusion and excision" (p. 32). [Direct Byzantine cross-reference for the Anatolian home faith; cf. `hist_tougher_eunuch_byzantine.md`.]
- "This shift in conceptual preference from 'castration' to 'emasculation' highlights an important historical transformation in the biological definition of manhood: from a cultural regime of the scrotum to a regime of the penis. Between the sixteenth and twentieth century, the anatomical measure of manliness changed from whether a man has balls to whether a man has a big stick. This fall of the scrotum and rise of the penis was accompanied by the process by which desire and libidinal pleasures replaced status and reproduction as the organizing principle for making sexual acts socially meaningful. In late imperial China, the decline of the status-centered paradigm directly led to the increasing legal relevance of a gender-performance paradigm" (p. 33, citing Sommer 2000).
- Quoting Gary Taylor: "Castration—in humanist Europe, as in previous human societies—attacked the scrotum. In twentieth-century psychoanalysis, by contrast, castration has been redefined as an attack on the penis" (p. 33).
- Late-Qing vernacular confirmation: in the Shanghai lithograph of the gambler Tang, "Tang's choice of the word ruandai, which is translated here as 'balls' but literally means an 'egg bag' [卵袋], goes a long way to show that people had not always considered the bodily target of castration to be the entire male genital organ in late imperial China" (p. 46). Benjamin Hobson's 1851 anatomy glossed the "outer kidney" (外腎) as responsible for "the change in voice and facial features alongside the elimination of reproductive power when castrated (閹之割之)" (p. 46).
- Dong Guo (1985), from conversations with Peking eunuchs: "when someone is made a eunuch at a relatively young age, the procedure resembles the gelding of pigs by removing or protruding the testicles" (p. 32) — contradicted by 1960s urological examination finding both penis and testes removed (p. 32). Chiang leaves "a historical residue of ambiguity surrounding the surgical parameters of castration" (p. 32).

**Eunuchs retained male gender (the book's central anti-"third sex" finding).**
- "Whether we consider the scrotum to be the seat of male fertility or the penis the locus of male pleasure, the intended effect of castration on eunuchs is the deprivation of their power to breed biologically. And that is it. They were not impotent in any other sense" (p. 44).
- Quoting Jennifer Jay (1993): "From both the historical sources and the anecdotal reminiscences of Qing eunuchs, it seems that with very few exceptions, the Chinese eunuchs were without gender confusion at the time of castration, and after the operation they experienced physiological changes but no apparent shift in their gender identity and male-oriented role in society" (p. 44).
- "Many eunuchs got married, adopted children, or had kids before offering themselves to the imperial court, suggesting that their masculine social role remained intact as they continued to embrace Confucian family norms. Quite simply put, undergoing castration did not indicate, to them and to their surrounding community, a complete erasure of their masculine identity. More importantly, eunuchs also faced a greater degree of opportunity and power in comparison to other female servants (宮女, gongnü) inside the palace" (p. 44).
- Quoting Taylor: the eunuch as "not a defective man but an improved one" (p. 45). "In imperial China, apart from the court officials, eunuchs were after all the people whom the emperor and his family trusted more than any other men. The tremendous political power Chinese eunuchs wielded extended beyond the bedchamber to the rest of the palace and, in well-known examples during the Tang and Ming dynasties, arguably throughout the Chinese empire" (p. 45). [Tang: cross-ref Hoeckelmann pass.]
- Chiang's own framing: "Chinese masculinity can thus be understood as neither a social extension of biological maleness nor the social meanings assigned to men per se, but a social relational indicator of a discursive cultural practice such as castration" (p. 25).

**Castration as ritual death and rebirth; eunuchs as its daily experts (closest the book gets to "castration as rite").**
- "On the level of personal-private experience, castration denotes a ritualized episode where the death of a man's biological fertility intersected with the birth of his new life as a eunuch" (p. 44).
- "the castration of male bodies also reproduces eunuchs socially and culturally in imperial China" (p. 43); "Eunuchs could not reproduce biologically, but the practice of castration made their social and cultural reproduction possible. So even if it was not physically feasible for them to give birth to future generations of their own kind, eunuchs frequently took an active role in overseeing the nuts and bolts of castration, the single most important procedure that defined their identity" (p. 45).
- "when instances of self-castration occurred on the streets of late imperial Peking, eunuchs were the authorities to whom people often turned for assistance" (p. 45); in the lithograph the pawnbroker "rushed off to a local official's residence to find a eunuch who could come to Tang's rescue" (p. 46); "no physician is either mentioned … or present" (p. 47).
- "the cultural survival of both practices entailed a homosocial environment in the occasion and demonstration of their corporeality. Footbinding was a custom conducted by women and on women; castration was a practice performed by men and on men" (p. 50). [For the faith this is the inversion point: Adelphai castration is performed by women on men.]
- Concealment as the logic of both footbinding and castration: "the naturalness of footbinding and castration depended on concealing the female and male bodies, because concealment links these customs to Chinese ideals of civility and culture (文, wen)" (p. 27). "Such detailed records as Stent's … were never committed in writing or visual imaging when castration was a widely accepted practice, because instructions for the practice were transmitted orally and demonstrated corporeally" (p. 42).

**The operation as described (Stent 1877, pp. 29–31; treat as secondary, see Chiang's critique pp. 31, 47–48).**
- Site: "just outside the inner Hsi-'hua gate (內西華門) of the palace … known as the Chang-tzu, 廠子, the shed" (p. 29). Operators: "tao-tzu-chiang, 刀子匠, 'knifers,' … They get a fixed sum—six taels—for every operation" (pp. 29–30). "Grown up men desirous of becoming eunuchs, but who are too poor to pay the necessary fees, make arrangements with the 'knifers' to repay them out of their salaries. But in any case the 'knifers' dare not operate on them unless they (the candidates) have securities to vouch for their respectability" (p. 30). [A **surety/guarantor** requirement — a sponsor-system analogue.] "The 'knifers' have generally one or two apprentices … almost invariably members of their own families, so that the profession may be said to be hereditary" (p. 30).
- Consent formula: "The operating 'knifer' then stands in front of the men—with his knife in his hand—and enquires if he will ever repent. If the man at the last moment demurs in the slightest, the 'knifer' will not perform the operation, but if he still expresses his willingness, with one sweep of the knife he is made a eunuch" (p. 30). [Ritual question-and-answer before the cut — usable liturgical template.]
- Procedure: ligatures, "bathed three times with hot pepper-water", "both testicles and penis—are cut off as closely as possible with a small curved knife, something in the shape of a sickle", "a pewter needle or spigot is carefully thrust into the main orifice", three days without drink, then "the sufferer obtains relief in the copious flow of urine which spurts out like a fountain. If this takes place satisfactorily, the patient is considered out of danger and congratulated on it" (pp. 30–31).
- Chiang's critique: "scholars have pointed out that no discussion of the 'knifers' could be found in the Qing palace archives" (p. 31); "As late as 1991, two urologists from Beijing Medical University still conceded that 'most people, including urologists, do not have a clear understanding of what is actually done to a man or boy to produce an eunuch'" (p. 31).
- Eunuch testimony (Ren Futian, Chi Huanqing): two establishments, "Biwu (Bi 'the Fifth') and Xiaodao Liu ('pocket knife' Liu)"; "Each season … they supplied the Neiwufu [Imperial Household Department] forty eunuchs" (pp. 47–48); candidates were "examined—for his appearance, conversational skills, intelligence, and genital organ (done with his pants on)—and admitted only if considered appropriate" (p. 48); "Antiseptic preparation was done simply by heating up the surgical knives with fire" (p. 48).
- Ma Deqing's childhood castration by his father (c. 1906): "my father succeeded in persuading me to lie on the bed and castrated me with his own hands … cutting his yaoming de qiguan ['organ for life'] from his body! … Ever since, my reproductive organ and I became two separate entities" (p. 49); dressings of "cotton pads soaked with white grease, sesame oil, and pepper-powder" (p. 49).

**Law and categories: voluntary/self-castration, natural eunuchs.**
- "One of the most popular alternative routes to serving in the palace, for instance, was voluntary castration (zigong, 自宮), a category that included self-castration. More prevalent in the Ming dynasty, self-castration became illegal under early Qing law. The lessons from Ming eunuchs' political corruption were difficult to ignore, so up to the second half of the eighteenth century, Qing emperors made it illegal for civilians to castrate themselves, which simultaneously curbed the number of available eunuchs. However, the legal codes that imposed death penalty for voluntary castration were not strictly enforced throughout the first hundred years or so of Qing rule. In June 1785, the Qianlong Emperor took a step further in loosening the codes to allow the Imperial Household Department to accept individuals who offered themselves after voluntary castration" (p. 48). "At one point Qianlong even promoted a policy of eunuch illiteracy" (p. 49). [Ming/Qing — out of window; cross-ref Tsai on Ming 自宮 in the earlier pass.]
- "persons born with ambiguous or dysfunctional genitalia were categorized by physicians as 'natural eunuchs' (tianyan, 天閹) and recommended for service in the imperial court as a typical solution. But in most cases of voluntary castration or zigong, the father was the person who performed the operation" (p. 49).
- Conclusion: "the demise of eunuchism depended on the collapse of the saliency of already existing categories such as tianyan ('natural eunuch') and zigong ('voluntary castration')" (p. 55).
- Coltman's 1890s cases of **self**-castration: a married father of a year-old daughter who "with one clean cut removed his external organs of generation entire" to seek palace employment (p. 51); a 16-year-old who, "Knowing that his father depended on him (his second son) for descendants to worship at his grave, this lad, after a quarrel with his father … took a butcher-knife and cut off his penis" (p. 51); another "to spite him, thus deprived him of all hopes of descendants at one blow—the dearest hope known to an elderly China-man" (p. 51). [Castration as filial sabotage: the strongest hostile-text motif in the book — the Confucian horror is lineage-ending, not gender-crossing.]
- Antiquity claim: "eunuchism, a cultural system with two thousand years of history in China" (p. 54; n. 116 cites Yuan Qu, *Diyici huanguan shidai*).
- Christian-West contrast and its error: Stent wrote "Eunuchs are only to be found in eastern despotic countries, the enlightening influence of Christianity preventing such unnatural proceedings" (p. 29); Chiang: "Stent's assertion that Christianity and monogamy saved the West from the 'unnatural proceedings' of castration is an erroneous interpretation, considering the important role played by the eunuchs in Byzantine history" (p. 29; n. 29 lists James 1997, Tougher 2002, Ringrose 2003, Hatzaki 2009).

**Absent from this book (do not source from here):** the preserved genitals/*bao*; eunuch burial (only the *title* of Jay 1993, "Castration, Adoption, Marriage, and Burial," is cited, p. 63 n. 80 — fetch Jay for burial and adoption); eunuch temple patronage and eunuch Buddhism/Daoism (the only mention is the 1958 photograph of the last eunuchs "mixed in with Buddhist and Daoist monks," p. 26); eunuch afterlife beliefs. Tang-specific institutional detail is entirely delegated to Wang Shounan 2004 and the Hoeckelmann pass.

### 2B. Hermaphrodites, "non-males", "non-females" (ch. 3)

- *Paṇḍaka*: "of obscure origin but that ultimately may be derived from apa + aṇḍa + ka, 'without testicles'" (Zwilling, quoted p. 69). "A disinclination to act according to prescribed gender norms prompted a metaphorical desexing of the offender's body" (p. 69). "Even as early as the period of the Atharva Veda, paṇḍakas were viewed as a distinct group, different from ordinary males and females, and apparently transvestite" (p. 69). Faure: "both hermaphroditism—a kind of hypersexuality—and impotence—a 'hyposexuality'—were seen as characteristic of the paṇḍaka, and as such, were threatening and/or despised" (p. 69).
- Vinaya exclusion: "Early Buddhist monastic codes (vinaya) explicitly excluded those judged to be sexually deviant, including the androgynous, asexual, and those with an aversion to the opposite sex. The logic was that cenobites 'must be capable of performing sexually while controlling their desire to do so'" (pp. 69–70). Mahāsāṅghika Vinaya anecdote: a monk caught fondling another says "'I am a princess.' … 'I go both ways. I am neither man nor woman,' and then explains that he/she became a monk because he/she heard that monks needed wives … This is followed by a long passage about how unmanly men should not be allowed into the saṅgha" (p. 70). Faure (n. 9, p. 88): "The same is true for castration, although the condemnation of the latter was in some cases attenuated by its ascetic motivations." [This directly fills the gap flagged in the earlier pass: Buddhist rules on castrates in clergy = exclusion, softened only for ascetic motive.]
- Chinese classification: "The five types of paṇḍaka were translated into Chinese as the 'Five Types of Non-males' (Wu zhong bu nan 五種不男 or Wu bu nan 五不男)": "(1) 扇搋 ṣaṇḍha … by birth impotent. (2) 留拏 rugṇa or ruṇḍa paṇḍaka; 'maimed,' i.e. emasculated males. Also written 犍黃門. (3) 砂梨沙掌拏 īrṣyā … those whose sexual desires are only aroused by jealousy. (4) 半擇迦 paṇḍaka are eunuchs in general, but in this category are described as hermaphrodites. (5) 博叉 pakṣa … impotent during one-half of the month" (p. 70). [Note 犍黃門 — *huangmen* 黃門 "Yellow Gate" is also the classical court title for palace eunuchs; the gloss links the Buddhist "maimed/emasculated" class to the eunuch office by name.]
- "This schema prompted a parallel categorization for women, the 'Five Types of Non-females' (Wu zhong bu nü 五種不女 or Wu bu nü 五不女). These are: the conch (luo 螺), the striped (jin 筋), the drum (gu 鼓), horned (jiao 角), and the pulse (mai 脉). This typology had wide-ranging influence on Chinese culture, where it reached beyond the ken of Buddhism into canonical medicine, with Li Shizhen 李時珍 (1518–1593) using the exact same terminology in his Systematic Materia Medica (Bencao gangmu 本草剛目) published in 1596" (p. 70). "As in the early Buddhist community, there was a place in late imperial China for those who were neither male nor female, but it was in a discretely circumscribed box" (p. 70). [The "stone maiden" (石女) is **not** named; the "Five Non-females" are its classificatory neighbours. Furth 1988/1999 is the cited authority for the medical side.]
- Gan Bao, *Soushenji* 搜神記 (Eastern Jin, d. 336), entry 195: "During the year of Emperor Hui [of Qin], in the capital of Luo[yang] there was a person with both male and female organs, both of which were functional. Furthermore this person was by nature enthusiastically lascivious. The empire suffered from military disorder … because of the disordering of male and female influences (qi), and monstrous physical forms were made" (p. 79). 惠帝之世，京、洛有人，一身而男女二體，亦能兩用人道，而性尤好淫。天下兵亂，由男女氣亂，而妖形作也。
- Byzantine parallel noted by Chiang for the operation (n. 37, p. 60): Lascaratos and Kostakopoulos, "Operations on Hermaphrodites and Castration in Byzantine Times (324–1453 AD)".

### 2C. Sex change and gender-crossing in religion (ch. 3)

**Buddhism.**
- Soma and Māra (*Saṃyutta Nikāya*): "To one for whom the question doth arise: / Am I a woman, or / Am I a man, or what not am I then? / To such a one is Māra fit to talk" (p. 71). Burton-Rose: "the Buddhist path to enlightenment is open to women on the condition that they do not speak of gender as such" (p. 71).
- Pure Land: "Chinese Pure Land (jingtu 淨土) practices required that women be reborn as men to gain access to a range of celestial paradises. This exclusivity was a Chinese innovation" (p. 71); n. 19: "Chinese Pure Land practices required that everything—from insects to domestic animals to women—be reborn as men" (p. 89). Women's prayers to "Transform into a male" (biancheng nanzi 變成男子) / "Transform from a woman into a man" (zhuan nü cheng nan 轉女成男); the illustrated *Foshuo zhuan nüshen jing* 佛說轉女身經 translated 1195 (n. 21, p. 89). [Song-period text on female-to-male transformation — in window.]
- Lotus Sutra (Kumārajīva, AD 406) dragon king's daughter: Śāriputra's objection, "A woman's body is filthy, it is not a Dharma-receptacle … a woman's body even then has five obstacles. It cannot become first a Brahmā god king, second the god Śakra, third King Māra, fourth a sage king turning the Wheel, fifth a Buddha-body" (p. 72); "The assembled multitude all saw that dragon girl in the space of an instant turn into a man, perfect bodhisattva-conduct … and achieve undifferentiating, right, enlightened intuition, with thirty-two marks" (p. 73). Sanskrit: "with her female faculties suppressed and male faculties displayed" (n. 28, p. 90). Burton-Rose: "She must cast it off to join the cosmic boy's club; for centuries afterwards female devotees sought to model their transcendent ambitions on hers" (p. 73).
- Wu Zetian: "Wu Zetian 武則天 (624–705), founded her own dynasty, the Zhou 周 (690–705), reigning as the 'August Thearch' (huangdi 皇帝), a term only applied to men before and since. Wu drew upon the dragon princess's refutation of the assertion that a woman could not become a Buddha or a king, lavishly funding a mural of this key scene in Dunhuang cave 331. She also quite literally became a Buddha by claiming for a three-and-a-half-month period from 694–695 that she herself was Maitreya … The gender transgressive imagery available in Buddhism served as a weapon in her arsenal" (p. 73).
- Chan: "From at least the seventh century on there was a consistent strain within Chinese Buddhism, particularly Chan, that asserted 'gender differences make no difference to attaining enlightenment.' Its adherents regularly drew upon the nāga princess episode and that of the abbotess of Moshan 末山, Liaoran 了然. The latter, in dialogue with a male student, described herself as having 'neither male nor female marks (xiang 相).' The student retorted: 'Why doesn't she transform herself?' to which the abbotess replied, 'She is not a spirit, nor a ghost. What would you have her become?' and thereby provoked his enlightenment" (p. 74). [Tang — in window.]
- Guanyin: "The Lotus Sutra … was also the impetus for the popularization of the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara in China and his transformation into the Goddess Guanyin … the Dragon Princess begins appearing as one of Guanyin's attendants beginning in the twelfth century. Despite Guanyin's celebrated sex change from the Tang dynasty (618–907) on, there is clearly no instance in which she is worshipped for this power. Rather, in one of her most popular manifestations, that of the White-robed Guanyin, as Yü Chün-fang puts it, she is 'a fertility goddess who nevertheless is deprived of sexuality.' Though Guanyin includes promises of sexual access to men within the domain of her upāyaistic practices … she rarely delivers consummation" (p. 74). Yü on mechanism: "each major form of the feminine Guanyin was originally anchored in one specific place, connected with one life story, and depicted with one type of iconography" (n. 36, p. 90). Faure on Japan: "Guanyin even condones homosexual love, as in the Japanese story of the old monk who … Kannon appears to him under the guise of the beloved novice" (n. 37, p. 90).
- Sponberg's four attitudes: "soteriological inclusiveness," "institutional androcentrism," "ascetic misogyny," and "soteriological androgyny" (p. 74); the last "formulated the goal of Buddhist practice psychologically as a dynamic state of non-dualistic androgynous integration" but is "not part of the core tradition shared by all forms of Buddhism" (p. 75); Vajrayāna deified insight/compassion "then caused to copulate with 'sexual union representing the androgynous ideal'" (p. 76). Burton-Rose's verdict: "what is liberating to women or transgendered people in a schema designed for men struggling with conflicts arising from institutionalized sexual repression? My answer is, without deliberate redeployment, nothing" (p. 76).

**Classicism ("Confucianism").**
- *Shijing* "Jiong zhuo" 泂酌 (Mao #251): "The triumphant and concordant lordling is/Father and mother of the people. 愷悌君子, 民之父母" (p. 76); Jia Yi (200–169 BC): "He causes the people to have the reverence of a father and to have the intimacy of a mother [toward him] … If not the pinnacle of virtue, who can be like this?" (p. 76). "This line is most intriguing as a remnant of a path not taken … for most of its existence the Classicist tradition plays gender cop more than gender outlaw" (p. 76).
- Lactating fathers as filial paragons in the 1681 *Shengyu xiang jie* 聖諭像解 (p. 77 and n. 50, p. 91): "Lactating men imply not only gender transgression but a different version of the capabilities of sexed bodies. Compare to Caroline Walker Bynum, Jesus as Mother."
- Portent logic: "For early medieval Classicists, gender disruptions were classifiable under cosmological portents" (p. 77); *Soushenji* has "17 of the 464 entries" on sex/gender transgression including "154 (men and women embracing one another's dress, causing disorder in the empire) … 188 (entire country imitates hairstyle of palace women's quarters, bringing disaster to the empire)" (p. 78).
- Entry 145: "During the Jianping reign period of Aidi [of the Western Han, 6–2 CE], a man transformed into a woman in Yuzhang. She married and bore one child. Chen Feng of Chang'an said: 'For the yang to change into yin will bring about destruction, the end of the lineage: it is an omen of mutual self-destruction.' … For this reason, when Aidi died, Pingdi was destroyed [after succeeding him], and then Wang Mang usurped the throne" (pp. 78–79). 陽變為陰，將亡.
- Entry 196: "there was a female child in Anfeng named Zhou Shining who, having attained the age of eight, gradually transformed into a male. Having reached the age of seventeen or eighteen, her male influences (qi) were complete, yet the transformation of her female form had not been fulfilled, nor had the male form disappeared. [This person] took a wife, but did not conceive children" (p. 79).
- Entry 300 (Gan Bao's principle): "When a man becomes a woman or a woman becomes a man, it is qi transposition … Males and females do not each possess the androgynous ability to transform and engender the way primal energy can … Things consecrated to the Dao should not be used by lesser beings" (p. 79). "Any disruption of clear gender boundaries—be it as minor as a change in sandal style or as significant as a sex change resulting in children—was considered troubling and potentially disastrous" (p. 80).
- Dong Zhongshu (179–104 BC) "applied the metaphysical terms, yin and yang, in designating human gendered relation" so that "men were institutionally relegated to a yin position in front of the ruler" (ch. 4, p. 104). Mencius: "the way of the concubine" (qiefu zhidao) for slavish compliance vs the da zhangfu "great man" who is "above the power of riches and honors to make dissipated, of poverty and force to make bend" (ch. 4, p. 107).

**Daoism.**
- Celestial Masters: "'Church Daoism' … began when Laojun, or 'Lord Lao,' descended to Zhang Daoling 張道陵 in the mountains of the Kingdom of Shu … The traditional date for this occurrence is AD 142. In part due to its persistent rejection of (ostensibly) celibate monasticism, Celestial Master Daoism in its formative period did not suffer from the same misogyny as Buddhism" (p. 80). "Women held positions of leadership and advanced to the same level as men, with the exception that they seem to have been unable to hold the ultimate hereditary position of the Celestial Master himself" (p. 80).
- Celibacy: "As early as the Northern Wei dynasty (AD 386–534) Daoists composed texts in imitation of the Buddhist vinaya … but the Celestial Master order did not initially adopt celibate guidelines. The first major order to do so was the Complete Perfection Teachings (Quanzhen jiao 全真教), which came into existence in the disordered period that preceded the Yuan dynasty. Yet, according to Vincent Goossaert, even at their peak in the Qing only 10 percent of priests lived in abbeys" (p. 80). [Quanzhen = Jin/Yuan, in window.]
- *Daodejing* "Valley Spirit" (gu shen 谷神) and "Mystic Feminine" (xuan pin 玄牝) (p. 80); but Hudson's corrective: "Medieval Daoists are essentially devoted to personal salvation … which ultimately leads beyond the realm of yin and yang to the purer realm of the One Qi … And this One Qi is yang. Because yang is 'pure,' 'high,' and associated with 'life,' while yin is 'impure,' 'low,' and associated with 'death' … usually these Daoists would ultimately be aiming for a state of pure yang" (p. 81).
- Male pregnancy / the Infant: "In the male pregnancy motif, the adept is enjoined to visualize a child within his or her body. The first reference to an inner embryo occurs in the Xiang'er commentary to the Laozi" (p. 82); the Infant (ying'er 嬰兒; chizi 赤子 "ruddy baby") of the *Laozi zhongjing* 老子中經 is "the hierogonic progeny of the Yellow Old (Man) of the Central Pole … and his spouse the Jade Woman of Mysterious Radiance of Great Yin … or the Pure Woman [Su nü 素女]" (p. 82); "I am the child of the Tao. The human being has me too, but it is not the individual 'I'. I am right there, at the canal of the stomach … My mother at my right; she carries me in her arms and feeds me; my father stands at my left to teach and protect me" (p. 83). Yellow Court Scripture: "節精育胞化生身 留胎止精可長生 — By coagulating the essence and fostering the womb, you will generate a body by transformation; by detaining the embryo and causing the essence to stop, you will live a long life" (p. 83). "Like female Buddhist adepts wishing to become Buddhas, this meditation technique promised autodivinization" (p. 83).
- Laozi's self-birth: "it was he himself who transformed his body from nothingness into the shape of mother Li, so was to return into his own matrix; there was never any other mother Li" (Taiwanese informant to Schipper, p. 84). "In this schema there is an explicit move to coopt female generative powers for male practitioners, a strategy that expands the gendered possibilities of men while undermining the biological prerogatives and associated essentialized qualities of women" (p. 84).
- Inner Alchemy (Song onward): "one of Inner Alchemy's greatest debts to Daoism is the centrality of the Infant in its program of transcendence" (p. 84); "the explicit goal of many late imperial texts being to 'become a transcendent, become a buddha' (cheng xian cheng fo 成仙成佛)" (p. 84). Process per *Xingming guizhi* (1615): purified yin and yang "are then caused to 'copulate' (jiaogou 交媾)—the word is unabashedly sexual, though not particularly erotic—in a cauldron located in the practitioner's lower abdomen. The gestation occurs over 10 lunar months … creates a perfect, pure yang body. This is then birthed out the sinciput and nurtured for three more years before it achieves independence. Once it does so, the new body can be multiplied infinitely" (p. 86). Men: "men's penises should shrink and detract as they advance on the path of transcendence" (p. 86); terms "suogui 縮龜 (retraction of the turtle[-head]) … mayin cangxiang 馬陰藏相 (the horse's nether-region hides its marks)" (n. 89, p. 95).
- **"Sexing Sexlessness" — the female adept's gender change by rite:** "In Inner Alchemy, female cultivators can embark upon the same path of transcendence as men, but to do so they must first change their bodies into male bodies by flattening their breasts and cutting off menstruation. The primary difference is in the first stage of practice, in which women refine blood into qi, rather than refining essence (jing), which is conflated with semen. This is consistent with the Chinese medical motto 'In women regard blood as the ruler' (nüzi yi xue wei zhu 女子以血為主). Thus the slogan is 'Refine the blood to transform it into qi' (lian xue hua qi 煉血化氣), rather than 'Refine the [seminal] essence to transform it into qi' (lian jing hua qi 煉精化氣). This leads to the cessation of menstruation, which is called 'decapitating the red dragon' (zhan chilong 斬赤龍). A regimen of breast massage combined with breathing and visualization exercises results in the diminishment of the breasts. Taken together, this causes the female adept's body to become 'like that of a man's' (ru nanzi yipan 如男子一盤). Just as men's penises should shrink and detract as they advance on the path of transcendence, so too should women regain a prepubescent body whose greater proximity to the cosmic origin is evinced by its lack of sexual differentiation" (p. 86). [The textual authorities cited are late Qing (Fu Jinquan in He Longxiang's *Nüdan hebian*, 1906; n. 88), so "cutting the red dragon" as a named women's regimen is documented **late**, even though neidan itself is Song. Do not back-date the term to the Song without another source.]
- Verdict: "women in the late imperial period regained access to the descendants of the meditational techniques they practiced as equals in the early Celestial Masters community. Yet in the later iteration, the ostensibly equal access path to transcending gender while transcending the mundane was predicated on a twofold denigration of themselves: first for possessing an impure yin nature as women, second for containing impure yin elements as a person" (p. 87).
- Female religious leadership, late Ming (ch. 4): "In religious practice, female deities began to replace their male counterparts as idols of worship; and some women assumed leading roles in religious communities" (p. 102; n. 19 cites Beata Grant on Linji Chan master Zhiyuan Xinggang, 1597–1654). Li Zhi praised the Buddhist woman Danran as "a 'prominent man' (dashi) and a 'gentleman hermit' (chushi zhangfu)" (p. 113). The 1890 White Cloud Monastery paintings for the goddess Niangniang (Bixia yuanjun 碧霞元君) show "the Goddess and her seated, meditating replicant" — a **female** adept producing the yang-spirit body (pp. 86–87, n. 92).

### 2D. Literature and law (chs. 4–5)

- *Renyao* 人妖 and forced castration (Pu Songling, *Liaozhai zhiyi*, c. 1680): "a man disguises himself as a woman to seduce another woman; his cross-dressing act was later discovered by the woman's husband, who then castrates the man and makes him his concubine instead" (p. 135). Based (via Furth) on "an earlier story about Sang Ch'ung from Shansi, a man who was raised as a girl with bound feet, lived close to female quarters, and was known to 'falsify' his gender to have sex with those women" (p. 135). Wenqing Kang: "in classical records, gender transformations and male same-sex relations always seemed to be appropriated and subsumed within the framework of normative gender relations" (p. 135). [Private punitive castration + enforced feminisation by the wronged husband — the nearest thing in the book to a penal-castration precedent, and it is **extra-legal and literary**.]
- *Lijiao* 禮教: "The ethics of lijiao centered on the doctrine of Three Bonds—the obligation of child to parents, wife to husband, and official to monarch—which was expressed ritualistically in ancestor worship, marriage ceremonies, and the complex rites at the imperial court" (Liu and Shek, quoted p. 134).
- White Snake precedents: "Chinese civilization creator Nuwa was often believed to embody a snake-body and a human face" (p. 131); "Taiping Guang Ji (太平廣記) in AD 978 … the demonic seducer actually incarnates in a male body and seduces a young girl, but the tale ends up reinforcing male heroism in which a knight shoots the snake demon to death" (p. 132); Han-era division between "fortuitous encounter" with immortals and "unfortunate run-in" with devils (p. 132). The monk's curse-word for the snake-woman: "Yechu (業畜/demonic animal)" (p. 134).
- Sodomy law: **not treated** in this book (Sommer 2000 is cited at p. 33 n. 46 only for the shift to a "gender-performance paradigm" in late imperial law).
