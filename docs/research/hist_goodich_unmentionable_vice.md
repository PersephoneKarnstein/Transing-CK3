# Goodich, *The Unmentionable Vice* (1979): design reading

Source:
- Michael Goodich, *The Unmentionable Vice: Homosexuality in the Later Medieval Period* (Santa Barbara: Ross-Erikson, 1979). ISBN 0-87436-300-4. Chapters 3 and 5 build on his articles "Sodomy in Ecclesiastical Law and Theory" and "Sodomy in Medieval Secular Law", *Journal of Homosexuality* 1 (1976).

Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo. Page numbers are the printed ones. The scan's running heads are irregular, so pages were fixed from the margin numbers. A page given here may be off by one at a page break; the margin-checked pages are 3, 5, 10-17, 19, 23-26, 28-29, 33-35, 42, 49, 53, 56, 59, 62-63, 71-72, 74-75, 79, 83-89 and 93.

**What was read.**
- **In full:** Preface and Introduction (pp. vii-xvii); ch. I "Homosexuality in the Middle Ages" (pp. 3-21); ch. II "Gregorian Reform" (pp. 23-39); ch. III "Conciliar Legislation" (pp. 41-49); ch. IV "The Fourth Lateran Council and Scholasticism" (pp. 51-69); ch. V "Secular Law" (pp. 71-88).
- **Skimmed:** the Appendix, a translation of the 1323-1324 Pamiers trial of Arnold of Verniolle (pp. 89-123), for dress, Jews and lepers only; the notes for the sources of the Venice, Glaber and Nablus passages (pp. 124-127).
- **Searched in context:** transvestism/cross-dressing/women's dress, effeminacy, eunuch, hermaphrodite/androgyny, *tumtum*, women together, Regino, Burchard, Reims, Nablus, Lateran, Templars, Rhineland and German cities, Italian statutes, Jews, Islam.

**Relation to existing notes.** Goodich is the second-hand source behind several Italian-statute and Venice items that the repo has from Karras or Ruggiero. He gives a date and an earlier Ruggiero citation for the Venice case in `later_starts/venice.md` and `hist_karras_medieval_sexuality.md` E179. His Regino passage is the one pre-1066 Latin text near Prüm that speaks of a man taking a woman's part. It bears on the Good Ladies (`v2/witch_design.md`). Boswell criticised his secular-law chapter by name (Boswell p. 291 n. 64, p. 292 n. 70); see `hist_boswell_social_tolerance.md`.

## 0. Framing warning

- **The subject is sex between men, called "sodomy" throughout** "in keeping with medieval terminology" (p. ix). The mod's daughters are women. They are not gay men, not cross-dressers, and their womanhood is not a vice or a costume. Goodich files a person who lived as a woman under "transvestite" and "homosexuality" (pp. xiii, 13). That is **his 1979 classification and the court's**, recorded here only as how a hostile record categorised her. It is never the narrator's.
- **Reception.**
  - Boswell's verdict on the secular-law material, made in 1980 against the 1976 article: Goodich does not ask what "sodomy" meant to the lawmakers. He assumes religious prejudice alone explains the laws. He lumps everything together as "late medieval" instead of ordering it by date (Boswell p. 291 n. 64).
  - Boswell also catches factual errors on English law (Boswell p. 292 n. 70; see Cross-checks).
  - The book's psychological readings are speculative and dated: Peter Damian's childhood as the cause of his "venom" (pp. 18-19); a "middle-class" reaction likened to Nazism and the Poujadists (pp. 85-86).
  - Use Goodich for dates, names, places and citations to follow up, not for interpretation.
- **Goodich on Jews.**
  - He calls Christian sexual rigour the victory of "the most moralistic and puritanical strain of Judaism" (p. x).
  - He repeats, without comment, "the alleged Jewish influence" at the court of Louis the Pious (p. 74).
  - These are his framings and are **not** to be echoed in any text. His summary of Jewish law (pp. 46-49) is recorded as context only.
- **What the mod may take:**
  - dated hostile law and clerical voices;
  - the machinery of denunciation after 1215 and in the Italian communes;
  - the gap between law and enforcement (pp. 66-69, 88);
  - the Venice record of 1354 as a date (see §1.6).
- **What it may not take:**
  - any narrator claim on a historical person;
  - the record's or Goodich's words for her as the narrator's;
  - any execution, torture or mutilation on screen or as a mechanic;
  - anything that casts Jews as wrongdoers;
  - Sabbat, pact or orgy imagery from the heresy material (rule 25; see §1.5).

## 1. Findings, by date and place

### 1.1 Penitentials and canon collections (Prüm, Trier, Worms)

- **c. 906, Regino of Prüm, *Libri duo de synodalibus causis*.** Written at the request of Archbishop **Ratbod of Trier** and dedicated to Hatto of Mainz, for local synods (p. 25).
  - Regino gives Ancyra's graded penances by age and marriage (p. 26).
  - He **"also quoted the Theodosian statute (390) that provided death by burning for those men who 'act the part of a woman'"**. Goodich adds that it is unclear whether this means sodomy, "transvestism" or same-sex marriage, and sees a conflict between thirty years' penance and execution (p. 26).
  - For acts between women he gives three to seven years, depending on whether she was a nun or "a dildo was used" (p. 26).
  - The one passage in Regino that comes near a person in a woman's role is a late Roman law he quotes as an old canon. Boswell (p. 183 and n. 49) reads it as a "curiosity" Regino did not follow; Goodich reads it as live. That disagreement is the hook (§3, row 1).
- **Burchard of Worms (c. 965-1025), *Decretum* bk. 19.**
  - Goodich calls it the "most influential" collection; it is drawn from Regino (p. 28).
  - Acts between women: three to seven years, "as in Regino's work" (p. 28); generally lower than for men (p. 64).
  - Penances range from ten days to life, by age, rank, marriage and frequency (p. 28).
  - Burchard's undifferentiated list gave the confessor "virtual free rein" (p. 64).
- **1049, Reims.** "A local synod excommunicated certain unnamed Gallican heretics along with 'sodomites'" (p. 7). Elsewhere Goodich puts Damian's speech "at the council of Reims (1048)" (p. 29). The council was Leo IX's of 1049; see Cross-checks.
- **Peter Damian (1007-1072), *Liber Gomorrhianus*.**
  - Four grades of the vice (p. 29).
  - The complaint that clerics escape degradation "unless the accused have acted with eight or ten persons" (p. 29).
  - The Basilian penalty for a cleric with boys, which Goodich gives in detail (pp. 27, 31). **Not for use.**
  - Leo IX "favored the reinstatement of penitent clergy" (p. 31).
- **Ivo (Yves) of Chartres, letter of 1097/98.** On "Flora", the man whose consecration as bishop of Orléans he opposed (pp. 31-32; Boswell pp. 213-214).

### 1.2 The hostile glosses and schoolmen (c. 1100-1270)

- **Anselm of Laon (d. 1117), *Glossa ordinaria* on Lev. 20:13 and 18:22.**
  - Men excel in "strength of soul" and must avoid "becoming effeminate in word and action; the penalty for such a sin is death" (p. 37).
  - He extends the prohibition to women.
  - He ties the sin to idolatry, magic and heresy (p. 37).

  Goodich also reports the gloss on Joel 3:8: Jews "allegedly acquired this habit" from Chaldeans and Assyrians (p. 37). **That is the gloss's anti-Jewish slur, recorded only.**
- **Alan of Lille**: *De planctu Naturae* (1165/82), sodomy as the metaphor for all vice; his *Liber poenitentialis* (c. 1192 and 1199/1202) (pp. 33-34).
- **Peter the Chanter (d. 1197), *Verbum abbreviatum*.** Sodomy equated with homicide. God's order "specifically implied the nonandrogynous nature of humanity" (p. 56).
- **Robert of Flamborough** (*Liber poenitentialis*, 1208-1215), confessor at Saint-Victor, Paris: sample interrogations (pp. 56-58). Thomas of Chobham (*Summa confessorum*, c. 1216) (pp. 58-59).
- **William of Auvergne** (bishop of Paris from 1228): "links androgyny and, by implication, homosexuality with the errors of the pagans" (p. 62). He also took part in the 1240 burning of the Talmud (p. 61). **Recorded only.**
- **Aquinas** ranks the sins against nature (p. 63). Goodich's own summary phrase for the canonists' fear is "the 'disorder of androgyny'" (p. 68).
- **Relief valves**: reserved sins, dispensations, indulgences and commutation, through which "many presumably escaped the full rigor of the law" (pp. 66-69).

### 1.3 Councils (1102-1300)

- **1102, Council of London.** Anselm's letter, written 1102/03, says the vice is so widespread that few are embarrassed by it. Laymen were to lose their legal status (pp. 41-42). Boswell says the decree was suppressed (Boswell pp. 215-216).
- **1120, Council of Nablus**, held before King Baldwin II and Patriarch Gormund to correct crusader morals.
  - Burning for adult offenders (p. 42).
  - Protection for anyone forced who "cries out loudly in protest" (p. 42).
  - Readmission for a penitent who confesses first, and exile from the Kingdom for a relapse (pp. 42-43).
  - The statutes passed into the thirteenth-century bourgeois law of Jerusalem (p. 43).

  Goodich's own explanation includes "absorption of Muslim behavior patterns" (p. 42). That is an orientalist assumption and is not to be repeated.
- **1179, Lateran III, c. 11**, quoted in full: clerics deposed or confined to a monastery, laymen excommunicated (p. 43). It entered the *Liber extra* (1234). Bernard of Parma's gloss (c. 1263) cites Roman law's capital penalties (pp. 43-44).
- **1215, Lateran IV, c. 14**: clerical incontinence; prelates who shelter offenders are punished (p. 44).
- **1215, Lateran IV, c. 3 (the inquest).** Bishops must visit parishes yearly and appoint **three men of good reputation** to name heretics and "all others whose 'life and habits differ from the normal way of living of Christians'" (p. 52). That net, Goodich argues, came to catch "sexual nonconformists" (p. 52).
- **Local synods and statutes**:
  - Paris 1212 (nuns not to sleep together; a lamp in dormitories); Rouen 1214 (pp. 45-46);
  - Angers 1216/19 (seven years' penance) (p. 46);
  - reserved-sin lists at Paris (c. 1196), Fritzlar (1246), Liège (1287), Cambrai (1300/10) (p. 46);
  - Cistercian expulsions from 1221, and prisons required in 1279 (p. 46);
  - Rouen 1248: two monks of Jumièges sequestered "pending an investigation" (p. 46).
- **Cambrai statutes (1300-1310), after Flandrin:** acts between women, and acts by youths under twenty, were **not** reserved to the bishop (p. xiv). The church ranked them lower.

### 1.4 Secular law by date (ch. V)

- **Early codes** (Æthelberht, Wihtred, Alfred; Lombard laws of 731 and 733): no penalty for male same-sex acts (pp. 72-73).
- **Visigothic Spain**: Chindasvinth (c. 650), castration; Toledo XVI (693) under Egica (p. 73). Goodich calls their anti-Jewish laws "the most severe ever enacted" (p. 73). **Recorded only.**
- **Carolingian** (p. 74):
  - 743, bishops may try adultery and incest;
  - 789, the Ancyra canon renewed;
  - **799, "Charles likewise prohibited the wearing of men's garments by nuns"**;
  - 802, monks' sodomy prohibited;
  - 803/814, a list of "abominable crimes";
  - 827, Louis the Pious's capitulary.

  The 799 rule is about women in men's dress. It is **out of scope** and not built on.
- **Roman law** as received (pp. 75-76):
  - 342, "exquisite punishment" for "passive sodomitical marriage";
  - 390, public burning for passive partners;
  - Justinian's *Institutes* (533) and Novels (538, 544);
  - the glossators Azo (c. 1230) and Accursius (d. 1263).
- **Thirteenth century** (pp. 77-78):
  - England, *Fleta* (c. 1290); Goodich says death by fire and that Britton is silent. Both claims are wrong; see Cross-checks.
  - Castile, *Siete Partidas* (1256/63); Goodich says castration then stoning, also wrong.
  - France: Beaumanoir; *Coutumes de Touraine-Anjou*; the *Établissements*; the Orléans collection (dated 1260 by Goodich), with women losing "a 'member'" for two offences and death for a third.
  - **Amiens, 1261**: a royal order made the bishop hand alleged offenders to the town court (p. 78).
  - **Bourges, 1310**: a Florentine "servant of the crown" accused; evidence insufficient (p. 78).
  - **1317**: Guiardus de Capis, accused by a bribed witness, banished and later pardoned by the royal court (p. 78).
- **Italy.**
  - The 1233 "Great Alleluia"; mendicant podestàs; confraternities as local agents of the Inquisition (pp. 79-82).
  - *Ad extirpanda* (15 May 1252) (p. 82).
  - Podestà oaths to pursue heretics and sodomites (pp. 51-52).
  - Statutes:
    - **Bologna 1245/67**, introduced 1265/66 by Loderingo d'Andalò. The Società di Santa Maria was given power to pursue "heretics and sodomites". Humbert of Romans wrote to it in 1255 (p. 82).
    - **Perugia 1342**: forty men, eight per quarter, to "investigate and denounce" (p. 82).
    - Ascoli Piceno: a bounty for informers (p. 83).
    - Pisa: a fine for harbouring (p. 83).
    - **Siena 1262/70**: listed with heretics and Patarenes; monthly expulsion on "the first Sabbath of each month"; offenders barred from testifying. In the 1309/10 redaction, fines (p. 84).
    - Florence 1325 (p. 83).
    - Siena's council of 13 September 1324 voted 219 to 7 to appoint officers "to honor the Lord, ensure true peace" (p. 85).
  - Penalties are given in Goodich; they are not reproduced here.
- **Ghent, 28 September 1292**: one of the few known capital cases in the north (p. 85). **Date and place only.**
- **German lands and the Rhine.**
  - **1233, Gregory IX's *Vox in Rama*** to the archbishop of Mainz and bishop of Hildesheim, on heretics accused of devil-worship and orgies (p. 9).
  - A Cologne Luciferian trial of 1336 (p. 9).

  **Both fall under rule 25 (Sabbat and pact imagery): recorded, never used.** Also:
  - Cologne's council enquiry of June-July 1484 (parish priests' estimates) (pp. 14-15);
  - complaints at Lucerne 1414 and in Holland 1446 (p. 15);
  - the German slang "Florenzer" (p. 83).

  Goodich notes that in the free cities such offences fell to communal, not imperial, officials (p. 15).

### 1.5 Political and heresy charges

- **The heresy link.**
  - Henrician heretics at Bucy-le-Long, 1114 (Guibert) (p. 7).
  - Bernard's 1135 sermon (p. 7).
  - Cathars, both in Alan of Lille's polemic (p. 8) and as "Bulgarian" *bougres* "by about 1170" (pp. 8-9).
  - Beguines of Silesia accused of kissing during sermons (p. 9).
  - An Apostolic Brother at Bologna, 1299 (p. 9).

  Under rule 25 none of this may tie any faith to Cathars or Waldensians in the narrator's voice.
- **Templars.** First accusations in the Agen region "1304 or 1305". The inquiry opened on 24 August 1307, arrests followed on 13 October 1307, and Clement V's order to all princes came on 22 November 1307. Suppression at Vienne (1311/12); the leaders were executed by royal edict by 1314 (p. 12). **Dates only.**
- **Kings:** Edward II, Gaveston and Despenser (p. 11); Magnus Eriksson warned in Bridget's vision (p. 11). Dante's seventh circle as political smear (p. 11).

### 1.6 People described as living as women, or crossing dress

These are the record's or Goodich's categories, not the mod's.

- **Venice, 1354: the person the repo calls Rolandina Ronchaia** (Goodich: "Rolandinus Ronchaia").
  - Goodich's account (p. 13): Rolandina "had always looked and acted like a woman". She had married at Padua some years before. After the wife's death she lived as a woman ("became a transvestite" in his words), and at her arrest in Venice she was working as a female prostitute.
  - He dates the arrest to **12 March 1354** and the end to "within one week" (p. xiii), citing Ruggiero, "Sexual Criminality in the Early Renaissance: Venice 1338-1358", *Journal of Social History* 8 (1975): 18-37, and Lombardo's edition of the Council of Forty's deliberations (n. 29, p. 127).
  - **Use:** the date and the citation only. The narrator never claims her, never adopts "transvestite", and never depicts the end.
- **Venice, 1443**: a report "even notes a transvestite homosexual", unnamed (p. 14). Record only.
- **Venice, 1480**: a law forbade **women** "wearing men's hairstyles and clothes", calling it "a kind of sodomy" (p. 14). **Out of scope** (women).
- **c. 1000, Ralph Glaber** on the nobles of Aquitaine and Auvergne: "effeminate" ways; "they shaved their faces and wore their hair parted [causing them] to look like mummers" (pp. 3-4). This is **dress-and-hair invective aimed at men**.
- **Orderic Vitalis** (Goodich: "ca. 1090") on Norman youth: "long hair, feminine behavior, and sodomy" (p. 5). John of Salisbury, *Policraticus* (1159), on "feminine fashions" (p. 5). James of Vitry (c. 1230) on the French as "proud, soft, feminine" (p. 10).
- Goodich, after Bullough, says the church "sometimes encouraged deviations it allegedly condemned: transvestism and nudity" (p. xii). This refers to the women saints who crossed toward manhood. **Out of scope** (see `faith_design.md` §16, Saints).

### 1.7 Eunuchs and the "both-sexed"

- **St Equitius** (sixth century), in Otloh's retelling (eleventh century): in a vision he is castrated by an angel to free him from lust (p. 17). A hostile or ascetic topos; not for daughters.
- **Maimonides** (*Mishneh Torah*, *Issurei Bi'ah*), as summarised by Goodich:
  - relations with an *androginos* through the male organ are liable;
  - with a *tumtum* or through the female organ, no crime, though flogging "for disobedience";
  - "The hermaphrodite, however, is permitted to marry" (p. 48).
- **Peter the Chanter's "nonandrogynous nature of humanity"** (p. 56) and **William of Auvergne's androgyny "with the errors of the pagans"** (p. 62). These are the Latin schoolmen's hostile frame.

### 1.8 Women together

- Regino and Burchard: three to seven years (pp. 26, 28).
- Lower penalties than for men (p. 64).
- Paris 1212: nuns not to share beds (p. 46).
- Cambrai 1300/10: not a reserved case (p. xiv).
- Orléans: punished (p. 78).
- Silesian beguines, accused (p. 9).
- Maimonides: "no clear legal prohibition" and no flogging by law. Such women "are even permitted to marry members of the priesthood"; husbands are told to keep wives from them (p. 48).

### 1.9 Jews (recorded only; never a mechanic)

- **Rashi** on Gen. 18: God threatened destruction only if the Sodomites persisted, which teaches judges to investigate before capital cases. Goodich calls it "a plea for mercy rarely found in Christian interpreters" (pp. 46-47).
- **Maimonides** (pp. 47-49):
  - a man should marry;
  - limits on stoning: warning, minors exempt, two eyewitnesses needed;
  - **the *Guide*: Jews are forbidden "the garments of the opposite sex" because heathens wore them in the worship of Venus and Mars** (p. 49).
- *Orḥot Ḥayyim* (Aaron ha-Kohen of Lunel) and *Sefer ha-Ḥinukh* (pp. 49).
- Christian charges against Jews in this book:
  - the gloss on Joel (p. 37);
  - the "alleged Jewish influence" under Louis the Pious (p. 74);
  - the mendicants' agitation against "usurers, Jews, and heretics" (p. 80);
  - the expulsion of 1306 (p. 12);
  - the 1321 leper plot story, "using the Jews as intermediaries" (Appendix n. 13).

  All are hostile fabrications or anti-Jewish policy. **Never an event premise.**

## 2. Cross-checks

1. **English law: Goodich against Boswell and `cmp_boyd_karras_rykener.md` line 28.**
   - Goodich says *Fleta* (c. 1290) prescribes "death by fire" and that Britton makes "no reference to sodomy" (p. 77).
   - Boswell, reading the texts, says *Fleta* buries offenders alive and *Britton* does name and burn them. He states that Goodich "wrongly states" this (Boswell p. 292 nn. 70-71).
   - Boyd and Karras (line 28) have "Fleta and Britton prescribe death", textbooks not codes. That agrees with Boswell.
   - **Do not cite Goodich for English law.**
2. **Castile: Goodich against Boswell and `v2/euro_scan.md` lines 65-67.**
   - Goodich says the *Siete Partidas* give "castration followed by stoning" (p. 77).
   - Boswell: the *Fuero real* 4.9.2 has castration and then death by hanging by the legs; *Partida* 7.21 has death (Boswell pp. 288-289). `euro_scan.md` (Hutcheson) agrees with Boswell.
   - **Goodich is wrong here.**
3. **Orléans, *Li livres de jostice et de plet*.** Goodich orders the penalties differently (p. 77-78) from Boswell (p. 290). Both have women punished and death on the third offence. Use Boswell, who prints the Old French. Goodich's date "1260" is his own.
