# Johnson, *The History of the Yorubas* (1921): design reading

Source: Samuel Johnson, *The History of the Yorubas from the Earliest Times to the Beginning of the British Protectorate*, ed. O. Johnson (London: Routledge, 1921), archive.org `historyofyorubas00john`.
Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo. Page numbers are the 1921 edition's, from the running heads.

**What was read.** Only the Ọ̀yọ́ palace and the cults that touch it: "Religion" on Ṣàngó and Ọya (pp. 34–37) and the Alapini (p. 30); "Government", the palace officers, the eunuchs, the Ilari and the ladies of the palace (pp. 57–67); and, found by search, the origin story of the Osi'wefa (pp. 162–163) and the legend of Moremi (pp. 147–148). Everything else (the kings' history, the 19th-c. wars, the coronation chapter beyond a glance, the appendices) was skipped.

**Relation to existing notes.** `south_reading_list.md` (West Africa, finding 4, candidate B) cites Johnson for the Iwefa (p. 59), "Baba" (p. 60), the Ilari and their female "mothers" (pp. 60–62), the Iyamode (p. 65) and the monorchid Alapini (p. 30), and rejects a Yoruba faith. This note checks those references, corrects one of them, and records what is usable as **reaction flavour** for Orisha courts.

**Date warning, for every line below.** Johnson, a Yoruba Anglican pastor, finished the book by 1897; it was edited and published in 1921. It describes the Ọ̀yọ́ palace of the 19th century, at the end of the empire, from oral tradition and memory. Ọ̀yọ́'s foundation date is unknown (proposals run from the 10th to the 15th century), and Johnson's early kings are legendary. Nothing here is evidence for 867–1453. Johnson also writes as a Christian hostile to the cults ("the greedy worshippers of this heartless god", p. 36). Ṣàngó's religion is practised today in Nigeria, Benin, Brazil and Cuba.

## 1. The argument in brief

Johnson describes an elaborate palace in which royal servants are made, not born, and in which titles cross sex freely. Eunuchs (*Iwefa*) are judges, priests and the king's double; *Ilari* are shaved, cut and renamed with a sentence of the king's will, each male paired with a female "mother"; titled women hold the treasury, crown the king, and command the eunuchs; one woman is called "father" by the king. The Ṣàngó cult is part of the state, with a eunuch keeping its shrine and a palace lady running the king's chapel.

## 2. What matters for the design (page refs)

### Eunuchs, *Iwefa* (pp. 59–60, 162–163)

- "The Eunuchs are called Iwefa or Iba-afin" ("lordlings of the palace"); the chief is the *Ona'efa*, with the *Otun'efa* and the *Osi'efa* as lieutenants, down to six grades, plus untitled ones and boys (p. 59).
- The *Ona'efa* hears suits and appeals when the king cannot, "and his decision is as good as the King's" (p. 59).
- The *Otun'efa* governs Koso, the town built for Ṣàngó, worships at the shrine for the people, and guards the king's children (p. 59).
- The *Osi'efa*, "although the least of the three yet is the most honoured": he represents the king, may command armies, use the crown, the umbrellas and the *kakaki* trumpet, has the run of the harem, makes the king's bed, checks on him at midnight and cock-crow, and leads the king's wives' dance at the Akesan market (p. 59).
- **The origin story** (pp. 162–163): in a lost battle against the Nupe ("Tapas"), the Osi'wefa Ajanlapa exchanged dress with King Ajiboyede, wore the crown and robes, and died under arrows; the enemy, seeing his fixed grin, fled. The king wished to honour Ajanlapa's son with the post of constant attendant; that "cannot be held by any other than a eunuch", so on the nobles' advice "he was emasculated" (p. 163). Johnson calls it "a painful necessity" (p. 163).
- Eunuchs outrank Ilari: "however young a Eunuch may be, he must be addressed as 'Baba' (father) by any Ilari even the oldest" (p. 60).
- Castration is said to have begun as punishment for incest or bestiality; later kings chose bought boys, to spare "the remorse of conscience" (p. 60). Adult castration remains the penalty for adultery with a king's wife, and only the capital may do it (p. 60).
- The king's wives give birth and nurse in a eunuch's house (p. 60). Titled eunuchs keep harems; their wives are *Awewo*, "one with hands tied", "doomed to be for ever childless" (p. 60).
- They wear their gowns gathered on the shoulders, arms bare (p. 60), and may seize anything in the market (p. 60).

### The Alapini (p. 30)

- Head of the Egúngún ancestral-masquerade cult and one of the Ọ̀yọ́ Mesi; "by virtue of his office he must be a monorchis. Thus qualified, he shares with the eunuchs in all their privileges" (p. 30).

### Ilari (pp. 60–62, 67)

- "The term Ilari denotes parting of the head" (p. 60); both sexes, in hundreds (p. 61).
- **The making:** "first shaved completely", small incisions on the occiput (male) and left arm, a charm rubbed in, and a new name that is "some attributes of the King, or ... his purpose, intention or will" — e.g. *Oba l'olu* "the King is supreme", *Madarikan* "do not oppose him" (p. 61).
- **Every male Ilari "has a female counterpart who is called his companion. The Ilaris themselves by courtesy call them their 'mother'"** (p. 62). They are made together and seek each other's interest, with no intimacy, exchanging presents at the yearly festivals (p. 62).
- Each has a clay image, *sugudu*, with the same cuts (p. 62). Half the head is shaved alternately every fifth day (p. 62).
- Female Ilari are shaved along the midline, with two plaited "horns" (p. 67); they "have the exclusive privilege of using the female head ties, or men's caps" (p. 67).
- At a king's death all Ilari drop their names and let their hair grow; the new king re-creates them in batches every five days with new names (p. 67).

### Ladies of the palace (pp. 63–67)

- Eight titled ladies, eight priestesses, other ladies of rank, the Ilari, and the king's wives (*Ayaba*) (p. 63).
- *Iya kere*: holds the treasury and the regalia; can stop any state reception by withholding them; crowns the king; is "the 'mother' of all the Ilaris male and female", who are made in her apartment, and keeps their *sugudu* (pp. 63–64). She can have the Osi'efa arrested; she is celibate for life (p. 64).
- *Iya Naso*: Ṣàngó worship and the king's Ṣàngó chapel (p. 64). *Iyafin Iku*: the king's "Adosu Ṣàngó", standing for the child every Ṣàngó worshipper must devote (p. 64).
- *Iyalagbon*: the crown prince's mother, or a stand-in "mother" (p. 64).
- **The *Iyamode* (p. 65):** she worships the spirits of the dead kings; the celibate women of the Bara (royal mausoleum) call her "Baba" (father); "The King looks upon her as his father, and addresses her as such"; he kneels to her and to no one else, and she kneels back, "never reclining on her elbow as is the custom of the women" (p. 65). Women "possessed" by dead kings are placed in her charge (p. 65). The king's bards (*Akunyungba*) are trained in her apartments (p. 65).
- *Ode*: head of Ọ̀ṣọ́ọ̀sì's worshippers, appears "dressed as a hunter" with a cowrie-strung bow on her shoulder (p. 66). *Obagunte*: represents the king in the Ogboni house (p. 66).

### Ṣàngó (pp. 34–37)

- Ṣàngó is the deified fourth king, a tyrant deposed and expelled (p. 34). His friends' sacrifices made them the *Mogba*, his priests, an hereditary office (p. 34).
- Initiation: a list of animals, beads and leaves; the candidate is purified, "seated on a mortar and shaved", cut on the shaven head, and the pounded mixture rubbed in (pp. 34–35). The same shave–cut–rub pattern as the Ilari (p. 61).
- After a lightning strike the house is interdicted, the "thunderbolt" dug up, and the family must "give over a son to the priests" and pay heavily (pp. 35–36). Johnson presents the bolt-finding as a fraud (pp. 35–36).
- **Ṣàngó's and Ọya's ends are told as suicides (pp. 34, 36). Do not use.** The brief forbids suicide content; the deification story should not appear in any form.
- Ọya's river is the Niger, "Odo Oya" (p. 36).

### Moremi (pp. 147–148)

- An Ife queen who let herself be captured by raiders to learn their secret, and freed her city with it (pp. 147–148). The same shape as the Mande Sugulun Kulunkan, who draws out the smith-king's secret (`waf_brett_smith_bamana.md`, pp. 250–251). A woman's courage, not a "manly" one.

## 3. Corrections to the candidate

1. **"Iwefa ... whose second officer keeps the Ṣàngó shrine at Koso (p. 59)"** is right: the *Otun'efa* (second in rank) governs Koso (p. 59). The reading list's "eunuchs addressed as 'Baba' (p. 60)" is narrower than it reads: Ilari must call any eunuch "Baba" (p. 60).
2. **The "mother" of each male Ilari** (p. 62) is a female *companion* of equal making; the Iya kere is "mother" of all Ilari (p. 63). The reading list has the first; add the second.
3. **The Iyamode** is called "father" by the king and "Baba" by the Bara women (p. 65). Correct in the reading list. Add: the king kneels only to her, and she does not take the women's posture (p. 65). That is the strongest gender-crossing *title* in the text, and it is FtM in direction: **reaction only.**
4. **The Osi'wefa story (pp. 162–163)** is the only account of a man made a eunuch as an *honour*, and it is forced on a grieving son. Do not adapt it as anything a daughter of the knife would welcome.
5. **Every item is 19th-century.** Candidate B's own grade (LATER) stands. Johnson does not mention "brides" (*ìyàwó*) of Ṣàngó or male priests in women's dress in the pages read; that material is Matory's 1980s fieldwork and must be cited to Matory, not Johnson.

## 4. Design implications (reaction and flavour only)

- **Orisha-court reaction to a daughter of the knife:** Ọ̀yọ́-style courts may offer an Iwefa title (*Ona'efa*, *Otun'efa*, *Osi'efa*) after 1400 at the earliest, flagged as a back-projection. Reaction line (Orisha courtier, approving): "The Osi'efa wears the crown when the king cannot; why should you not wear a name?" Narrator note: Johnson 1921, p. 59, describing the 19th-century palace.
- **Reaction to Badenya's naming rite:** Ọ̀yọ́ courtiers would recognise the Ilari pattern of shave, cut and new name that states a will (p. 61). Line (Orisha courtier): "In Ọ̀yọ́ the king names his servants for his will. Your bards name her for her own." Keep it comparative and outside the Mande faith; never make the Badenya rite an Ilari rite.
- **Paired "mothers"** (p. 62) echo Badenya's "a woman claims her as her mother's child". Reaction only; do not borrow.
- **The Iyamode** (p. 65) gives Orisha courts a reason to be at ease with titles that cross sex. A one-line modifier description or loc flavour; FtM, so not part of any daughter's path.
- **Moremi** (pp. 147–148) can be an Orisha-world parallel for the Mande sister's courage in a cross-faith event: "Ife has its Moremi; Mali has its Kulunkan."
- **Do not use:** Ṣàngó's and Ọya's suicides (pp. 34, 36); the king's mother "asked to 'go to sleep'" (p. 63); the castration penalties (p. 60); the *Awewo* (p. 60); Johnson's mockery of Ṣàngó priests (pp. 35–36); any claim that Ṣàngó's devotees are, or were, trans women.
