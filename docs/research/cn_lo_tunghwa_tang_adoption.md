# Lo Tung-hwa 羅彤華, "The Types of Adopted Children in the Tang and Their Status in Ritual and Law" (2013), design reading

**Citation (verified on the PDF's first page and its English abstract page).** 羅彤華，〈唐代養子的類型及其禮法地位〉，《臺大歷史學報》（*Historical Inquiry*, Department of History, National Taiwan University）第 52 期（2013 年 12 月），頁 1–51. DOI 10.6253/ntuhistory.2013.52.01. The English title is "The Types and Confucian and Legal Status of the Adopted Child in the Tang Dynasty". The journal is 《臺大歷史學報》, not 《漢學研究》, and this matches item 47 in `china_reading_list.md`.

**What was read.** The whole article (pp. 1–45), in Chinese, plus the English abstract (pp. 50–51). The PDF's pages match the journal's. Translations are mine.

**Article map.**
- 前言 (pp. 2–5): terms, past scholarship, and the four general types plus two special ones.
- 一 (pp. 6–22): the general types: (1) same-lineage adoption, (2) adoption outside the surname, (3) adopted daughters, (4) adopting people of base status.
- 二 (pp. 22–30): the special types: (1) stepchildren who came with a remarried mother, (2) eunuchs' adopted sons and daughters.
- 三 (pp. 30–43): legal and ritual status: surname, register, genealogy, mourning, property, titles and protection privilege, and return to the birth lineage (歸宗).
- 結語 (pp. 43–45): conclusion.

## 1. The argument

- **Tang law permits only same-lineage adoption of the right generation.** The Household Statute (戶令) reads 「無子者，聽養同宗於昭穆相當者」, "one without sons may adopt a lineage member of the matching generation" (p. 7). Lo argues that the Tang was the first to separate the adopted son (養子) from the designated heir (嗣子). An adoptee *may* be the heir but need not be (pp. 7–8, 43).
- **Adopting outside the surname is a crime, with one exception for foundlings.** Code art. 157 (養子捨去) reads 「即養異姓男者，徒一年；與者，笞五十」, "whoever adopts a boy of another surname: one year's penal servitude; the giver, fifty strokes" (p. 12). A foundling of three or under may be adopted "even of another surname" and takes the adopter's surname, 「雖異姓，聽收養，即從其姓」 (p. 17). A child who was lost rather than abandoned must go back to the parents who identify it, and they pay the finder 「乳哺之直」, "the cost of nursing" (p. 17).
  - The ritual reason is old: 「神不歆非類，民不祀非族」, "spirits do not accept offerings from those not of their kind; people do not sacrifice to those not of their lineage" (pp. 2, 12).
  - **Practice ignored it.** Adopting a sister's son was common enough that Dunhuang kept a fill-in-the-blank contract for it (S.5700, copied as S.5647) (p. 13). Empress Wu made her sister's son Helan Minzhi her father's heir under the surname Wu, and he lost the surname when he was exiled (pp. 13, 43). Lo's summary is that the gap between ritual and law on one side and human feeling on the other was "very large" (很大) (abstract, p. 1).
- **Adopting a daughter is not punished, whatever her surname.** After the penalty for adopting a boy of another surname, the commentary adds 「養女者不坐」, "adopting a daughter is not punished" (p. 19). The reason is that daughters do not carry the line or the sacrifices (p. 19). Adopted daughters are found at every level, from the palace down. Emperors, women officials (尚宮) and eunuchs all adopted girls. Gao Lishi's widowed adopted daughter in Luoyang "could speak of palace affairs" and was once taken for the lost Empress Dowager Shen (p. 19). Lo calls daughter-adoption a matter of affection, 「人間溫情與家庭溫暖」, "human warmth and the warmth of a family" (p. 20).
  - **The catch is property.** Under the statute on dividing property (應分), an adopted daughter gets a dowry, not a son's share. The Dunhuang draft contract P.4525, in which Kang Yuanchang adopts a daughter, says that if she is filial to the end there will be 「留念衣物」, "clothes to remember us by". If she "will not obey halfway", she must repay the nursing gifts and leave, and 「不許論訟養父家具」, she "may not sue for the adoptive father's goods" (p. 38).
- **Status lines are stricter than surname lines.** Art. 159 forbids commoners to adopt people of base status, whether a son or a daughter, under the rule that different status groups may not adopt each other, 「別色準法不得相養」 (pp. 20–21). The legal workaround was to free the person first and then adopt. Lord Wang of Taiyuan freed two maids and a manservant when he fell ill. A **Khitan maid "begged the surname Wang"**, was given a birth-order number (fifteenth), and became the daughter of his second wife, Lady Jia. He "held a vegetarian feast and gave a handwritten deed before the monks, sealed as proof", 「設齋，對僧士並與手書，用印為證」 (pp. 21–22).
- **Eunuchs as adopters (pp. 26–30).**
  - Yang Fugong (d. 894) is the extreme case: 「諸假子皆為節度使、刺史，又養宦官子六百人，皆為監軍」, "his fictive sons were all military governors and prefects; he also adopted six hundred eunuch sons, all made army supervisors" (p. 26).
  - Lo separates the *jiazi/yi'er* (假子, 義兒, "fictive sons" taken for armed power) from the *yangzi* (養子) taken to continue a family. His article is about the second kind (pp. 15–17, 26).
  - Eunuch families still ran like lineages. Gao Lishi's line held eunuch posts for at least six generations, and so did Yang Zhilian's line down to Yang Fugong (p. 28). Gao Lishi's designated heir was a nephew who left eunuch service for a regular office, while his other adopted sons stayed eunuchs (pp. 27–28).
  - Eunuchs **adopted daughters as well and married them into other eunuch and official houses**. Qiu Wenyi had six daughters, and five of them married eunuchs (p. 30).
  - A 791 edict allowed only eunuchs of fifth rank and above to adopt, and only one son, of the same surname, under the age of ten. Lo calls it "a dead letter" (一紙不切實際的空文) (p. 29).
- **Scholar-officials used names to deny eunuchs kinship (p. 40).** When Qiu Shiliang asked for protection privilege (蔭) for his son, the supervising secretary Li Zhongmin ruled 「開府階誠宜蔭子，謁者監何由有兒？」, "the rank of Kaifu truly merits protecting a son, but how does a Palace Steward come to have a boy?" Under Yizong, Zheng Xun wrote 「內謁者監不合有男」, "an Inner Palace Steward ought not to have a son." Lo reads these rulings as hostility to eunuch power that "also refused to recognise that they could adopt".
- **An adopted child is treated exactly like a born one.** The Code says 「若養者，與親同」, "the adopted are the same as kin" (pp. 24, 31).
  - **Surname and genealogy:** an adoptee from another surname takes the adopter's name and enters the adopter's genealogy. The adoption is announced to kin and done by written deed, as in Liu Fangyu's 「請嗣券書」 (p. 31).
  - **Register:** the adoptee leaves the birth household's register at 18. A younger child stays on it, 「即所繼處，有母在者，雖小亦聽析出」, "unless there is a mother in the adoptive household, in which case even a small child may be transferred" (p. 34).
  - **Mourning:** the adoptee wears the full three years for the adoptive father and one degree less for the birth parents (pp. 35–36).
  - **Property:** the adoptee takes an equal share with any brothers. The Dunhuang contracts P.3443 (Hu Zaicheng) and Shi Fansan's deed add a clause that an unfilial adoptee is 「空身逐出」, "driven out with nothing" (pp. 36–37).
  - **Titles and protection privilege:** both pass to the adoptee (pp. 39–40).
- **The bond can be undone (歸宗), but mostly in one direction (p. 41).** An adoptee who abandons adopters who have no son gets two years' penal servitude. The adopters may send the adoptee back whenever they choose, son or no son. The adoptee may leave only if the birth family has lost its sons. Lo calls the bond 「擬制血緣關係」, a "fictive blood relation", less stable than birth, and adoptees often longed for the birth family (pp. 32–34, 42–43).
- **Clergy.** Lo does not discuss Daoist or Buddhist clergy as adopters. Monks appear only as witnesses to Lord Wang's freeing-and-adoption (p. 22). For nuns and monks adopting daughters at Dunhuang, use Hao Chunwen (`cn_hao_monks_nuns_dunhuang.md`).
