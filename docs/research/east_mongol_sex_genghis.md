Source: Baasanjav Terbish, *Sex in the Land of Genghis Khan: From the Times of the Great Conqueror to Today* (Lanham: Lexington Books / Rowman & Littlefield, 2023). ISBN 9781666937497. Plain text at `scratchpad/books/east_08.txt` (~77k words, 7,389 lines). Page numbers below are the printed page numbers recovered from running headers in the text file.

Purpose of this pass: (1) hostile-faith text/events when Transbian Maximalism meets the steppe; (2) a possible later steppe branch; (3) on-map precedents for gender variance, eunuchs, adoption, female rule. Design is locked; this file supplies sourced material only.

## 1. Book map

**Author.** Baasanjav Terbish — "an affiliated researcher at the Mongolia and Inner Asia Studies Unit of the University of Cambridge. He has a PhD in social anthropology from Cambridge University" (p. 175); previous book *State Ideology, Science, and Pseudoscience in Russia* (2022). (Mongolian-born; the acknowledgments thank Gaelle Lacaze, Richard Armstrong and Franck Billé as readers, and the book was funded by "the generous scholarship provided by the Mongol American Cultural Association", p. vii). The author describes his own position: "my subjective gaze, conditioned by my gendered male body born in socialist Mongolia but having been subjected to liberal values for a number of years" (p. 4).

**Type.** Academic-press (Lexington/Rowman & Littlefield) but written in a popular, often jokey register; explicitly a "broad brush" survey (p. 2). For the medieval period the author works "mainly in English secondary literature and English translations" (p. 3) — Rashid al-Din (Thackston tr.), Juvaini (Boyle), Carpini and Rubruck (Rockhill/Dawson), Marco Polo (Moule-Pelliot), Yuanshi via van Gulik, Secret History via de Rachewiltz, plus Broadbridge, Favereau, Holmgren, Atwood, Jackson, Morgan, Landa, Amitai, El-Azhari, El-Rouayheb, Ruan, Hinsch, Kim ("The Third Gender: Palace Eunuchs"). So the medieval chapter is a competent synthesis of standard scholarship, not new archival work.

**Reliability note.** Strong on synthesis and refreshingly explicit about source bias (e.g. Juvaini's motive to slander the Ögedeid house, p. 32 n.49; Rashid al-Din's motive to reconcile Islam with Yasaq, p. 17). Weaknesses for our purposes: (a) the author's central thesis — that sodomy was *never* banned under Genghis and that the Great Yasa may not have existed — is an argument from silence that he himself labels "reverse reconstruction" (p. 15) and rests on Morgan 1986; treat as a defensible minority-to-mainstream scholarly view, not fact. (b) Breezy editorialising ("had the balls to take on the Mongol garrison", p. 26) and some sweeping comparative-religion claims (on Islam, p. 8) are the author's voice, not evidence. (c) No Mongolian-language primary sources on sex for the medieval period exist, and he says so (p. 3). For CK3 flavour text, the quotations from Carpini, Rubruck, Rashid al-Din, Juvaini and the Secret History are the gold; use the author's interpretive claims as "a scholar argues".

## 2. Precedent catalogue

### 2a. Chapter 1 — the imperial period (13th–14th c.)

**E1. Shamanic indifference to sex; no genital rite** | 13th c. steppe | Terbish argues Tengrism had "a relaxed attitude toward carnal pleasure" and, crucially, no rite of genital cutting. | "Indifferent to humans' sexual desires and sexual organs, shamanism did not include a culture of confession of sexual guilt, or self-punishment for 'impure' thoughts, or genital mutilation of children, or persecution of 'infidels,' as is the case in Abrahamic religions." (p. 8) | Baseline for hostile-faith text: to a steppe Tengrist the castration knife is not sacred but alien and *Abrahamic*. A steppe branch must import the rite, not find it.

**E2. Rulers set sexual law at will (no priestly veto)** | 13th c. | Because the gods are silent, "tribal leaders could impose new sexual norms or prohibitions among their followers at will". | "if adultery was not punished under a particular leader, then under the next it could become an offense punishable by death, as was the case under Genghis Khan." (p. 9) | A khan who adopts the faith can legislate it by decree; equally the next khan can ban it. Mechanically: steppe faith status should flip with the ruler, not with a clergy.

**E3. Hunter's abstinence / marriage to the Lord of the Forest's daughter** | modern Mongolia, author claims "probably of ancient origin" | Hunters abstain from sex before hunting, construed as fidelity to a female forest spirit "bride". | "hunting is understood as an activity characterized by a temporary 'marriage alliance' between the hunter and the female shamanic spirit." (p. 9) | Ritual chastity framed as spirit-marriage — a hook for a steppe-branch chastity tradition without Christian ascetic language.

**E4. Khatun regency: Töregene, Sorkaktani Beki, Ebüskün, Ogul Kaimish** | 1241–1251, Mongol Empire | After Ögedei's death the whole empire was run by Genghisid widows; Töregene ruled 1241–46 and called the kurultai that elected Güyüg; Ogul Kaimish ruled 1248–51 until Sorkaktani out-manoeuvred her and installed Möngke. | "all four of Genghis Khan's sons … were dead, and the empire had been presided over by Genghisid's widows, including Töregene Khatun (who as empress presided over the whole empire), Ebüskün Khatun (… who ruled Central Asia), and Sorkaktani Beki (… who ruled over eastern Mongolia …). Only the Golden Horde of Russia was under the control of Batu Khan" (p. 10); "Even when not organized by them, kurultais were attended by Genghisid's wives, whose opinions carried weight." (p. 10) | Strongest on-map precedent for female rule within the Mongol world; all four women are real CK3 characters or ancestors in the 1241–51 window. Khatun-alliance events.

**E5. Mother (eke) superior to all children; mother-son bond sacred** | 13th c. | "the mother (eke) was always considered superior to all of her children, including her oldest sons" (p. 10); the words *ekener* "wife" and *eke* "mother" share a root (p. 31 n.6); a 1240s Golden Horde birch-bark poem of mother–son longing expresses "a feeling deemed sacred in Mongol cosmology" (p. 31 n.12). | Reverence for the maternal line is native; a matrilineal-adoptive heirship can be framed to a Mongol as honouring *eke*.

**E6. Women's economic and martial competence (Carpini)** | 1246 | "Their women make everything, leather garments, tunics, shoes, leggings and everything made of leather; they also drive the carts and repair them, they load the camels, and in all their tasks they are very swift and energetic. All the women wear breeches and some of them shoot like the men." (p. 10, quoting Carpini) | Women in breeches who shoot: steppe gender norms already blur Latin-Christian dress markers. Good flavour for a Mongol observer finding the faith's women *less* alien than its men.

**E7. Rubruck on women drinking and dancing** | 1253–55 | "Mongol women of all social origins enjoyed getting as drunk as their men and joined in the singing and dancing." (p. 10) | Flavour.

**E8. Marriage by capture; abducted women become legitimate wives; taking names from the vanquished** | 12th–13th c. | "Once abducted, females, whether married or single, were regarded as the captor's legitimate wives, not servants or slaves. In the steppe world, overpowering one's enemy meant taking everything they owned: women, herds, and even names. For example, Genghis Khan was born as Temüjin, a name taken from a Tatar man captured by his father." (p. 11) | Hostile interaction: a khan who captures the faith's women will consider them wives, and may consider its castrates… what? (see E26). Name-taking as a conquest rite is a cultural note usable for a steppe branch's naming-on-the-rite.

**E9. Unlimited polygyny (Carpini)** | 1246 | "has as many wives as he can keep, one a hundred, another fifty, another ten—one more, another less" (p. 11); Rashid al-Din gives Genghis "nearly five hundred wives and concubines" (p. 31 n.8). | Weakens any separatist "one woman, one household" model on the steppe.

**E10. Levirate and permitted kin-marriage (Carpini)** | 1246 | "It is the general custom for them to marry any of their relations, with the exception of their mother, daughter and sister by the same mother. They can however take in marriage their sisters who have only the same father, and even their father's wives after his death; also, a younger brother may marry his brother's wife after his death; or another younger relation is expected to take her." (pp. 11–12) Examples: Ögedei married Möge Khatun; Möngke married Tolui's widow Ogul Kaimish; Khülegü married Dokuz Khatun; Abaga married Öljeitü Khatun; Öljeitü married Eltuzmish (p. 31 n.15). | Widows are kept *inside* the lineage — the structural opposite of a women-leaving-to-join-a-sisterhood faith. Expect hostile text about the faith "stealing widows".

**E11. Genghisid princesses remain in husbands' households after widowhood** | 13th c. | "After the death of their husbands, Genghis Khan's daughters did not return to him but remained in the households of their deceased husbands by marrying their husbands' kin." (p. 31 n.17) | Same as E10.

**E12. Lax attitude to virginity** | 13th c. | "These practices—kidnap marriages, concubinage, polygamy, and levirate marriage—whereby women changed multiple hands also explain why the Mongols had a lax attitude toward virginity." (p. 12) | A faith that does not prize virginity meets a culture that does not either — a rare point of non-hostility.

**E13. Alan Goa, widow-progenitrix; all a wife's children are the husband's; Jochi** | legendary / c. 1180s | Genghis's line descends from a widow who bore three sons after her husband's death "and there was no shame in recording this particular story" (p. 12). Börte returned from Merkit captivity "heavily pregnant with Jochi"; "any child born to a married woman was also considered the child of her husband … Genghis Khan … always treated his oldest son, Jochi, who was not his biological progeny, on a par with his other sons. In fact, this custom is still the norm in Mongolia today" (p. 13). | **Social, not biological, fatherhood** is native to the steppe. Adoptive heirship has a Mongol analogue: the Golden Lineage itself rests on a widow's sons of uncertain paternity. Use in a khatun-alliance event.

**E14. Adultery a capital crime under Genghis; Yuan code lets husband kill adulterous wife; Kirakos** | 1206–; Yuan; Armenia 13th c. | "the ban on adultery on pain of death" (p. 13); "The Yuan code promulgated under Kublai permitted a Chinese husband to kill an adulterous wife if caught in a sexual act" (p. 13); Kirakos of Ganjak: "They [Mongol men] took as many wives as they liked, but they in no way spared adulterers with their wives." (p. 13) | Hostile text: the faith's rite that takes a *husband's wife* into a priesthood may be read as adultery/wife-theft, i.e. a capital matter.

**E15. Ögedei's punitive mass rape of 4,000 Oirat girls** | c. 1230s–41 | Rashid al-Din: Oirats married their daughters off to avoid an imperial marriage levy; Ögedei "ordered … all the girls of that tribe above the age of seven be assembled. . . . Four thousand girls were assembled. Then he (Ögedei) ordered those who were daughters of amirs to be separated, and a command was given for all present (soldiers) to have intercourse with them. Two of the girls died. The rest were put into two rows. Those who were worthy of court were sent to the harem; some he gave to cheetah keepers and falconers; others he gave to various court retainers." (p. 14) "a few he (Ögedei) sent to brothels and the emissary hostel" (p. 22). | The Dorley precedent's enemy archetype at scale: sexual violence as *state* punishment. A faith that castrates violent men would read this as the paradigm crime. Event seed: survivors of such a levy flee to the faith.

**E16. Concubines taken in plunder are "the Mongol's custom"; Ghazan abstained** | 1295–1304 | "Although it is the Mongol's custom to keep such girls in concubinage in order to enjoy themselves in times of war and conquest, the Padishah of Islam (Ghazan) was never so inclined" (p. 14). | Flavour; a pious Ilkhan can be a sympathetic contact.

**E17. "With one's slaves one may do as one pleases" (Rubruck)** | 1253–55 | Mongol men "freely had sex with their slaves or concubines without legal repercussions" (pp. 14–15). | Hostile baseline: a captured priestess is a slave.

**E18. Sodomy not banned under Genghis (author's thesis)** | 1206–1368 | "sodomy—I argue—was never banned under Genghis Khan" (p. 15), by "reverse reconstruction": the 1640 Mongol-Oirat Regulations, the Chajin Bichig, the Yuan code, Carpini, Rubruck and the Secret History are all silent. "Had Genghis Khan banned sodomy, his immediate successors would have preserved this ban, which, as we see, is not the case." (p. 15) | Supports a steppe where the faith is hated for *cutting*, not for same-sex intimacy. Also: "the Yuan code … lacked any mention of sodomy, presumably under Mongol influence." (p. 16)

**E19. *Anda* sworn brotherhood; Temüjin and Jamukha under one blanket** | 12th c. | "sworn brotherhood, known in Mongolian as anda. Aimed primarily at establishing political alliances between unrelated male teenagers or men, the initiation ritual involved two friends swearing to one another 'to share but a single life' and 'not to abandon one another.'" Temüjin and Jamukha "declared themselves sworn friends and loved each other" and "enjoyed themselves reveling and feasting, and at night they slept together, the two of them alone under their blanket." (p. 15, quoting Secret History §116–17). Author adds the caveat: "Mongols share a blanket not only with their spouses or lovers but also with their siblings and guests to stay warm." (p. 32 n.30) | **Non-marital, ritually sworn kinship between unrelated persons is native and politically primary.** The faith's sponsor bond and sister-making can be explained to a Mongol as *anda*. Key for (i) and (o).

**E20. Ahmad Tegüder, the Qalandars and penis-rings** | 1282–84, Ilkhanate | First Muslim Ilkhan; called the Sufi Hasan Mengli "brother" (karyndash); Qalandars "were perhaps most notorious for the rings with which some of their members pierced their penises to enforce sexual abstinence" (p. 16). Accused by al-Nuwayri of "having an inclination toward 'sons of the Mongols' and 'secluding himself with them [boys]'" and of disregarding "Genghis Khan's rules" (p. 16). After his execution Hasan Mengli "was cooked alive in boiling water" (p. 16). | **On-map genital modification as ascetic rite** in the Ilkhanate — the Qalandar ring is a precedent for a body-marked celibate order that a Mongol ruler tolerated, then purged. Also: the "disregards Genghis Khan's rules" accusation is the template for how a khan gets deposed for patronising an alien sexual order.

**E21. Geikhatu deposed amid pederasty accusations** | 1291–95 | "linked to his alleged pederasty among the keshig (imperial guard) pages and his incestuous relationship with his cousin Baidu's young son Kipchak" (p. 17); author warns accusations may be "leveled against them posthumously to justify their overthrow" (p. 17). | Template for a deposition event.

**E22. Ghazan executes sodomites "in accordance with Islamic law and Yasaq"** | 1295–1304 | Rashid al-Din: "His (Ghazan's) noble mind never allowed anyone else to indulge in sodomy or abominations, and he constantly issued strong-worded edicts forbidding such things. He had several persons executed for that sin in accordance with Islamic law and Yasaq." (p. 17) Author: "Rashid al-Din's notion that Yasaq also banned sodomy should be taken with a pinch of salt" (p. 17). | For Muslim khanates (Ilkhanate, Golden Horde, Chagatai after conversion) the faith is doubly outlawed: sharia + Yasaq. Hostile-text register: "abominations" (Rashid's term).

**E23. Oirat boys among the Mamluks; "beardless" bodies as feminised objects** | c. 1296, Egypt/Syria | al-Maqrizi: Oirat refugees "With Asiatic features and smooth, hairless faces … immediately became objects of sexual attention" (p. 18). Ghazan asks the Mamluk envoy: "How [did it happen] that your amirs left women and are using boys? That is—beardless boys." Envoy blames the Oirats: "he came to us with boys from the sons of Tatars, and the people [started] to be engaged in them instead of women." (p. 18) | Beardlessness read as sexual femininity — the same somatic logic by which eunuchs are "feminine men" (E26). Good for text where a Mamluk or Persian reads the faith's castrates as *ghulman*.

**E24. The Great Yasa: existence doubted; courts (*yargu*) used mixed sources** | 1206– | "the Great Yasa is never mentioned in the Secret History of Mongols … but was popularized later by foreigners" (p. 20); "I believe that the Mongol state did not necessarily have a single, overarching code or book of laws attributable to Genghis Khan" (p. 20); courts "could pass judgments based on local circumstances and conventions by using a variety of historical and contemporary sources, not all of which were written down" (p. 20). Judges: "precedents of previous great judges such as Shigi-Khutugu" (p. 20). | For (l) outlaw framing: there is no single Yasa clause to cite; the faith's legality is decided *case by case by a yargu* under the khan. Design a "judgment of the yargu" event rather than a fixed law.

**E25. Fatima of Karakorum: slave, procuress, regent's favourite, executed by orifice-sewing** | 1240s | "Fatima came to prominence and reached enviable political influence under Töregene Khatun … denounced as a witch by Töregene's sons Köden and Güyüg … her upper and lower orifices were sewn up, and she was drowned in a river. Later historians, including Juvaini, contend that … Fatima had run a trade managing prostitutes at the newly built Mongol capital." (pp. 21–22) Author: Juvaini "could have had political motives … slandering those of the house of Ögedei" (p. 32 n.49). | A foreign woman with "unrestricted access to the regent's ordu" who is destroyed as a witch — the exact fate a priestess at a khatun's court risks. The orifice-sewing execution is a genital-adjacent punishment on-map; reuse as a hostile-event outcome.

**E26. Eunuchs: none on the pre-imperial steppe; first met in China as "castrated and feminine men"** | 13th c. | "In pre-imperial times, Mongol nobles are not known to have kept eunuchs on the steppe. Mongols first came across 'castrated and feminine men' (as eunuchs were understood to be) in China." (p. 24) | **The single most important line for this pass.** The Mongol encounter with castration is late, foreign and *sedentary-court*. A Tengrist khan has no category for a castrate except "Chinese palace servant"; the faith's castrates will be read through that lens — as feminised court-things, not as warriors.

**E27. Kublai's harem and the Khongirat maiden levy (Marco Polo)** | 1270s–90s | "every second year a hundred maidens, the most beautiful … are chosen and are brought to the Great Kaan … he had them kept by the elder ladies of the palace … makes them lie with them in one bed to know if she has good breath … every three days and three nights six of these girls are sent to wait on the lord" (p. 24). | Flavour for Yuan court; also shows senior women ("elder ladies") as gatekeepers of the harem — a female-run institution.

**E28. Eunuchs as harem managers, secretaries, pornographers and sometimes the emperor's lovers** | Yuan China | "eunuchs collected pornography and sexual manuals and were well versed in the art of the bedchamber … Some eunuchs also served as sexual partners to the emperor." (p. 24) Han founder Gao-di "took eunuchs as lovers" (p. 33 n.62). | Eunuch as sexual partner, not neuter: supports (d) castrates regarded as women in the sexual economy.

**E29. Korean eunuch levy and the new fast castration method** | Yuan–Goryeo, 13th–14th c. | "Mongols adopted the Chinese eunuch system, but they also exported it to their vassal dominions, including Korea. Soon the Korean court found itself obliged to provide the Yuan with Korean eunuchs. The Yuan Dynasty's demand for Korean eunuchs … became so extensive that a new surgical method of castration was introduced in Korea to speed up the process, which involved using a small curved knife to swiftly cut off the private parts, including the testicles, as close as possible to the candidate's body." (pp. 24–25; cites Kim, "The Third Gender: Palace Eunuchs") | On-map (Korea is in the mod) **state-organised castration as tribute**, with a named surgical technique and the secondary source literally titled "The Third Gender". A Korean branch or a Yuan-era event can borrow this directly.

**E30. Ayyubid eunuch commanders and soldiers defeat the Mongol garrison (Ain Jalut 1260)** | 1260, Syria | "The fiercest military machine in the region, the Ayyubid army was commanded by atabegs, the majority of whom were castrated men. Not only did commanders have their manhood mutilated, but many rank-and-file soldiers were also eunuchs. … half of Saladin's formidable army was composed of eunuchs." "eunuchs were considered better servants and more enduring soldiers" (p. 25). "The Mongol cavalrymen … took a good beating from the advancing Ayyubid eunuch units, which put an end to the myth of Mongol invincibility." (p. 25) | (Terbish overstates — Qutuz and Baybars were Mamluks, and "half the army" is from El-Azhari — but the claim is in the source.) Hostile text: Mongols have *been beaten by castrates*; a khan's contempt for the faith's castrates can carry a sting of remembered humiliation. Also supports a martial castrate role for a steppe branch.

**E31. Two kinds of eunuch: khisyan (testicles only) vs majbub (all removed)** | Islamic world | "khisyan (singular khasiyy), who had only their testicles removed; second, majbub, who had their entire sexual organs chopped off. The former could still have sexual desire and were capable of copulation, whereas the latter were asexual. … most khisyan eunuchs, considered to have better physical endurance than an average man, were used in military and public offices, while the majbub, who had childlike voices and a distinctive gait, were used primarily in the harem" (p. 26). "If castrated at a young age, eunuchs also did not grow beards and looked boyish, resembling ghulman boys … Hence some eunuchs served their masters as homosexual lovers." Caliph al-Amin and his eunuch Kawtar: "his religion and world, his illness and cure" (p. 26). | Supplies the two-grade distinction the faith's rite will be *compared to* by Muslim observers. The Ilkhans "kept eunuchs in the household to serve their women" and did not promote them (p. 26).

**E32. Tantric "Discipline of Pairs" at the late Yuan court; Sixteen Dakini; "Companions" (inag)** | 1333–68, Dadu | Yuanshi on Togon Temür: "the Emperor thereupon practiced this method, which is called 'Discipline of Pairs.' … assembling for this purpose great numbers of women and girls … made them perform the dance of the Sixteen Dakini and the Eight Males. The brothers of the Emperor and those men who are called 'Companions' (ina[g]) all engaged in front of the Emperor in these lewd embraces, men and women being naked. The hall where these things took place was called 'Everything Without Obstacle.'" (p. 27) | On-map **sexual mystery rite at a Mongol court** with a named hall; and Confucian hostile reporting of it ("evil Tibetan monks") is the exact register in which Ming annalists would describe the faith. *Inag* "companion" is a court-intimacy title usable for sponsors.

**E33. Kublai, 'Phags-pa and the Buddhist marriage alliance** | 1260s–70s | "Kublai even married a Mongol princess to 'Phags-pa lama's younger brother" (pp. 26–27). | Precedent for a khan binding a *clergy* to the Golden Lineage by marriage — a route for a priestess-order into a khanate.

**E34. Qabus-Nama; discourse vs deed; liwat narrowly defined** | 11th c. Tabaristan; Ilkhanid Persia | "a chapter dwelling on the pros and cons of a man sleeping with other men and sleeping with women, concluding … that sleeping with both is the best" (p. 28); "Defined as anal penetration, sodomy (liwat) was strictly prohibited by Islamic law, whereas male-on-male kissing, fondling, flirting, gazing, and suchlike were regarded as less serious transgressions." (p. 28) | For Muslim-khanate hostile text: the faith's castrates are legible, its *rite* is the crime.

**E35. Ghazan marries his father's widow Bulugan Khatun against sharia; threatens apostasy** | 1295 | "had he not had his way and not been permitted by Muslim jurists to marry her … Ghazan would have discarded his new faith. Ghazan did not stop with his father's widow but also married his uncle Ilkhan Geikhatu's widow." (p. 29) | Shows Mongol custom overriding scripture when it suits the khan — a khan who wants a priestess can likewise bend his court clergy.

**E36. Abu Said, Baghdad Khatun and Dilsad: "any woman sought by the khan must be given a divorce"** | 1320s–35 | "Abu Said, appealing to Mongol custom, demanded of Chupan that any woman sought by the khan must be given a divorce by her husband and sent to the khan's harem" (p. 29); later "forced Shaikh-Hasan to divorce Baghdad Khatun"; married her niece Dilsad "for it was allowed according to Mongol custom" (p. 29). | A khan's sexual claim overrides marriage: the *khan's right to any woman* is the mechanism by which a priestess can be seized. Hostile event: the khan demands a priestess "as a woman"; the order must decide whether she *is* one for this purpose.

**E37. Nestorian Kereit and Naiman; Qiu Chuji given authority over Taoists** | 12th–13th c. | "Some tribes … did partially follow Nestorian Christianity, including the Kereit and Naiman elites who spoke Turkic" (p. 30 n.4); "Genghis Khan granted Qiu Chuji supreme jurisdiction over the Taoists" (p. 31 n.4). | A Christian heresy arriving on the steppe is not the first Christianity there; the Kereit/Naiman Nestorian elites are the natural first contact. ## 4. Terminology & names (usable for a steppe branch; all forms as the author spells them)

**Kinship, lineage, status**
- *anda* — sworn brother; initiation oath "to share but a single life" and "not to abandon one another" (p. 15). → the sponsor bond.
- *karyndash* — "brother", Ahmad Tegüder's address to the Sufi Hasan Mengli (p. 16).
- *inag* / *ina[g]* — "Companions" of the Yuan emperor in the Tantric rite (p. 27). → title for sponsors/companions of the priestess.
- *eke* "mother", *ekener* "wife" — "the Mongolian words ekener ('wife') and eke ('mother') have the same root" (p. 31 n.6). → "the line of the eke".
- *obog* (tribe, shared ritual ancestry), *urug* ("male lineages"), *Altan urug* (Golden Lineage), *Kiyad Borjigin* (pp. 31–32 n.18). → hostile text: "a lineage without an urug".
- *aqa nar* — Ilkhanid military nobility (p. 32 n.34).
- *keshig* — imperial guard (p. 17).
- *ordu* — "nomadic palace"/harem (pp. 21, 22). → the priestess order as "an ordu without a khan".
- *tümen* — unit of nominally ten thousand (p. 18).
- *darkhan* — tax-exempt/inviolable man; sacred mountain; champion wrestler; *darkhan ber* "venerable bride", eldest son's wife among Darkhads (p. 125). → protected status title.
- *erliiz* — mixed-race person, pre-socialist self-identifying as Mongol (p. 133).
- *khüükhen* — girl / young single woman; later sex-seller, non-pejorative (pp. 56–57); derivatives *khüükhende, khüükhemse, khüükhemseg* (p. 60). *Khüükhen Khutugtu* "Girl the Reincarnate" (p. 42) → a feminine office-title borne by a man.
- *chabagantsa / chabagants* — shaven-headed female lay renunciant (pp. 58, 59, 130).
- *shavi / Shabinars* — monastic serfs / ecclesiastical estate (pp. 45, 48).
- *badarchin* — wandering lamas (p. 44).
- *bandi* — young novice (p. 49).
- *manin* — "hermaphrodites" (p. 122).

**Law and judgment**
- *yeke jasag* / *Yasa* / *Yasaq* — the (contested) Great Law (p. 19); "Genghis Khan's rules" (pp. 16, 17).
- *yargu* — Mongol court (p. 20); *Shigi-Khutugu* — great judge whose precedents were cited (p. 20).
- *kurultai* — assembly (p. 10).
- *Chajin Bichig* (15th c.), *Yeke Chagaja-yin Bichig* / Mongol-Oirat Regulations (1640), *Khalkha Jirum* (1709) (pp. 45, 47).
- "nine" — fine unit: "two horses, two cows, two mature oxen, two three-year-old oxen, and one two-year-old ox" (p. 62).
- *Hömün-ü eme ba ühin-i boshagulugsan [hömün] hömün-ü eme dur orugsan luga adali* — "stealing daughters or their mothers was equal to having sex with somebody else's wife" (p. 69 n.52). → event title "The Stolen Daughter".
- *Ulaan Khatsart* "Red-Cheeked" (Red Cover) court register (p. 45).
- *tamga* — commercial tax (p. 23).
- *chingsang* — prime minister (p. 37).

**Religion and body**
- *Tengri* — "the Eternal Sky consisting of ninety-nine 'white' and 'black' skies" (p. 8).
- *Kököchü / Teb Tengri* — Genghis's shaman, son of Mönglik (p. 106 n.14).
- *Lord of the Forest* and his daughter, the hunter's spirit-bride (p. 9).
- *yab-yum* — Tantric paired deities (p. 27); "Discipline of Pairs", *yen-t'ieh-erh*, "Sixteen Dakini and the Eight Males", hall "Everything Without Obstacle" (p. 27); "Joyful Buddhas" (p. 28).
- *Genen, Rabjan, Getsel, Gelen* — four vows (p. 43).
- *urin tachaalal* — carnal desire (p. 50).
- *emle-* — "to render a male a female"; *bandida-* — "to make use of a young monk"; *bandi shuumryn yos* — "the way young novices act [in bed]" (p. 50).
- *um khumaa niilüüle* — "to unite the um and the khum letters"; *umai* "womb, vagina" (pp. 59, 70 n.66); *üülen boroony yavdal* "the act of cloud and rain" (p. 59).
- *khisyan / khasiyy* (testicles removed), *majbub* (all removed) — Islamic eunuch grades (p. 26); *ghulman* — "young boys" (p. 18); *liwat* — sodomy as anal penetration (p. 28); *hurs* (p. 8).
- *nüürendee galtai, nüdendee ochtoi* — "fire on his/her face, sparkles inside his/her eyes", attested in the Secret History (p. 67 n.4). → gender-neutral beauty formula.
- Proverbs: *duutai boroo khurgüi / tsuutai em khurimgüi*; *neree khugalsnaas yasaa khugal* (p. 56).
- Taboo names: *Muunohoi* "bad dog", *Oroolon* "vampire" (p. 78).
