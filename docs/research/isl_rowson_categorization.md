# Rowson, "The Categorization of Gender and Sexual Irregularity in Medieval Arabic Vice Lists" (1991): design reading

Source: Everett K. Rowson, "The Categorization of Gender and Sexual Irregularity in Medieval Arabic Vice Lists", ch. 3 in Julia Epstein and Kristina Straub, eds., *Body Guards: The Cultural Politics of Gender Ambiguity* (New York and London: Routledge, 1991), pp. 50-79 (text pp. 50-74, notes pp. 74-79). ISBN 0-415-90388-2 (hb), 0-415-90389-0 (pb).
Read in full, notes included, from an OCR text extraction kept outside the repo (Internet Archive scan; page numbers recovered from folios). No other chapter of the volume was read. Quotes verbatim.

Rowson's base texts are three eleventh-century anthologies: al-Jurjani (d. 1089), *Kinayat al-udaba'* (book of euphemisms); al-Raghib al-Isfahani, *Muhadarat al-udaba'*; and al-Abi (d. 1030), *Nathr al-durr*, whose three back-to-back chapters on *mukhannathun*, *lutis* and *baghgha'un* carry the argument. He treats the material as broadly representative of the Middle East "from the ninth century to the present" (pp. 72-73). That covers both the 867 and the 1066 starts.

**Relation to existing notes.** `hist_rowson_mukhannathun.md` covers Rowson's Medina article (Umayyad *mukhannathun*, the 717 castrations). This chapter is its Abbasid-era companion and the reference Marmon cites for *mukhannathun* (Marmon n. 20). It adds what the Medina article lacks: a **taxonomy**. It explains where the Islamic neighbours file a person like the daughters, and why. It says almost nothing about eunuchs (one anecdote, p. 65) and nothing about the *khuntha* beyond a medical aside (p. 64). For those, see Ayalon, Marmon and Sanders.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **The grid is drawn from the adult man.** Al-Jurjani's chapter order is set by "the viewpoint of a conventional adult male, who is sexually identified first and foremost as someone who penetrates" (p. 54). Everything else is a deviation from that point: women, boys, the passive man, the *mukhannath*.
- **Men and not-men.** "Free adult men virtually defined the public world" (p. 65). "The public badge of a dominant male was his beard ... Beardless non-men—women and boys—were his natural sexual partners" (p. 65). Shaving another man's beard was a standard humiliation (p. 58).
- **Act, not object choice.** Islamic law "is concerned exclusively with definable acts, and says nothing about 'propensities' or 'identities'" (p. 59). Active *liwat* made a man a sinner but "did not imperil the status and honor due him as a man" (p. 66).
- **The passive man is sick, but not a woman.** His desire is "uniformly considered sick, perverted, and shameful" (p. 64). It is the one sexual condition treated in medical texts, next to "facial hair on women and hermaphrodites" (p. 64). Yet "nowhere ... is he in any way assimilated to women" (p. 69).
- **The *mukhannath* is defined by gender, not sex.** "This 'effeminate' is defined by nonsexual feminine gender characteristics, not by sexual role" (p. 70). He plucked his beard, wore feminine garb "at least to some extent", wore his hair long, and used women's speech. "No attempt was made, however, to 'pass' as a woman" (p. 70). Rowson has seen no reference to *mukhannathun* wearing veils (n. 52, p. 78).
- **Feminine forms of address and names.** *Mukhannathun* "address each other in the feminine gender" (p. 70). Asked "You are the Father of whom?", one answers "I'm the Mother of Ahmad!" (p. 70). "'Abbada is a feminine name, as are the names of virtually all the mukhannaths we hear of from the ninth century on" (n. 57, p. 78).
- **From the ninth century, assumed passive.** "From the ninth century on, mukhannaths were always assumed to be sexually passive with other men" (pp. 70-71). Before that, in Arabia, they were not (p. 73). Their "gender inversion ... rationalized a sexual behavior pattern that in conventionally gendered males ... was considered pathological" (p. 71).
- **Wit, music and a corporate identity.** *Mukhannathun* served at court as buffoons. 'Abbada was jester to al-Mutawakkil (847-61). They were musicians on "a particular kind of lute" and on drums, "of which one variety was apparently played only by them" (p. 71). They "seem in fact to have enjoyed a kind of camaraderie with women ... not shared by other men" (p. 71). One acted as a marriage broker (n. 58). Their boast: "when we speak, you laugh; when we sing, you are delighted; and when we lie down, you mount" (pp. 71-72).
- **Public persons, never respectable.** They had "a distinct identity (certainly corporate as well as individual)". They were "public persons" of a demimonde "where public appreciation, and even fame, were accessible, but respectability was emphatically not" (p. 72).
- **The core thesis: public gender versus private sex.** "Sexual behavior is, essentially, a private matter; gender role is a public one" (p. 72). Those not marked as adult men were "no threat". "Men who voluntarily removed themselves from the dominant category, by behaving as women, lost their respectability, but could be tolerated and even valued for their entertainment value" (p. 72). The man who looked like a man in public and was submissive in private was the one "eminently attackable" (p. 72). The *mukhannathun* "renounced any claims to dominance altogether" (p. 72).
- **Women who take up men's attributes.** Women who wore "male attire and swords, riding horseback" were "sometimes admired, sometimes criticized", but "never associated with any particular form of sexual irregularity" (p. 68). Men reacted ambivalently to such women: they could "empathize with an entirely rational aspiration toward dominance" (p. 72). (Context only; trans men are out of scope.)
- ***Ghulamiyat*.** "Singing slave girls who were dressed up as boys (on occasion complete with painted mustaches)" were "the rage at the caliphal court in ninth-century Baghdad". They were "competing with boys for the attention of men" (p. 68). Rowson's 2003 chapter (`isl_rowson_gender_irregularity.md`) develops this.
- **Eunuchs appear once, as the harem's side door.** Harun al-Rashid hears a grammarian define three kinds of panderer and replies he has been one "for forty years without knowing it". Rowson reads the joke as referring "implicitly to the eunuchs who guarded his harem" (p. 65).
- **A grieving caliph and a *mukhannath*.** Harun, inconsolable for a dead son, is told by a *mukhannath*: "I am a man who imitates women, as you see; what would you do, then, if your son were alive and looked like me?" Harun "finally dismissed the attendant wailing women" (n. 60, p. 79). The joke works only because the *mukhannath*'s life counts as a fate worse than death.
- **Continuity with antiquity, minus Christianity.** Rowson finds the Arabic system closer to classical Greece and Rome than to the Christian late antiquity Islam inherited. One difference stands out: Islam did not read the passive man as effeminate, which "seems to have been automatic in the Greek and Roman traditions" (p. 73).
- **A love between women ending in a convent.** Al-Raghib traces the "origin" of *sahq* to "an enduring love affair between an Arab noblewoman and the wife of an Christian Arab prince of pre-Islamic Iraq". After the noblewoman died, "the princess founded a convent at the site of her tomb, to which she retired" (p. 68). Rowson does not name them.
