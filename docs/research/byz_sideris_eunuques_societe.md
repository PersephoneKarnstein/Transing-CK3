## 1. The argument in brief

- **A legal sex category, three *genê*.** "Eunuchs constitute a category of sex [catégorie de sexe] in the Byzantine empire, beside the male and female sexes" (p. 89). The category is in law from Justinian's time. Leo VI's legislation (Novel 98) "distinguishes three *génos*: men, women and eunuchs". A eunuch is any man unable to beget (p. 89). Note 1 cites the *Historia Augusta* (c. 400) for "third sex" [troisième sexe] as an early label.
- **Natural vs castrated eunuchs.** Natural *spadones* may marry and adopt; the wife's side may dissolve the marriage after three years without consummation. Castrates and *thlibiai* (testicles crushed in infancy) may not marry and could not adopt "until the 9th century", when Leo VI (Novel 26) let "all eunuchs" adopt (p. 90).
- **Pessinus and Phrygia come first.** Eunuchs in the Roman East "first call to mind the eunuch priests of Cybele ... whose great cult centre is Pessinus, in Phrygia" (p. 90). The cave at Hierapolis in Phrygia "breathes out a deadly breath sparing only the eunuch priests" (the Galli; Pliny, Strabo, Ammianus) (p. 91).
- **Phrygian eunuch clergy (heretical milieus).** Two Anatolian epitaphs, "perhaps Montanist". Bishop Heortasius of Appia in Phrygia is called "Honoured Eunuch" [Timios Eunouchos] (4th c.?). A eunuch priest, Aurelios Appa, is commemorated at Laodicea in Phrygia. It is unknown whether either was castrated (p. 91). Melito of Sardis, a 2nd-c. eunuch bishop; Origen's self-castration c. 206 (p. 91). In 344 "an Arian eunuch priest from Phrygia, Leontios", became bishop of Antioch and kept the peace (p. 97).
- **Martyred palace eunuchs.** Under Diocletian eunuchs "once all-powerful" were executed at Nicomedia. The memory of their martyrdom later legitimised castrated servants beside a Christian emperor (p. 92).
- **The court eunuchs settle in Constantinople under Arkadios (395–408).** Before that, the court travelled and Antioch was their city (pp. 95–99). Under Arkadios, Eutropios became praepositus, then consul in 399, and built a following in the Senate (p. 99).
- **Two Chambers.** There were separate Chambers for the emperor and the empress. Pulcheria got her own praepositus first, so from Theodosius II there were two. Eunuch guards of the women's quarters [gynécée] "allowed the empress to develop an autonomous sphere of power within the Palace" (p. 101). From Leo I a slave eunuch "becomes free on entering the Palace" (p. 101).
- **"State eunuchate" [eunucat d'État].** Eunuchs held: the Houses of Cappadocia (crown estates funding the Chamber); the *castrensis* (palace steward: table and kitchens); the *comes sacrae vestis* (wardrobe); the *spatharioi* (eunuch guard); the *sakellarios* (privy purse); eunuch *chartoularioi*; and the *parakoimomenos*, who by the middle period was "the most powerful eunuch of the palace and one of the most important persons of the empire" (pp. 101–102). Retired praepositi sat in the Senate. Ourbikios proposed that the empress Ariadne choose Anastasios in 491 (p. 102).
- **A court society that puts itself on show.** In the middle period eunuchs formed their own *taxis* with their own ladder of dignities. In ceremony "eunuchs surround the emperor as the angels stand beside Christ" (p. 103, citing the 10th-c. *Vision of the monk Kosmas*). The same display happened at the Hippodrome and in processions.
- **Eunuchs in the city: the *oikos*.** Great eunuchs held suburban estates (*proasteia*: Ourbikios's Kalamousos, shared with a patrician) and workshop-shops [ergastèria], for which they sought tax exemptions (pp. 104–105). They built palaces: Antiochos's (with the church of St Euphemia), and Lausos's, which held the Aphrodite of Knidos and Pheidias's Zeus (p. 105). Above all they founded *oikoi*: whole named quarters with palace, church, hospice and hospital. Examples: "ta Narsou" (Narses, under Justin II: palace, hospital, hospice, church); Smaragdos's quarter with baths; Amantios's quarter and church of St Thomas (pp. 105–106). Eunuchs "structure the quarter and give it a personality of its own" (p. 107).
- **Patronage reaching the street.** Mouselios restored the Mouseion; Narses restored the land walls. Chrysaphios became "patron and protector of the Green faction" (p. 107). In 963 Basil the Parakoimomenos raised "friends, kin and servants, 3,000 persons in all", attacked his rivals' houses and brought Nikephoros Phokas into the city as emperor (pp. 107–108).
- **Eunuchs inside imperial families.** Basil the Parakoimomenos was Romanos I's illegitimate son and great-uncle of Basil II. John the Orphanotrophos made his brother Michael IV and his nephew Michael V emperors, and two more brothers were eunuchs. Theophylact of Ohrid wrote an "apology of eunuchism" for his eunuch brother (p. 108).
- **Church and monasteries.** Amantios the *castrensis* was a "true servant of God", giving alms, fasting and receiving guests (p. 108). Gratisimos founded St Kyriakos and retired there. Ourbikios inherited a rich patrician's estate through the abbot Hypatios, who had sheltered the patrician from a brother who mistreated him, and later built the monks' tomb (pp. 104, 109). Theodore the *castrensis* gave 15–20 *kentenaria* to the poor in a year and freed his slaves (p. 109). **"Convents of women resort to eunuch priests, in particular to give communion to the nuns or defend the convent's interests, since they can approach the abbess and link her to the outside male world"** (p. 109, Justinian Nov. 133.5). Eunuch patriarchs, including Ignatios (847–58, 867–77), are listed. Leo VI founded the eunuch monastery of St Lazaros in the Topoi (899–900), and Michael Attaleiates a house for eunuch monks in 1077 (p. 110).

**Net finding.** Byzantine law treated eunuchs as a third *genos*, and from c. 400 they were a rooted urban elite. They held named quarters, charities, shops and clients, and they had a recognised place as go-betweens for women's houses. The Phrygian material (Pessinus, the Hierapolis Galli, eunuch clergy at Appia and Laodicea, Leontios) puts eunuch religious life in the faith's own province.

## 3. In-world lines for loc

- "The Emperor's law counts three kinds: men, women and the cut. The Mother counts two." (Rite of the Knife, Aristotelian)
- "Let them be monks among monks, as at Saint Lazaros. Not sisters." (Orthodox reaction)
- "At Appia they cut on his stone 'Honoured Eunuch'. We say: Honoured Mother." (Dorleyaian holy-site text; the faith's claim)
- "The breath of the cave at Hierapolis kills every man, and spares only the Mother's own." (Pessinus-memory event; after Strabo and Ammianus)
- "Her House has a church, a hospice and a bath, and the whole street goes by her name." (Sisters' House oikos modifier description)

## 4. Flags

- **Date correction:** cite the chapter as 2010 (print), not 2017. It is not yet listed in byzantium_reading_list.md.
- **Consistent with existing notes:** Leo VI Nov. 26 (adoption) and the eunuch patriarch list agree with hist_tougher_eunuch_byzantine.md. The "three *genos*" of Nov. 98 matches the "strange sex" wording in NV p. 53.
