# al-Saʿdī, *Tarikh es-Soudan* (c. 1655; Houdas trans. 1900): design reading

Source: ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Saʿdī, *Tarikh es-Soudan*. French translation by O. Houdas, with E. Benoist, *Tarikh es-Soudan* (Paris: Ernest Leroux, 1900; Publications de l'École des langues orientales vivantes, 4e sér., vol. 13). Public domain; archive.org `tarikhessoudanpa00sadauoft`.
Read from the archive.org OCR text, kept outside the repo. Page numbers are the 1900 translation's, from its running heads. The OCR is poor in places: some words are broken and some notes run into the text.

**Grade and flag.** The chronicle was written in Timbuktu in the 1650s; the author mentions "soixante-cinq ans" of Moroccan rule since 1591 (p. 38). For Mali and Songhay before 1453 it is a **17th-c. retrospective**, written by a Timbuktu scholar hostile to "pagan" kings and to Sonni ʿAlī. Grade: BACK for the Mali and early Songhay chapters; LATER for anything after 1464. Every use must say "a Timbuktu chronicle of the 1650s says".

**What was read.**
- In full: chs. I–VIII (pp. 4–44): the Zā and Sonni king lists, the origin of the Sonni, Kankan Mūsā, the kingdom of Mali, Jenne and its scholars, Timbuktu's foundation, the Tuareg.
- Searched: the rest of the volume, for eunuchs, concubines, sisters, mothers, idols and the Mali court. Passages read with context:
  - p. 107 and pp. 109–110 (Sonni ʿAlī and the scholars' women);
  - p. 122 (the Mossi king consulting ancestors);
  - p. 138 (Akil's sister's son);
  - p. 149 (Askia Muḥammad's eunuch);
  - pp. 156–157 (Kamkoli and the office of Dendi-Fāri);
  - p. 193 (eighty eunuchs sent to Morocco);
  - p. 199 (4,000 eunuch horsemen);
  - pp. 212–213 (the askias' mothers).
- Not read: the scholars' biographies of ch. IX onward, except where a search term fell; the Moroccan period (chs. XX–XXXVIII).

**Relation to existing notes.**
- `waf_gomez_african_dominion.md` §2 reports the Songhay eunuchs (the *hu-kokorey-koi*, 700 in silk, the 4,000 eunuch cavalry) from the Arabic via English. This note confirms the cavalry and the eighty-eunuch gift in Houdas's French, and gives the pages.
- `waf_ibn_battuta_mali.md` §5 lists "Kankou" as LATER, from "17th-c. Timbuktu chronicles", *unverified*. The *Tarikh es-Soudan* names Mūsā "Kankan-Mousa", but Houdas glosses "Kankan" as a **town**, not a mother (§3).
- `waf_ibn_khaldun_mali.md` gives the Mali king list. The *Tarikh* has no matching list; it conflates Mali with Ghana (§2.2).

## 1. The argument in brief

- **What it is.** A dynastic and scholarly history of Songhay and of Timbuktu and Jenne, written to celebrate the Muslim askias (from 1493) and the scholars. It condemns Sonni ʿAlī (r. 1464–92) as "le tyran, le libertin" (the tyrant, the libertine) (p. 106).
- **Mali in the chronicle.** Mali is the powerful predecessor whose domination of Songhay and Timbuktu ended in decline. The Mali material is short, legendary and second-hand.
- **Women.** Women appear as:
  - mothers named in genealogies;
  - co-wives;
  - captives given as concubines;
  - victims of a tyrant;
  - in one case, a slave woman whose name became Timbuktu's.
- **Gender crossing.** Nothing in it is about gender crossing. The eunuchs are Songhay palace and cavalry personnel of the 16th c.

## 2. What matters for the design

### 2.1 Origin of the Sonni: sister co-wives, seniority by washing, princes as hostages (ch. II, pp. 9–12)

- **The two mothers.** ʿAlī Kolon and Selmān Nār, sons of Zā Yasiboï, had mothers who "étaient deux sœurs germaines" (were full sisters): Ommā and Fāti (pp. 9–10).
  - The favourite wife, Fāti, was childless. She told her husband: "Épouse ma sœur Ommâ, peut-être te donnera-t-elle une postérité" (marry my sister Ommā; perhaps she will give you offspring) (p. 10).
  - The chronicler says the king did not know the Islamic law against marrying two sisters (p. 10).
- **Seniority by washing.** Both sons were born the same night and laid in a dark room. They were washed next morning, because custom delayed the washing of a child born at night. "Le premier nouveau-né lavé" (the first newborn washed) was counted the elder (p. 10).
  - Houdas suggests the dark room kept each mother from knowing her own child, to avoid rivalry (p. 10 n. 3).
- **Princes as hostages.** "Il était d'usage que les fils des rois fussent astreints au service de leur suzerain" (it was the custom that kings' sons had to serve their overlord) (p. 10). That is how the two princes came to serve the king of Mali.
  - ʿAlī Kolon used his raids to learn the roads home, then escaped and freed Songhay from "le joug du peuple de Melli" (the yoke of the people of Mali) (pp. 6, 11–12).

### 2.2 Kankan Mūsā and the kingdom of Mali (chs. III–IV, pp. 12–21)

- **The pilgrimage.**
  - Date: the chronicle puts it "dans les premières années du neuvième siècle de l'hégire" (in the first years of the ninth century AH), which is wrong. Houdas corrects it to 724/1324 from Ibn Khaldūn (p. 13 and n. 1).
  - Size: 60,000 men, with 500 slaves going before him, each holding a gold staff of 500 *mithqāl* (p. 13).
  - Alms: he gave only 20,000 gold pieces in the holy cities, against Askia Muḥammad's 100,000 (p. 14). The chronicle's bias favours the askias.
- **Songhay and Timbuktu.**
  - Songhay submitted while he was away. On his return he built a mosque with a *miḥrāb* outside Gao and took Timbuktu, "le premier souverain qui s'en rendit maître" (the first sovereign to master it) (p. 14).
  - He built the palace *Ma-dougou* (p. 14) and the great mosque's minaret (p. 16).
  - Mali held Timbuktu for 100 years from 737/1336–37. The Tuareg then held it for 40 years from 837/1433–34 (p. 37). Ch. III gives the order of events (pp. 16–17):
    - a Mossi sack under a later Mali king;
    - Mali's return to the city;
    - Tuareg raids;
    - Mali's withdrawal.
- **Ibn Baṭṭūṭa at second hand.** The chronicle quotes him on Mūsā's Cairo debts and the poet Abū Isḥāq al-Sāḥilī's tomb in Timbuktu (pp. 15–16).
- **"Melli" as told.**
  - The first prince was "Qaïamagha", whose capital was **Ghana**. There were 22 kings before the Hijra and 22 after, "de race blanche"; their subjects were the "Oua'kori" (Wangara, i.e. Mande) (p. 18).
  - So the chronicle **conflates Ghana with Mali**. The second dynasty, "de race noire", is Mali proper (pp. 18–19).
- **The provinces.**
  - Three principalities, Kala, Bindougou and Sibridougou, each with twelve "sultans" titled place + *Koï* (p. 19).
  - The **Fala-Faran** "avait le pas sur ces sultans" (took precedence over these sultans): "c'est lui qui prenait la parole pour demander conseil au roi" (he was the one who spoke to ask the king's counsel) (pp. 19–20). Houdas reads him as a chamberlain-intermediary.
  - Two commanders: the *Sanqar-Zouma'* (south) and the *Faran-Sourâ* (north) (p. 20).
- **Mali's fall.** Divine soldiers "sous la forme de jeunes hommes encore adolescents" (in the form of young adolescents) cut down the arrogant court (pp. 20–21). Mali split into three would-be sultanates (p. 21).
  - Mali fought Jenne 99 times and lost every time. A hundredth battle is foretold, which Jenne will also win (p. 21).

### 2.3 Jenne: conversion, the idol temple, the qāḍī (chs. V–VI, pp. 22–35)

- **Conversion.**
  - Jenne was founded by pagans in the mid-2nd c. AH and converted "vers la fin du sixième siècle de l'hégire" (c. 1200) under Sultan Konboro (p. 23).
  - He renounced paganism before 4,200 ulama, pulled down his palace and built the great mosque in its place (pp. 23–24).
- **Twelve commanders.** Jenne's sultan kept twelve commanders on the west "uniquement destinés à guetter l'occasion d'attaquer le Melli-Koï" (solely to watch for a chance to attack the king of Mali) (p. 25).
- **Sonni ʿAlī's siege and marriage.** He besieged Jenne for "sept ans, sept mois et sept jours" (seven years, seven months and seven days) (p. 26).
  - At the surrender he found the sultan a boy, whose father had died in the siege. He seated him on his own carpet as an equal (p. 27).
  - He "demanda la main de la mère du jeune homme et l'épousa" (asked for the young man's mother's hand and married her) (p. 27).
  - That is a queen-mother taken in marriage as part of a peace.
- **Before the qāḍī.**
  - The saint Foudiya was told in a dream to settle in the town. He "fit démolir le temple de l'idole que les païens avaient adorée" (had the temple of the idol the pagans worshipped pulled down) (p. 31).
  - Before Askia Muḥammad appointed him the first qāḍī, disputes went to the preacher (*khaṭīb*), "qui les tranchait en conciliant les parties" (who settled them by reconciling the parties). "Telle est encore la coutume des nègres" (such is still the custom of the Blacks) (p. 33).
  - In the chronicle's world, settlement by reconciliation is the older, non-qāḍī mode.

### 2.4 Timbuktu: the old slave woman, "never soiled by idols" (ch. VII, pp. 35–42)

- **The name.** Travellers left their goods with "une esclave appelée Tombouctou, mot qui, dans la langue du pays, signifie « la vieille »" (a slave woman called Timbuktu, a word meaning "the old woman" in the local language). "C'est d'elle que ce lieu béni a pris son nom" (from her this blessed place took its name) (p. 36).
  - Houdas doubts the etymology (p. 36 n. 2). It is Saʿdī's folk etymology, 17th c.
- **No idols.** "Jamais Tombouctou n'a été souillée par le culte des idoles" (Timbuktu was never soiled by the worship of idols) (p. 36). This is the clerical town's self-image, and the model for how Timbuktu's scholars would treat a non-Muslim faith.
- **The Timbuktu-koï.** Under Mali and then the Tuareg the town was governed by the *Timbuktu-koï*, Mohammed-Naddi. Of him the chronicle records only that "la mère [...] était la fille de Souma-'Otsmân" (his mother was the daughter of Souma-ʿOtsmān) (p. 38).
- **Tuareg rule.** The end of Tuareg rule brought violence: "ils entraient de force dans les maisons et y violaient les femmes" (they forced their way into houses and raped the women) (p. 40). **Do not use.**

### 2.5 The Tuareg and the line through women (ch. VIII, pp. 42–44; p. 138)

- **Kinship through women.** The Ṣanhāja Tuareg "n'ont de liens de parenté avec les Berbers que par les femmes" (are related to the Berbers only through the women) (p. 43). They took up the veil "à l'imitation de ce que faisaient leurs femmes" (in imitation of what their women did) (p. 44).
- **A sister's son, 1528.** In 1528 the Maghcharen-koï who married the sister of Ismāʿīl was "fils de la sœur de Akil" (the son of Akil's sister) (p. 138).
- **Grade.** These are 16th–17th-c. Berber data. They agree with Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's Massūfa and Tagaddā (`waf_ibn_battuta_mali.md`) but are not Mande.

### 2.6 Women under Sonni ʿAlī and the askias (pp. 107, 109–110, 212–213)

- **Sonni ʿAlī's persecution.** He imprisoned the mother of a jurist, "Sata", and killed her two brothers (p. 107).
  - He had thirty scholars' daughters marched on foot to become his concubines, and had them killed when they could walk no further (p. 107). **Do not use.**
- **Captive Fulbe women.** He sent captive Fulbe women to Timbuktu's notables "en guise de cadeau" (as a gift), ordering them taken as concubines. "Ceux qui n'observent point les devoirs de la religion" (those who neglected their religious duties) did so; the observant "les épousèrent" (married them). The author's own great-grandmother's grandfather was among those who married (pp. 109–110).
- **Mothers named.** The chronicle names every askia's mother and many princesses (pp. 212–213):
  - Askia Muḥammad's mother **Kasaï**;
  - **Zâra-Koboronki**, who was in turn servant to the Koboro-koï, captive of Askia Muḥammad and woman of the Boussa-koï, and bore a ruler to each;
  - Meryam-Dabo, Keltoum, Amina-Kiraï, Amina-Qâya-Barda, Fatma-Boso;
  - daughters such as Ouaïza-Bâni, ʿAïcha-Kara, Kiboro, Farâsa and Fati-Hindo.
- Even a Muslim court chronicle of the 1650s identifies princes by their mothers. That supports the faith's mother-naming, as a regional habit, not a Mande rule.

### 2.7 Eunuchs (pp. 149, 156–157, 193, 199)

- **Askia Muḥammad's eunuch, 1530s.** The deposed askia sends his son to "un de ses eunuques" (one of his eunuchs), who holds a deposit of gold. A secret bodily sign proves that the son is his envoy (p. 149).
- **Kamkoli.** The *Hoko-Koraï-Koï* Kamkoli is promised the office of *Dendi-Fâri*, then passed over. He rises, throws earth before Askia Dāwūd and says: "ce n'est pas le Très-Haut qui t'a suggéré ce choix, c'est de toi-même que tu l'as fait" (it was not the Most High who suggested this choice to you; you made it yourself) (p. 157). He receives the office later (p. 157).
  - Houdas does not gloss *Hoko-Koraï-Koï*. Gomez identifies the *hu-kokorey-koi* as master of the palace eunuchs (`waf_gomez_african_dominion.md`).
- **The gift to Morocco.** Askia al-Ḥājj sends Morocco a return gift that includes "quatre-vingts eunuques" (eighty eunuchs), c. 1580s (p. 193).
- **The eunuch cavalry.** In 1588 the *Hoko-Koraï-Koï* leaves the army "à la tête de 4.000 cavaliers eunuques" (at the head of 4,000 eunuch horsemen) (p. 199).
- **Grade.** All LATER (16th c.), Songhay. **No eunuch is placed at the Mali court in this chronicle.** That agrees with Gomez's verdict (one Marinid gift-eunuch, ʿAnbar, from Ibn Khaldūn).

## 3. Corrections to the candidate and to existing notes

1. **"Kankou / Kanku" is not from this chronicle as a mother's name.** The *Tarikh es-Soudan* calls Mūsā "Kankan-Mousa". Houdas notes that "Kankan, c'est le nom d'une ville" (Kankan is the name of a town) (p. 15 n. 2).
   - The reading "Kanku, his mother" comes from the *Taʾrīkh al-fattāsh* via Gomez. In `waf_ibn_battuta_mali.md` §5, change the "Kankou" source from "17th-c. Timbuktu chronicles (*Taʾrīkh al-Sūdān*)" to "*Taʾrīkh al-fattāsh* (via Gomez); the *Taʾrīkh al-Sūdān* writes Kankan".
2. **The chronicle's Mali begins at Ghana** (p. 18). Do not cite it for Mali's pre-Sunjata kings or for a Mande "sister's son" rule; it has none.
   - The only matrilineal data here are Tuareg (pp. 43, 138) and the chronicle's habit of naming mothers.
3. **No eunuchs at Mali** (§2.7). Confirms `waf_gomez_african_dominion.md` §3.3.
4. **The Timbuktu dates** (Mali 1336/7–1433/4; Tuareg 1433/4–1468/9; p. 37) are usable for event gating with "the chronicle says". The c. 1324 pilgrimage date comes from Ibn Khaldūn, not from this text.

## 4. Design implications

### 4.1 Gate

- **Seniority by washing** (p. 10). In the faith, a daughter's seniority in her House counts from her **first washing** after the bush step. Brett-Smith's sculptor washes like a new mother (`waf_brett_smith_bamana.md`).
  - Two daughters washed the same night are ranked by who was washed first. This settles Elder Sister ties.
  - Faith voice: "We are not counted from our births but from our washings." Narrator flag: the custom is told of Songhay princes in a 17th-c. chronicle.
- **A Muslim reaction to the gate.** Foudiya pulled down the idol's temple (p. 31), and Timbuktu boasts it was "never soiled" by idols (p. 36). A Timbuktu or Jenne scholar's response to a House in his town:
  - petition the ruler to close it;
  - or, if the ruler is a Badenya faithful, curse her (the Mourimagha story, pp. 29–30, where a man's prayer drives a scholar out).

### 4.2 Line

- **Sister co-wives** (p. 10). A Badenya event: a childless favourite wife asks her husband to marry her sister. The two sisters' children are raised so that neither mother knows which child is hers.
  - Badenya reads this as badenya at its fullest: all the children of one mother-line.
  - Muslim reaction (from the chronicle's own comment): the jurists forbid it.
- **Mothers in the records.** The faith's court chronicle names only mothers (`waf_jansen_kangaba_griots.md` §4.4). Even the askias' Muslim chronicler named every prince's mother (pp. 212–213).
- **Hostage daughters.** The Mali court took vassals' sons into service (p. 10). Badenya can take vassals' **daughters** (or would-be daughters) into a House's service. A hostage who passes the gate there becomes a House sister: a soft-power wardship.
  - Narrator flag: the hostage custom is told of Mali by a 17th-c. chronicle.

### 4.3 Holy sites

- **Timbuktu.** Do not make it a Badenya site; its self-image is "never soiled by idols" (p. 36). The old slave woman who kept the travellers' goods could be **flavour in a Badenya event**, not a site.
  - Faith voice: "Even Timbuktu is named for an old woman who kept other people's things."
- **Jenne.** It can be a hostile clerical town (a qāḍī reaction source). After its c. 1200 conversion its palace became a mosque (pp. 23–24).

### 4.4 Names (all LATER, Songhay or Timbuktu; for court and NPC stock, not faith-founder names)

- **Women:** Ommā, Fāti, Kasaï, Meryam, Keltoum, Amina, Fatma, Ouaïza, ʿAïcha, Kiboro, Farâsa, Sata, Zâra (pp. 10, 107, 212–213).
  - Do not use Zâra-Koboronki's compound name or story: a captive passed among three men.
- **Titles for Mali-period court NPCs** (BACK):
  - *Fala-Faran* (the speaker who asks the king's counsel, pp. 19–20);
  - *Sanqar-Zouma'* and *Faran-Sourâ* (commanders, p. 20);
  - *Timbuktu-koï* (p. 38);
  - *Melli-koï* (king of Mali, p. 15).

### 4.5 Festival

- Nothing on a Mande festival. The chronicle's Muslim calendar items are the Friday prayer and the pilgrimage.
- One usable flavour: Kankan Mūsā built a Friday mosque wherever he passed a Friday (p. 14). A Badenya festival should avoid colliding with Friday prayer at a Muslim court. Jansen's Kangaba ceremony was moved for the ʿĪd, and this agrees with that.

### 4.6 Do not use

- Sonni ʿAlī's killing of the thirty girls (p. 107).
- The Tuareg rapes (p. 40); the soldier's rape that "caused" Jenne's fall (p. 28).
- The captive-concubine gifts as a mechanic (pp. 109–110).
- The divine adolescents' massacre of Mali (pp. 20–21).
- Eunuchs as a Mali-court office.
- The chronicle's racial classing of dynasties ("race blanche", "race noire", p. 18).
