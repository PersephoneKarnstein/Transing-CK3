- ch. 4 "Phaethousa and Sex Change in Early Modern Europe" (pp. 97–128): from the late 16th century writers read her as proof that sex *cannot* change. A one-sex reading gives way to a two-sex one about 200 years before Laqueur's date.

## 1. The argument

- **The target.** Laqueur's *Making Sex* (1990) claims a "one-sex" body "dominated thinking about sexual difference from classical antiquity to the end of the seventeenth century", "where the boundaries between male and female are of degree and not of kind" (p. 2). King: "I have found it difficult to identify any historical period in which a 'one-sex' model dominated" (p. 7).
- **Galen is not the one-sex champion.** The famous inside/outside passage (*Usefulness of Parts* 14.6) "is clearly a thought experiment" (p. 35). Laqueur cut the reciprocal clause "Consider first whichever ones you please", which makes it balanced, not "man is the measure" (p. 11). In *On Seed* Galen says "the male animal differs from the female in its entire body". A person tells bull from cow, or man from woman "with their clothes on", because "they differ in their whole bodies" (pp. 37–8). In *Doctrines of Hippocrates and Plato*: "in one respect women are similar to men, in another they are opposite" (p. 37). "In Galen, both models can be found" (p. 40).
- **Aristotle and Galen must not be merged.** "Users of Laqueur have been too quick to merge" Galen's one-sex image with Aristotle (p. 40). For Aristotle there is no female seed. Woman is "as it were, a deformed man" or "a mutilated man" relative to the male as *telos* (GA 737a26–30), "however, in Aristotle, in another sense the telos of both men and women is to reproduce" (p. 41). Galen pushes back: "you ought not to think that our Creator would purposely make half the whole race imperfect and, as it were, mutilated" (p. 42). The 'Aristotelean-Galenic' one-sex label is wrong: "the position of these ancient writers is ... far from being identical" (p. 44 n. 60).
- **"Not so much that the female is inferior as that the inferior is female"** (Flemming, quoted p. 41 n. 50). The Aristotelian "deformity" is about fluids and heat, not organ shape.
- **Both models were always available.** King adopts Laqueur's own early admission that "a two-sex and a one-sex model [have] always been available" (p. 8). The Hippocratic *Diseases of Women* already holds a fully two-sex view: women's flesh is "like unprocessed fleece, men like a closely woven garment" (pp. 44–8).
- **Eunuchs.** These are marginal in King. Galen-era eunuchs got gout, Hippocratic-era ones did not, after Banister 1578 (p. 121). Agnodice was accused of being "a eunuch" because beardless, an identity "neither that of a man nor that of a woman". Eunuchs "created after puberty could still achieve erection" (pp. 220–21, citing Tougher 1997).

**Bearing on the design.** King gives no direct reading of castration. Her value is structural. (1) "Aristotelian" and "Galenic" really are distinct positions; neither is "one-sex" in Laqueur's sense. (2) Aristotle's model is a hierarchy of heat and privation (female = lacking male). Galen's in *On Seed* is closer to two positive natures. (3) This difference *predicts* the castration doctrines. In Aristotle's privation model, removing the male principle slides the body toward female (GA 784a, 787b). In Galen's model, each sex has its own power, so removing the testes from either sex leaves a neuter "third thing" (*De semine*, K IV 585). The labels in the design follow from the deep logic of each author, not just from isolated sentences.
