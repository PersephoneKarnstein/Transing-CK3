**Nature of the books.** Narrative surveys built on the dynastic-history biographies of empresses and consorts, with unofficial and fictional sources marked as such. McMahon is careful to separate rumour from record. There is little on religion, and almost nothing on Daoist ordination, Dunhuang or gender-crossing. The books' value for the mod is regency mechanics, scholar-official rhetoric, and a large stock of in-window characters.

## 1. The argument, with what matters for the Way

### 1.1 The core rule and how women got round it
- **Title thesis.** "Two closely related norms in Chinese history were that only men should rule and only men should be polygamists" (WSNR 9). Women ruled "when men were unqualified to rule, for example, because they were too young, or were ill" (WSNR 16).
- **The dictum.** "Hens should not announce the dawn" (*pin ji wu chen* 牝雞無晨) (WSNR 15–16). Rule by women was "a sign of male weakness and decline. Heaven abhorred them because rule by women was unnatural" (WSNR 16). Empress Zhangsun quoted it to refuse to discuss politics (WSNR 188).
- **The sexual slander rule.** "One thing was common among female rulers: the tendency of others to slander them, especially by accusing them of sexual crimes" (WSNR 13). After the Song, such reports vanish from the official histories and survive only as rumour (CW xxi, 273–274).
- **Closing line of WSNR.** The rule left a mystery: "women were always women, but men predictably failed to be men" (WSNR 274).

### 1.2 The regency toolkit, dynasty by dynasty (867–1453)
- **Behind the curtain (垂簾).** The first dowager to rule from behind a curtain was Chu Suanzi of the Eastern Jin (4th c.), who "put up a white gauze curtain … and held court with the emperor in her arms" (WSNR 147). Wu sat behind Gaozong "shielded by a curtain" (WSNR 190).
- **The Song turned the curtain into law** for Empress Liu (regent 1022–1033), and nine Song regents worked under it (CW xvii, 11–12):
  - Her rule was labelled "temporary" (*quan* 權).
  - She held court every five days, never alone, with the boy emperor seated to the left and herself on the right "behind a curtained divide".
  - She was barred from *zhen* 朕, the masculine imperial "I".
  - A proposal to found ancestral temples for her own Liu family failed.
  - The regency ended when the curtain was physically taken down: in 1065 Han Qi "ordered [it] immediately removed, thus signaling the end of her regency" for Dowager Cao (CW 16).
- **Liao: the opposite rules.**
  - Before he died, Jingzong issued an edict letting Chengtian use *zhen* (WSNR 261).
  - Khitan and Mongol women regents "did not use a curtain to divide themselves from courtiers" (WSNR 272; CW 59).
  - Chengtian "performed a rite normally limited to emperors when assuming the throne, the ritual of rebirth (*zaisheng yi* 再生儀)". It used a special building that the ruler entered "in order to be reborn", libations, incantations, and undressing and redressing (WSNR 261).
- **Ming: none at all.** The founder's rule was that women "were never to involve themselves in politics" (CW 78). Dowager Zhang (r. 1435–1442) refused to "rule from behind the curtain" because it "violated the ancestral mandate", and ruled informally instead (CW 93).
- **Yuan: widows ran the household.** It was Mongol custom that "when the male head of house died, the widow took over until a son or other male relative of the husband was selected" (CW 62). Töregene ruled 1241–1246; Sorghaghtani, Targi and Budashiri followed.
- **Dowagers outlasted emperors.** Great Empress Dowager Guo lived through five Tang reigns (821–848) (WSNR 227). Chu Suanzi was regent three times.

### 1.3 Liao empresses (inside the window)
- **Yingtian (878–953).** She kept her own army and sat facing Abaoji in audience (WSNR 258). At his death in 926 she claimed the right to pick a successor, killed over a hundred opponents, and, instead of following him in death, "cut off her right hand to place in the coffin": "I am willing to give him my severed arm" (WSNR 259). Later banished after a failed war against Shizong (WSNR 260).
- **Chengtian (954–1009), Xiao Chuo.**
  - She ran the state during Jingzong's illnesses and was regent from 982 for her 11-year-old son Shengzong (WSNR 260–261).
  - **She rode with the army that routed the Song invasion of 986.** McMahon calls this a "unique position in Chinese history" (WSNR 261; CW 9).
  - She **ordered about a hundred captured boys castrated**; one became Shengzong's favourite eunuch (WSNR 261–262).
  - Her Han Chinese chief minister Han Derang (941–1011) was given the imperial Yelü surname. A Song ambassador, Lu Zhen, reported her at about fifty in "a cap of kingfisher-feather adornments and jade pendants" with a boy who looked like Han, "probably" their son (WSNR 262). Their tombs lie side by side (n. 30, citing Johnson).
- **Qin'ai (d. 1058)** "had a dark face and wolfish eyes" (WSNR 263). In 1031 she seized the regency, drove out Empress Qitian, and in 1034 plotted to replace her own son. The Song's Empress Liu formally recognised her (CW 12).
- **Renyi (d. 1076)** "led a section of the royal guard to repel the attackers" in the 1063 rebellion (WSNR 264).
- **Xuanyi (d. 1075),** a poet and pipa player, was falsely accused of adultery with a musician by the minister Yelü Yixin (WSNR 264).
- **Consort Wen** wrote satirical poems against Tianzuo and plotted to enthrone her son in 1121 (WSNR 265).
- **Structure.** Two intermarrying clans: the Yelü supplied emperors, the Xiao supplied empresses and held hereditary posts. Xiao women were often older than their husbands (WSNR 256–257). Shengzong's 1027 order demoted the sons of concubines "unless the son of a concubine was adopted by the main wife" (WSNR 263).
- **Xixia.** Dowager Liang ruled in the 1070s–1080s and "even imprison[ed]" her son in 1081 (CW 35).

### 1.4 Palace women as nuns and Daoists
McMahon is thin here. Every case he gives:
- **Cloister as storage.** After Taizong died, "she [Wu] and other sonless consorts were placed in a nunnery, where they would normally have remained widows the rest of their lives" (WSNR 188).
- **Daoist ordination as laundering.** To take his son's wife, Xuanzong "had her leave her husband and be registered as a Daoist nun in 741", with the religious name Yang Taizhen 楊太真, and then took her into the harem in 745 (WSNR 217).
- **Cloister as banishment.**
  - Li Bian of the Southern Tang "ordered her [Consort Zhong] to enter a nunnery" for promoting her own son (WSNR 245).
  - Later Tang Empress Liu fled the palace in 926, "intending to become a nun" (WSNR 242).
  - Song Empresses Xie and Quan ended their lives "in a nunnery" in the north after 1276 (CW 31).
- **Cloister as refuge from the levirate.** The Jurchen mother of Jin Shizong "avoided marriage to her husband's younger brother by shaving her head and becoming a nun … her choice became common among widows wanting to avoid the pressure to remarry" (CW 44). Sorghaghtani refused the levirate and asked for her own domain instead (CW 62).
- **Daoist title for a deposed empress, inside the window.** In 1429 the Xuande emperor made Empress Hu step down and gave her the title "Tranquil and Loving Master of Immortality" (*jingci xianshi* 靜慈仙師) (CW 91).
- **Daoist nuns as consorts.** Song Lizong "liked Daoist nuns, one of whom he raised to consort" (CW 30).
- **Daoist court cults.** Huizong's priest Lin Lingsu titled Consort Liu "Celestial Consort of the Nine Flowerings of Pure Jade" (CW 22–23, n. 42).
- **Not in McMahon:** Song Empress Meng's period as a Daoist after her deposition in 1096. CW 19–20 tells her deposition and her regency of 1127 without it. This needs another source (Chung 1981 or Lee 2010) before any event uses it.

### 1.5 Eunuch–empress alliances (inside the window or as precedent)
- **Tang, 756–762.** Empress Zhang and the eunuch Li Fuguo were "powerful allies". When she tried to change the heir, he had her killed (WSNR 223–224).
- **Tang, 820s–840s.** Eunuchs urged Dowager Guo to rule in 824, and she refused: "Are you saying that I should become another Empress Wu?" (吾效武氏耶) (WSNR 227). Eunuchs, not women, chose emperors from 820 to 888 (WSNR 229–231, 234).
- **Southern Han, 958–971.**
  - Liu Chang "trusted only eunuchs" because they had no families, and supposedly "officials who wished to present themselves for service had to have themselves castrated first" (WSNR 251).
  - Power sat with a palace woman, Lu Qiongxian 盧瓊仙, a eunuch, and a sorceress, Fan Huzi 樊胡子, who "claimed to represent the Jade Emperor … set up a tent in the palace from which she issued predictions and reviewed major government decisions" (WSNR 251, n. 16).
- **Song, 1033.** A eunuch helped Renzong depose Empress Guo (CW 15). In the 1190s Empress Li "allied with eunuchs" to keep Guangzong from his father (CW 28).
- **Ming, 1435–1442.** Dowager Zhang summoned "a group of female palace officials, who were armed with knives and swords" and had one hold a sword to the neck of the eunuch Wang Zhen. She spared him only when the boy emperor begged (CW 94–95). In 1449 Wang Zhen led the emperor to capture at Tumu. Dowager Sun and Empress Qian gathered ransom, and Sun installed both a regent and an heir (CW 95).
- **McMahon's frame:** eunuchs "resembled women in that they were an influential force that stood apart from men … when powerful, they were viewed as the sign of a weak emperor" (WSNR 220–221).

### 1.6 How the scholar-officials attacked women's rule
- **Classical and stock lines.** The "hens" dictum. Ouyang Xiu wrote that Wu only "called herself" emperor (WSNR 188). Sima Guang treated her as a dowager. Zhu Xi counted her son as the ruler throughout (WSNR 188).
- **Names as weapons.** "The human hog and the drunken bones" (人彘, 骨醉) was a stock phrase for Empress Lü and Wu (WSNR 245). "The calamity of Wu Zetian is again before us" was shouted at Ming favourites (CW 76). Song Empress Li was compared "to Empress Lü and Wu Zetian" (CW 28).
- **To a regent's face.** Asked his view of Wu, an official told Empress Liu, "She was a Tang criminal who nearly destroyed the dynasty!" He then made her carriage ride behind her son's, citing the "three followings" (CW 12).
- **Women repeating the rule.**
  - Southern Tang Empress Song: "That would be to take after Empress Wu, something I could never possibly do" (WSNR 244).
  - Dowager Guo: "Since ancient times, when has a woman ever ruled the world and established order like Yao and Shun?" (WSNR 227).
- **The praise word.** A good regent was "a Yao and Shun among women" (*nüzhong Yao Shun* 女中堯舜): Song Dowager Gao, and Ming Dowager Zhang (CW 18, 95).
- **Ethnic contempt.** Ouyang Xiu blamed Shatuo "barbarians" for adopting sons "from different lines of descent", and wrote of their "overbearing" empresses (WSNR 240–241). This bears on Temple Society adoption across surnames.
- **Robes as transgression.** In 1033 Empress Liu wore "the emperor's robes and crown for sacrifices at the imperial ancestral temple" despite opposition (CW 13). She also threw down a flatterer's painting of Wu (CW 13).

### 1.7 Other in-window cases
- **Palace scholars who refused marriage.** The five Song sisters "had all sworn never to marry". In 788 they entered the palace as "scholar academicians", "never became consorts", and taught princes and princesses; Song Ruozhao became a matron of the palace service (WSNR 228–229). This is the nearest Tang palace analogue to a House of unmarried women.
- **Women's palace bureaus.**
  - Song: six bureaus and an Office of Surveillance, staffed entirely by women, with "over two hundred" administrators. Over 80% of Northern Song consorts came from them (CW 7).
  - Ming: "female officials (*nüguan* 女官)" served under eunuch oversight. In 1459 the palace recruited "husbandless women below forty … good at writing and arithmetic" (CW 80–81).
- **Wuzong's Talented Lady Wang.** She rode beside him dressed so like him that "it was difficult to tell them apart" (WSNR 232). Her death is a suicide; see §2 row 13.
- **Lady Huarui,** consort of the Later Shu, a Sichuan court near Mount Qingcheng. She told the Song emperor of the surrender: "not one of them could be called a true man" (WSNR 249). Later legend says she tried to poison Taizu (CW 8–9).
- **The queen as fallback.** Mongol: Chinggis made four daughters queens of their own realms (CW 61). Xiwangmu "ruled an independent realm without a man" (CW 32). Song Empress Liu's portrait crown carries a figure of the Queen Mother. McMahon: "In the Tang, she especially appealed to women who did not fit the normative roles of mother, wife, and daughter, such as nuns, courtesans, and artists" (CW 32).
- **Ming exclusion.** Rule against elite wives (CW 75, 79). Five Ming empresses were deposed (CW 79).
- **Forced deaths at the emperor's funeral.** Sonless consorts were made to die at imperial funerals (*xun* 殉) from 1398 until 1464; 38 died for the Hongwu emperor (CW 84, 86). **Excluded from design** (no-suicide rule).

## 3. In-world loc lines (faith, court or character voice; never narrator)
- Scholar-official: "The hen does not announce the dawn. Her Majesty will listen from behind the curtain, and only while the Son of Heaven is a child."
- Scholar-official, on a Way ruler: "She calls herself sovereign. The histories will say *called herself*."
- Minister, to a regent who asks about Wu: "A criminal of the Tang who nearly ended it. Your carriage follows the emperor's."
- Dowager (refusal, `tm_yao_shun_among_women`): "Would you have me be another Empress Wu? The boy can choose his own ministers."
- Way nüguan, answer: "They praise the woman who refuses the throne and curse the one who takes it. Either way it is a woman who decides."
- Khitan Way ruler, *zaisheng* rite: "My ancestors went into the hut and came out reborn kings. I went in and came out what I am."
- Sister, to a ruler who would register a woman in the guan to take her later: "The guan is not your anteroom. She came here to stay."
- Jin widow, taking vows: "My husband's brother may have his horses. He will not have me."
- Ming eunuch clerk, month 3 (comic): "Female official, eunuch, female crowned one... The register has a column for everything but what you are."
- Women's-bureau officer, at the regent's side: "The sword is from the bureau of ceremonies. We are the ceremony today."
