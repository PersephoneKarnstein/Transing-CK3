## 2. What forced conversion was, and how people lived it

- **The law changed, not just the mood.** "The Almohads abolished this protected status, and forced Jews and Christians to convert to Islam" (Stroumsa p. 56). The most plausible explanation is that they applied to all their lands the rule that bars non-Muslims from the Hijaz (Stroumsa p. 56 n. 15, after Fierro).
- **Uneven enforcement.** It was strict in Iberia and "relaxed at times in North Africa" (Stroumsa p. 57). Some communities were offered exile, abandoning all property. Many who owned property "opted to feign conversion" (p. 57). The Christian communities withered, and the Jewish ones "managed to weather the storm" (p. 57).
- **Fes was easier.** Supervision of converts was looser in North Africa. Stroumsa suggests this explains why the family chose Fez, "close to the Almohad capital" (pp. 59–60). Kraemer agrees and adds a respite late in ʿAbd al-Muʾmin's reign, when Jews and Christians were needed as administrators (ch. 5, "Why Fez?").
- **Converts were expected to learn the Qurʾan.** Ibn Abi Usaybiʿa says Maimonides "memorized the Quran and became engaged in [the study of] Islamic law" (Stroumsa p. 60).
- **The "decorous hush".** Jewish sources lament the forced conversion of whole communities but never name an individual convert. Stroumsa: "a decorous hush was preserved regarding this painful part of an ex-convert's biography" (p. 59). Raising it in a quarrel was "a dirty campaign" that might "boomerang" (p. 59). The only exemption recorded is for itinerant Italian merchants (p. 59).
- **Al-Mansur's suspicion** (al-Marrakushi): the converts "pray in the mosques, they teach their sons the Quran … but God alone knows what they hide in their hearts" (Stroumsa pp. 57–58). Kraemer gives the dress rule: "dark robes with long sleeves" down to the feet, and instead of turbans "ugly caps down to their ears" (ch. 5, "Forced Conversion and Crypto-Jews").
- **Martyrs and survivors.** At Sijilmasa the Jews "fasted and prayed" for seven months. 150 were killed, and the community's judge, Joseph b. ʿImran, was "the first of the apostates" (Solomon Cohen's letter, Kraemer ch. 5). Solomon "did not blame the apostates" (ch. 5). Refugees in Fustat included a scholar of Sijilmasa who could find no post and a kohen of Fez who "ended up selling clothing in the bazaar" (ch. 5).

## 3. Jews at the Maghrebi courts (service as protection)

- **Physicians to the Almoravids.** Two Andalusi Jewish physicians, Abu ʾl-Hasan Meir ibn Qamniel of Saragossa and Abu Ayyub Solomon ibn al-Muʿallim of Seville, served ʿAli b. Yusuf at Marrakesh. Both had the honorific title of vizier (Kraemer ch. 5, "Medical Studies"). Ibn Qamniel moved from Spain to Fez. Kraemer: "Jews had a reputation for their medical skills, and so the ruler disregarded their religious identity" (ch. 5).
- **A Jewish physician explains the Song of Songs to the amir.** Ibn ʿAqnin tells how one Jewish physician read the Song of Songs to ʿAli b. Yusuf as an erotic poem, and Ibn Qamniel corrected him with the allegory (ch. 5).
- **Women physicians at the Almohad court.** The Ibn Zuhr family had "five generations of physicians, including two women, who served the Almohad ruler Abu Yusuf Yaʿqub al-Mansur" (Kraemer ch. 5, "Medical Studies"). They were Muslim. This is the only evidence in these two books of women practising medicine in the Maghreb. Kraemer names neither woman.
- **Medicine was taught in families.** "Medical lore was often passed on by members of a family" (ch. 5).

## 4. The two epistles

**Maimon b. Joseph, *Epistle of Consolation* (Fez, 1159/60)** (Kraemer ch. 6):
- It was written to comfort, not to blame. It urged converts to keep what commandments they could, above all prayer.
- His image: one in exile is drowning; the commandments are a rope let down from heaven, and "one who clings even with the tips of his fingers has more hope than one who lets go altogether".
- He gave the short form of the Eighteen Benedictions, which "one may offer in an Arabic version". He used Islamic phrases ("God and his apostle") that the forced converts had absorbed.
- He told everyone to recite Psalm 90 daily.

**Moses b. Maimon, *Epistle on Forced Conversion* (1160–65)** (Kraemer ch. 6; Stroumsa pp. 59–61):
- It answers an anonymous scholar who ruled that anyone who said the *shahāda* was a gentile, his prayers worthless and his testimony void.
- Maimonides separates what is done willingly from what is done under compulsion. Saying the words is not belief: "Belief is not the notion that is uttered, but the notion that is represented in the soul" (Kraemer ch. 6).
- The Muslim authorities "know that in no way do we believe in him … we only deceive the ruler" (Stroumsa p. 61).
- Forced converts who keep commandments in private remain Jews. Their testimony stands, and they should be welcomed back.
- "Those who would send men, women, and children to the executioner" were answered with "that you may live by them and not die by them" (Kraemer ch. 6, "The Masada Complex").
- His counsel had three parts: accept Islam outwardly, keep the commandments as far as possible, and **leave** for a place where one can live openly (Kraemer ch. 6, "The Obligation to Emigrate").
- Maimonides includes himself: "Let our disgrace cover us" (Jer. 3:25; Stroumsa p. 60).

Stroumsa adds that his habit of codifying without recording the sages' disagreements may echo the Almohad legal reform. The Almohads rejected *ikhtilāf* and wrote short compendia without chains of transmission (pp. 55, 62–63). His ban on music at weddings recalls Ibn Tumart's zeal in the streets of Tlemcen (p. 61).

## 5. Family

- **The family in Fez.** It was the father (about 60), Moses (early twenties), his brother David, and three sisters "about whom little is known" (Kraemer ch. 5, "Voyage to Fez").
- **Miriam.** One sister, Miriam, later wrote to Maimonides in Egypt "asking him to locate her errant son"; where she lived in 1165 is unknown (ch. 8). Kraemer cites the letter as Goitein, "A New Autograph by Maimonides and a Letter to Him from His Sister Miriam" (endnote). Its text is not in Kraemer.
- **Marriage in Egypt.** Maimonides married late, about 1173, the daughter of Mishael b. Isaiah ha-Levi, a physician and official. Her brother Abu ʾl-Maʿali ʿUzziel married Maimonides' sister, so the two men were "brother-in-law twice over" (ch. 12). The sister's son Abu ʾl-Rida became a physician and was trained by his uncle (ch. 12). The bride's name is not given (ch. 12).
- **David.** About 1169–71 David crossed the desert to ʿAydhab and wrote to his brother. He asked Moses to "calm the heart" of his wife, "the little one", and her sister. Among his fellow travellers he named "Salim, the son of the female broker" and "the brother of Sitt Ghazal (Lady Gazelle)" (ch. 14). He died at sea in the Indian Ocean.
- **Women and Torah.** Maimonides held that women are exempt from Torah study but not forbidden it. If a father did teach his daughter, "it is not as though he taught her nonsense" (ch. 18, Kraemer's paraphrase). The daughter of Samuel b. Eli, head of the Baghdad academy, taught men "through a latticed window" (ch. 18). That is Baghdad, not the Maghreb.

## 6. What is absent

Neither book has anything on Jewish women in Fez by name, on *ḥerem* or *niddui* in the Maghreb, on who licensed a Jewish court there, or on Purim, cross-dressing or gender variance among Maghrebi Jews. Kraemer says nothing of Alfasi at Qalʿat Hammad. These are findings, not gaps in the reading.
