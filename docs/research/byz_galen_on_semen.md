## 1. What the text says

Galen's target here is Aristotle's loom-weight theory of the testes (GA 717a, 787b–788a; see `byz_aristotle_generation_animals.md`). His own claim is that the testes are a *source of a power* that runs through the whole body. Castration therefore subtracts something in both sexes.

- **Castration removes maleness, and spaying removes femaleness.** Cut animals lose "not only the making of seed ... but also their manliness and, as one might say, their maleness (*arrhenotēs*)". Likewise, "if you excise the testes of a female animal" (the ovaries), it never comes into heat or accepts the male, "and loses, as one might say, its femaleness (*thēlutēs*)" (K IV 569).
- **A spayed female becomes like a castrated male.** In Asia "as far as Cappadocia" people spay sows, "and they all become very like the castrated [males], well fed and fat, with sweeter meat than other females, just as castrated males have than entire males" (K IV 570). Spaying is riskier because the "female testes" sit inside, beside the uterus.
- **What the cut become: colder, weaker, older, hairless, "like women" in their veins, and barely animals.** Those who lose the testes "are chilled, deprived of a second source of innate heat, and all their tone collapses as if they had grown old". They become "hairless, not only on the chin but over the whole body; they have small veins, like women; they do not desire sex, as though they were something other than an animal" (*hōs allo ti mallon ē zōon*) (K IV 572). For Galen the testes outrank the heart: the heart is the source of living, the testes of "living well" (K IV 574).
- **Galen quotes Aristotle's sentence and rewrites it.** He quotes GA 787b20 exactly: "All animals, when castrated, change into the female" (K IV 575). Then he restates Aristotle's point as: in castration "animals change toward the weaker" (*epi to asthenesteron*, K IV 576). Galen reads "female" as "weaker", and his whole rebuttal is about strength and heat, not about becoming female.
- **He states the problem in Aristotle's terms.** He wishes Aristotle had left "the problem of eunuchs" alone, rather than answer badly why "at the cutting of the testes the whole body is made female" (*holon ekthēlunetai to sōma*, K IV 582). Galen accepts that castrates *look* womanish. He disputes the cause.
- **The "third thing" passage (K IV 585), the key text.** A power passes from the testes into the whole body, "which in males is the cause of vigour and maleness, in females of femaleness itself. And for this reason the female animal with its testes cut out becomes like the castrated male. For all the other parts have the same powers in both; but that which was special in each, by which the one was male and the other female, once removed, the remainder is the same in both, **as if it had been born from the beginning neither female nor male, but some third thing, differing from both and neither of them** (*all' ti triton, diapheron men amphoin, oudeteron de ekeinōn*)."
- **Two-sex frame.** Each sex has its own positive power. Femaleness is not a lack of maleness, since a female can *lose* it. This matches King's point (ch. 1) that *On Semen* insists "the male animal differs from the female in its entire body" (II.5, CMG p. 180–83). This is not Laqueur's one-sex Galen.

## 3. In-world loc lines (own translations; verify against De Lacy before quoting as Galen)

- "Take the stones from a boar and he loses his maleness; take them from a sow and she loses her femaleness. What is left is a third thing, neither." (Galenic catechism)
- "The Philosopher said the cut become female. Galen said they become weaker. Ask which of them had ever been weak." (Aristotelian polemic)
- "Galen wrote that the cut are something other than an animal. The splinter calls that a third estate." (Mother of Mothers)
- "Neither female nor male, but some third thing: that is the physician's word, and the court pays him." (Rite of the Knife sponsor)
