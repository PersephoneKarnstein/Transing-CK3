# Gesink, "Intersex Bodies in Premodern Islamic Discourse" (2018): design reading

Source: Indira Falk Gesink, "Intersex Bodies in Premodern Islamic Discourse: Complicating the Binary", *Journal of Middle East Women's Studies* 14, no. 2 (July 2018): 152–173. DOI 10.1215/15525864-6680205. Duke University Press for the Association for Middle East Women's Studies.
Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo. Read in full: text (pp. 152–170), notes (p. 171) and references (pp. 171–173). Page numbers are the journal's. Quotes are 25 words or fewer.

**Scope warning.** This article is about the *khunthā*: people the jurists and physicians read as having both sets of genitals, neither, or an organ they could not classify. Gesink maps these onto modern intersex conditions (pp. 155–156). It is **not** about trans women, eunuchs or *mukhannathūn*. Her two pages that touch effeminacy (pp. 156, 161) keep the *mukhannath* apart. Her two late "case" stories (pp. 167–168) are where the categories blur, and they are the most dangerous material in the article for this project (§2 row 3). The *khunthā* never stands in for a Sister, and a Sister's story never resolves through a *khunthā* finding (`isl_noralla_egypt_trans_law.md` §2 rules 2–3).

## 1. The argument in brief

### 1.1 Thesis and frame

- **The target.** Today the field holds that Muslim jurists allow sex change because each person has "a 'true' sex" inside the binary (p. 152, citing Almarri, Bucar and Sanders). Gesink calls projecting that into the past "anachronistic" (p. 152). It feeds Orientalist narratives of "a monolithic rigid code" (p. 152).
- **The modern hook.** She opens with modern rulings: Shaykh al-Azhar Jad al-Haqq's fatwas of 1981 and 1983 permitting sex-change operations "for intersex persons", a Saudi ruling of 1990, and Khamenei's of 1999 (p. 152).
- **The base.** "Forty-one Islamic juristic, medical, lexicographical, and biographical sources", eighth to eighteenth centuries, "from al-Andalus to Central Asia" (p. 152).
- **The claim.** The "dominant strand ... tolerated ambiguity and flexibility" (p. 152). The discourse "widely recognized three to five sexual categories" (p. 152). The count of five comes from Ibn Qudāma: male, female, and three kinds of *khunthā* (p. 160). Experts saw sex "as fluid and changeable" (p. 152). A *khunthā* could be assigned or could "adopt a legal sex of male, female or neither" (pp. 152–153).
- **The spectrum.** She describes sex as running "between all-male/protectors and all-not-male/protected" (p. 153). Abū Ḥanīfa's ruling sets up "an operative binary between protector and protected" (p. 157). Her last question is whether the binary was about "protector and protected" rather than male and female (p. 170). **This matters for the project.** Her "medial sex" is a rank on that scale, as she says herself: "higher than women and lower than men" (p. 170).
- **Two strands, not one law.** Al-Sarakhsī's views were "one of two strands", older than the legal schools (p. 153). The binary strand, she argues, became the majority only in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Reformers "living under Western colonialism erased indigenous discourse on sexual ambiguity" (p. 170). Her parallels are Dreger on Europe and Najmabadi on Qajar Iran (pp. 169–170).
- **The tone.** Prior work "underestimate[s] the nonjudgmental character of legal and medical discourse" (p. 152). The *khunthā*'s "ambiguity was neither unexpected nor intolerable" (p. 166). She also says the sources are prescriptive. They cannot tell us how *khunthā*s lived "or understood themselves" (p. 166).
- **Sanders's own revision.** Sanders reconsidered the 1991 essay in an unpublished paper, "Hermaphrodites Revisited" (2008, on academia.edu). She gave it to Gesink and "encouraged" this critique (pp. 153, 171 n. 1). It was not read here.

**Egypt, the Fatimids and the Ismaʿilis.**
- **Fatimid and Ismaʿili.** Only al-Qāḍī al-Nuʿmān counts, and Gesink reads him through Almarri's translation, not in Arabic (pp. 155, 166).
- **Egyptian.** Two late items. The 1506 Cairo case (pp. 167–168) is Mamluk, 440 years after the window. Ibn Manẓūr is the other.
- **Not here.** No Mālikī *khunthā* ruling appears. The only Mālikī is al-Māzarī on women at prayer (p. 164). There is no Egyptian physician, and no al-Rāzī.

### 1.3 Medicine

- **Greek theories.**
  - *Hippocrates*: both parents give seed, and the larger contribution decides sex (p. 160).
  - *Aristotle*: semen fights menstrual matter, and any partial failure yields "'incomplete' or 'deformed' males" (p. 160).
  - *Galen*: heat and the side of the womb decide sex. "Male was the default", and too little heat yields "any degree of not-maleness" (p. 160).
  - Gesink: these ideas "easily explained nonbinary sex" (p. 160). She leans on Laqueur's one-sex model (p. 160), so King's caution applies (`byz_king_one_sex_body.md`).
- **Ibn Sīnā.** She calls the 11th century the first age of "popular Islamic medical texts" (p. 160).
  - He tells physicians to weigh the patient's "health and pleasure" (p. 160).
  - When neither parent's seed dominates, sex "might be ambiguous" (p. 160). Ibn Manẓūr's lexicon says the same: a *khunthā* forms when neither "fluid" wins (p. 160).
  - He files the *khunthā* under the **male** reproductive system (p. 161). Of their sex lives: "I have heard ... although I hardly credit this" (p. 161). He allows surgery to remove a non-functioning organ (p. 161).
  - **The split that matters.** He "does not ascribe any fault" to *khunthā*s. He calls passive sodomy (*al-ubna*) "a disease and damaging defect" (p. 161).
  - Analogous conditions in the women's chapter are treated as women's problems, not as *khunthā*. "Extra flesh" is cut away "from its base" (p. 161).
- **Al-Zahrāwī.**
  - He describes three kinds of *khunthā*: two "presenting as male", for whom he had "no cure", and one presenting as female, whose "extra flesh" he removes (p. 162).
  - He describes men whose breasts swell at puberty. Surgery is offered "for those who dislike" it (p. 162).
  - Surgeons stopped cosmetic surgery "upon patient distress" (p. 163).
- **Gesink's reading.** These physicians "aimed for congruence between sexual identity and physical appearance" (p. 163). They did not see "gonads or visual markers alone" as deciding sex (p. 163).
- **Her own doubt.** She asks whether the patient or "the husband or mother-in-law" sought the surgery (p. 162).
- **Clitoral surgery.** The texts call it a cure of an "ugly" organ. Sabuncuoğlu called it an "Arab practice" (p. 163). **Not for the mod** (§2 row 2).

### 1.4 Lexicography

- **Al-Khalīl's definition.** *Kitāb al-ʿayn* defines *khunthā* as "that which is neither male nor female" (p. 156).
- **The root.** He derives *mukhannath*, "effeminate male", from *khunthā* (p. 156). Gesink notes that the root kh-n-th carries "doubling" and "pliability", like folding the neck of a waterskin (p. 156).
- **Three ways of address.** Al-Khalīl gives one vocative for each:
  - *yā khunathu* for a legally male person "with doubled or pliable sexual qualities";
  - *yā khanāthi* for a legally female one;
  - *yā khunathatu* or *yā khunaythatu* for "an effeminate male".
  
  Gesink reads this as a spectrum "from all-male to all-not-male", citing Rowson 1997 (p. 156). The source gives the *mukhannath* **his own form**. Shared root, separate word.

### 1.5 Law

- **Urination (*ḥukm al-mabāl*).** It "eventually became the dominant method" in all schools (p. 154). It is probably pre-Islamic, but each lineage claimed it: the Fatimid judge for ʿAlī, the Ḥanafīs for Abū Ḥanīfa (p. 155).
  - Fatimid ʿAlī: the first flow decides. If both flow at once, "half a man's inheritance and half a woman's" (p. 155, via Almarri).
  - Ibn Ḥanbal counted the ribs. Jābir had the person urinate against a wall. Neither "conferred certainty", so the site of urination won (p. 155).
  - **Modesty.** No man could look at a woman's genitals and no woman at a man's. Imam ʿAlī al-Hādī advised looking with a mirror (p. 155).
- ***Khunthā mushkil*.** The term is used when urine comes equally from both openings, or when there is no organ (p. 155). Gesink translates *mushkil* as "complex", "the complex sex" (p. 156). Other renderings are "problematic", "ambiguous" (Sanders), "mixed" and "ungendered" (p. 156).
- **Not neutral.** A *khunthā* "was neither ungendered nor sexually neutral" (p. 156). Only al-Sarakhsī compares adult *khunthā*s to prepubescent children (p. 156).
- **Legal, not "true", sex.** Jurists sought a dominant legal sex, "a legal sex that most did not call 'true' sex" (p. 156). Sex could be challenged "in court or in bed" (p. 156). A person kept the name *khunthā* after assignment. Ibn Qudāma has three *khunthā* classes: legally male, legally female, and *mushkil* (pp. 159–160).
- **Puberty makes all early findings provisional (p. 157).**
  - Signs that make a person male: a beard, an erection, penetrative sex with a woman.
  - Signs that make a person female: menstruation, conception, breasts, lactation, vaginal sex.
  - Ibn Qudāma holds back a child's inheritance until puberty (p. 157).
- **Inheritance.** Abū Ḥanīfa gave a woman's share, on the protector logic (p. 157). Abū Yūsuf ruled half and half, "articulating khuntha as a medial sex" (p. 158). Gesink says "Abu Yusuf's ruling of medial sex dominated subsequent discussions" (p. 158). Al-Shaybānī, al-Thawrī, Ibn Abī Laylā, al-Shaʿbī, al-Luʾluʾī and Ibn Qudāma took similar half-share lines (p. 158).
- **Pilgrim dress.** Abū Yūsuf (8th c.) "simply did not know" what a *khunthā* should wear (p. 158). Al-Shaybānī chose women's dress (p. 158). Al-Sarakhsī and al-Marghīnānī "summarized both rulings without directly pronouncing either correct" (pp. 158–159).
- **Marriage.** Al-Sarakhsī ruled minor marriages invalid "but of no harm to either party", and advised waiting until puberty (p. 159). He shows "no concerns about potential sex between same-sex partners" (p. 159), as Sanders also found (her p. 88).
- **Self-report.** Al-Marghīnānī (12th c.): a *khunthā* who is not *mushkil* is believed, "for he knows his own state better than anyone else" (p. 159). Al-Ḥalabī (16th c.) agrees (p. 159). After puberty and a claim, though, any contrary sign required examination (p. 159).
- **Prayer, burial, dress and travel.**
  - *Prayer.* Al-Shaybānī put the *khunthā* "between the lines of men and women" (p. 164). Al-Sarakhsī: "behind the rows of men and in front of the rows of women", so that every prayer is valid (p. 164).
  - *Repeating prayer.* Al-Sarakhsī preferred, without requiring, that a *khunthā* who stood with the women repeat the prayer, "to observe prudence in matters of worship" (p. 165).
  - *Burial.* The order is man, *khunthā*, woman toward the qibla. Al-Sarakhsī "considered a khuntha more worthy than a woman" (p. 164).
  - *Dress, travel and circumcision.* The rule is the most cautious of either sex. No silk (as if male). Sewn pilgrim dress and no travel without a *maḥram* (as if female). A bought slave woman circumcises (pp. 165–166).
- **Gesink's conclusion on the law.** Jurists did not default the *khunthā* to "woman" when in doubt (against Kugle and Sanders, p. 165). They "recognized intersex persons as inhabiting a third category, a medial sex" (p. 170).

### 1.6 The case stories

- **Shuraih and ʿAlī (Fatimid source, pp. 166–167).**
  - Gesink stresses that the petitioner asked for the divorce "on the grounds that she was a man" (p. 166).
  - She reads the ruling as a "legal fiction" (Kecia Ali). If the petitioner was a man, sex with a slave woman was lawful and not *zinā*, and an unwanted marriage was dissolved (p. 166).
  - She also reads it as Fatimid legitimacy polemic. Fair judgment of *khunthā* sex "elevates ʿAli's claim to greater wisdom" over Muʿāwiya (p. 167).
- **Cairo, Ramaḍān 1506 (pp. 167–168).** The Sufi Ibn Salāma married a veiled bride, and a jilted cousin or neighbours denounced the couple.
  - *Ibn al-Ḥimṣī's version:* "an obvious khuntha woman" (p. 167).
  - *Ibn Ṭūlūn's version:* the court found "a boy in girl's clothing", who then "claimed khuntha sex". The women examiners saw "only a male organ". A second examination found the bride "lying" (p. 167).
  - Amir Ṭarābāy had the bride beaten to death. Gesink reads it as anti-Sufi polemic: the amir used the case "to equate Sufism with sodomy" (pp. 167–168).
- **Damascus, 1545 (p. 168).** ʿAlī b. al-Rifāʿī was "a beardless youth" and a bookbinder who loved a man.
  - Questioning found him "a khuntha who inclined toward femininity".
  - Physicians examined him and cut a membrane. The court renamed him ʿĀliya, and she married the man and bore "numerous" children.
  - Gesink reads this as tolerance and as praise of wise judges (p. 168).
- **Al-Ramlī (17th c., pp. 168–169).** He ruled that "ambiguity only occurs before puberty". Gesink reads this as "a juristic turn toward favoring binary sex" (p. 169).

### 1.7 The *mukhannath* in Gesink

There are only two passages.
- **The root.** Al-Khalīl's shared root and separate vocative (p. 156).
- **Blame.** Ibn Sīnā's blameless *khunthā* is set against the diseased *ubna* (p. 161).

Gesink cites no *mukhannath* law. **Her "medial sex" argument covers the *khunthā* only.** She does not link the two groups beyond the root. Her 1506 and 1545 stories show what happens at the seam: a gender-crossing person is pushed into the *khunthā* category, by a claim (1506) or by a court (1545).

### 4.1 `hist_sanders_khuntha.md`
1. **Inheritance (Sanders row 7, and "Inheritance" in §1).** Sanders's "lesser status" share is Abū Ḥanīfa's ruling. Gesink says Abū Yūsuf's half-share "dominated subsequent discussions" (p. 158), and al-Shaybānī, al-Thawrī and others followed it. Add: "Ḥanafī law was split: Abū Ḥanīfa gave the lower share; Abū Yūsuf and al-Shaybānī gave half and half (Gesink p. 158)." This strengthens Almarri row 7: the Sunni and Shiʿi difference is smaller than that row suggests.
2. **Pilgrim dress (§1, "Pilgrimage"; row 11).** Gesink attributes the "did not know" to **Abū Yūsuf** in the 8th century. Al-Sarakhsī and al-Marghīnānī only summarise it (pp. 158–159). Sanders has al-Sarakhsī admit it (her pp. 83–84). Proposed wording: "Abū Yūsuf, as reported by al-Sarakhsī, did not know." The in-game qadi line ("I do not know the answer") still works.
3. **"Physicians do not seem to have been participants" (row 8).** This holds for the legal examination in Sanders's Ḥanafī texts. But physicians operated on *khunthā*s (Ibn Sīnā, al-Zahrāwī, pp. 161–163), and physicians examined in the 1545 Damascus court case (p. 168). Narrow the claim to "in the jurists' procedure".
4. **Irreversibility.** Gesink stresses that a finding before puberty is provisional (p. 157), and that a *khunthā* stays a *khunthā* after assignment (p. 159). She agrees that after a claim at puberty, contrary signs need examination (p. 159, al-Sarakhsī and al-Ḥalabī). Both readings fit the texts. Add the nuance; row 5 is unaffected.
5. **Galenic medicine as "the binary's own medicine" (row 2).** Gesink reads the same theories as easily explaining "nonbinary sex" (p. 160), but she leans on Laqueur. The project's mechanical conclusion does not change. Note the dispute and King's caution.
6. **Women by default.** Gesink argues that jurists gave the *khunthā* "the most cautious requirement as applied to either sex", not the woman's (p. 165). Sanders's "almost always gendered as female" for *satr al-ʿawra* (her p. 82) is narrower and survives. Her wider "accord the inferior status" (her p. 81) is contested.
7. **Sanders 2008.** Add to the wanted list: Paula Sanders, "Hermaphrodites Revisited: A Reconsideration" (2008, academia.edu 35003377), cited by Gesink pp. 153, 171 n. 1, 173.

### 4.7 Errors in Gesink to flag
- **Al-Sarakhsī's dates.** He is "1009–90" on p. 153 and "d. 1096" on p. 158. She places him in "eleventh-century Persia" (p. 159) and "the northwest of today's Iran" (p. 163). Sarakhs lies in the north-east, on the Turkmen border (general reference).
- **Al-Qudūrī.** He is cited as 1952, 1953 and 1972 (pp. 157–158). Only the 1953 Bousquet and Bercher translation is in the references (p. 172).
- **The first medical texts.** She calls the 11th century the first age of "popular Islamic medical texts" (p. 160). That passes over al-Rāzī and al-Majūsī (10th c.; general reference). She cites neither.
- **Ibn Manẓūr.** She calls the *Lisān* "a fourteenth-century dictionary from Mamluk North Africa" (p. 160). The compiler worked in Mamluk Cairo and died 1311/12 (general reference). Do not copy the description.
- **Translated quotes.** Gesink's quotes from the Arabic are her own translations, with bracketed glosses. Several, such as the al-Ḥalabī passage on p. 165, are partly paraphrase. Do not treat them as verified primary wording.
