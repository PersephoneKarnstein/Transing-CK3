## 1. The argument in brief

- **Method: continuity for women, change for men.** For men in the half-millennium covered, "the theme of change would undoubtedly predominate"; for women "continuity is more striking" (pp. 15-16). The Christian authors of the sagas of Icelanders projected their own program (fidelity, consent) back onto pagan ancestors. Jochens trusts them only where laws and contemporary sagas agree, as they do on easy divorce (pp. 63-64).

- **Pagan marriage was a purchase between two men.** A woman was "totally absent from the negotiations" (p. 27). The handshake formula was the same one used to buy "land, a chieftainship, and an ocean-going vessel" (p. 27). The groom's family paid the bride price (*mundr*, at least one mark). The bride's family paid her dowry (*heimanfylgja*), an advance on her inheritance (p. 26). A woman's marital guardian, in order: her legitimate son, son-in-law, father, brother; only "in a family that lacked these suitable men" could a mother arrange a daughter's marriage (p. 25).

- **Consent was a Christian import that the sagas backdate.** In the Borgarthing Christian law, "If she says yes or is silent," the marriage is valid (p. 45). The secular Jónsbók then made both "father and mother" decide a daughter's marriage, and disinherited a woman who married without her kin (p. 46). In the sagas, all five marriages made against a woman's stated will "ended in disaster": clerical teaching cast as pagan history (p. 48).

- **Concubinage was endemic and pagan in origin.** *Skilfengin* ("lawfully wedded") and *skilgetinn* ("lawfully begotten") imply other partners and children, and the distinction "carried no Christian connotations" (p. 20). In Iceland a concubine's children did not originally inherit. Norwegian and Danish law let them inherit from a free concubine kept publicly "for twenty years or more", bedded "without concealment" (pp. 20-21). Terms: *frilla* (concubine), *fylgikona* (acknowledged mistress), *fylgja at lagi* (to live together by agreement, without rites), *elja* (co-wife), the word Hróðný uses to Njáll's wife (pp. 34, 39).

- **A child had to be "carried" to be kin.** The baby was carried to the man, who named it and sprinkled it with water. A child he did not accept was *úborinn*, "un-carried", "a condition with serious legal consequences" (p. 82). Frostathing law: "every child must have a father" (p. 81). In Norway a woman named the father in labour, with her guardian standing at the door, and he became the child's "half-father". If she stayed silent, the child became a slave after a month (pp. 83-84). In Iceland the guardian and five neighbours could "torture her" until she named him (p. 83).

- **Mothers made kings.** In the civil wars (1130-1240), "The deciding factor remained paternity, not legitimacy" (p. 96). In the end, "it was the woman who could best vouch for paternity" (p. 97): Sverrir's mother Gunnhildr confessed his royal father on pilgrimage. "King's brother" became a quasi-title for a king's maternal half-brother, "who possessed no royal blood but had shared the same womb" (p. 97).

- **Divorce was easy, and women used it more.** Grágás opens with "divorce shall not exist in this country", then gives "nearly a dozen closely argued pages" of exceptions (p. 55). The automatic grounds were:
  - poverty, or refusing to support a spouse's needy kin;
  - a husband taking his wife's property abroad, where "She simply had to call in witnesses and declare herself divorced";
  - "greater wounds";
  - prohibited kinship;
  - three years of non-consummation by neglect (pp. 55-56).

  The bishop could also grant divorce for incompatibility (pp. 56-57). In the sagas, "wives take the initiative twice as often as men" (p. 58). The procedure was to name witnesses at the bed, declare herself divorced, repeat it "by the main door", and then have a man repeat it at the þing (Unnr, *Njála* ch. 7; p. 59). If the husband was at fault she took both *mundr* and dowry; if she was at fault she still took her dowry (p. 60). The conclusion: "women retained their economic independence both in marriage and after" (p. 59).

- **Divorce was the inciter's lever.** "As part of the image of the female inciter, women used the threat of divorce to incite men." The threats were "you shall never come in my bed again" and "I shall let my father repossess my property" (p. 58). None of the threatened divorces is carried out, "because the women get their way" (p. 58).

- **Widowhood was a woman's peak.** A widow "was best able to embody masculine ideals" (p. 63). Jochens calls it "gender blurring": *drengr* and *skǫrungr* are used of "a few admired women, mostly middle-aged or beyond" (p. 61). Her examples:
  - Auðr the Deep-minded claimed "as much land as any man" (p. 62).
  - Sigrfljóð paid a killing's compensation "from her own purse" (p. 62).
  - The widow Katla accepted a praise poem for her daughter, gave the poet a ring, "and bestowed a name on him" (p. 63).
  - Guðrún Ósvífrsdóttir, on her fourth wedding bench, refused to give up a fugitive she sheltered: "I am not willing to turn over to your weapons those men I want to keep" (pp. 61-62).

- **Women were barred from the law's offices.** Women could not prosecute manslaughter. Even as principals in cases touching their own safety and property, "they were not expected to prosecute (sœkja) on their own but to delegate (selja)" to male kin (p. 113). Judges had to be "a male (karlmaðr), sixteen or older", and witnesses "men (karlar)". The test of "assembly-fit" (*þingfœrr*) was a man's (pp. 113-14). Young girls, "doubtless as capable riders and as mature as boys", were "never considered assembly-fit" (p. 114).

- **Fostering appears in the law as near-kin.** The six women whose lover a man could kill on the spot were his "wife, daughter, mother, sister, foster daughter, and foster mother" (p. 32). A foster son was one of four males who could stand in for an unfit man at the þing (p. 113). Guðný took a grandson as foster son and provided for him in her will (p. 9). Guðrún Ósvífrsdóttir fostered her granddaughter Herdís (p. 15). Dalla, given a young child to foster, "immediately shows her affection by 'cutting' clothes for him" (p. 147).

- **The völva's grave under the church.** Herdís dreams that under the spot where Guðrún knelt to pray "an ancient sibyl or sorceress (vǫlva) was buried", in a grave known as a *vǫluleiði*. The völva complained that the prayers disturbed her. Guðrún had her bones dug up with her brooch and "large magical wand (seiðstafr mikill)" (p. 15). Separately: "in Norse sources these sibyls or sorceresses (vǫlur) appear only outside Iceland" (p. 104). Public performance belonged "to men and prophetesses" (p. 104).

- **"A friend to my friend."** In 1255, after the Flugumýrr burning, Guðrún Gjúkadóttir appears in dreams to the girl Jóreiðr: "It should not be of any concern to you whether I am Christian or heathen, because I am a friend to my friend" (p. 14).

- **Textile is the economy, and it is women's.** The distaff (*rokkr*) and spindle (*snælda*) were carried everywhere: "women undoubtedly spun whenever their hands were free" (p. 136). The warp-weighted loom kept weaving female in Iceland until the late eighteenth century, whereas the horizontal loom made it men's work in Flanders (pp. 139-40).
  - *Darraðarljóð* turns the valkyries' battle role into weaving fate, carrying the norns' thread "to a logical conclusion" (p. 138).
  - Jochens sides with von See that the poem is about war, not a work song: twelve women cannot work one loom (p. 137).
  - "Cloth-worked (váðvirkta) him at the end" was a Norwegian circumlocution for killing a man, glossed as an Icelandic "set phrase ... when women finish their web" (p. 139).
  - Homespun became "the medium in which fines, compensations, and land were expressed" (p. 148), and the law speaker was paid in it.
  - A female settler would not take land as a gift and gave a spotted cloak "and wanted to call it a purchase" (p. 135).

- **Vocabulary of the soft man.** King Haraldr asks whether Stúfr's father was "the hard or the soft cat" and concedes "the person who is soft (blauðr) could not be a father" (p. 76). The paragraph of *ýki* insults (*ragr*, *stroðinn*, *sorðinn*) was outlawed whether "carved in wood ... or spoken aloud" (p. 74).

- **Other law useful to the design.**
  - Grágás permitted the castration of beggars: those doing it "were not punished, even if they seriously wounded or even killed the victim" (p. 22).
  - Yngvildr, "hair cut and dressed as a man, fled to join" her young lover abroad in the 1150s (p. 39). No dress suit follows in Jochens's account; the scandal recorded is the affair, not the clothes. (Jochens does not discuss the dress clause here; the reading against K §254 is ours.)
  - On enforcement in general: "only powerful men could enforce the law" (p. 39).

**Contradictions and flags.**
- Row 9: the design's travelling völva in **Iceland** has no saga support. Jochens finds no living völva in Iceland in Norse sources, only the Helgafell grave.
- Row 1: declaration "before the thing" assumes the declarer speaks there. Women attended the þing "for social purposes exclusively" (p. 114). The declaration can stand as speech at the gathering, but her *suit* against the shouter must go through a man or a friend.
- Row 11: Jochens's "manly widow" framing conflicts with the constraint that a daughter's courage is never "manly". Use her evidence, not her label.

**Primary cases usable as events or characters.**
- Yngvildr and the cut hair, 1150s (p. 39): a woman in men's clothes, not prosecuted.
- Guðrún Ósvífrsdóttir's fourth wedding bench (pp. 61-62).
- The Helgafell *vǫluleiði* (p. 15).
- Jóreiðr's dream of Guðrún Gjúkadóttir, 1255 (p. 14).
- Unnr's divorce at bed and door (p. 59).
- Sigrfljóð paying compensation (p. 62).
- Katla naming the poet (p. 63).
- Dalla cutting clothes for her foster-child (p. 147).
- The female settler (Landnámabók) who made her land a purchase with a cloak (p. 135).
