## 1. The argument in brief

- **A Mediterranean renaissance under Church lordship.** Provençal Jewry's culture is translation, philosophy, halakhah and rhymed satire. It flourished in the space between Church and Crown (Introduction; pp. 285–296).
- **Jewish self-rule was granted from outside.** Communities everywhere depended on a lord's *privilegia* (p. 29). In 1144 Conrad III gave the archbishop of Arles jurisdiction over the Jews of Arles (p. 26). From 1251 most of that jurisdiction passed to the Angevin count (p. 29).
- **Women: the literature silences them, the archives do not.** Hebrew literature is "homotextual" and keeps women silent. Latin notarial records show women lending, trading, suing and endowing (pp. 114–122). Ben-Shalom then looks for women's own voices and finds two: Esther Tsarfati of Avignon (1383) and Bona Dona of Arles (1397) (pp. 122–130).
- **1306 was the decisive break.** Philip IV's expulsion destroyed Narbonne, Béziers, Lunel and Montpellier as centres. Angevin Provence, east of the Rhône, took in the refugees (pp. 143–156). Ben-Shalom dates the end of creative kabbalah in Provence to 1306 (p. 404). Provence itself expelled its Jews in 1498–1501, after its annexation to France in 1481 (pp. 211–212).

## 2. What matters for the design

### 2.1 Kalonymus's "desire to be a woman" (pp. 112–114, 128)

- Ben-Shalom translates the whole lament (p. 112). It includes:
  - "spinning with our hands" and twisting yarn "by the moonlight" with her friends;
  - the three women's commandments, "blood and fire and bread";
  - the prayer: "if only you would transform me from male to female";
  - "I would be the lady of the house";
  - the men's blessing, said "softly and in a hushed tone" (p. 113).
  - **This confirms** the mod's loc for 0001 (`desc_touchstone`, "in a low voice"), 0003.c ("I am the lady of the house"), 0020 (the three lamps) and the spinning-by-moonlight line in the README.
- **Three readings, and no consensus** ("there is no consensus among scholars regarding its meaning", p. 112):
  - **Schirmann:** an amusing parody that relieves the gloom. **Fleischer:** a satire that fails here (p. 113).
  - **Grossman:** social criticism of women's confinement. On this reading Kalonymus "is by no means envious of the fate of women" (p. 113).
  - **Rosen:** a man imitating a woman for a male audience. But it is also "an expression of male gender difficulties", and the image shows "the instability of gender divisions" (p. 114). Ben-Shalom summarises Rosen in a phrase: "transtextuality becomes a mark of transsexuality" (p. 114).
  - **Ben-Shalom's own view:** the text is polyphonic, and "readers are free to understand and interpret the text as they see fit" (p. 114). Even as parody, it "sowed it with the seeds of subversive ideas" (p. 114).
  - He later cites Bloch's "ventriloquistic imitation" with Kalonymus as the example (p. 128).
- **For the design:**
  - The current loc ("The learned call it a jest; the ḥevrah says he meant it") is accurate. "The learned" really are split three ways.
  - Ben-Shalom's line about readers' freedom (p. 114) licenses the ḥevrah's reading as a legitimate act of reception. It is not a claim about Kalonymus himself.
  - The narrator must still not say Kalonymus wished to be a woman in earnest. Grossman says the opposite, and Rosen's "transsexuality" is a figure for the text, not the man.
  - A Jewish critic in event text can voice Grossman ("he pitied women; he did not envy them") or Bloch ("a man speaking as a woman").

### 2.2 Kalonymus: dates, places, Robert of Naples

- **Dates disagree.**
  - Ben-Shalom gives "1278–1343" (p. 71). Shatzmiller gives 1286–after 1328/9. The `tm_arles` comment gives "1287" (after Chotzner).
  - *Even Boḥan* is dated **1323** (pp. 71, 151, 257). Shatzmiller's dating is Tebet 5083, i.e. Dec. 1322–Jan. 1323.
- **Where he wrote it.** He wrote *Even Boḥan* "in 1323, he resided in Barcelona" (p. 151). This confirms the Catalonia correction already applied. He also lived in Naples and Rome (p. 257).
- **Robert of Anjou.**
  - Kalonymus worked "during the years 1324–8" at Robert's court in Naples, "by the king's invitation" (p. 293). Robert stayed in Avignon in 1319–24, and Kalonymus probably travelled to Naples with the royal entourage (p. 294 n. 37).
  - He translated Averroes' *Tahafut al-tahafut* (in part) from Arabic into Latin, beginning in Naples and finishing in Arles in 1328 (p. 294).
  - He received "a modest annual stipend from the king's agent in Arles" (p. 294): six gold ounces, or 30 florins, recorded in 1329 (n. 37).
- **The translator won privileges for his people.**
  - Immanuel of Rome thanks Kalonymus for "the seals [privilegia] he has obtained from our lord the king" (p. 294). Ben-Shalom: "The Jews of Rome thus obtained privilegia from the king through the efforts of Kalonymos" (p. 294).
  - Immanuel says the Jews of Rome hosted him, because there was "no Jewish community" in Naples (p. 293). He wrote this on their behalf to Kalonymus's brother Samuel, who wanted him home in Arles (p. 293).
  - **This directly confirms** the mechanic of `tm_hipukh.0030.a`: commission a translation, and take her people under protection. The real chain was translation, then the king's favour, then *privilegia* for the translator's community.
- **Masekhet Purim** was written "during his stay in Italy, 1322–8". Ben-Shalom calls it the first whole parodic work for Purim, the start of the genre (p. 97 n. 362). This agrees with Rosen, who places it in Rome.

### 2.3 Purim and carnival in Provence

- ***Even Boḥan* on Purim** (Even Boḥan p. 30; Ben-Shalom p. 258): "men dressing up as women ('this one in women's attire, necklaces round his throat')". Ben-Shalom adds: "It is unclear from the text whether the author deems such 'world upside down' behaviour borderline or prohibited" (p. 258).
  - The feast is attended by men and women: "Timbrel and dance, jubilation and lutes, both men and women together" (p. 258).
  - It runs into the next day: "let the Jews be given tomorrow as well" (Even Boḥan pp. 31–2; p. 258 n. 86).
  - **This confirms** `tm_hipukh.0011` (anklets, necklaces, the dress worn for the joke) and the Shushan Purim line in 0002 ("as the scroll allows").
- **The Purim king** (Ralbag, *Megilat setarim*, Avignon 1332; pp. 97–98).
  - A Purim king was chosen on 1 Adar. A staff passed from member to member until 16 Adar, and he then led a procession and ordered food to be shared out.
  - Davidson saw the Feast of Fools behind this rite, "known in this form only in Provence" (p. 97).
  - A Provençal Purim statute, written after 1332, survives (p. 97; Davidson pp. 135–139).
- **Arles's own carnival.** In Kalonymus's city "women also took part in the festivities". The 'bishop of the innocents' visited the "'abbess of fools' at the monastery of Saint Césaire" (p. 96). Christian Arles, too, had a licensed day of reversal with women in it.
- **Women's place in *Masekhet Purim*.** Kalonymus says the women "do not leave the kitchen ('between oven and stove') from 11 Adar until 16 Adar" (p. 97 n. 363). This is his irony, and the same "between oven and stove" as the lament (p. 113).
- **The Judaeo-Provençal Esther.**
  - Crescas (Israel) Caslari wrote a rhymed Esther poem in Judaeo-Provençal, c. 1327. It was "probably intended for public reading on Purim day" and written for "a wider audience including women and children" (p. 252).
  - Provençal prayer books in Hebrew characters were made "probably for the use of women who did not know Hebrew" (p. 252).
  - **This supports** `tm_hipukh.0010` ("explains it in the common tongue"). Caslari's poem is the period model for an elder reading Esther to women in Provençal.
- **A hazard: Purim and Christian accusations** (pp. 197–198).
  - At Manosque in 1306, a Purim carnival procession mocked an adulterous couple, "with a man playing the role of the woman". An adulterer was then flogged, and the Christian court read the whole as a parody of the Passion (p. 197).
  - Similar charges were made at Lunel (1319), Hyères (1344) and Marseilles (p. 197). Ben-Shalom links these to Horowitz and to the history of the blood libel (n. 118).
  - **Hard limit:** no event may tie the Turning, or any Purim custom, to mockery of Christianity, to Haman-as-Jesus, or to Christian "Purim parody" charges. The Manosque cross-dressing exists only inside that accusation, so it must not be used as a precedent for the sect.

### 2.4 Women's religious life and literacy

- **Women taught by women.**
  - Esther (Trina), daughter of the physician Jacob Tsarfati of Avignon (d. 1383 in the plague), "knew Scripture and its translation twice as well as any other woman". She chanted it and translated it into the vernacular (p. 125).
  - She names her sister Sarah as her teacher. Her father calls his learned daughters "the house of Jacob, that is, the women" (p. 127).
  - Ben-Shalom calls this "a secondary oral culture of Jewish women in Provence, including women's rituals" (p. 127).
  - At her deathbed stood "wise mourning women" and "wise women" (p. 126).
- **Customs passed from mother to daughter.**
  - Asher ben Saul of Lunel (c. 1215) saw "worthy women" fast from 17 Tammuz to 9 Av, because "so it was passed down to them from their mothers, generation after generation" (p. 126).
  - The women of Narbonne washed their hair on the Ninth of Av, against the halakhic norm. Me'iri called it "improper". The old sages of Narbonne defended it: on that day the messiah will be born (p. 127).
- **No women's spaces, no women mystics.** Idel explains the absence of women kabbalists by "the lack of Jewish spaces specifically for women, such as the Christian convents" (p. 122). Grossman objects that widows had both time and standing (p. 122). **For the design:** the ḥevrah is exactly the space Idel says was missing. That makes it the sect's claim, not a recorded institution.
- **The women's synagogue.** Ben-Shalom does not discuss a women's section. Arles and Carpentras sold synagogue seats as heritable property, through Christian notaries (pp. 98–99). The Arles synagogue burned in 1459 (p. 99). Hildenfinger's finding of a women's synagogue below the men's (`judaism_reading_list.md`, finding 1) is neither confirmed nor contradicted.
- **Books owned for women.** The 1397 will of Dieulosal de Stella, a surgeon of Avignon, lists "a scroll of the biblical book of Esther, two Hebrew prayer books for women, and a medical book in Arabic script" (p. 71).

### 2.5 Women's economic and public roles

- **Arles.**
  - Venguessona Nathan, a widow and the mother of Isaac Nathan, was a draper with a shop in the Jewish market. She signed 326 loan contracts in 1431–40, three times the next lender. She was the wealthiest Jewish landowner in Arles in 1425–30 (pp. 120–121).
  - In 1436–40, 11 of 43 Jewish moneylenders in Arles were women (over 25%), against 3 of 63 Christian lenders (p. 120).
  - Regina Bonete was one of three trustees of maître Helias's school for the poor, founded in 1407 (p. 120).
  - Bona Dona wrote to her father in 1397, threatening to "send the Christian" to him (p. 129).
- **Physicians and healers.**
  - Sarah of Saint-Gilles, a widow, taught "the art of medicine and physics" (Marseilles, 1326; p. 116).
  - Three of eleven Jewish medical practitioners at Manosque (1262–1348) were women, among them the *phisica* Mayrona (p. 118).
  - Yemimah of Aimargues, a healer, was accused of extracting an oath "by witchcraft" (p. 116).
- **Women consenting to ordinances.** The Reillanne takanot of 1313 were "approved by a consensus of all community members, men and women" (p. 32). Rashba thought that impractical: consensus would need "the women as well as the men, for how can decisions be made regarding their money without their permission?" (p. 32).
- **Widows.** Grossman estimates that about 20% of Jewish women were widows (p. 121). Many preferred not to remarry and to keep their independence (pp. 116, 122).
- **New names for `tm_give_daughter_name_effect`.**
  - Venguessona (Arles, 1430s); Douce (Avignon, 1383, p. 126); Ketsinah (Arles/Perpignan, 1385, p. 117); Bélest (Forcalquier, 1326, p. 117).
  - Esther is now attested as a real Provençal woman's name (Esther Tsarfati, Avignon). It no longer rests only on Shatzmiller's "Aster".
  - Leave out Murada and Blanca of 1484, who were killed in the Arles riot (p. 211 n. 11).

### 2.6 Community organisation and confraternities

- **Arles is old and central.** An eleventh-century responsum calls Arles "a great assembly [kahal]" whose ordinances bind the surrounding towns (p. 25).
- **The 1215 charter** (pp. 27–28). Michel de Mourèse, archbishop of Arles, granted it.
  - Each year at Sukkot the Jews chose three *rectores*, who swore "by the Holy Mosaic Law".
  - They were chosen through three electors, "according to the custom of the Christian consuls".
  - Their verdicts had to be unanimous. If one rector dissented, the archbishop appointed a replacement.
  - The rectors later became the *baylons*, three and then four, still serving one-year terms. All were drawn from the upper class, "especially doctors" (p. 28).
- **Bans came from outside.** "The authority to impose a ban did not come from within the community but was granted from without, by a representative of the governing power—the viguier or, in the case of Arles, the archbishop." It was granted for a period matching the *baylons'* year in office (p. 35). In 1349 the *baylons* asked the viguier to approve even an ordinance against luxury spending and dice (p. 29). **This confirms** revision 6, the `tm_turning_niddui_effect`.
- **Charitable societies** (pp. 55–56).
  - Provençal communities kept *talmud torah*, *ḥolim*, ***ma'or* ("illumination of the synagogue")**, *tsedakah*, *kabarim* (burial) and *arba kosot* (Passover) societies. Others paid dowries for poor brides and ransomed captives.
  - "Their founding constitutions ... required the approval of a Christian magistrate" (p. 55).
  - Narbonne, Arles, Aix, Avignon and Marseilles kept hospices. "In some communities, such as Salon, there were separate hospices for men and women" (p. 55).
  - The Arles ḥesed society had eighteen founders from the artisan and middle class: a builder, a brick merchant, a broker, used-clothing sellers, a wheat measurer and a physician or surgeon. No member also served as a *baylon* or office-holder. Ben-Shalom suggests the society gave "those excluded from leadership positions some compensation for their lack of political influence" (p. 56).
- **For the design:** this sharpens the reading-list recommendation (finding 2) to model the sect as a circle inside a mixed *ḥolim* confraternity.
  - The *ma'or* society, which kept the synagogue lamps, is a real confraternity type. It fits the Three Lamps.
  - The ḥesed society's members are the people the *baylons* kept out. The worthies who judge her in 0003 are a different social class from the ḥevrah that shelters her. Event text can make that class tension explicit.
  - A confraternity's statutes needed a Christian magistrate's approval. That is a second licence the lord holds, beside the ban.

### 2.7 Residence, the street, the lord

- **No ghetto.** Jewish residence "was generally limited to one or two streets, although residence in these areas was not compulsory" (p. 131). Urban V (1362–70) ordered the Jews of Arles confined to a separate quarter, and his legate's inquiry found "close interaction between Jews and Christians in the city" (p. 131).
- **The quarter.** It lay in the parish of Saint-Martin, on the *magna carreria juzatarie* (now Rue du Dr Fanton). The Dominican church, built in 1361, stood at its heart (p. vii).
- **Jewish physicians served the archbishop.**
  - The archbishop of Arles and his family were treated by Mosse de Roquemartine (1424) and Bendich de Borrian (1442) (p. 69).
  - Bendig Aym, a *baylon* in 1386, was physician to Queen Joanna I from 1339, and was exempted from tax for it (p. 69).
  - Medical licences were approved by the viguier after examination, for example Solomon Avigdor in 1402 (p. 68).
  - In 1306 Charles II forbade Jewish doctors to treat Christians. The ban was widely ignored (p. 68).

### 2.8 The 1306 expulsion

- **Dates.** The order was given on 21 June 1306. Jews were arrested on a single day, 10 Av (22 July), and expelled about a month later (pp. 143, 150).
  - **Montpellier** was then held by the king of Majorca as a vassal of France. Its Jews were expelled in October 1306 (Marḥeshvan 5067), after negotiation between the two kings, and most went to Perpignan and Catalonia (p. 148).
  - **Narbonne** had 165 Jewish households in December 1305: 50 under the archbishop and 115 under the viscount. It was the largest and wealthiest community in Languedoc (p. 145).
  - Lunel and Béziers were emptied. Simeon Duran's lament names Lunel, Béziers and Narbonne in turn (pp. 153–154).
- **Arles took the exiles, through Kalonymus's brother.** Charles II approved the opening of Provence's borders on 10 August 1306. "A patent to allow the exiles to settle in the county was obtained by Samuel, brother of Kalonymos ben Kalonymos of Arles." Samuel brought to Arles the seneschal's order not to "molest" the foreign Jews (p. 149). Abba Mari of Lunel came to Arles, left, returned, and presided over its rabbinic court until after 1315 (pp. 36, 150).
- **Kalonymus's response.** He called the expulsion "the destruction of my native land", quoting Esther 8:6 (p. 151). For him, Languedoc was part of one *Proventsa* that ran from the Alps to the Pyrenees (p. 151). **For the design:** this ties the Purim scroll to 1306. The verse the elder reads in Adar is the one Kalonymus used for the expulsion.
- **Afterwards.**
  - Louis X let the Jews return in 1315 "for only twelve years". Communal property (synagogues, schools, cemeteries) was restored (p. 156).
  - In 1320–1 came the Shepherds' Crusade, the Talmud burning and the Lepers' Plot, which Kalonymus mourned (p. 158). There was a further expulsion in 1322 (p. 148) and the final expulsion from France in 1394 (p. 171).
  - Ben-Shalom dates the end of creative kabbalah in Provence to 1306 (p. 404).
- **Material not to use.** Duran's lament describes destitute women selling their bodies (p. 154). The 1306 sale records list the property of individual Jews in detail (pp. 145–146). Neither belongs in event text: no expropriation inventories, and no sexual degradation of refugees.

### 2.9 The end of Provençal Jewry (outside the game's dates)

- The Manosque riot of 1424 opened a wave of violence: Aix in 1430 (killings, twelve forced baptisms), Pertuis in 1430, and Carpentras in 1459, where more than sixty were killed (p. 210).
- René of Anjou (in Provence from 1447) was protective, but pressed conversion by persuasion (pp. 210–211).
- Provence passed to France in 1481. Harvest workers attacked the Arles quarter in 1484. Arles expelled its Jews in 1493, and Louis XII ordered the general expulsion in 1498, repeated in 1500 and 1501 (p. 211).
- About half of those remaining converted and stayed (p. 212). Converts kept lighting the Sabbath lamps "as they had seen their mothers do", "and especially the women" (Ibn Verga, quoted p. 213). The four papal communities of the Comtat (Avignon, Carpentras, Cavaillon, L'Isle) survived to 1789 (p. 214).
- **CK3 ends in 1453**, so none of 1484–1501 is in play. The sect's late history in-game runs through 1306, 1315, 1320–2, 1348 and 1394, and the 1420s–30s riots.
- **Avoid:** the forged 1489 "letters of the Jews of Arles and Constantinople" (p. 212), an antisemitic conspiracy forgery. They must not appear in any form.

### 2.10 Kabbalah in Provence

- **The Arles circle.** A kabbalistic circle was active at Arles in the first half of the thirteenth century. It was oral, and it studied the "doctrine of evil", the left-side emanation. Its founding legend was a quire brought by "Gershom Damascus" (pp. 374–377). Naḥmanides is said to have received a tradition "in the city of Arles, at the yeshivas of R. Shelemyah" (p. 377).
- **In 1323** Kalonymus still names kabbalah as a living current among the factions: "another adheres to kabbalah, and another to philosophism" (p. 405).
- **For the design:** the Arles circle's subject (sefirot of impurity, accusers, demons) is a poor fit for the sect, and it risks "demon" imagery near Jews, which the hard limits forbid. Do not make the Nehpakhot kabbalists. At most, in the sect's own voice, an elder can know that Arles once had "sages of the tradition" who taught by mouth (p. 375). Ben-Shalom notes that no women are known among the Provençal kabbalists (p. 122).

1. **`tm_arles` comment** (`common/religion/holy_site_types/tm_holy_site_types.txt`): "in the one street the archbishop allowed the Jews (Chotzner 1900, p. 128)".

   - Reword to: "in the Jewish street of Saint-Martin parish (Ben-Shalom 2024, pp. vii, 131)".

2. **`holy_site_tm_narbonne_effects`**: "whose nasi held houses and fields under the viscounts".
   - The *nesi'im* held their lands **in allod**: owned outright, under no lord. The tradition credits this to a charter of Charlemagne (pp. 18–20). The 1308 sale lists houses Momet Toros held "in franco et libero alodio" (p. 145).
   - "Under the viscounts" inverts the point. See §4.1 for new loc.
3. **`holy_site_tm_montpellier_effects`** and the faith-file comment ("the Tibbonids, Lunel and Montpellier").
   - The translation movement began at **Lunel**, where the yeshiva's patron-sages commissioned Judah ibn Tibbon (pp. 285–286). Montpellier was the later centre of Jacob ben Makhir (Don Profeit Tibbon) and of Jewish–Christian collaboration (p. 294).
   - Montpellier was Aragonese, then Majorcan, until 1349, and was never royal French before then (p. 148 n.). The loc is right in substance. See §4.1.
4. **The 1322 gate** (`current_date >= 1322.1.1` in 0001 and 0003). Both authorities put completion of *Even Boḥan* in **Dec. 1322–1323** (Shatzmiller: Tebet 5083; Ben-Shalom: 1323).
   - A gate of **1323.1.1** is safe under both datings. 1322.1.1 is a year early.
   - Minor. The user's decision.
5. **The `tm_hipukh.0030` code comment** cites Chotzner for Robert of Naples. Replace it with Ben-Shalom pp. 293–294. That source has the 1324–8 dates, the stipend paid at Arles, and the *privilegia* the translator won.
6. **README, "the Jews of Arles lived under their archbishop".** This is right for 1144–1251. From 1251 most jurisdiction over the Jews of Arles passed to the Angevin count (p. 29), and licences came from the viguier as well as the archbishop (p. 35). Consider "under the archbishop and the count".

### 4.1 Holy-site loc for Narbonne and Montpellier (and Arles)


### 4.2 The 1306 expulsion as an event

Ben-Shalom gives a playable shape that does not reproduce the expulsion's own violence.

- **Trigger (*unverified* that CK3's history files make this hold):** a yearly pulse in 1306–1307.
  - It fires if `c_carcassonne` (Narbonne) or `c_montpellier` is held by, or de jure under, the holder of `k_france`, and Nehpakhot or other Jewish characters live there.
  - If the Languedoc counties are not French in the player's game, it does not fire. The 1306 expulsion was a French royal act (pp. 143–144).
- **"The Patent at the Rhône"** (`tm_hipukh.0050`, proposal). The player is the holder of `c_venaissin` (Arles), or any Provençal lord. Exiles from Narbonne and Montpellier arrive at the river.
  - **a.** "Let them settle, and let no one molest them." The lord gains the patron modifier, or a new `tm_exiles_received_modifier`. Exiled Jewish courtiers may join the court, and the arrival of Marked seekers is weighted toward this county. This mirrors Charles II's patent and Samuel ben Kalonymus's order (p. 149).
  - **b.** "Let them pass through." Neutral.
  - **c.** "Close the bridge." The lord loses opinion with Jewish characters. No gold reward.
  - **Hard rule:** no option gains gold from Jewish property and no option expels anyone. Expropriation was the Crown's motive (pp. 144–145), and the mod should not make it a reward.
- **For the sect.** If the ḥevrah holds a House or members in Narbonne or Montpellier:
  - its members move to the Arles House (Sisters' Houses already "send the sisters on to another House rather than deleting them");
  - the moved members get a short-lived `tm_exile_of_1306` modifier (stress, piety).
- **Elder's line** (sect's voice), for 0010 in 1306–1315: "This Adar she read the verse the Touchstone's author read for Languedoc: how can I bear to see the destruction of my native land?"
- **1315 follow-up (optional):** "for only twelve years" (p. 156). A Jewish character may return to Narbonne or Montpellier, and the House there can be refounded.
- **Leave out:** martyrdom scenes (the 1320–1 passages, pp. 162–165; the no-suicide rule), the Manosque Purim affair (§2.3), Duran's women "bowing to shame" (p. 154), and the 1308 sale inventories.

### 4.3 The patron mechanic

- **Confirmed and sharpened.** Kalonymus's translation for Robert won the Jews of Rome *privilegia* (p. 294). He was paid a yearly stipend through "the king's agent in Arles" (p. 294), not a one-off fee. Robert was praised as the "new Solomon" (p. 293).
- **Suggested changes:**
  - **0030.a:** add a small yearly upkeep instead of a single 50 gold. The model is "six ounces of gold", 30 florins a year (p. 294 n. 37). For example, `remove_short_term_gold = 25` now, plus a modifier with `monthly_income = -0.2` for its ten years. Optional.
  - **Rename the modifier's description** to name the real exchange. Proposed loc: `tm_translation_patron_modifier_desc:0 "This character paid a translator a yearly stipend and, at her asking, sealed privileges for her people."`
  - **A physician variant**, "A Physician of the Street", as Shatzmiller's note proposed. Ben-Shalom adds the licence: examination before a panel, then approval by the viguier (Solomon Avigdor, 1402; p. 68). He also adds that the archbishop of Arles was treated by Jewish physicians (1424, 1442; p. 69), and that a woman taught medicine (Sarah of Saint-Gilles, 1326; p. 116). A daughter of the Turning as physician is therefore period-plausible.
- **The worthies' licence** (`tm_worthies_licence`) can stay as is: the ban licence ran for the *baylons'* term (p. 35), and the Narbonne and Carpentras charters ran for years (p. 29).
- **A new lever:** confraternity statutes needed "the approval of a Christian magistrate" (p. 55). A protecting lord could also **approve the ḥevrah's statutes**, which could gate founding a Sisters' House for this faith. *Proposal only*; it adds a third licence and may be one too many.

### 4.4 The sect's era

- **What Ben-Shalom dates.** All the textual ground of the Turning falls in **c. 1320–1332**:
  - *Even Boḥan* (1323);
  - *Masekhet Purim* (Italy, 1322–8);
  - Caslari's Judaeo-Provençal Esther (c. 1327);
  - Ralbag's Purim king (Avignon, 1332).
  - The women's blessing "qui fis mi fenna" is a fourteenth- to fifteenth-century manuscript (reading list, finding 3).
  - Organised community at Arles is attested from the early eleventh century (p. 25), and the *baylon* system from 1215 (p. 27).
- **Implication.** The "present at every start" decision (faith_design §13, decision 1) stands as a game convention, but the sect's history is a fourteenth-century one. Three options for the user:
  1. **Keep as built.** The sect exists from 867. Kalonymus content waits for 1323. This is the cheapest option.
  2. **Quiet before 1306.** Before 1306 the Turning cannot fire outside `c_venaissin`'s neighbourhood. The ḥevrah's spread to other Houses becomes possible only after the refugees of 1306 arrive. Ben-Shalom's 1306 is the moment the Midi's Jewish population concentrated at Arles (pp. 149–150).
  3. **Founded in 1323.** The sect does not exist at the 867 and 1066 starts, and a scripted event founds it at Arles in Adar 1323. This is the most faithful option, and it breaks the other faiths' convention.
- **Era-end hooks inside 1453:**
  - 1348: plague riots in eleven Provençal communities, and migration into Arles (p. 54).
  - 1394: the final expulsion from France (p. 171).
  - 1424–1430: the riots at Manosque, Aix and Pertuis (p. 210).
  - Nothing past 1453 is needed.

### 4.5 Smaller loc and mechanic changes

- **0010.** Add Caslari's Provençal Esther as what the elder reads, "as Crescas of Caslar set it in our tongue" (p. 252). *Only after c. 1327*; before that, "explains it in the common tongue" stands.
- **0020, "The Three Lamps."** Ben-Shalom's *ma'or* society (p. 55) allows a variant option: "Pay the *ma'or* dues, and trim the synagogue lamp with the others."
- **0001, a Grossman-voiced rebuttal** for a Jewish critic. Proposed: "A learned guest says the Touchstone pities women and does not envy them." The source is Grossman's reading, as reported on p. 113.
- **The women's oral culture** (pp. 126–127) supports the line "an elder can teach the prayer to one who will need it", and makes it the sect's central form of transmission.
- **"The women spin together by moonlight"** is Kalonymus's own image (p. 112), and *Mishnah Sotah* 6:1 is behind it (p. 113). The README line is sound.
- **Names.** Add Venguessona, Douce, Ketsinah and Bélest (§2.5).

## 5. Hard limits reinforced by this source

- No Purim mockery of Christianity, no Haman/Jesus identification, and no Christian "parody of the Passion" charge as event content (pp. 197–198).
- No blood-libel material, including the *rex judeus* of Narbonne as Thomas of Monmouth used it (p. 17).
- No 1489 Arles–Constantinople forgery (p. 212).
- No moneylending as trait or motive. Women's lending figures (§2.5) are economy only.
- No martyrdom or suicide scenes from 1320–1 (pp. 162–165).
- No kabbalistic demonology attached to the sect (§2.10).
