## 0. Framing warning

- **Moreh's thesis.** He argues that the medieval Arab world "had a well-established tradition of live theatre, if only at a popular level" (quoted by Rowson, p. 466).
- **How he gets there.** He builds chains of synonyms: *laʿʿāb* = *mukhannath* = actor; *ḥikāya* = *khayāl* = play. Rowson's verdict is that this "significantly weakens" a sound case. Moreh tortures "his evidence into submission", letting "speculations solidify into hard facts" (p. 467).
- **On the *mukhannath*, Rowson's key sentence.** Effeminates "were clearly associated with some sort of acting (acting out? acting up?)". But "this association by no means obliterated the primary significance of the term" (p. 467).
- **Two practical consequences:**
  1. **Moreh's translations hide gender terms.** He renders *mukhannathūn* and *muʾannathūn* as "entertainers" or "actors" (pp. 25, 50, 69, 75, 91). Check the Arabic transliteration he gives, or the source, before quoting any Moreh English.
  2. **Nothing in Moreh makes the *mukhannath* "really" an actor.** For the mod, the *mukhannath* stays what Rowson calls him: a not-man whose public trade included performance. The narrator never calls him an actor in place of that, and never calls him a trans woman.

## 1. The argument in brief

### Rowson's review (pp. 466-468): where Moreh over-reaches, and what stands

**Over-extended:**
- **Synonym chains.** If *laʿʿābūn* ("players") sometimes appear with *safāʿina* (slap-takers), the slap-takers become "actors". And "when one version of an anecdote about *laʿʿābūn* substitutes the term *mukhannath*", the *mukhannath* "becomes henceforth simply 'actor'" (p. 467). Rowson also doubts Moreh's emendation of *Farāghina* to *safāʿina* (pp. 46, 48).
- **Category slips.**
  - *Maḍāḥik* (p. 64) are "jokes", not "jesters".
  - *Samāja* means a mask, dressing up or a masquerade. It is "never a collective noun for such 'masked actors'" (properly *aṣḥāb al-samājāt*), "as he repeatedly renders it" (p. 467).
- **Is it drama?** Rowson asks whether stuffing a pillow under one's robe to mock ʿAlī, "as the mukhannath ʿAbbāda did for the caliph al-Mutawakkil", is drama (p. 467).
- **The "Trial of the Caliphs".** The ascetic's "Trial of the Caliphs" (Ibn ʿAbd Rabbih) is taken at face value. Its source is the court theologian Bishr al-Marīsī; it "conforms to official ʿAbbasid propaganda"; and calling the ascetic a "Sufi" is anachronistic (p. 467).
- **The *Ḥikāyat Abī l-Qāsim al-Baghdādī*** is read as a script. Rowson: "There is in fact no real evidence for linking this text with any kind of 'performance'" (p. 467). Moreh's claim that the *maqāma* preserves lost plays "suggests a certain desperation" (p. 468).
- **No script exists.** "We simply do not have anything resembling a script, or even a detailed description, of an actual play" (p. 468).
- **Mistranslations.** Jaḥẓa's verses (Moreh p. 88) are mistranslated. So is al-Khafājī's "*khayāl Jaʿfar al-rāqiṣ*", "the dancing play of Jaʿfar", which Moreh makes a dancer's live theatre (p. 468).
- **Apparatus.** The endnotes on p. 150 (nn. 111-122) are misnumbered. Sources are listed in several editions without saying which the page numbers follow. For "Figure 5" on p. 59 read "Figure 4" (p. 468).

**What stands:**
- Moreh "has unquestionably made his case for the existence of some kind of 'dramatic performance'" (p. 467).
- Some *ḥikāya* and *khayāl* citations "do indeed imply some degree of plot and characterization going beyond simple impersonation" (p. 468).
- His distinction between *khayāl* and *khayāl al-ẓill* (shadow play) is sound. So is his finding that many *khayāl* passages were wrongly read as shadow play (p. 467).
- His chronological list of *ikhrāj* ("production") is "a model of clarity" (p. 468).
- The citations are "a wealth of new information" (p. 468).
- Rowson's closing point: whether Arabic literature "lacked" drama is "more than a little Eurocentric". The better question is what forms the culture did produce (p. 468).

### The *mukhannathūn* in Moreh (pp. 22-28, 69-70, 75, 89-91)

- **Moreh's equation** rests on these passages:
  - **Al-Amīn's night** (Baghdad, before 813). Al-Ṭabarī's "the players (*al-laʿʿābūn*) playing" becomes, in Abū l-Faraj al-Iṣfahānī's *Aghānī* (10th c.), "the *mukhannathūn* blowing flutes and drumming" (p. 25).
  - **The *Naqāʾiḍ* commentators** (Abū ʿUbayda, d. 822, and his transmitters to al-Yazīdī, d. 922). They gloss *kurraj* as "a play played by *mukhannathūn*" (pp. 25, 30).
  - **Two late renderings** (p. 26):
    - The Arabic version of the Syro-Roman law book (translated before 1408) renders "infamous" as *mukhannathūn*.
    - The *Thesaurus Syriacus* gives *mīmos* as *al-mukhannath al-muḥākī al-maskharī*.
  
  Rowson's objection applies: the association is real, the equation is not.
- **ʿAbbāda.** Al-Shābushtī (d. 998) says of ʿAbbāda the *mukhannath*: when his father, al-Maʾmūn's cook, died, *takhannatha*. Moreh renders this "joined the actors and jesters" (p. 26). Al-Maʾmūn sent him to Zubayda "so that she will see him and laugh" (p. 26).
- **The Medinan four.** Al-Suhaylī (d. 1185) says four *mukhannathūn* in the Prophet's time "did not practise homosexuality". They spoke softly, dyed hands and feet, and danced like women, and some "used to play with the *kurraj*" (p. 26).
- **A troupe hired to a house.** In al-Ḥuṣrī al-Qayrawānī's *Dhayl Zahr al-Ādāb* (d. 1022), "a son of ʿĪsā b. Jaʿfar invited a troup of *mukhannathun*". They played and danced. One "had no repertoire", and the host threatened to make him eat dung or embers. He prayed at length, asking God "to transform me into an ostrich … or into a pig" (pp. 26-27). Moreh reads this as improvisation under threat (p. 27).
- **A wedding, and a master.** Ibn ʿAsākir (d. 1176) tells of a ninth-century villager near Damascus. He sought *laʿʿābūn* for his brother's wedding and called them *mukhannathūn*, but "the ruler (*sulṭān*) had forbade them to perform" (pp. 23-24). Moreh infers that they "were led by a master" (p. 24); the text has a joke about the *raʾīs* of the ascetic singers.
- **Satirical sketches.** These are Moreh's best *mukhannath* evidence (p. 89):
  - **The mother threat.** A *mukhannath* threatens a poet that he will "produce" (*akhraja*) the poet's mother in a sketch. It is told in three versions:
    - Ibn Abī ʿAwn (d. 934), *ḥikāya*;
    - al-Shābushtī (d. 998), *khayāl*, said by ʿAbbāda to Diʿbil;
    - Ibn ʿĀṣim (d. 1426), *laʿba*.
  - **ʿAbbāda's ʿAlī.** For al-Mutawakkil (r. 847-61), ʿAbbāda tied a pillow on his belly, bared his bald head and danced while singers sang "The bald one with the paunch is coming". Al-Muntaṣir called him "this dog" (pp. 89-90; Ibn al-Athīr). Moreh: silent mimicry with the words sung by singers (p. 90).
  - **ʿAlluwayh's sketch.** The singer ʿAlluwayh (reign of al-Amīn, 809-13) "composed a play (*ḥikāya*) which he handed over to the dancers (*zaffānūn*) and actors (*mukhannathūn*)". It mocked the judge al-Khalanjī, whose turban had been glued to a pillar. The judge resigned and asked for a post in Damascus or Ḥimṣ (pp. 90-91; *Aghānī* 11:338f). "Ridiculing judges and scholars seems to have been popular" (p. 91).
- **Mimicking the *mukhannath*.** The Baghdad street comic Ibn al-Maghāzilī (reign of al-Muʿtaḍid, 892-902) imitated "a bedouin, grammarians, entertainers (*mukhannathun*), judges", a eunuch and a Turk. Al-Masʿūdī's account has the eunuch who brought him to court laughing at his imitation of eunuchs (pp. 69-70). The *mukhannath* was himself a stock turn for other comics.
- **Notoriety (*shuhra*) and the shaming parade** (p. 75):
  - In tenth-century Bukhārā a baker was paraded before execution "in the company of entertainers" (*bayna yadayhi al-mukhannathūn*).
  - In eleventh-century Iraq a condemned vizier rode a camel "wearing the conical cap of a clown" (Moreh's source note is n. 70, Ibn Ẓāfir, but the note numbers in the extraction cannot be matched with certainty).
- **The Fusṭāṭ broker.** Moreh cites al-Musabbiḥī pp. 104 and 68 for al-Mukhannath al-Baghdādī al-Dallāl and the "pimp of five women" (p. 26 and n. 54). This is the earliest scholarly use of these entries found so far. But he says the broker "was killed in 415/1024 by his lover in a gaming house", and the chronicle reports only a rumour (Cairo note §1.1).

### The *kurraj* (pp. 27-37), in full

- **What it is.** A Persian word for colt, donkey or mule (Middle Persian *kurrag*, p. 41 n. 61). Arab lexicographers define it as "a foal of wood with which one plays" (p. 27).
- **What it is not.** It is not a child's stick-horse. The player stood hidden inside drapery, with no legs or wheels (p. 37).
- **Ibn Khaldūn's description** (14th c.): "decorated wooden figures of horses attached to robes (*aqbiya*) such as women wear". The players "attack and withdraw and compete in skill", to tambourines and flutes, at "parties, wedding, festivals". "Women also used to simulate cavalry duels" (p. 31).
- **Early evidence:**
  - al-Bukhārī (d. 870): a *laʿʿāb* among the Muhājirūn on a raid (p. 28);
  - ʿUmar would have expelled a *kurraj* player from Medina had he not seen it in the Prophet's time (p. 23, al-Suhaylī);
  - in Mecca it was played at every feast until 252/866 (al-Fākihī);
  - al-Masʿūdī records a dance "the camel and the hobby-horse" under al-Muʿtamid, 870-93 (p. 28).
- **Gender in the insult.** Jarīr mocks al-Farazdaq, dressed in silk with a bracelet, as a "laughing-stock" wearing "the two ornamented belts and small bells of a *kurraj*". Jarīr himself is the armed husband and the *kurraj* player the wife (pp. 29-30, Bevan's *Naqāʾiḍ*). One commentator calls al-Farazdaq here a *samāja* (p. 44).
- **Moreh's inference.** "The association between *kurraj* and *mukhannath* was so strong that during later periods effeminate men came to be known as *kurrajīs*" (p. 28; source al-Shābushtī p. 199 n. 9 and Bosworth's *Underworld*, n. 70).
- **Moreh's own limits:**
  - The purpose of the plays is unknown. "Only through analogy with central Asian Shamanic and seasonal fertility rituals is it possible to venture a conjecture" (p. 27). His shamanic reading (pp. 27-28, 31, 34-35, 37) is conjecture, as he says.
  - "No author … mentions any dialogue in connection with the *kurraj*, only singing and dancing" (p. 37).
- **Al-Amīn in the *kurraj*.** Before 813, al-Amīn danced inside a *kurraj* all night among servant girls and *mukhannathūn* while a love song was repeated (p. 30; al-Ṭabarī; *Aghānī* 18:71f). Moreh reads this as an ecstatic trance (p. 31). That reading is his, and Rowson's warning applies.
- **Egypt.**
  - **Maimonides** (in Egypt from c. 1166) names a *faras al-ʿūd* (horse-headed stick) that entertainers ride "when performing *khayāl*" (p. 32).
  - **A woman player.** Maimonides also has a hobby-horse **actress** who goes house to house in a hair-net and a comically ugly shirt (p. 76).
  - **The procession of Dhū l-Ḥijja 415/1024-25.** Al-Musabbiḥī's sugar banquet (*simāṭ*) is "led by forerunners, *afrās al-khayāl*, the Farḥiyya Sudanese drummers" (p. 32; al-Musabbiḥī pp. 79f).
  - **Moreh's guess.** He suggests that these "wooden mares" were played by men called *raqqāṣūn*, who "served also as guards who punish offenders" (p. 32). See §4 on this word.

### Fatimid and Egyptian festival material (pp. 9-11, 46-54, 70, 88, 124-125, 147)

- **The Coptic Nawrūz** (Moreh puts it on "March 21 every year (in the month of Tut)", which contradicts itself; see §4). It resembles the pagan Maiumas: lamps by the river, seven days of celebration (pp. 9-10).
  - Masked actors (*samāja*), singers and musicians performed at it "in both the Byzantine and the Islamic eras". The documentation is "particularly rich for the Fatimid period" (p. 10).
  - "Muslim rulers would prohibit the celebration of Nawruz and other Coptic feasts from time to time" (p. 10).
- **Correction (consolidation pass, checked in the Arabic):** the *muʾannathūn* line below is **not Ibn Zūlāq's and not 975**. In *Khiṭaṭ* (ed. Sayyid 1:727–728) the Ibn Zūlāq entries for 363–364 end with the market masks and al-Muʿizz's ban; the *muʾannathūn* under al-Luʾluʾa come later on the page, from al-Qāḍī al-Fāḍil (written 584/1188) describing the Fatimid past in general. Moreh runs the two together. See `isl_shoshan_popular_culture_cairo.md` (correction block).
- **Fatimid Nawrūz, 364/975.** Ibn Zūlāq (d. 997), quoted by al-Maqrīzī (*Khiṭaṭ*, ed. Wiet 4:245f), says al-Muʿizz had banned the fires and water-splashing. Yet in 364/975 the market people paraded for three days with comic masks and jewels. Then, in Moreh's translation: "The entertainers (*muʾannathun* = effeminate men) and lewd women (*fāsiqāt*) would assemble near al-Luʾluʾa palace so as to be seen by the caliph". They had musical instruments in their hands (p. 50). **Secondhand** for the Arabic. Moreh's transliteration is *muʾannathūn*; it needs checking against Sayyid's *Khiṭaṭ* edition.
- **Where masks were worn.** In Egypt the *samājāt* "are not mentioned as performing in the rulers' courts but rather in the streets and markets of Cairo". "Most of the Fatimid caliphs encouraged such celebrations" (p. 48). The Mamluk Barqūq banned them (1382-89).
- **Joseph's Prison, Jumādā I 415/1024.** Al-Ẓāhir stayed two days at the Prison of Joseph until the performers returned with sugar statues, comic figures, *ḥikāyāt* and *samājāt*. "He was amused by them" (p. 53; al-Maqrīzī after al-Musabbiḥī pp. 39, 41-43).
  - For two weeks the market people roved the streets "going up to Cairo with that so that the Commander of the Faithful would see them". "They returned with a scroll to the effect that none of them was to be prevented from coming and going" (p. 53).
  - The performers' name, *al-ramādiyya*, Moreh derives from faces painted with ash (*ramād*) (p. 62 n. 41). This is his conjecture.
- **The end of a word.** *Samāja* is last used of a living practice under al-Ẓāhir (1021-36). After the eleventh century historians use it only when copying older chronicles (pp. 46, 54).
- **Mimicry at court.** On 20 Muḥarram 415/1024 "the Persian Junior Sharif" rode behind al-Ẓāhir "mimicking (*yaḥkī*) Shams al-Mulk", twisting his head and miming nose-blowing in his face. Shams al-Mulk was so offended that "al-Zahir had much trouble conciliating him" (p. 88; al-Musabbiḥī).
- **A change of term.** From the eleventh century "the usual word for mimicry and theatrical performances was *khayāl*", and *ḥikāya* moved toward "story" (p. 89).
- **Other Egyptian performers:**
  - Coptic actors (*mukhāyil al-Qubṭ*, *Khiṭaṭ*, Būlāq ed. 1:488; p. 147 n. 42);
  - a comedian who visited patients in an Egyptian hospital with "imitations with comical masks", reported by Umayya b. ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz (d. 1134) (p. 70).
- **Late material, not Fatimid evidence:**
  - Ibn al-Ḥājj's (d. 1336) Amīr al-Nawrūz in Egypt: lime-white face, fur beard, red or yellow dress, conical *ṭurṭūr*, ridden on a donkey to extort coins (pp. 75-76).
  - His "Scene of the Judge" played by *mukhayyilūn* (pp. 129-130).
  
  Both are fourteenth-century and must not be dated to 1066.

## 4. Corrections / answers to open questions in other notes

Other notes were **not** edited. Proposed changes:

1. **`later_starts/mukhannathun_baghdad_1066.md` §5 Q11 ("Read Moreh pp. 27-37 and 90-91 directly, given Rowson's review").** **Answered.**
   - Rowson's review does find Moreh's reading of *mukhannathūn* as actors over-extended: the synonym chain on p. 25 turns an association into an identity (Rowson p. 467).
   - What survives: a hired troupe (p. 26); satirical sketches with *mukhannathūn* (pp. 89-91); the *kurraj* glossed as their play by the commentators (pp. 25, 30); "*kurrajī*" as a later nickname (p. 28).
   - Pp. 27-37 add no further *mukhannath* evidence. They are about the *kurraj* and Moreh's shamanic conjecture.
2. **Same note §1.2c ("the *kurraj* hobby-horse (Rowson 2003, p. 60; Moreh, secondhand)").** Now direct: Moreh pp. 25-37. Drop "secondhand".
3. **Same note §1.2b, *Muntaẓam* 15:320 (441/1049), "instruments of mimicry" (*ālāt al-ḥikāya*).** Moreh p. 32 translates "entertainers with hobby-horses". That is his gloss, not the text. Keep "instruments of mimicry".
4. **Same note §2.3 and §3.** For 1066 loc, prefer ***khayāl*** to *ḥikāya* for a sketch: by the eleventh century *khayāl* was the usual term (Moreh p. 89). *Samāja* is obsolete after c. 1030 (p. 54).
5. **Same note §5 Q7 (weddings).** Partial: a ninth-century Syrian case, reported in the twelfth century (Moreh pp. 23-24). Still none for eleventh-century Iraq.
6. **Same note §5 Q9 (al-Azdī's *Ḥikāyat Abī l-Qāsim*).** Partial: it borrows "curses by Dajāja al-Mukhannath" from al-Tawḥīdī's *Imtāʿ* (Moreh p. 95). Mez dated it c. 1009-10 (p. 94). Rowson finds "no real evidence" that it was performed (review p. 467). Selove is still unread.
7. **`later_starts/mukhannathun_fatimid_cairo.md` §3.1 and §1.6 / §6 Q2 (Fatimid Nawrūz).** Answered (Arabic checked): al-Qāḍī al-Fāḍil, describing the Fatimid past, via al-Maqrīzī (*Khiṭaṭ* ed. Sayyid 1:728), has ***muʾannathūn*** with instruments at al-Luʾluʾa to be seen by the caliph at Nawrūz (Moreh misattributes it to Ibn Zūlāq, 975). That is effeminate performers at a Fatimid Nawrūz. Cross-dressing is not stated. **Secondhand**: check the Arabic in *Khiṭaṭ*, ed. Sayyid, and Shoshan ch. 3. This revises "not attested for 969-1171" in §3.1 for effeminates, though not yet for dress.
8. **Same note §1.1 (the Baghdadi broker).** Moreh 1992 (p. 26, n. 54) cited al-Musabbiḥī pp. 104 and 68 before Rowson 2003. Moreh's "killed … by his lover in a gaming house" states as fact what the chronicle gives as rumour. Do not cite Moreh for the killer.
9. **Same note §3.1 (masked pageant, 415/1024).** Moreh p. 53 adds detail:
   - al-Ẓāhir stayed two days at the Prison of Joseph;
   - the performers are *al-ramādiyya* (Moreh: "ash-faced", his conjecture, p. 62 n. 41);
   - the scroll guaranteed free passage;
   - people "stopped work".
   
   Moreh n. 40 places it in al-Musabbiḥī pp. 39, 41-43, which fits the note's pp. 42-43.
10. **Moreh's *raqqāṣūn* (p. 32) vs Lev's (p. 154).** Moreh reads al-Musabbiḥī's *raqqāṣūn* as hobby-horse dancers who "served also as guards". Lev (after Goitein) reads the same chronicle's *raqqāṣūn* as low-status policemen or runners. Lev's reading fits the Geniza usage. **Do not build "dancing guards" on Moreh.** Flag for `isl_lev_state_society_fatimid.md` §4 item 6.
11. **`isl_rowson_gender_irregularity.md` (ʿAbbāda's pillow skit).** Moreh pp. 89-90 gives the source as Ibn al-Athīr, *Kāmil* 7:55f. Rowson's review uses the same case to ask "is it drama?" (p. 467). For loc, it is mockery for a patron's pleasure, not theatre.
12. **Ibn al-Zubayr's date.** Moreh p. 48 says he "wrote ca. 440/1048". The *Kitāb al-Dhakhāʾir* is usually dated after c. 1070, so treat Moreh's date as unreliable (Lev cites it without a date). Use "11th c." in notes.
13. **Date of the Coptic Nawrūz (any Cairo event).** Moreh p. 10 says it was "celebrated on March 21 every year (in the month of Tut)". March 21 is the Persian Nawrūz. The Coptic New Year is 1 Tūt, at the end of August (Julian). Lev p. 170 puts it just before 6 Oct. 1023. That is also late summer or autumn, not spring. **Never fire a Cairo Nawrūz event in March.** Check the date against a calendar converter before scripting.
