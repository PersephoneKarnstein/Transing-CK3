# Ford, *Dress Codes* (2021): design reading

**Citation.** Richard Thompson Ford, *Dress Codes: How the Laws of Fashion Made History*. New York: Simon & Schuster, 2021.

Read from the epub's text, kept outside the repo (original in the user's `transreads` folder). The epub has no page breaks. Print page numbers below come from the book's own index where it gives them (marked "p."); otherwise citations are by chapter and section. Ford's endnotes were read for every passage used, and his sources are named so the facts can be checked at source.

None of these has pre-1300 material.
- **Searched:** Byzantium, Justinian, purple, Islam, caliph, Assyria, ninth/tenth/eleventh century. The book has **nothing on Byzantium, the Islamic world, India or Africa before modern times**. Its medieval material is Latin Europe, almost all Italian, English and French, and almost all after 1150. The front-matter timeline ("Historical Milestones") did not survive in the epub text.

## The argument in brief

- **Dress codes do two jobs.** Clothes communicate status, and they let people fashion a self. Dress codes are attempts to fix the meaning of clothes against fashion's drift (Introduction).
- **Sumptuary law (ch. 1).** Ancient Sparta, Athens and Rome had laws against luxury. In medieval Europe, "The earliest medieval European law prohibiting excessive luxury was passed in Genoa in 1157" (from Killerby 2005, p. 34). From the 1300s such laws focused on clothes and multiplied, peaking in the 14th–17th centuries. Their real aim was to keep status symbols for the elite: "sumptuary law was a way of keeping presumptuous upstarts in their place". An upstart's fine clothes were "seen as a kind of counterfeit". Ford's dated examples: Genoa 1157 (sable trim); Siena 1249 (trains); Castile 1258 (scarlet for the king, silk for nobles); France 1279 (by landholding); the papal legate in the Romagna 1279 (all women veiled); Florence 1322 (black for widows only); England 1363; Lucca 1337 (veils for nuns only); the Venetian Senate 1551: "[A]ll new fashions are banned".
- **Tailoring and the sexes (ch. 2).** Before the 14th century, elite European clothing for both sexes was draped: robes, gowns, tunics. Tailoring began with linen worn under plate armour. "Elite men adopted this, the first tailored clothing, and abandoned the draped robes previously common to both sexes" (citing Hollander 1994, p. 43). After that, women, clergy and lawyers kept the draped form.
- **Faith and sex (ch. 3).** The Church tied dress to sin, sex and faith, from Tertullian (c. 200; p. 48) to Bernardino of Siena (1420s). Prostitutes were made to wear particular colours and trims. Jewish women and men were marked by Christian law after the Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 (Pisa 1322, Rome 1360, Venice 1397, Ferrara 1416), sometimes with the same signs imposed on prostitutes (from Hughes 1986). The nun's habit was a voluntary dress code: by the sixth century "the habit was well recognized as a symbol of commitment to a life of religious service, as significant as formal vows", and an 11th-c. Archbishop of Canterbury told King Harold's daughter Gunhild that wearing it openly "is in itself a manifest and undeniable profession" (via Kuhns 2005, p. 66). From the tenth century some orders consecrated nuns as brides, in a white dress and veil, and "she would change her name".
- **Cross-dressing (ch. 4, pp. 67–74).** Joan of Arc was condemned in 1431 partly on Deut. 22:5 and rehabilitated in 1456 under the "necessity" exception. Ford quotes Aquinas: "Nevertheless, this [cross-dressing] may at times be done without sin due to some necessity", to hide from enemies or for want of other clothes; and Hildegard of Bingen (*Scivias* II.6): "Men and women should not wear each other's clothing except in necessity", that is, when a man's life or a woman's chastity is in danger (p. 69). Medieval romance and saints' lives liked women in men's clothes (Hotchkiss, p. 70). Festivals, carnivals and masquerades licensed cross-dressing, and city laws later banned it outside them: "a Florentine law of 1325 banned cross-gender dressing along with games in which young people dressed as old men or in which 'anyone transformed himself'"; also Ferrara 1476 (masks, "women dressed as men"), Brescia 1481, Gubbio 1507 (from Killerby, p. 64). Court cases tie cross-dressing to sex offences: Rykener 1395, Hetzeldorfer 1477, de Poorter 1502 (p. 72, from Bennett and McSheffrey 2014). Ford's summary: "Gender cross-dressing is probably as old as gendered clothing itself, but it became more conspicuous and more provocative in the 1300s".
- **Gender codes today (ch. 14).** A gendered dress code does not care which garment anyone wears; its purpose "is to ensure that clothing clearly identifies the wearer as either male or female". He follows Talia Mae Bettcher: gendered attire represents genital status, and trans people are punished for opting out of that system. "'Women's clothing' isn't clothing that is especially suited to female bodies—it is simply any clothing that women typically wear." The chapter recounts a modern murder trial in detail; it is not used.

## Relation to existing notes

## Hostile-voice and in-world paraphrases

All attributed to named speakers of vanilla faiths. None for the narrator.

- *A Catholic chaplain, from 1150 (after Hildegard):* "No one may wear the other's clothing except in need. Tell me your need, and I will tell the lord it is a disguise."
- *A Catholic jurist, from 1270 (after Aquinas):* "It is no sin to put on a woman's dress to escape your enemies. Who are you hiding from?"
- *A daughter's answer to either:* "I am not hiding. This is the dress I am seen in."
- *A Catholic preacher (after Tertullian):* "God did not paint your face. Whoever paints it says he made it badly."
- *An Italian city crier, from 1325 (after Florence):* "By order of the commune: let no one go masked, nor change his dress for the other sex, nor make himself into anything he is not."
- *A Catholic courtier, from 1157 (after the sumptuary laws):* "Scarlet is for the count. Silk is for the count's wife. What is she, that she wears both?"
- *A Byzantine bishop, any era (after Trullo 62, via Betancourt):* "The holy synod forbade a man the dress of a woman, and the masks of the pagan feasts with it."

## Cautions

- **Jewish badges.** Ch. 3 is about anti-Jewish law. Use it only as attributed hostile Christian law, never in a narrator voice, and never before 1215. Rome 1360 marked Jewish men and women with different garments, so a hostile city law would force a daughter of the Turning to choose which mark to wear. That is a strong scene, but it puts her body at the centre of an anti-Jewish law; it should not be built without the user's decision and a Provençal source (Ford has none for Provence).
- **Moneylending and plague.** Ford's ch. 3 repeats anti-Jewish polemic about pawnbrokers, usury and disease (via Hughes and Sennett). Excluded entirely (Ha-Nehpakhot guardrails: no moneylending, no creditor).
- **Self-regulation.** Ford cites a Jewish communal dress code of 1418 written "to avoid arousing the envy of the Gentiles" (from Hughes). It rests on the wealth stereotype; do not use.
- **Dating errors.** "1229 King Louis VIII" is impossible (he died in 1226). Ford's Deut. 22:5 is labelled KJV but is not the KJV wording; quote the verse only from the Hebrew, per §13. His endnote for Aquinas names the wrong part of the *Summa*. His "Jews first migrated north to escape the worst of the Inquisition" is vague on dates. Check any Ford fact at its named source before it enters loc.
- **Western only.** Every medieval example is Latin European. Nothing here is evidence for Byzantium, the Tamil country, Mande or the steppe.
- **Trans men out of scope.** Joan of Arc, Hetzeldorfer and most of ch. 4's court cases are people assigned female living or dressing as men. They are the hostile law's background only. Ford's own aside that Joan may have been "transgender" is not used.
- **Pronouns.** Ford calls Eleanor Rykener "he" and Gwen Araujo by her birth name in a quotation. Use she/her for trans women in all notes and loc.
- **Violence.** Ch. 14's account of a murder is not used, and no trans-panic narrative goes into any event (§16 rule 5).
- **No "turning" puns.** Florence's "anyone transformed himself" must not become a Christian joke about the Turning (§13 hard limits).
