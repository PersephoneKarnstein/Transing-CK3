# Bury, *The Imperial Administrative System in the Ninth Century* (1911), with Philotheos' *Kletorologion* (899): design reading

**Citation (checked against the PDF).** J. B. Bury, *The Imperial Administrative System in the Ninth Century, with a Revised Text of the Kletorologion of Philotheos* (British Academy Supplemental Papers I), London: published for the British Academy by Henry Frowde, Oxford University Press, 1911. 179 pp. (study pp. 1–130; Greek text of Philotheos pp. 131–179, with the De Cerimoniis Bonn pagination in the margin). Public domain. Philotheos, *protospatharios* and *atriklines* (master of court banquets), compiled the *Kletorologion* in September 899 under Leo VI and Alexander. **What was read:** section D, "Dignities and offices of the eunuchs" (pp. 120–129), in full; Philotheos' own text on the eunuch dignities (pp. 143–145 = Bonn 721–726) in Greek; the preliminary on Philotheos (pp. 15–16); and by search the passages on the *zoste patrikia* (p. 33), the Strategos of the Anatolics (p. 40), offices closed to eunuchs (pp. 69, 74), the empress's staff (pp. 25, 123, 126, 128) and eunuch protovestiarii (p. 96). The rest (sections B–C on bearded dignities and offices) was skimmed for eunuch references only. Translations from the Greek are mine.

**Relevance:** very high for the planned court-office mechanic. This is the official ninth-century table: eight eunuch ranks with their insignia, ten offices reserved to eunuchs, and the short list of offices they could *not* hold. The *Sidéris* and *Guilland* notes list offices from secondary literature; this note gives the 899 structure itself. Low relevance for adoption, the boy trade and Dorylaion.

## 1. The argument in brief

### How the eunuch order was built (pp. 120–121)
- In the fifth century the *cubicularii* under the *praepositus sacri cubiculi* were "the most important class of the Palace servants" (p. 120). They ran the wardrobe, the table and cellars, and the bedchamber.
- Two processes made the ninth-century system: subordinate officials were "elevated to independent, co-ordinate positions", and "titles of office are adopted as grades of rank" (p. 120). So the bedchamber staff split off under the *parakoimomenos*, the wardrobe under the *protovestiarios*, the table under *ho epi tes trapezes*; the cleaners, lamp-lighters and porters became the domain of the *papias* and the *deuteros* (p. 120).
- "In a wide sense of the term all the eunuch officials belonged to the cubiculum. They were graded in eight ranks" (p. 120).

### The eight eunuch dignities (*axiai dia brabeion*, "by insignia"), lowest to highest (Bury p. 121; Philotheos p. 143)
Philotheos: "there are eight in all, the dignities by insignia given to them" (Bonn 721). Each was conferred by the emperor's own hand, and each new holder paid fixed fees to the praipositoi, the *papias*, the *deuteros* and the *primikerios*.

| # | Dignity | Insigne | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *nipsistiarios* | linen tunic with a purple basin embroidered | presided over the imperial hand-washing (p. 122) |
| 2 | *koubikoularios* | tunic edged with purple, *paragaudion* | the generic word, "appropriated to one of the lowest" grades (p. 122) |
| 3 | *spatharokoubikoularios* | gold-handled sword | eunuch sword-bearers, distinct from bearded *spatharioi* (p. 122) |
| 4 | *ostiarios* | gold rod with jewelled handle | "door-keepers"; already a rank, not a job, by 787 (p. 122) |
| 5 | *primikerios* | white tunic with gold-embroidered shoulders | there was also a *primikerios* of the empress's bedchamber (p. 122) |
| 6 | *protospatharios* (eunuch) | gold necklet with jewels **and pearls** | own dress: white tunic with gold, red doublet (p. 123) |
| 7 | *praipositos* | ivory tablets, uninscribed | see below |
| 8 | *patrikios* (eunuch) | ivory tablets, inscribed | outranks the bearded *anthypatos-patrikios* |

- Only two of the eight, *protospatharios* and *patrikios*, were shared with the bearded; the other six "are ... names of office which have become grades of rank" (p. 121).
- **Precedence:** "The eunuch Patricians had precedence over the *anthypatoi* and *patrikioi*" (p. 124; Phil. Bonn 727, 730). The Strategos of the Anatolics, the senior general, came after the magistri, the Praipositos if a patrician, and "eunuchs of patrician rank" at court receptions (p. 40).

### The *praipositos* (pp. 123–124)
- In the 5th–6th c. one of the highest officials in the empire; he also managed the imperial estates in Cappadocia (p. 123).
- By the 7th–8th c. "praipositos" became a rank, and the chief chamber officers became "independent of any higher control than the Emperor's" (p. 123). In the 9th–10th c. the Praipositos ran the ceremonial once done by the Master of Offices (p. 124).
- **There was also a praipositos of the Empress's bedchamber** (p. 123, after Cod. Just. 12.5.3 and Peter the Patrician, "the two *praipositoi*", 491).
- **Regency:** under Basil I, the praipositos Baanes, also *sakellarios*, acted as regent (*apomoneus*) in Constantinople with the chief magistros and the City Prefect while Basil campaigned against Tephrike; Constantine VII calls this "the customary arrangement" (p. 124).

### The ten offices reserved to eunuchs (*axiai dia logou*, "by edict"; Bury pp. 124–128; Philotheos p. 145 = Bonn 725)
Philotheos names ten:

1. *parakoimomenos* of the emperor ("he who sleeps beside"): chief of the *koitonitai*, the bedchamber eunuchs; "supposed to be confined to eunuchs", yet Michael III gave it to Basil the Macedonian, and under Basil I it was left vacant (p. 125).
2. *protovestiarios* of the emperor: the private wardrobe, which was "probably a treasury" and paid the court's gratuities (p. 125). The second emperor and the Caesar had their own (p. 125). Sent on campaign: Prokopios to Sicily under Basil I (p. 125).
3. *epi tes trapezes* of the emperor (the table). Constantine, holder under Leo VI, commanded an expedition to South Italy (p. 126).
4. **epi tes trapezes of the Augusta** (the empress's table). He also kept the empress's private barges (p. 126). A seal survives of Niketas Xylinites, holder for Eudokia, wife of Basil I, who was "suspected of an intrigue with his mistress" and tonsured. Bury: "up to that time the office was not necessarily confined to eunuchs" (p. 126).
5. *papias* of the Great Palace: buildings, gates, cleaning, lighting. "The keys of the gates and doors were in his possession, and in the case of a Palace conspiracy a great deal might depend upon his attitude" (pp. 126–127), as at the fall of Leo V in 820. Under him: chamberlains on weekly shifts, bath attendants, lamp-lighters, stokers, clock-keepers (p. 127).
6. *deuteros* of the Great Palace: thrones, furniture, curtains, imperial robes and the insignia of all dignities, kept in the oratory of St Theodore (pp. 127–128).
7. *pinkernes* (cup-bearer) of the emperor.
8. **pinkernes of the Augusta** (p. 128).
9. *papias* of the Magnaura.
10. *papias* of the Daphne (a creation of Michael III, p. 128).

- **Everything else was open to eunuchs.** Philotheos: "and all the other dignities too, as many as the bearded hold, except those of the Eparch, the Quaestor and the Domestics" (Bonn 725, Bury p. 145). Bury confirms the City Prefect's office was "one of the few which could not be held by a eunuch" (p. 69), and the Quaestor's exclusion points to "its ancient associations and prestige" (p. 74). The Hetaireiarch "might be a eunuch" (p. 108).
- Philotheos was copying an *ekthesis* issued by Leo VI himself in the paragraph on the eunuchs: "these things are to be kept ... firm, as our pious and royal custom has had it from ancient times" (pp. 15–16).

### The empress's household (scattered)
- A *praipositos* and a *primikerios* of the empress's bedchamber (pp. 122–123); her own *epi tes trapezes* and *pinkernes* (pp. 126, 128); "four silentiaries were appropriated to the service of the Empress" (p. 25).
- **The *zoste patrikia*** (p. 33): "the only lady who was *patrikia* in her own right"; Bury translates "mistress of the robes". The earliest on good authority is **Theoktiste, mother of the Empress Theodora** (wife of Theophilos, 829–42). The investiture ceremony is in *De Cer.* I.50 and "probably dates from the ninth century".
- **The *basileopator*** ("Emperor's father"): created by Leo VI c. 894 for Stylianos Zaoutzes, father of his second wife Zoe; later given to Romanos Lekapenos (pp. 114–115). Not a eunuch office, but an in-law office.

## 3. In-world lines for loc

- "The gold rod for the doorkeeper, the pearl collar for the sword-bearer, the inscribed ivory for the patrician. The Emperor hands them out himself, and every one is paid for." (court-rank flavour)
- "The keys of the palace are in the Papias's purse, and every plot in the city knows it." (Papias position description)
- "The Emperor's law closes three doors to the cut: the city, the law-court and the army. All the rest stand open." (faith or court commentary; after Philotheos)
- "She is the only woman in the empire who is a patrician in her own right." (Zoste Patrikia description)
- "They trust us at the Empress's table because they think we are nothing. We are her sisters." (Dorleyaian daughter in the Augusta's household)

## 4. Flags

- **Nuance to the Sidéris 2010 note row 5:** the *castrensis* is a fifth-century office; by Bury's account it "seems to have disappeared by the sixth century" (p. 120), with only a later *kastresios* of uncertain place (p. 126). Do not use "castrensis" as a ninth-century office name; use *epi tes trapezes* for the table.
- **Nuance to the Guilland note:** the *parakoimomenos* was "supposed to be confined to eunuchs" in Philotheos, but Bury notes Basil I held it in 866 and it stood empty in his reign, and the empress's table-master under Basil I was not necessarily a eunuch. The reservations were real but not absolute in the 860s.
- **Events or characters in the start window:** Basil the Macedonian as (bearded) parakoimomenos, 866; Baanes, praipositos-regent under Basil I; Niketas Xylinites and Empress Eudokia Ingerina (tonsured, 870s); Theoktiste, first known *zoste patrikia* (d. after 842).
