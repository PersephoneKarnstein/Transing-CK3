# Bettcher, *Beyond Personhood* (2024): design reading

**Citation.** Talia Mae Bettcher, *Beyond Personhood: An Essay in Trans Philosophy*. Minneapolis and London: University of Minnesota Press, 2024 (copyright page reads 2025). ISBN 978-1-5179-0256-8 (hc), 978-1-5179-0257-5 (pb). Dedicated to Alexis Marie Rivera. Parts adapt Bettcher, "Trapped in the Wrong Theory," *Signs* 39.2 (2014).

**Sources read.** The whole book, read in order in chunks: front matter, preface, introduction, chapters 1 to 9, conclusion, glossary and every endnote. The index was skimmed only to check terms. Text file: `scratchpad/books/bettcher.txt` (12,277 lines, `pdftotext -layout`). It stays in the scratchpad and is not copied into the repo.

**How cited.** **B:line** = line number in that text file. A quote that runs across a line break is cited by its first line, or by a range.

**Quotes.** Each quote is verbatim and 25 words or fewer. Soft hyphens and end-of-line hyphenation have been removed and curly quotes made straight; nothing else has been changed. Everything else is paraphrase.

**Mod context checked.** `docs/faith_design.md` §0, §1–9, §14–16 (writing rules 1–26); `README.md` (Sisters' Houses, Declare Before Witnesses, Set Down the Name, Sister-Making, Keep Quiet in This Land, Teach Her the Answers, the hostile world, the Women's Chamber, the Galenic wing, Hide the Name, the call); `docs/beliefs.md` rows A3, A4, E3, H25, H26, H39. Sibling notes: `book_bhatt_chu.md`, `book_serano_whipping_girl.md`, `book_heaney_feminism_against_cisness.md`, plus the earlier Bettcher entries in `book_tsr_2.md` (ch. 23), `tsq_keywords_2014.md` and `book_ford_dress_codes.md`.

**Scope.** This is a work of philosophy, written from Los Angeles trans subcultures between 1994 and 2022. Bettcher says plainly that it is local and partial (B:450–475). It contains no medieval material. Eunuchs, *hijra*, "third gender" and "third sex" get **zero** hits, and baeddel, Marx and Bhatt get none either. Everything in "For the mod" is a lens for writing and design. None of it is historical evidence.

## 2. Key concepts (what the book actually argues)

**2.1 Reality enforcement (ch. 1, B:774–1027; ch. 4, B:3835–4163).** Misgendering in which a trans person is taken to be "really" one thing disguised as another. It is "easily the most life-shaping form of transphobia" (B:777). Its shape is a double bind: "either risk exposure as a deceiver or else come out as a make-believer" (B:802). Its "reality" side ends in *genital verification*. That can be literal forcing, or the euphemistic question ("Have you had the surgery?", B:841), or newspaper prose such as "was found to actually be a woman" (B:844–845). The whole system makes people tell an intimate truth through their clothes. Since forcing someone to disclose intimate information is a violation, Bettcher calls the system "inherently abusive" (B:858–866). Its targets are not picked out as "trans": the street version is "Hey, look, a man in a dress! Let's go harass him!" (B:738). A note adds that the two sides blur. Someone who does not pass can still be suspected of deceit, "(why else wear this costume)" (B:10041).

**2.2 Playing along, and the calamities of intimacy (B:868–1027, B:3976–4118).** This part is new since the 2007 article. Seeming acceptance can itself be make-believe: a friend uses the right words but treats her as fiction. That pretence shows only at a "calamity", which comes in two kinds. One is the fear of sex with the "wrong sex" (B:913). The other is her presence in sex-segregated intimate space. She is also kept a "dirty little secret", never brought into public (B:931). Three lessons follow. Transition requires "integration in systems of interpersonal spatiality. Without this uptake, gender presentation remains nothing but a disguise" (B:941–943). Inclusive vocabulary may be "nothing more than playing along by another name" (B:949). Pronouns alone do not end misgendering. On sex-segregated space: "the point of citing the calamity isn't to protect females at all. Rather, it is to constitute females as ever in need of protection" (B:3999–4000).

**2.3 Between appearance and reality, not between the sexes (B:976–978).** "Rather than occupying a space in between man and woman, these beings occupy a space in between appearance and reality." This is the book's clearest rejection of in-between and third-category framings of trans women. Neither the beyond-the-binary nor the wrong-body account captures it.

**2.4 Interpersonal spatiality (ch. 2, B:1526–2425; glossary B:9686–9972).** Every encounter, by sight or by speech, can be closer or more distant. Boundaries make intimacy possible, because one can only show what is normally held back. The theory's vocabulary is as follows.
- **Self-display** (moving oneself closer) and **attention** (drawing the other closer).
- **Pathways**: set sequences of gestures. **Strips**: boundaries on one person, ordered by moral weight.
- **Boundary violation** (jumping ahead) and **intimate disregard** (refusing to answer a gesture).
- **Vulnerability**: being more exposed to the other than they are to you, and knowing it.
- **Self-collection**: the feeling of dignity or poise. **Intimate hemorrhaging**: constant exposure. **Intimate deadening**: being unable to be vulnerable at all.
- **Comportment and avowal**: the two capacities that make one an *agent object*.
- **Interpersonal identity**: the "who" whose biography others impute to you.

Boundary systems differ between cultures. Even so, "intimacy and distance are supreme values" (B:1645), so a whole system can be judged abusive. Comparing practices seen from inside and outside, she likens the "who" to the gods in a temple. It exists in practice, while the beliefs that sustain it may be false (B:2245–2260). On avowal: answering "I'm really sad" "by saying, 'No you aren't,' is to be confused about what the first person is doing" (B:1914). This gives first-person authority an ethical basis rather than an epistemic one (developed at B:10955–10978).

**2.5 Ontological pluralism: dominant and trans subcultural meanings (ch. 3, B:2423–2628, 2828–2887).** This is the book's version of the brief's "multiple meanings". The phrase itself is not used. The terms are *worlds*, *overworld* and *underworld*. Bettcher refuses the comfortable claim that transphobes are simply wrong. When someone keeps calling a trans woman "sir", "There's a force to such assertions—an attempt to make her a man" (B:2531–2532). What trans people face is "an entire world of meaning—a system of interpersonal spatiality—that is ultimately annihilating to us" (B:2609–2610). Validity is grounded in resistant worlds that already exist. Her own example: "There I learned from trans women of color that it was possible to be a full and complete woman without surgical or even hormonal intervention" (B:2677–2679). In one such practice "trans woman" is a basic term. A trans woman is a paradigm woman, and "There's a fact of the matter whether she's a woman. And the fact is she is" (B:2865–2866). A penis "goes absolutely no distance at all in detracting from womanhood any more than does wearing a blue hat" (B:2870–2871). She gives resistant worlds an *ethicopolitical* priority, not an ontological one. There is even a consolation: "there are good reasons for not wanting to be that sort of thing in the overworld anyway" (B:2621–2622). She says she is describing existing practices. She is not proposing reforms to how the overworld speaks (B:2643–2706; B:10235–10263).

**2.6 Moral sex and the physical person (B:3066–3189; ch. 4, B:3478–3834).** In the dominant ("folk") system, "man" and "woman" do not name biological kinds. Fictional aliens can be men or women, and *man* and *woman* existed in Old English before *male* and *female* (B:3155). They name kinds of interpersonal object, sorted by boundary structures: *moral sex*. The physical person is "the person one has rather than is" (B:3481). Clothing is "the uniform of personhood" (B:3562). Later she calls it "the most basic and essential technology of personhood we've ever had" (B:5177–5178). The "naked body under the clothes" is a myth. Nakedness is a social mode of appearance, not a thing hidden underneath (B:3591–3686). Because clothes are read as revealing the body, a trans woman's clothes are read as a disguise. "Through this change in intimate meaning, clothing becomes a disguise" (B:3941–3942). *Deception* turns out to mean only that "the trans person is retroactively regarded as failing to disclose the pretense" (B:3969–3970), and that happens after the fact, when she is exposed. *Moral genitalia*, the genitals one is "entitled to", explain why reality enforcement continues after surgery (B:4331–4368). An ambiguous presentation brings a third shape: "being abusively called an 'it'—a form of misgendering and, in this case, also an explicit denial of folk personhood" (B:4408–4409).

**2.7 Against the wrong-body model (ch. 5, B:4810–5299; ch. 6, B:5777–6289).** The wrong-body account (WBA) has three parts: an inner sense of self at odds with outer appearance, that inner sense being innate, and innateness proving one's sex (B:654–657). She argues against the first two. Some trans women live their lives as women and never change their bodies. Recoding practices show that phoria is about interpretation. Constructionism cannot then explain dysphoria either. Her conclusion: "it simply won't make any sense that one can possess an innate sense of one's moral sex" (B:5264–5265). She allows that the body one is born with may shape one's "affective makeup" (B:5255–5263). She sets aside the usual causal question: "The question isn't 'What's wrong with me?' The question, rather, is part of a more general 'What the hell is going on here?'" (B:5280–5282), and "A better question is why it doesn't arise for others" (B:5296–5297). Ch. 6 is generous to the WBA in use. Trans people use it to turn reality enforcement around ("really a woman, trapped in a misleading body", B:6180–6181), and this is real resistance (B:6128–6182). It is also "resistance predicated on privilege" (B:6212): surgery costs money, the WBA makes surgery the goal, and other ways of being trans fall outside it. The counter-narrative she likes: "I shall say that I was a boy who grew up to become a woman" (B:5906–5907), which "is much like a conversion narrative" (B:5909). Retelling one's past does not require the claim to have always been a woman.

**2.8 Self-recognition, the blank open, the call (ch. 5, B:5300–5717).** A familiar experience: in sanctioned make-believe (Halloween, a mother's dress) one suddenly "sees oneself", and then loses it when the game ends (B:5394–5412). "Far from deluded, I say, self-recognition is a profoundly perceptive apprehension" (B:5493–5494). What it grasps is that "the make-believe needn't be make-believe" (B:5496–5497). In the "blank open", the meaning of one's clothes is left open, and that indeterminacy protects. One is a "proto-object" (B:5606), in a "germinative stasis" (B:5618). The feeling is "almost as if one were visited by oneself from an otherworldly future" (B:5628–5629), and what that summons demands is *realization*: becoming an interpersonal object inside real networks. Solitude cannot finish it: "To face oneself is to take heed of the call, to open up to others, to seek the relational sustenance on which any reality depends" (B:5715–5716). The notes tie this to van Gennep's rites of passage (separation, margin, reaggregation) and to Turner's liminality and *communitas*. Bettcher says self-recognition is like *la marge* "without the ritualistic components" (B:10737–10800).

**2.9 World-travel and life in the House-equivalent (ch. 6, B:6330–6697).** Realization comes by moving into a resistant world. It does not come from rewriting one's identity in the overworld: "world-travel does not lead to the overwriting of a previous identity. The previous identity continues on in the overworlds one has left" (B:6337–6339). In the resistant world "what had been a disguise or costume becomes proper appearance simpliciter" (B:6359–6360). Clothing no longer announces the body, and "learning about a person's intimate appearance becomes part of the negotiation toward intimacy" (B:6383–6384). Saying "I am a woman" in the first person works like a performative self-naming or an avowal (B:6421–6422). It can be a deep self-revelation, or a polite refusal to share. Either way, "the addressee of the avowal must tacitly refrain from imposing their own interpretation of the gender term" (B:6447–6448). Bettcher is pessimistic that the overworld can be changed (B:2694–2706), and does not seek realization there (B:6290–6329).

**2.10 Complex vulnerability and recognition among one's own (B:3360–3412, 6616–6697).** Telling someone one is trans can land either as an intimacy or as an admission of being "really" the other sex, and one cannot know which in advance. One is aware of oneself as "a potential one that has yet to be received" (B:3380). Only someone who understands that complexity can receive it. Most failures of intimacy come from people who know only one system (B:3384–3392). *Infraintimacy* is the saving grace: an oblivious perceiver never truly sees one, so something stays untouched (B:3393–3412). Lugones's "complex communication" (B:6637–6697) is a creative, "third-order" intimacy across different margins.

**2.11 Coloniality, sexism and the "dignity of man" (ch. 7, B:6703–7550).** Imposing clothing and nakedness on colonised peoples was itself an imposition of a system of intimacy. It is tied to land theft (B:6840–6966) and to erasing what artefacts had signified, such as "rank, religion, age" (B:6940–6942). Two origin stories of nakedness are told. In the older Christian one, "nakedness bears the mark of sin, becoming an object of religious shame" (B:7130–7131), and Eve is the temptress (B:7124–7160). This one is medieval in shape. The secular, racialised "primitive" story is colonial and modern (B:7161–7247). On sexism: "the so-called dignity of man is merely the effect of differentially rendering women vulnerable" (B:7403–7404). Bettcher qualifies Lugones. Colonised people *were* called men and women, and "not human in species" is a simplification (B:7266–7300). Moral sex is not universal, but intimacy and distance are, so "man" and "woman" can be reclaimed (B:7426–7475). A note says that ancient Greek and Hebrew systems were also sex-representational, though not colonial/modern (B:11084–11090). On the *joyas* of Spanish California she only asks questions: "Were joyas men who acted like women? Were there women and men at all?" (B:7499–7500).

**2.12 Beyond personhood (ch. 8–9, conclusion).** The philosophical "person" (Locke) and "subject" (the table-gazing model) treat people and things as equally open to access. This *object fungibility* "is crucial in the dehumanization of those who were enslaved and colonized" (B:296–300). It is also built into the old maxim to treat others as subjects and not objects. Her reply: "it's not wrong to treat somebody as an object" (B:8702). Being an object for others is how one displays oneself. The wrong is violation or contempt. Medieval scholastics had no "self" in this sense, and their *persona* stressed individuality and rational nature (B:7736–7754). The book ends by calling for "spaces that move well beyond the catastrophe we call 'personhood'" (B:9683–9684). Throughout, this is a claim about **Enlightenment philosophical concepts**. It is not a claim that people do not matter. Old senses of *person* (Old French *personne*, "presence, appearance", c. 1135; "role, guise", c. 1230) are listed with approval as what the Enlightenment buried (B:2368–2392).

## 3. Relation to the sibling notes

**Serano** (`book_serano_whipping_girl.md`).
- *Agree.* Bettcher's deceiver/make-believer bind is Serano's "deceiver and pathetic" archetypes, analysed structurally. Both are against gatekeeping, and neither makes surgery the gate to recognition. Bettcher's "why it doesn't arise for others" (B:5296) matches Serano's trans-interrogation ("Why do cissexuals exist?"). She uses Serano's account of gradual realisation (B:4836–4839) and her critique of autogynephilia (B:10455).
- *Disagree, directly.* Serano's "subconscious sex" is the *incongruence account* that ch. 5 sets out to refute. Bettcher quotes Serano's claim that her female subconscious sex was not the result of socialisation (B:4991–4996), grants its pull, and denies that there can be an innate sense of moral sex (B:5264–5265). Serano roots sexism in the devaluing of femininity. Bettcher roots it in a dignity/vulnerability asymmetry built into boundaries (B:7403–7404). The two can coexist, but Bettcher says little about femininity.
- *Bearing on the mod.* The Serano note says the Marked seed "is Serano". Bettcher would accept a seed only as an *affective disposition* (B:5255–5263), not as innate knowledge of a sex. See §4 row 7.

**Heaney (ed.)** (`book_heaney_feminism_against_cisness.md`).
- *Agree.* Bey's "confession" questions ("Have you had the surgery?", Heaney p. 161) are Bettcher's genital verification (B:841). Gabriel's point that transition is neither subversive nor reactionary matches Bettcher's two moves: refusing Butler's subversion test (B:4429–4662), and defending the WBA as resistance while criticising it (B:6128–6230). Mitra's warning against idealising trans people matches Bettcher's call to fit trans politics into its right place in a wider struggle (B:299–302).
- *Same exposure.* Bettcher leans on Lugones and the colonial/modern gender system as Kristjansson & Heaney do. Bhatt's charge that gender is treated as a "white invention" therefore partly applies to her ch. 7. She is more careful, though. She calls two of Lugones's claims exaggerations (B:7272–7280), says moral sex can be reclaimed (B:7457–7475), and notes that ancient Greek and Hebrew systems were sex-representational too (B:11084–11090). So she does not say patriarchy began in 1492.
- *Differ.* Unlike Velocci ("perhaps none at all", Heaney note §2.5), Bettcher makes no universalising move. She keeps "nontrans" as the mere absence of a trans identity and declines "cis", which she thinks takes a politics on board uncritically (B:9998–10003).

**Bhatt** (`book_bhatt_chu.md`; `book_bhatt_brown_trans_les.md` as cited in the Heaney note).
- *Agree.* Bhatt's "No Third Sex" and belief A3 match Bettcher's "between appearance and reality, not between man and woman" (B:977–978). Both reject the beyond-the-binary and subversivist accounts as the main explanation of trans oppression (ch. 1 and ch. 4 against Bhatt's "Subversivism"). Bhatt's "No one believes trans women" is reality enforcement as a denial of credibility. Bhatt's "sex under patriarchy is policed" lines up with moral sex being *assigned in the act of perception* rather than read off biology (B:3066–3110).
- *Differ.* Bhatt is a materialist: sex-class defined by extracted labour. Bettcher's base is boundaries and intimacy. She treats labour as allocated *on top of* moral sex (glossary B:9870–9880), so the two can bridge but are not the same. Bhatt insists that transsexuals change sex. Bettcher distinguishes material from moral genitalia and explains why surgery does not end reality enforcement (B:4355–4368). That is compatible with Bhatt's complaint, but says something different.

**Chu** (`book_bhatt_chu.md`, Part II). Bettcher cites Chu's 2018 *New York Times* essay, not *Females*, against the happy before-and-after story (B:5336–5355).
- *Agree.* Chu's "gender is something other people have to give you" matches Bettcher's *realization*, which requires uptake by others (B:941–943). Both reject the fairy-tale ending.
- *Differ.* Bettcher rejects any account that reduces the wish to transition to sexual desire ("libidinal investment", B:5080–5128). That cuts against Chu's sissy-porn thesis. Bettcher gives the first person *ethical* authority in underworlds (B:10972–10974), where Chu calls "valid" tautological. And Bettcher never universalises in the way of Chu's "everyone is female".

**Earlier Bettcher already in the repo.** `book_tsr_2.md` (ch. 23, the 2007 "Evil Deceivers"), `tsq_keywords_2014.md` ("Transphobia": the unpersoning and exposing shapes) and `beliefs.md` H25 ("No shame in the veil", sourced "3W (Bettcher)").
- *What the book adds:* playing along (B:868–1027), ontological pluralism, the interpersonal account of phoria, and the colonial chapters.
- *Correction for H25.* The book does not argue that concealment is a virtue. It argues that "deception" is a charge applied *after the fact*, inside a system that compels disclosure abusively (B:858–866, B:3963–3975). H25 should therefore cite the book for "keeping quiet is not lying", not for "disguise is virtue".

## 5. Where Bettcher should **not** be used

- **Not as history.** The book has nothing on eunuchs, galli, *hijra*, the Middle Ages (beyond one line on scholastic *persona*, B:7736–7754) or any of the mod's regions. Its "folk system" is the colonial/modern Anglo-American system, and Bettcher says moral sex is *not* universal (B:7426–7445). Do not project "the folk system" onto 867 Phrygia, Tang Shazhou or Kirina as if it were the same. Use it as a lens for *writing* the hostile world and the House, nothing more.
- **No colonial-origin story** (rule 2; Heaney note §7). Chapters 7 to 9 concern the Americas from 1492, Atlantic slavery and Locke. Do not import "gender is a colonial imposition" into Badenya, Mālai or Yutai Dao. Bettcher herself rejects the strong version (B:7268–7300).
- **Joyas stay flagged** (H27, H30). Bettcher models the right caution by asking questions rather than mapping (B:7499–7510). Never make the *joyas* (or Two Spirit people, named once in a quote, B:1339) a faith, rite or precedent.
- **"Beyond personhood" is not in-world metaphysics.** No faith doctrine says "there are no persons" or "the self is false". Her target is an Enlightenment concept from 1641–1659 (B:7756–7790). Medieval *persona/personne* senses are period, but any wordplay on them needs a period source first (rule 9 spirit).
- **Ontological pluralism does not make the narrator neutral.** Bettcher's priority is ethicopolitical, and she does not split the difference. The narrator never says both records are equally right. Hostile counts belong to named speakers (rule 3).
- **No third category.** Do not read "between appearance and reality" as an in-between gender. It says the opposite (B:977–978). A3 stands.
- **Sexual material is not for loc:** the "wrong sex" calamity, the "dirty little secret", sex-work analysis (B:1170–1265), sexual recoding practices (B:4119–4163), the de Cuneo letter (B:6995–7010) and the account of slavery (B:7055–7090, ch. 9). Row 4 is built on marriage and the baths only.
- **No suicide or self-harm** (rule 17). A note on aspirational desire mentions suicide in passing (B:10668). Do not use it.
- **Intersex material** (non-consensual surgery, B:4380–4400): not for the mod.
- **"Egg"**: Bettcher uses it (B:6478; B:10952). Rule 21 stands. It is never used in loc except in a daughter's own voice about her own past.
- **Modern words**: "nontrans", "cis", "trans", "dysphoria", "phoria", "realization" and "existential WTF" (B:536) never appear in in-world text.
