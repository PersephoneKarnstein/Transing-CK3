## 0. Framing warning

- The editor sorts the texts by value and puts eyewitness accounts of "living" paganism first. All the authors are Christian or Muslim outsiders, who describe pagan religion "from the perspective of superiority" (pp. 1–2).
- The translations are the editors' own, from named editions; for Saxo, Christiansen's text (p. 177). They are not always exact. A few slips are listed in §3.4. Quote the Latin where it matters.
- The thematic index has an entry **"Priestess"** (p. 532) but **no entry** for eunuch, castration, cross-dressing, effeminacy or gender. A keyword search found one eunuch passage (PVL), one *malakia* gloss, and **one medieval Slavic-area cross-dressing prohibition** (Poznań, §2.51.1).
- Ibn Fadlan's Rus' funeral (§7.2.1, pp. 463–464) is the source of the old woman called "Angel of Death". It narrates the rape and killing of an enslaved woman. **Do not use** it for any mod content (hard rules: no suicide content; it also centres a slave's killing).

## 2. What matters for the design

1. **The women's ash rite is in Saxo, as claimed** (pp. 183, 188). It is the only Rugian rite the text gives to women. The text stresses chance: the lines are drawn "without counting" (*absque supputatione*), then counted. A gate built on it is a chance roll the woman makes herself, without a priest. That differs from the Bædling völva, who sees *for* the seeker.
2. **The 1209 seeress is stronger in the Latin than in the editor's English.** *Habebant ... ducem belli phitonissam* means "they had a pythoness as war-leader". The English "The duke had a fortuneteller for the war" weakens it, and "according to her" mistranslates *ut ferebatur* ("as was said"). A woman seer did lead or guide the army, and the victor killed her first (p. 211). This is a Christian army in 1209, so it is evidence of survival, not of a pagan office.
3. **One medieval cross-dressing prohibition in a Slavic land, at carnival** (Poznań, §2.51.1, pp. 243–244). Levin finds the same carnival context in Orthodox penitentials (`cmp_levin_orthodox_slavs.md`, p. 280 n. 195). Together they show that festival dress-crossing was real enough to be legislated against in fifteenth-century Poland and in Serbian and Russian confession books. Both are *two-way* (men as women, women as men), and both are seasonal.
4. **The head of faith outranks the king** (Helmold 2.108 (12), p. 165), and the priest's mark is long hair and beard (Saxo, p. 185). The PVL shows the other side: Rus' punished pagan sorcerers by pulling out their beards (p. 288). In both Wendish and Rus' sources, the beard carries religious authority.
5. **Goddesses exist but are few.** Siwa (Helmold), Mokoš (PVL 980), the carved goddesses at Riedegost (Thietmar), the Lutici goddess-banners (Thietmar 7.64, p. 74), Fortuna among the "Vindelici" (Malmesbury), Dzyewana and Marzyana (Długosz). No goddess has a named priestess.
6. **Women keepers of the sacred, outside the temple:** the Wolin widow who hid Triglav in an oak (Ebo 2.13); the hearth women of Rügen (Saxo); the 1209 seeress; Libuše the judge-prophetess (Cosmas, Dalimil, Neplach). Every one stands at the edge of or outside the male temple. This supports the 2023 book's reading of women's divination surviving "away from power".
7. **Nothing on eunuchs** except PVL 1089/90, and nothing on gender crossing in any pagan rite. Finding 4 of the reading list holds.

- Bniński's statutes: "Laetare" is rendered "Fourth Sunday of Easter" (p. 245); it is the fourth Sunday of **Lent**. *Columbationes* is rendered "burials", which is doubtful (p. 244).

## 4. Design implications

1. **Gate for a Wendish expansion faith: the ash and the horse, in that order** (2023 note §2.B option 3, now grounded in the primary texts).
   - The candidate sits at the hearth with the House's women, who draw lines in the ash "without counting". Even means the god has seen a daughter (Saxo, pp. 183/188).
   - The temple then confirms with the horse over crossed spears, as Riedegost confirms lots with the horse (Thietmar 6.24, pp. 71–72).
   - Failure at either stage defers to the next harvest feast (Malmesbury: last day of November, p. 110; Saxo: after harvest, p. 185).

   Loc: "Even the women count the lines in the ash." / "The god rode him last night; look, he sweats."
2. **The sieve, as a hostile-court or Polish event, not a gate** (2023 note option 2). Since the primary text calls her *dux belli*, use it as a war event: a Wendish or Polish daughter-seer carries river water in a sieve before the army. If the battle is lost, the victor's men seek her first (p. 211). Options: keep her at the front (morale bonus, death risk) or hold her back.
3. **Office title and head of faith.** Helmold's flamen, of whom "the king is held in lower esteem" (p. 165). The priest's mark is long hair and beard (p. 185). The invented inversion stays as the 2023 note proposed: the priestess keeps "the old length of hair". The README must say this is design.
