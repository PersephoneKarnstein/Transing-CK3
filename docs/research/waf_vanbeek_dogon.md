# van Beek, "Dogon Restudied: A Field Evaluation of the Work of Marcel Griaule" (1991): design reading

Source: Walter E. A. van Beek, "Dogon Restudied: A Field Evaluation of the Work of Marcel Griaule", *Current Anthropology* 32.2 (April 1991): 139–167, with CA✩ comments by R. M. A. Bedaux, Suzanne Preston Blier, Jacky Bouju, Peter Ian Crawford, Mary Douglas, Paul Lane and Claude Meillassoux, and the author's reply. DOI 10.1086/203932.
Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo (JSTOR scan; the two columns are interleaved in the OCR, but every passage used was legible). Page numbers are the journal's.

Not read: Calame-Griaule's rejoinder, "On the Dogon Restudied", *CA* 32.5 (1991): 575–577, and van Beek 2004, "Haunting Griaule", *History in Africa* 31. Neither is in the scratchpad.

This note checks that claim and narrows it. It pairs with `waf_brett_smith_bamana.md`, whose informants "never mentioned the *wanzo*" (Dieterlen's Bambara impurity removed by circumcision). ## 1. The argument in brief

The fieldwork:
- Van Beek worked at Tireli, a village of 1,800 people 9 km from Sanga, where Griaule had worked. He spent April 1979 to July 1980 there, then made eight return visits over ten years (p. 143).
- He worked in Sanga too, with informants from inside and outside Griaule's circle (p. 143).

Griaule's corpus falls into three layers, and they are inconsistent with one another (pp. 139–142):
- the descriptive work to 1947, chiefly *Masques dogons* (1938);
- *Dieu d'eau* (1948, "DE"), Ogotemmêli's cosmology;
- *Le renard pâle* (with Dieterlen, 1965, "RP"), a second, different cosmology.

Van Beek accepts the first layer. He and Paulme's, de Ganay's and Lifchitz's accounts "still" fit the field (p. 145). The second and third layers "proved impossible to replicate in the field, even as the shadowy remnant of a largely forgotten past" (p. 139).

His explanation is not fraud. He describes a fieldwork culture of "the initiated", Dogon and "Nazarene" both (p. 155). Its ingredients:
- Griaule's confrontational, "military" method (p. 153);
- his colonial power and his search for a Dogon equivalent of *mana* (p. 153);
- a few long-paid informants and interpreters (pp. 154–155);
- Dogon courtesy toward high-ranking questioners;
- a Dogon readiness to make new stories "traditional" within a generation (p. 152).

The result is "intercultural fiction" (p. 158): a bricolage of Dogon folktales, neighbouring peoples' tales, the Bible, and Griaule's own astronomy (pp. 156–157).

## 2. What matters for the design (page refs)

### The androgyny material, as Griaule gave it

- **DE's creation** (summarised p. 140):
  - The earth is a female body, and Amma "excised" its clitoris, a termite hill, "thus creating the fox".
  - The Nommo twins are born.
  - Amma makes humans with the help of the Nommo, male and female. They beget "the first eight ancestors of the Dogon, who were still bisexual".
  - Circumcision, excision and the appearance of death "are linked with the creation myth" (p. 140).
- **RP's creation** (summarised pp. 140–141): every being is twinned, "each pair counterbalanced by half a placenta" (p. 140). The rebel Nommo, Ogo, is punished by Amma, who "circumcises him, and reduces him to a fox" (p. 141).

### What van Beek found (the five statements, p. 148, and their detail)

1. **"The Dogon know no proper creation myth."** Neither Ogotemmêli's version nor RP's "is recognizable to informants" (p. 148). Shown the stories, informants said they had "never heard of them" (p. 148). One elder asked whether the tellers had been "present at the creation" (p. 150). Ogo is unknown as a being; the word *ogo* means seniority, wealth and power, and is the title of the village eldest, the *hogon* (p. 149).
2. **The supernatural world is vaguer than Griaule's.** "The water spirit Nommo is not a central figure in Dogon thought and has none of the characteristics of a creator or a redeemer" (p. 148). Nommo is real, but as a feared spirit of open water, often a male-female pair, who "grabs people" (pp. 146, 151). Amma, the high god, and Lebe, the earth spirit, are real (pp. 146, 149). Lebe "does not feature at all" in RP and DE (p. 149).
3. **Symbolism is restricted and fragmentary.** Body symbolism "plays a very limited part" (p. 151). Number symbolism exists in limited form: "three for male, four for female, and eight for the binu", confined mainly to medico-magical rites (p. 148; also p. 151).
4. **There is no *nyama*.** "The crucial concept of nyama, allegedly 'vital force,' is irrelevant to Dogon religion" (p. 148). The word "does not belong to the Dogon lexicon and may be Bambara in origin" (p. 151).
5. **Religion does not pervade Dogon life.**
   - "Twins have limited ritual importance and are not central in Dogon thought or ritual" (p. 148). Dogon consider twins "intellectually slightly inferior", since one intelligence is split between two (p. 152).
   - Blacksmiths and griots "do not function as culture heroes" (p. 148). Their real niche is caste work: "dampening conflicts" and a few rites, such as at stillbirths (p. 152).

**Further points.**
- The fox is only one of many divining animals. Dogon say fox divination reached the area in the second half of the 19th century; cowry divination is older (p. 149).
- The Sirius double star was unknown to everyone (pp. 149–150). Van Beek traces it to Griaule's own reading of astronomy (p. 157).
- Dogon secrets are not esoteric doctrine but "skeleton in the cupboard" shame: past quarrels and witchcraft (p. 150). Bouju agrees independently (pp. 159–160).
- RP borrows heavily from the Bible: Ogo's rebellion, atonement, the Nommo sacrificed upright on a tree with arms outstretched and resurrected, and "eight people saved in an ark" (p. 156). The key informant Ambara had frequented a Protestant mission since youth (p. 157).
- The Bambara myths that Dieterlen recorded have a Near Eastern origin "commented upon by de Heusch" (p. 156 n. 16).
- Griaule predicted Bambara variants of the Dogon system. The Bambara, "as described by Zahan [1974] and Dieterlen [1957]", "never produced an Ogotemmeli" (p. 142).
- **What is genuinely Dogon** in the corpus: ants' nests, the black ant, twins "as a special category (though not that special)", the eight of the binu, three and four for male and female, and "the preoccupation with sexuality and the fascination with objects" (p. 156).

### What van Beek does **not** say

He does not discuss by name the "dual soul" (the doctrine that each person is born with a male and a female soul). Nor does he discuss the claim that circumcision removes the female part, and excision the male. The reading list's wording ("could not corroborate ... the androgyny myths") goes further than the text. What the text supports:
- these doctrines belong to the DE/RP creation corpus that informants did not recognise (pp. 140, 148);
- the creation myth is the frame that "subsumes most of the others" (p. 148);
- "with the demise of the creation myth the whole structure of explanation and correlation in the two texts dissolves" (p. 149).

So the dual-sex soul and the circumcision gloss are **unverified at best**, and stand on the same footing as Sirius and Ogo. They are not shown false item by item.

### The comments

- **Bouju**, after ten years among other Dogon groups, confirms all van Beek's points except two (ancestors and binu) (p. 159). He tested RP at Kolountanga with an old priest and hunters, who found that it "evoked nothing at all". None of the origin myths he collected resembles RP's "central events and personages" (p. 159 n. 2).
- **Lane**, from 1980s work around Sanga, found "no evidence for a concept of nyama or the use of any numerical system for categorising objects". He also found little sign that body symbolism shapes house or village plans (p. 162). He adds that elders use "tradition" to legitimise imbalances "between elders and juniors, men and women" (p. 162).
- **Meillassoux** describes the Griaule research group as "more an initiatory school than a research laboratory" (p. 163). The published materials cannot be critiqued, because one cannot "distinguish between what originates from the informants and what comes from the anthropologist". He has "given up using such materials" (p. 163).
- **Douglas, Bedaux and Crawford** object to method and tone rather than findings:
  - Douglas: the charge is old; who were the informants; are there unexplored esoteric specialists (pp. 161–162)?
  - Bedaux: there is no data to evaluate, and 50 years have passed (p. 158).
  - Crawford: this is a matter of trust, not truth; and what of Rouch's films (pp. 160–161)?
  - None contradicts the field data. Van Beek notes this in reply (p. 165).
- **Blier** points to Griaule's explorer self-image, the war, and Dieterlen's hand in the later works (pp. 158–159). Van Beek accepts that RP was "strongly influenced by her" (p. 164).
- **Reply.** Van Beek frames the test as "recognizability": "a search not so much for truth as for untruth" (p. 165). He says he did explore the specialist secrets of medicine and sorcery (p. 165).

## 3. Corrections to the candidate and existing notes

1. **The reading list overstates van Beek on androgyny.** He does not test the dual soul or the circumcision doctrine by name; he dissolves the creation corpus they sit in (pp. 148–149). Reword finding 6 as: "Van Beek could not find Griaule's creation myths, the creator-Nommo, Ogo, *nyama* or the number system in the field. The androgynous ancestors and the circumcision gloss belong to that corpus and are unverified."
2. **The Nommo is real; the Nommo of Griaule is not.** "Avoid the Nommo" should mean avoiding the androgynous creator-redeemer twin. Do not deny that Dogon fear a water spirit of that name (pp. 146, 151).
3. **The caution extends to the Bambara.** Dieterlen's *Essai sur la religion bambara* (1951) and Zahan's work belong to the same school. Their Bambara myths have a Near Eastern element (p. 156 n. 16) and no independent Ogotemmêli (p. 142). Brett-Smith's informants not knowing the *wanzo* now has company. Cissé 1994 (reading-list item 12, a Dieterlen collaborator) should be read with the same suspicion.
4. ***Nyama* is safe for Mande, not for Dogon.** Van Beek finds it un-Dogon and "may be Bambara in origin" (p. 151). Mande *nyama* rests on McNaughton and Brett-Smith, not on Griaule. Badenya may use it; no Dogon character should.
5. **Vanilla CK3 already ships Griaule.** CK3 1.20, `common/religion/religion_types/00_west_african.txt` and `localization/english/religion/religion_paganism_l_english.yml`, gives the religion that vanilla labels "Mandé" (`west_african_religion`) these names:
   - high god "Amma";
   - water god "The Nummo" (`dogon_water_god_name`);
   - good gods "Yurugu" and "Lébé";
   - devil "Jackal";
   - priest "hogon" and head "Chief Hogon";
   - realm "Ardyenne".

   All are Dogon, not Mande. "Yurugu" as a god and "Jackal" as devil come from Griaule (DE called the fox a jackal; RP corrected it, p. 140 n. 2). Van Beek finds the fox no privileged being (p. 149). Badenya sits inside `west_african_religion`, so **its faith must override every god-name key** rather than inherit `$west_african_high_god_name$` and the rest. Otherwise the game will have Badenya swear by Griaule's pale fox. Amma, Lebe and *hogon* are real, but they are Dogon. Use them only for a Dogon character, never for Badenya.

## 4. Design implications

### What Badenya must not become

- **No androgyny cosmology.** No primordial bisexual ancestors, no twin soul, no "circumcision removes the female part", no creator-Nommo, no Ogo, no pale fox, no Sirius. None may appear as a gate, a doctrine or a faith claim. Even in the faith's own voice they would make Badenya Griaule's, not Mande.
- **No grand esoteric system.** Griaule's lesson is that a coherent hidden cosmology, revealed to one eager outsider, is the very thing the field could not find (pp. 148, 155). Badenya's creed should stay social and attested: a mother's house, a sister's child, a bard's word before a king. That is where `south_reading_list.md` already put it. Do not add a "deep" layer to make it feel profound.
- **Secrets are shame, not doctrine.** If Badenya has secrets, make them Dogon- and Mande-style secrets of shame and quarrel (p. 150; Bouju pp. 159–160). Do not make them initiatory revelations. A good event shape: a jeli knows a shameful thing about the House and can sing it or keep it.
- **Smiths are mediators, not culture heroes.** Van Beek's Dogon smiths and griots dampen conflict (p. 152); Griaule's smith stole fire from the Nommo (p. 140). Badenya's smith's road should carry the first role, which also fits the Mande *nyamakala*. It should never carry the second.
- **The mod is an eager outsider too.** Van Beek's model is a powerful questioner with a thesis, and informants who supply what he wants (pp. 153–155). That is the position of a mod that wants trans women in medieval Mali. The narrator's reconstruction note for Badenya should say so plainly: "No source before 1453 records this. The faith is a reconstruction; the Griaule school shows how easily such things are mistaken for discovery."

### Dating

- Nothing Dogon in this article is dated before 1453.
- The Dogon tell a variant of the Mande migration myth: the trek from Mande to the escarpment (p. 150). That is tradition, not chronology.
- The mask-and-*sigu* complex is said by its own myths to come from Yugo Doguru, not Mande (p. 150 n. 11).
- Fox divination is 19th-century by Dogon account (p. 149). The funeral song cycles are attributed to a 19th-century singer (p. 150).

### Reaction lines: Dogon characters (if the team adds or finds Dogon culture on the Bandiagara escarpment)

These lines are flavour. They rest on 1979–89 fieldwork and are not period evidence.

- **Public harmony, private silence.** The paramount Dogon vice is false accusation, and shame governs speech (p. 150). A Dogon elder will not denounce a daughter in public. Line: "What is said in the men's house cannot be unsaid. Let the House settle it." (The ward's men's house appears in van Beek, p. 148, fig. 6.) Mechanic: Dogon characters never trigger public-mockery events against a daughter. Hostility, if any, comes as private distrust (opinion −5, hidden from the "reasons" list where possible) and never as a scene.
- **A new thing made old.** Dogon readily make foreign elements "traditional" within a generation (p. 152): Noah became Dogon, and the Muslim alms feast became *sadaka*. Line, after a Badenya House has stood in a Dogon county for 20+ years: "The sung name? Our grandmothers had it." Narrator note: "Van Beek observed this habit in the 1980s (p. 152); it is used here as flavour."
- **Do not use:** twins as dim, the *yasigine* ("sisters of the masks", p. 147), the *sigu*, or any mask. These are living institutions with secret parts.
