Source: Boaz Shoshan, *Popular Culture in Medieval Cairo*, Cambridge Studies in Islamic Civilization (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1993; pb 2002). ISBN 0-521-43209-X (hb), 0-521-89429-8 (pb).
Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo. Page numbers are the book's printed pages, taken from the running heads; endnotes are cited as "p. N n. M", where p. N is the page the note is printed on.

**What was read.**

- In part: ch. 4, "The politics and 'moral economy' of the Cairene crowd", for the Fatimid grain and protest passages (pp. 57–63, notes pp. 124–127). Also ch. 5, "Popular culture and high culture", for the Nile plenitude and processions (pp. 72–75, notes pp. 131–133).
- The whole extraction and the index were searched for *mukhannath*, *muʾannath*, transvestite/transvestism, effeminate, *khawal*, mask/*samāja*, *khayāl*, singer, prostitute, eunuch, disguise and Fatimid. Chs. 1–2 (Sufism; al-Bakrī's *sīra*), the Introduction and the Appendix were not read beyond these hits. None of them has Fatimid material on gender.

**Relation to existing notes.** `later_starts/mukhannathun_fatimid_cairo.md` §3.1 and §6 Q2 and `later_starts/region_islamic-world.md` §2A and §5 Q1 all ask whether Shoshan attests cross-dressing at a *Fatimid* Nawrūz. This note answers that question (§1.1). Shoshan is a Mamluk historian. Almost all of his evidence is 14th–15th-century, so every claim below is dated by its primary source.

**Answer in brief.** **No cross-dressing; but see the correction below.** Shoshan attests no cross-dressing at any Nawrūz between 969 and 1171. His "1188" passage, read in the Arabic, turns out to describe *muʾannathūn* performing before the Fatimid caliph in the dynasty's past, dress unstated. His Egyptian cases are:
- **913**, under an ʿAbbasid governor, from al-Kindī;
- **1188** in date of writing, from al-Qāḍī al-Fāḍil via al-Maqrīzī, where the key word has a textual variant. **But see the correction below: the passage describes the Fatimid past.**

**Checked against the Arabic (2026-10-26, consolidation pass).** The "1188" passage is al-Qāḍī al-Fāḍil's *Mutajaddidāt* for 14 Rajab 584 (*Khiṭaṭ*, ed. Sayyid, **1:728**; Būlāq/Wiet I:269). He is describing how Nawrūz went "in Egypt in the days past and **the departed dynasty** (*al-dawla al-khāliya*)", that is, **under the Fatimids**, whose chancery he had served. The *muʾannathūn* and *fāsiqāt* gather under the Pearl Palace "where **the caliph** watches them", instruments in hand *(trans.)*. In 1188 no caliph sat in Cairo. So this is a **retrospective, hostile account of Fatimid practice**, not an Ayyubid one. Sayyid's text reads *al-muʾannathūn*; his apparatus gives Būlāq's *wa-yajtamiʿ al-mughannūn* ("singers") as the variant. The same passage also puts the **Amīr al-Nawrūz** (mock emir, mock taxes on great houses) in the Fatimid past, so the Emir is not only Mamluk-sourced. Dress is not stated, and the period within the dynasty is not dated (al-Fāḍil knew the late, 12th-century court). The Pearl Pavilion was destroyed by al-Ḥākim in 1011–12 but **rebuilt by al-Ẓāhir** (r. 1021–36) and used in the flood season until Badr's vizierate (*Khiṭaṭ* ed. Sayyid 2:529–532), so it stood in 1066.

The undated "Emir of Nawrūz with men disguised as women" in the earlier snippets runs two passages together. Shoshan's Emir of Nawrūz has no cross-dressing, and his sources for it are 14th–16th-century. The men "disguised as female prostitutes" belong to a carnival in **Fez, Morocco**, recorded c. 1900.

## 1. The argument in brief

**Thesis.** The Egyptian ("Coptic") Nawrūz was a carnival in the sense of "a festival characterized by status reversal and riotous revelry" (p. 46). "At least for one day every year, ordinary people 'turned' their universe 'upside down'" (p. 49). Shoshan tests three readings against it: the safety-valve, Gluckman's ritual of rebellion that strengthens order, and Bakhtin's ritual of equality. He leans on Natalie Davis: rites of reversal "can renew the system, but they cannot change it" (p. 48, quoting Davis). The authorities banned it again and again, and it had disappeared from Cairo by about 1400, while surviving in Upper Egyptian villages into the 20th century (pp. 49–51).

**Date and calendar.** Egyptian Nawrūz fell on **1 Thout (Tūt)**, the Coptic New Year, at the Nile's crest, when vintage and date harvest ended (p. 42). Shoshan gives "11 September", which is the modern Gregorian equivalent. For 1066 the Julian date is **29 August** (see §2).

### 1.1 Cross-dressing: every case in the book, with its source and period

|---|---|---|---|---|

| 300/913 | ʿAbbasid governor (after the Tulunids, before the Ikhshidids) | On Nawrūz and Mihrajān the governor "ordered transvestites (*mu'annathun*), who were dressed in 'their special costume' (i.e., women's garb?)" and playing instruments to be marched round the old mosque of Fusṭāṭ. | written 584/1188, describing the **Fatimid** past | Fatimid (retrospective; see correction above) | "In 1188 transvestites and prostitutes (*fasiqat*) gathered under the Pearl Palace, carrying musical instruments", to be seen and heard by "the Egyptian ruler". | al-Maqrīzī, *Khiṭaṭ* I:269, quoting al-Qāḍī al-Fāḍil | p. 43; nn. 28–29, pp. 112–113 |
| c. 1300 | Mamluk | Ibn Baydakīn al-Turkumānī's tract against innovations "condemned transvestites". Not tied to Nawrūz. | *Kitāb al-lumaʿ*, ed. Labīb (1986), I:93 | p. 113 n. 28 |

**The 1188 reading is not secure.** Shoshan's word is *muʾannathūn*, which he glosses as *mutakhannithūn*, "those imitating women", after a 14th-century fatwa by al-Adhraʿī (d. 1381). Wiet's edition of the *Khiṭaṭ* translates the word as "mignons", and the Būlāq edition reads ***al-mughannūn*, "singers"**. Shoshan calls that reading "probably erroneous (or is this word a euphemism?)" (p. 112 n. 28). His own verdict on the evidence: "Transvestism in medieval Islam is a phenomenon we know little about, and information seems meagre" (p. 112 n. 28).

**Where the "men disguised as women" come from.** Three passages, none of them Fatimid:
1. **The Fez carnival.** "A fat qadi … accompanied by two or three scribes and three or four men disguised as female prostitutes" accuses dignitaries of unpaid debts (p. 45). The source is Westermarck, *Pagan Survivals* (1933), describing Fez in Muḥarram in the late 19th and early 20th centuries (p. 115 n. 51). Shoshan offers it only as a parallel to the Emir of Nawrūz.
2. **European carnival in general.** Transvestism usually meant "the man being disguised as a grotesque, cavorting woman" (p. 46). The sources are Burke and Davis on early modern Europe (p. 117 nn. 64–65).
3. **Christmas-to-Epiphany feasts in Europe.** "Masks, men dressing up as women" (p. 47). The source is the French Feast of Fools (p. 117 n. 70).

**The Emir of Nawrūz has no cross-dressing.** Shoshan calls the procession "the high mark of Nawruz in medieval Egypt", but gives it no date (p. 43). The crowd elects him. He rides naked, or in red or yellow, with his face whitened, a false beard and a palm-leaf *ṭurṭūr* cap, on a donkey. He carries a register and extorts "debts" from officials, breaking gates and soaking doorsteps (pp. 43–44). The description is "a composite" drawn from Ibn Iyās's *Badāʾiʿ* (d. c. 1524), citing al-Maqrīzī under 787/1385, and from Ibn al-Ḥājj's *Madkhal* (d. 1336) (p. 113 n. 34). It is Mamluk evidence and cannot be dated earlier.

Notes on the table:
- **The *khayāl* reading for 975 is a conjecture.** The *Khiṭaṭ* has *ḥīla*. Casanova read it as *khayāl* and Wiet doubted that reading. Shoshan supports it by the pair *khayāl* and *samājāt* in al-Musabbiḥī under 415/1024 (p. 113 n. 31). The 1024 pageant was not a Nawrūz (see §4).
- **The mock elephants** may mock the two real elephants in al-Muʿizz's procession two years earlier (Wiet's suggestion, p. 113 n. 32).
- **The "1023" date is off by a year.** That is shown in §4.

**What is *not* Fatimid in the rough play.** Most of the colour comes from later sources:
- egg-throwing, leather-slapping, highway "ransoms", murders left unpunished, and soaking dignitaries (pp. 42–43; *Madkhal*, *Badāʾiʿ*, *Ṣubḥ*: 14th–16th c.);
- the water games in which "naked bodies could be seen through clothes" (p. 43; *Madkhal* II:51–52, 14th c.);
- teachers thrown in fountains (p. 43; *Madkhal* II:49–50).

According to al-Maqrīzī, egg-throwing and slapping were "first introduced in the year 1196" (*Khiṭaṭ* I:69; *Sulūk* I:136–137; p. 112 n. 26). That is Ayyubid.

**The one 11th-century description is Iraqi, or of uncertain place.** A passage from *Kitāb al-hafawāt*, attributed to Ghars al-Niʿma (1025–1087/88), describes Nawrūz in Iraq: colleges shut, professors ransomed, Muslims drinking in public, and water thrown so that clinging garments "would reveal the female figure". Its wording is almost identical to the *Madkhal*'s. Shoshan cannot tell whether the two describe one custom, or Iraq or Egypt (p. 113 n. 33, quoting Tritton 1972). For a 1066 setting this is the closest contemporary picture, but it is not Cairene.

**Bans after the Fatimids** (for contrast; p. 49):
- before 1198 (*Sulūk* I:142);
- 1380 (*Sulūk* III:394);
- 1385, when Barqūq abolished the festival, with clubbings, hands cut off and gallows (*Khiṭaṭ* I:269; *Badāʾiʿ*);
- 1435 (*Inbāʾ* VII:369).

### 1.3 Other Fatimid-period material in the book

- **Grain and Byzantium.** In 1054 or 1055 al-Mustanṣir asked Constantine IX for 400,000 *irdabbs* of grain, "but the Christian ruler died before the transport left" (p. 125 n. 83; Ibn Muyassar p. 13). In the same years merchants paid peasants in advance for future harvests (p. 59; *Ittiʿāẓ* II:226). Fatimid grain reserves normally exceeded 300,000 *irdabbs* (p. 58).
- **Crowds and the caliph.**
  - In 1008 Cairenes protested to the caliph over the bread (p. 62; *Ittiʿāẓ* II:74).
  - In 1023, a low-Nile year, they went to the Muqaṭṭam with Qurʾans to pray for the flood (p. 62; *Ittiʿāẓ* II:134–135).
  - In the famine of 1024, al-Ẓāhir was "cursed by one man of noble descent" while riding through Fusṭāṭ. The man was punished and grain prices were fixed (pp. 62–63; *Ittiʿāẓ* II:164–165; p. 127 n. 100).
- **The Nile plenitude.** The festival "is first recorded in Fatimid sources" (p. 73).
  - The caliph led the perfuming of the Nilometer (p. 73). Sanders dates that ceremony after 1092; see `isl_sanders_ritual_fatimid_cairo.md`.
  - At Dār Ibn Maʿshar, a public stand by the dam, seats were rented to spectators until it collapsed in the 12th century (p. 73; *Ittiʿāẓ* III:232).
  - A new stand was built in 1124–25 and burned in 1163 (p. 73; Ibn Muyassar p. 97).
  - "About 1,000 boats" sailed in the Fatimid period (p. 132 n. 72, *Ṣubḥ* III:517, a Mamluk source).
  - Merry-making, wine and "sexual promiscuity" at the Nile festival are reported only from Mamluk chronicles (p. 73; p. 132 n. 70).
- **Mamluk evidence only.** No *mukhannath*, *khawal*, eunuch or singer occurs in the book's Fatimid material. Singers appear only in Mamluk processions (pp. 57, 73, 74–75).
