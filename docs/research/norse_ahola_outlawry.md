## 1. The argument in brief

- **Outlawry is lawful revenge.** With no executive power, the plaintiff carries out the sentence. "Outlawry was essentially a juridical approval of blood revenge" (p. 103). It narrows the legitimate target to one person and so stops the feud from spreading (p. 103). It is both a collective rejection and a private act in a two-party feud (p. 104). The harshness of the penalty pushed people to arbitrate (p. 102).
- **Forms.** Full outlawry (*skóggangr*, "forest-going") means loss of property and all rights, civil, family and church: no support, no passage abroad, killable by anyone (pp. 85-86). Lesser outlawry (*fjörbaugsgarðr*) is three years abroad, a "life ring" fee, and three summers to find a ship, staying meanwhile among three named homesteads a day apart, with immunity within a bowshot (pp. 88, 98-99). District outlawry (*héraðssekt*) keeps rivals apart and is usually agreed rather than judged (p. 88). The confiscation court is held two weeks after the assembly. In *Hrafnkels saga* a man is not fully outlawed until it is held (p. 97).
- **The pursuer carries a burden.** Only the plaintiff is *obliged* to kill the outlaw, and releasing him is itself outlawable (p. 98). The sagas mock plaintiffs who fail, yet they also praise ceremonies of release and show "countless" chieftains protecting outlaws (p. 98). Harbouring has limits: "endlessly harboring outlaws would become impracticable for a chieftain, whose power ultimately depended on his popularity" (p. 97). Björn Hítdælakappi, summoned for aiding outlaws, settles for a fee (p. 213).
- **The way back.** Outlawry is negotiable after the verdict. Kolr rides round the district winning sympathy, raises the compensation, and is pardoned without the chieftains and against their will (*Flóamanna saga* 19, p. 100). A law dated to the famine winter of c. 975 rewards an outlaw who kills other outlaws: one kill buys passage abroad, two reduce the sentence to lesser outlawry, three bring full acquittal. It does not apply to thieves or murderers (p. 100). Víga-Glúmr's son hides in his father's house while people believe he is abroad, and his sentence is later cancelled in a settlement (p. 211).
- **Three endings.** *Tragic*: hunted and killed. *Fortunate*: escapes abroad or has the sentence mitigated. *Pioneering*: settles a new land and is honoured afterwards, like the settlers of Iceland, many of whom left Norway as outlaws (p. 106). The two schemas are offence, (arbitration), sentence, (flight), death, (revenge), and offence, (arbitration), sentence, avoiding the pursuers, (re-establishment) (pp. 189-90). "The tragedy of outlawry therefore turns out to be an opportunity" (p. 190).
- **The outlaw sagas.** *Gísla saga* is a failed sworn-brotherhood that destroys everyone in it. Gísli is outlawed when absent from the assembly, is pinned to his wife's remote farm by a curse, and spends a year on Hergilsey, because the sorcerer forgot to make the curse cover the islands (pp. 108, 252). His sister Þórdís first betrays him, then avenges him and divorces his killer's backer (pp. 108-9). *Grettis saga*: the strongest outlaw, sheltered by his mother, half-trolls and a chieftain's wife, killed on Drangey by witchcraft (pp. 110-11). *Harðar saga*: a chieftain's son leads as many as two hundred outlaws on the islet Geirshólmr and raids nearby farms. The band falls through treachery when a feigned friend lures them ashore in small groups. Hörðr's sister Þorbjörg organises the revenge, twenty-four men killed, though he had once attacked her own farm (p. 113).
- **Outlaws are seldom alone.** Ahola divides society into a *public field* (the assembly, contract and law, male) and a *private field* (kin and the farm). The outlaw is cut from the first but not the second: "The private field consisted of those relations that an outlaw could expect to be hospitable in spite of his outlawry" (p. 197). Help from kin must be *motivated*, since the law forbids it. That conflict is the dramatic centre of *Gísla saga* (p. 213).
- **Outlawry is feminising.** "Conceptually, outlawry was a conviction that was feminizing" (p. 195). It strips a man of the assembly, which was a male right. The *kolbítr* youth who lies in the kitchen is part of the outlaw-hero's pattern (pp. 195-96). "As a feminizing conviction, outlawry seems to have brought the sentenced men closer to the female sex" (p. 199).
- **Women are outlawable, but the sagas show none.** Grágás: "A woman is outlawed to the same extent as a man if she slays or injures a man or a woman" (p. 198). Yet "the outlaws in the Family Sagas are all men" (p. 198). Women were barred from speaking at assemblies and, after the failed suit for Arnkell goði, from bringing manslaughter cases (p. 199 and n.). Ahola reads Auðr's revenge ride from a shieling in *Laxdæla* 35 as touching the motif, through "the arguable association of outlawry with the female sex" (p. 254).
- **Women shelter outlaws.** "Women tend to assist outlaws in the Family Sagas even without family ties to the outlaw" (p. 227). Gísli's wife Auðr strikes the bounty-hunter with his own purse ("Take this, together with shame and disgrace", *Gísla saga* 32, p. 225) and later clubs him in the final fight. Þórdís promises to give Gunnarr up in the morning, then fills the farm with her kin overnight (p. 225). Grettir's mother Ásdís arms him and gives him refuge again and again, and Grettir's verse says "a mother is best for a child" (p. 221). In myth, the giantess Gríðr arms Þórr in hostile country (p. 322).
- **Foster-mothers have power.** "Foster mothers are attributed with supernatural gifts remarkably often" (p. 221). Búi's foster-mother Esja leads him to a furnished cave with a warm pool (pp. 223, 250). Hrolleifr's mother gives him a shirt that weapons will not bite and threatens a landslide on his pursuers (p. 222).
- **Helping the outlaw moves honour.** Any help that thwarts the pursuers raises the outlaw's family honour "at the expense of his pursuers' honor". A chieftain can gain honour by protecting an outlaw who is not his kin (p. 226).
- **The margins are a map.** Caves, shielings (*sel*) at the edge of owned land, outbuildings and storehouses, cellars, remote farms under glaciers, and islands (pp. 249-55). The outlaw is "conceived as a master, húsbóndi, of the piece of land he happened to occupy", and is usually stronger than his pursuers there (p. 256). Places named after outlaws, such as Grettisbæli and Grímsbyggð, "turned these outlaws into pioneers and associated them with the first settlers" (p. 256).
- **Bands and hired men.** Outlawed chieftain types lead bandit groups and reward loyalty, but Hörðr "has to yield to their wishes" against his own judgement (p. 210). Chieftains hire outlaws as assassins (*flugumenn*). The outlaw is also the "unreliable companion" or masterbuilder figure: a hired outsider whose price turns out to be death (pp. 212, 315-16). Young men prove themselves by killing outlaws (p. 211).
- **Disguise and false names.** Outlaws dress as tramps or in Óðinn's hat and staff, and take names like *Gestr* ("Guest") or *Grímr* ("Mask") (pp. 323-27). Þormóðr buys a famous tramp's clothes to scout his targets (p. 324).
- **Exile can lead to kingship.** In the kings' sagas, exile is "an approved phase of a royal career that occasionally appears to be an almost obligatory stage preceding acknowledged kingship" (Óláfr, Sverrir, p. 401). Icelanders outlawed by the Norwegian king are told sympathetically (p. 393).
- **The conversion and the crown.** Full outlaws were denied burial in consecrated ground. Grettir leaves a sack of bones at a church door and does not go in (pp. 97-98 and n.). After the crown law of 1270, outlawry was imposed by a foreign king's officials. Ahola argues that only then could Grettir become a protective hero, "after outlawry no longer embodied the violation of communal peace" (pp. 393-94).
- **On *ergi*.** Ahola says little directly. One note links female sexual "insatiability" to male *ergi*, citing Meulengracht Sørensen and Ström (p. 202). The gender argument rests on outlawry as feminising, not on *ergi*.
