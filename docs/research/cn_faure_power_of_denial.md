## 1. The argument in brief

**Thesis.** Buddhism handled women through three rhetorics that work together:
- **subordination:** five obstacles, defilement, blood;
- **salvation:** you may be saved, after becoming male;
- **equality:** gender is empty, so nothing need change.

Even equality "left the structure of domination intact" (ch. 4, opening). For the design this means a Buddhist neighbour has three ready answers to the Way's daughters, and none of them is "she is a woman".

### 1.1 Women's bodies and the five obstacles
- **The five obstacles** (*wuzhang* 五障): a woman cannot be reborn as Brahmā, Indra (Śakra), Māra, a universal monarch (*cakravartin*), or a buddha (ch. 2 §"Five Obstacles"; Lotus T. 9, 262: 35b–c). The Vinaya version has "Four Kings" and "non-retrogressing bodhisattva".
- **It began as a rank limit and became a judgement on women.** Following Kajiyama, Faure says the list "was at first merely a kind of technical or juridical restriction". Only later was it read as moral and ontological inferiority (ch. 2).
- **The Three Dependences** (on father, husband, son) are Confucian as well (*Yili*). Buddhist texts pair them: "internally women have five obstacles, externally three dependences" (ch. 2).
- **The defiled body.** Early texts call the female body "a vessel of impurity, full of stinking filth" (ch. 2 opening). Daoxuan (the Tang Vinaya master) traces the eight places where one cannot meet the Dharma to "woman" (ch. 2).
- **Chinese reception.** A Chinese verse, "I am ashamed that I did not cultivate virtue in my last life: / And so received this [female] body with its five obstacles" (ch. 2). Faure sources it to the *Cankui yin*; in ch. 4 the *Cankui yin* belongs to the Qing laywoman Tao Shan, so the verse is Qing, not period.

### 1.2 Blood
- **The Blood Bowl sutra** (*Xuepen jing* 血盆經) is "a short apocryphal scripture of Chinese origin". Women who die in childbirth fall into a pool of blood because their blood "contaminated the ground and provoked the wrath of the earth god" (ch. 2 §"Blood Bowl").
  - There is a Daoist version, the *Xuehu jing* 血湖經 (Blood Lake).
  - "The notion of a Blood Pond Hell meant strictly for women seems to appear during the Song period" (ch. 2). Earlier recensions split the sin of childbirth three parts to the man, seven to the woman.
  - **This is in the game window**: a Song-era hostile belief the Way's daughters would meet at funerals.
- **The Way's month-6 "red dragon".** Faure (ch. 2 and n. 77–78) says Daoist women were told to "slay the red dragon", after which "the breasts … become flat and her menstrual blood is purged, and she emerges with a male body". His source is **a Qing text**, *The Queen Mother of the West's Ten Precepts on the True Path of Women's Practice* (Mann 1997; Wile 1992). Ch. 3 n. 42 repeats it as women having to "jump the gender divide". This confirms the Wang Mu note's flag: **the red dragon is late.**
- **Changing the sex of the foetus.** "By the Tang with the Daoist Sun Simiao, and later with the *Furen liangfang*, we already find suggestions as to how one might change the gender of the fetus" (ch. 2 n. 127). This is female-to-male, for lineage. Japanese Ucchuṣma rites did the same, "motivated by patrilineal strategies, rather than by soteriological ambitions" (ch. 3 n. 51).
- **The red and the white.** In Chinese funerary thought the red flesh comes from the mother and the white bones from the father. Secondary burial keeps the bones: "a victory of the white over the red" (ch. 2 §"Red and White"). This is useful for the Temple Societies' burial rites.

### 1.3 The dragon girl and becoming male (ch. 3)
- **Retelling.** Faure follows the Lotus (see `cn_lotus_sutra_dragon_girl.md`): the jewel, then "suddenly turns into a man", then buddhahood in the south.
- **The Chinese text is more modest than the Sanskrit.** In the Sanskrit, "King Sagara's daughter's female organs vanished, and the male organs became visible". "the reference to the girl's sexual organs is missing in the Chinese translation by Kumārajīva" (ch. 3 §"Becoming Male").
- **Salvation and discrimination are one logic.** "even those women can become buddha owing to the power of the Lotus" proves the sutra supreme. The girl stands for "children, animals, and women", all redeemed by the scripture (ch. 3).
- **She never became a woman.** "She never had the time to really become a woman"; being prepubescent, "she is spared the guilt of 'blood defilement'" (ch. 3).
- **Where the change goes.** In the Tendai allegory, going south means becoming yang, "that is, to become male"; the southern world is implicitly "a place without women" (ch. 3).
- **Amida's 35th vow**: "In my land there will be no women; if you want to be reborn in my land, you will become men" (ch. 3, T. 12, 360: 368c).
- **The girl-bodhisattva sutras** are all female to male and all about prepubescent girls (ch. 3):
  - *Asheshi wang nü jing* (T. 337), a girl of 12;
  - *Ligoushi nü jing* (T. 338), a girl of 12 who becomes a boy of 8;
  - *Sumati* (T. 334/335), a girl of 8;
  - *Zhuannüshen jing* 轉女身經 (T. 564), an unborn girl who hears the Dharma in the womb and is born male.

  In Japan the last was "read during women's funerals".
- **Against Schuster.** Faure rejects reading the change as transcendence of gender. Why is "the abandonment of ordinary life for a man … not symbolized by the reverse sex change?" (ch. 3 §"Interpretative Divergences").
- **Chinese commentaries that drop the change.** The *Fahua yishu* (T. 1721) and *Fahua wenju ji* (T. 1719) "downplay … or omit" it (ch. 4 n. 7). The *Sāgara-nāgarāja* sutra (T. 598) has the nāga girl predicted to buddhahood "without any gender change" (ch. 4).

### 1.4 Male-to-female: confirm or correct
**Correction to earlier readings: Buddhist male-to-female changes do exist, but none is a path or a reward.** Faure gives these:
1. **Early Buddhism took bodily sex change in this life as routine, in both directions.**
   - "in early Buddhism, the gender fault-line was easily crossed, but in both directions" (ch. 3 §"Becoming Male").
   - "the possibility of an actual sex/gender change in the present life was routinely accepted on karmic grounds by early Buddhism. It is hardly mentioned in later Buddhist literature" (ch. 3 §"A Feminine Topos").
2. **In Chinese, man-to-woman is a karmic penalty.** The *Shanjianlü piposha* 善見律毘婆沙 (Chinese *Samantapāsādikā*, T. 24, 1462; translated 489, a standard date not given by Faure) "explains … that a man could turn into a woman after committing acts such as adultery". A woman becomes a man when her bad karma is exhausted and she has "a strong aspiration to become a man" (ch. 3 n. 40, after Bapat 1957).
   - This is the closest Buddhist precedent to the Way's daughters, and it is **hostile**: becoming a woman is a demotion for sin.
   - The Vinaya still keeps "echoes of the earlier theory", including monks and nuns "crossing the gender line" (ch. 3 n. 41). Faure points to his *Red Thread* (1998), pp. 77–78, and gives no details here. The *Shisong lü* nun-to-monk case is in `cn_geng_nudan.md`. **The monk-to-nun Vinaya ruling is still unverified.**
3. **The *Vimalakīrti* goddess turns Śāriputra into a woman**, temporarily, as a lesson that "her female state is nowhere to be found because it is empty" (ch. 4 §"Gender Equality in Mahāyāna"). Faure: these early notions of unstable gender "were never developed as a rationale for transsexuality".
4. **Bodhisattvas take female form as *upāya*** (Gadgadasvara "in the inner courts of a king", Lotus ch. 24; ch. 3 n. 49). "Never do we find a female bodhisattva manifesting herself in a male body" (ch. 4).
5. **Wu Zhao's *Dayun jing*** offered "a bodhisattva [who] manifests as a woman to save sentient beings" (ch. 6). This is rule in a female body. It is not a change of body, and the *cakravartin* title she claimed was itself one of the five obstacles.
6. **Outside the window or outside China:**
   - Woman Huang (*Huangshi nü*), reborn **male** as an official: the Ming–Qing morality-book title *Nüzhuan nan shen*, "The Woman Who Was Transformed into a Man" (ch. 2).
   - The Edo *Mimibukuro* entry "Becoming a Man, or a Woman", treated as pathology, not salvation (ch. 3).
   - Deguchi Nao (1892) as a "transformed male" and her disciple as a "transformed female" (ch. 3).

**Bottom line:**
- In Buddhist sources a man becoming a woman is a **punishment** (*Samantapāsādikā*), a **disguise** (*upāya*), or a **reductio** (*Vimalakīrti*).
- No Buddhist text in the window treats it as a holy path.
- The Way's claim is therefore new, and Buddhist neighbours have a ready hostile reading of it.

### 1.5 Nuns: a model for the nüguan and the Houses (ch. 1, ch. 4)
- **The Eight Strict Rules** (*bajingfa* 八敬法). Under them nuns:
  - must ask monks about the rules twice a month;
  - cannot hold or close the retreat without monks;
  - are ordained before both assemblies;
  - "cannot admonish monks officially";
  - must bow to any monk, "even to those that have just been ordained" (ch. 1 opening).

  Faure, after Liz Wilson: the rules hold "buried tributes to her disruptive power" (ch. 1).
- **China's order of nuns was unusual.** "a rather unique institution, managed by women and for women", recruited "from the start" from "daughters of the aristocracy" (ch. 1 §"Evolution").
  - The first convents were in Nanjing in the mid-4th century.
  - **"At the turn of the eighth century, nuns accounted for almost half the Chinese clergy"**, out of some 120,000 (Zürcher).
- **Ordination was irregular from the start.** Jingjian (c. 292–c. 361) was ordained in 357 "by the assembly of monks" only, yet she ran "a prosperous convent". Huiguo and some 300 nuns were re-ordained in 434 before eleven nuns who had come by ship from Sri Lanka. Baochang called the second ordination one "augmenting" the first, not replacing it (ch. 1 §"Ordination").
- **A Tang case: Huichi and Huiren.**
  - **Who:** Tiantai nuns and blood sisters. They criticised the Chan master Puji (651–739).
  - **What happened:** a lay disciple of Puji petitioned to have them censored. Emperor Xuanzong sent Yixing to investigate; Yixing "allegedly paid obeisance to them", and the emperor made "large donations".
  - **Afterwards:** some forty years later their disciple, the nun Benjing, built a nunnery by their grave and commissioned the stele (ch. 1; ch. 4; Faure 1998b; the inscription is by Liang Su).
  - **Use:** a ready-made event of a censor sent against women clergy who is turned.
- **The trajectory.**
  - The female order "flourished under the Tang"; Song Chan nuns were "usually from wealthy families" (Levering).
  - After the Yuan, neo-Confucianism lowered the clergy. In Ming and Qing "convents recruited nuns from the poorest families" (ch. 1).
  - **For stage 2 "estates revert to the crown":** status falls after 1368.
- **Song Chan nuns: robes but no lineage.**
  - Miaocong "even received a purple robe".
  - Dahui (1089–1163) named five nuns and one laywoman among his 54 successors, but "none of them appears in the official lineage" (ch. 4).
  - "Chan epigraphy reveals that a number of eminent nuns reached awakening, but … the stele omits mention of their gender" (ch. 4).
- **Tonsure.** At tonsure nuns "usually adopted nongendered, or rather neutered, religious names" (ch. 1 §"Status"). This contrasts with the Way's naming rite, which gives a daughter's name.
- **"Great man" (*dazhangfu* 大丈夫).**
  - The *Lidai fabao ji* (c. 774, Sichuan) has Wuzhu call his woman disciple a "great male": "why [think of yourself as] 'a woman'?" Her kinswoman "tonsured herself", and both became "a leader among nuns" but were never listed as successors (ch. 4).
  - Faure: an "ambivalent expression of praise, which implies a denial of her sex".
- **Liaoran of Moshan** (9th c.) is the only woman with her own entry in the *Jingde chuandeng lu* (1004).
  - Asked what the occupant of Moshan is like: "(S)he has neither male nor female form". Told "Why doesn't she transform herself?", she answers: "She is not a spirit, nor a ghost. What would you have her become?"
  - The monk Zhixian bows and serves three years as the nunnery's gardener (ch. 4; T. 51, 2076: 289a).
- **The nameless nun and Tangong.** She cites the eight-year-old dragon girl to claim the right to lecture. Tangong: "Can you just make one transformation for this old monk?" She: "Even if one can transform oneself, one is nothing but a wild-fox spirit." He "kicked her out" (ch. 4; T. 51, 2076: 294c). In Chan, then, a woman who transforms is called a fox spirit (cf. `east_sommer_fox_spirit.md`).
- **Nunneries as refuges.** Mostly a Japanese pattern in Faure: convents for widows and orphaned daughters that monks later took over (ch. 1). Faure also says Buddhism was "the ultimate refuge for women who wanted to escape their destinies as spouses and mothers" in China (ch. 4).

### 1.6 Wu Zhao (ch. 6)
- Faure is hostile to "Diana Paul's rehabilitation" of her.
- He gives Bokenkamp's view: Wu "presented herself as 'mother of the realm'"; her "gender is asserted throughout the texts written by and for her" (ch. 6).
- **Nothing on Dunhuang caves, Cave 331 or Cave 96.** The Cave 331 question stays with `cn_zhang_dunhuang_lotus_murals.md`.
