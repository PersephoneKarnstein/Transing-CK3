# Govindacharya, *The Holy Lives of the Azhvars, or The Dravida Saints* (1902): design reading

Source: Alkondavilli Govindacharya, *The Holy Lives of the Azhvars, or The Dravida Saints* (Mysore, 1902). Public domain. The Google Books scan is on archive.org; its DjVu text is in `docs/research/sources_pending/south/india/govindacharya_1902_holy_lives_azhvars_djvu.txt`.
Read from that text, copied to the scratchpad. The OCR is poor, so diacritics are lost or garbled. Page numbers are the book's, taken from the running heads.

**What was read.**
- In full: "Life of St. Āṇṭāḷ" (pp. 41–58) and "The Life of St. Nammāḻvār (St. Śaṭhakopa) and of his disciple St. Madhurakavi" (pp. 191–226).
- In part, from the Introduction: the list of sources (p. xxi); the defence of the 3102 BCE date for Nammāḻvār (pp. lxviii–lxix); and a footnote on sex in the spiritual world (pp. iv–v).
- A search for "nāyakī", "bride" and "female" across the whole book. Its other hits concern other saints and are not used.
- Skipped: all other lives, and the rest of the Introduction.

**Relation to existing notes.**
- `ind_venkatesan_secret_garland.md` gives the scholarly frame. The garland story is "an amalgamation" of hagiographies, "since the eleventh century". The poems do not tell it. Śrīvilliputtūr's Āṇṭāḷ temple is c. 1571.
- `ind_orr_2013_anandakichenin.md` dates the *Guruparamparāprabhāvam* family: the 6,000 version by Piṉpaḻakiya Perumāḷ Jīyar, and the 12,000 version c. 16th c.
- This note records what the **late, Guruparamparā-based tradition** says, as Govindacharya retold it for English readers in 1902.

**Grade of everything below: late hagiography, retold by a modern devotee.**
- **His sources** (p. xxi):
  - *Divya-sūri-carita*, which he gives to Garuḍavāhana Paṇḍita, "contemporary and disciple" of Rāmānuja;
  - *Prapannāmṛta*;
  - *Guru-paramparā-prabhāva*;
  - Vedānta Deśika's *Prabandha-sāra*;
  - Maṇavāḷa Māmuṉi's *Upadeśa-ratna-mālā*;
  - *Paḻanaṭai-viḷakkam*.
- **His Āṇṭāḷ origin story** comes from a *sthala-māhātmya* he bought at Śrīvilliputtūr in March 1901 (p. 55 n. 1).
- **His dates are devotional.** Āṇṭāḷ is born in "Kali 97" (p. 45). Nammāḻvār is born 43 days after Krishna left the world, in 3102 BCE (p. 192). He defends this date against scholars (pp. lxviii–lxix).
- **His framing is Victorian-Christian.** Āṇṭāḷ's merging into the image is "like Christ's Resurrection" (p. 55). Nammāḻvār's psalms are "superior to David's" (p. 194 n.). Kālidāsa, Schiller's *Maid of Orleans* and Samuel Rutherford are quoted in the notes (pp. 49–50, 52).

## 1. Āṇṭāḷ (Goda), pp. 41–58

### 1.1 The life as told

- **Her divine descent.** She is the earth goddess (Bhū) come down, near kin to Nīḷā (Nappiṉṉai). She is "the only one" woman among the twelve saints (pp. 41–44).
- **Found in the earth.** Viṣṇucittaṉ (Periyāḻvār) grows *tulasī* and weaves daily wreaths for the Vaṭapatraśāyī ("Sleeper on the Fig-leaf") of Śrīvilliputtūr. He finds the baby in the soil of his garden, "just in the manner that king Janaka discovered Sītā" (p. 44). He names her Godā, "of the sweet curls" (p. 45). A note glosses Godā as "she who presented her garland of hymns to God" (p. 45 n. 1).
- **The worn garland** (pp. 45–47).
  - In her father's absence she wears the wreaths meant for the god, looks in a mirror and asks whether she matches him (pp. 45–46).
  - Viṣṇucittaṉ catches her, scolds her, and sets the flowers aside as unfit, so the god goes without that day (p. 46).
  - **The dream is his.** The god asks why there were no flowers, and answers: "rather say consecration!" He will have no other wreaths but hers (p. 46).
  - The father then worships her as the god's spouse and brings the god her worn garlands. She is named *Cūṭikkoṭutta Nācciyār*, "the Queen that wore and gave" (pp. 46–47).
  - **There is no hair in the garland.** He is told by the god, not by the temple.
- **The vow and the songs** (pp. 47–48).
  - Love-sick, she "began to imitate Krishna's exploits". She did this like the cowherd girls of the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa*, who played at being Krishna: "See, I am Krishna's self" (pp. 47–48).
  - She keeps vows (*vrata*), ablutions and invocations to Kāma. The *Tiruppāvai* is "the Sacred Vow" and the *Nācciyār Tirumoḻi* is "the Queen's Blest Speech" (p. 48).
  - **The month is not given.** Govindacharya does not mention Mārkaḻi.
- **The refusal** (p. 49). Asked which mortal she will marry, she answers with NT 1.5, glossed so that her life would leave her if she were promised to a man. That breaches the brief's suicide rule, so it is not used.
- **Choosing the god** (pp. 50–51).
  - Her father names the 108 shrine-lords for her: Pāṇḍya, western, northern, the "Lords of Tonda Circle", Śrīvilliputtūr and Tirumāliruñcōlai.
  - He ends with Raṅganātha of Srirangam, and her heart chooses him (NT 11.2).
- **The second dream, and the procession** (pp. 51–53).
  - Raṅganātha tells the father in a dream that he will ask for her himself.
  - A "Divine afflatus" seizes the Srirangam priest, who sends the god's insignia (umbrellas, fly-whisks, fans, maces) to bring her.
  - She is carried in a screened litter, "for no more dare sinful mortals gaze" on God's bride (p. 52). The Pāṇḍya king is among the crowd (p. 53).
- **The ending** (p. 54).
  - She steps to the god's feet, which earns her the title "Keśava's foot-serving maid". She mounts the serpent-bed and "had passed into Him", no longer visible to "mortal ken".
  - The god honours the weeping father as his father-in-law and sends him home.
  - **This is the disappearance ending.** The brief forbids it, and the Venkatesan and Orr notes already reject it.
- **The temple's origin story** (p. 55 n. 1; the 1901 *sthala-māhātmya*). Bhū asks to be born as Viṣṇucittaṉ's daughter. The Lord agrees: "we shall be pleased to wear the flowers rendered fragrant by your wearing."

### 1.2 What this late version adds or changes

1. **The garland story is told whole, with the father's dream at its centre.** This confirms the Venkatesan note's correction: no hair, and someone else dreams the god's preference.
2. **There are two dreams, and both are the father's.** Āṇṭāḷ's own dream wedding (NT 6, *Vāraṇam āyiram*) is **not** in the narrative. The hagiography gives the dreaming to the man and gives her the litter and the merging.
3. **The girl played the god.** The cowherd girls "imagined ... to be herself Krishna" (pp. 47–48). This is girls taking a male god's part in play, not a gender crossing. Under the brief it is at most reaction data.
4. **Srirangam sends a fly-whisk retinue** (pp. 51–52). In this tradition the honours Orr's temple women bought (the fly-whisk, a place in procession) are the god's honours for his bride.

## 2. Nammāḻvār (Śaṭhakopa, Parāṅkuśa), pp. 191–226

### 2.1 The life as told

- **His names** (p. 191 n. 1).
  - *Śaṭhakopa*, from his conquest of the *śaṭha* "wind" that dulls souls at birth.
  - *Māṟaṉ*, "he who is cross to the ways of the world".
  - *Nammāḻvār*, "our own saint", a title given by Raṅganātha.
  - *Parāṅkuśa*, "Victor over false faiths".
  - *Vakuḷābharaṇa*, "adorned with *vakuḷa* flowers".
  - **"Parāṅkuśa" is glossed only as "victor over false faiths". "Parāṅkuśa Nāyakī" (the girl-persona) does not appear anywhere in the life.**
- **Birth** (pp. 192–197).
  - He is Viṣvaksena, the god's chamberlain, incarnate in the Pāṇḍya land at Tirunagari on the Tāmraparṇī (p. 193). His lineage is that of the local chiefs (p. 194).
  - His parents pray at Tirukkuṟuṅkuṭi. The god promises to be born through them, "bestowing on them as a sign of His Divine favour the holy basil-garland worn by Himself" (p. 196).
  - So in this tradition the **god's worn garland given to devotees** announces his birth.
- **The silent child under the tamarind** (pp. 197–200). He does not cry, feed or speak. He sits sixteen years under a tamarind tree, which is Ādiśeṣa incarnate.
- **Madhurakavi** (pp. 197–203, 225–226).
  - A Brahman pilgrim follows a southern light past Srirangam to the tree (pp. 200–201).
  - He drops a stone and asks a riddle; the youth answers (pp. 202–203).
  - He becomes the saint's disciple, "who knew no God other than his master" (p. 205).
- **Vision and the four works** (pp. 204–206). Viṣṇu appears with Śrī. The saint pours out the *Tiruviruttam*, *Tiruvāciriyam*, *Periya Tiruvantāti* and *Tiruvāymoḻi*, the "essences" of the four Vedas.
  - The shrine-lords "of Tirupati, Kanchi, Srirangam, Melkote" appear to him under the tree (p. 206 and n.).
- **After death** (pp. 208–212).
  - A dog that ate the Vaiṣṇavas' leftovers at Tirunagari goes to heaven (pp. 208–209).
  - Madhurakavi builds his images and sets up daily, monthly and yearly festivals (p. 210).
  - At Madurai the "academy" of 300 poets tests a palm-leaf of TVM 10.5.1 on a floating plank. The plank keeps it and throws off the rest, and the poets submit (pp. 210–212).
- **Doctrine** (pp. 212–214). He quotes Nammāḻvār: Brahmā sits in Viṣṇu's navel, and Śiva ("he who burst the three cities") in his side (TVM verses, pp. 213–214). **This places Śiva inside Viṣṇu's body**, which is Vaiṣṇava subordination of Śiva.
- **The girl appears only in a translated verse** (p. 220). Govindacharya's specimen from the *Tiruviruttam* (verse 67, per his heading) opens: "Lo! here's my Soul—a virgin bride of God". He renders the poem's girl as **the soul** outright.
- **The theology, in the Introduction** (pp. iv–v, footnote).
  - Govindacharya quotes an unnamed writer: "in the spiritual world, we change sexes every moment". The passage continues: "I adore the greater worth in another, and so become his wife."
  - He adds Upaniṣadic cross-references and a jab: "Let those who hate wives in Heaven, first begin to hate their own wives on earth!"
  - I identify the quotation as Emerson's essay on Swedenborg. This is my identification and is *unverified* here.
  - Grade: a 1902 devotee borrowing American Transcendentalism, not medieval theology.

### 2.2 What this late version adds or changes

1. **The nāyakī is absent from the hagiography.** The tradition's life of Nammāḻvār is about silence, the tamarind, the disciple and the plank. The girl-voice lives in the poems and the commentaries, and Govindacharya meets it only as "my Soul". The faith should source the poet's womanhood to the poems and commentaries (Venkatesan p. 32; Narayanan; Ramanujan), never to the life story.
2. **The worn garland is a Vaiṣṇava sign of grace** for him too. The Lord gives his own worn basil-garland to the parents (p. 196), as he takes Āṇṭāḷ's worn wreath (p. 46). The tradition treats the garland *both ways*: what the god has worn is grace, and what the devotee has worn is consecration.
3. **The tradition subordinates Śiva** (pp. 213–214). This is the in-tradition reason why a crossing faith will be resented from the Vaiṣṇava side.

## 3. Corrections to the candidate

1. **Grade the whole Guruparamparā life as late.** Govindacharya's sources run from Rāmānuja's circle, as the tradition claims (12th c.), to Maṇavāḷa Māmuṉi (15th c.) and a temple *māhātmya* bought in 1901 (pp. xxi, 55). The narrator says "the tradition says", as the Venkatesan note already rules.
2. **Āṇṭāḷ's dream wedding is the poem's, not the life's.** Gate step 5 ("I dreamed it, friend") stays on NT 6 (Venkatesan). The hagiography's dreams are the father's. They can feed gate step 4, where *someone else* dreams the god's preference, which matches Govindacharya p. 46.
3. **"Parāṅkuśa Nāyakī" cannot be sourced to the hagiography,** nor to Narayanan (see that note). Source the voice to the poems and to Piḷḷai (Venkatesan p. 32); the name itself is still *unverified*.
4. **The Srirangam merging (p. 54) is the ending the faith must refuse.** Use the retinue and the litter as the god *coming for* her, and drop the merging.

## 4. Design implications

- **Gate step 4, revised wording (faith's voice).** "Her guardian set the worn garland aside. That night the Lord asked him where his flowers were." (p. 46)
- **Decision or event, "The Garland Sent Back".** The god's worn garland is given to a Garland daughter as a sign of favour, as to Nammāḻvār's parents (p. 196) and to the 15th-c. temple woman (Orr p. 175). It can be the morning-after success marker the Venkatesan note proposed.
- **Event, "The Retinue from Araṅkam".** After success, the temple sends umbrella, fly-whisk and fan to escort her home. She is not hidden in a screened litter: **she rides unscreened**, against p. 52. That gives prestige, and a hostile Śrīvaiṣṇava reaction.
- **Name list (faith's epithets).** "She who wore and gave" (*Cūṭikkoṭuttāḷ*). "Foot-servant of the Lord" (p. 54). "Of the sweet curls" (Godā, p. 45). Use them as epithets for Garland daughters, never as names for Āṇṭāḷ's own person in events.
- **Reaction lines.**
  - Śrīvaiṣṇava elder (p. 213): "Śiva lies in His side and Brahmā in His navel. Whose bride did you say you were?"
  - Śaiva poet: "Your Vaiṣṇavas sing the girl and then hide her in a litter."
  - Garland dissenter (from p. 54): "The tradition carried her to the Lord and lost her there. We do not lose ours."

## 5. Conclusion: Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, or a current that crosses both?

**Recommendation: a current that crosses both.** Make the Worn Garland a Tamil bridal-bhakti current that takes its *bride* from the Vaiṣṇava poets and its *daughters, rite and street* from Śiva's temples. It holds that every soul is a woman before the god, whichever god the temple houses. The evidence across the six notes points this way.

| Strand | Vaiṣṇava evidence | Śaiva evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **The bride's voice** | Āṇṭāḷ (9th c.); Nammāḻvār's girl; Piḷḷai's "guise" (13th c., Venkatesan p. 32); Deśika's soul-as-bride (14th c., Ramanujan note §4) | Māṇikkavācakar's *Tiruvempāvai* in the girls' voice; Basavaṇṇa 703 and Mahādēvi 68 (Kannada, 12th c.; Ramanujan note) |
| **"Every soul a woman"** | Govindacharya's "my Soul—a virgin bride of God" (1902, p. 220); Deśika | Mahādēvi 68, "men, all men, are but women" |
| **Daughters of God** | *āḻvāṉ-aṭiyāḷ* at the Kanchipuram Vaikuṇṭha Perumāḷ temple, 1118 (Orr 2000 p. 138); women reciters at Varadarāja, Kanchipuram, 13th c. (Narayanan note) | *tēvaṉār makaḷ*, 850–985, all at Śiva temples (Orr 2000 pp. 52–59); Rudrakanyā, 12th c. (Goodall pp. 110, 121–125) |
| **Initiation and title** | none attested for women | Nandin's staff, *talaikkōli* / *śirodaṇḍinī* (Goodall pp. 110–112) |
| **The Mārkaḻi song women sang** | *Tiruppāvai*: no inscription has women singing it; the Vaiṣṇava hymns were men's (Orr 2000 p. 108) | *Tiruvempāvai*, bought and sung by temple women (Orr 2000 pp. 17–18, 105–108) |
| **The worn garland** | Āṇṭāḷ and Srirangam (Uyyakoṇṭār *taṉiyaṉ*; Govindacharya pp. 45–47, 196) | none |
| **Shared institutions** | Inscriptional form and vocabulary are the same across Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava and Jain temples (Orr 2013 ¶¶2–3, 26). Gaṇikās appear in Pāñcarātra as in Śaiva texts (Goodall p. 107). Rājarāja's 400 came from Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava temples alike (Tanjavur note). | |

Why a crossing current is the strongest choice:
- **A purely Vaiṣṇava faith** would have the bride and the garland, but no women in its temples. The Vaiṣṇava Mārkaḻi hymn and the *Tiruvāymoḻi* were men's to sing (Orr 2000 p. 108). Its girl-voice is, in the tradition's own words, a man's "guise" (Venkatesan p. 32).
- **A purely Śaiva faith** would have daughters, a rite and a women's song, but no bride. The Śaiva "daughter" model is filial, not bridal (Orr 2000 pp. 58–59), and marriage to Śiva is post-12th c. even in the ritual texts (Goodall pp. 125–127).
- **Distinctness.** The crossing is what makes the faith unique among the mod's six. It is also the most honest reading of the theology: the claim that every soul is a woman before the god appears on *both* sides (Mahādēvi; Deśika).
- **The crossing itself is reconstruction.** No medieval sect is known to have joined the two. Name this as a reconstruction in the narrator's voice. Let both sectarian sides attack it: Ben-Herut's Śaiva–Vaiṣṇava court contests (review p. 253) and the Vaiṣṇava placing of Śiva in Viṣṇu's side (Govindacharya pp. 213–214).

### What this does to the gate: the Mārkaḻi vow becomes the *pāvai* vow

- **Name the gate "Keep the *Pāvai* Vow"**, not "the *Tiruppāvai*". Both songs are *pāvai* songs, share refrain and genre, and belong to the same month (Venkatesan p. 19; n. 27 p. 46). The faith keeps the month and lets the woman choose which song to keep it with.
  - The *Tiruvempāvai* is the in-period women's song, sung at the *Tiruvātirai* festival in Mārkaḻi (Orr 2000 pp. 17–18).
  - The *Tiruppāvai* supplies the companions (TP 6–15) and the drum refused (TP 29).
- **Step "Buy a verse"** (Orr note) uses the *Tiruvempāvai*: the right to sing a verse at the Śiva temple's Tiruvātirai, "for her and her descendants" (Orr 2000 NK 134, 1213).
- **Step "Not for the drum"** stays from the *Tiruppāvai*.
- **Step "The staff on the head"** is new (Goodall). The faith's public marking borrows the Śaiva staff-initiation in place of any *araiyar* "Marked" position. This also takes the weight off the *araiyar*, a Brahmin men's right that the Venkatesan note warns against romanticising.
- **Step "The worn garland" stays Vaiṣṇava** (Srirangam, Āṇṭāḷ), and **the dream told to a friend stays from NT 6.**
- **A doctrine choice the player makes: "Whose Mārkaḻi?"**
  - *Tiruvempāvai*: bonuses with Śaiva neighbours and the temple-street line.
  - *Tiruppāvai*: bonuses with Vaiṣṇava neighbours and Srirangam.
  - Keeping both is the orthodox Garland answer.

### What this does to the seat: Kanchipuram, not Srirangam

- **Seat: c_kanchipuram** (d_tondai_nadu). It is:
  - the only "temple-woman center" attested through the whole 850–1300 period (Orr 2000 pp. 139–140);
  - home to a Śaiva temple founded by a Pallava queen with fifteen named women on its staff in 758 (Orr 2013, Text A);
  - home to the Kailāsanātha, where Śiva dances with Nandin (Goodall figs. 3, 5);
  - Viṣṇu's *viṇṇakaram*, sung by Tirumaṅkai (Orr 2013 ¶11), with a Vaiṣṇava temple woman in 1118 (Orr 2000 p. 138) and women reciters endowed at Varadarāja in the 13th c. (Narayanan note);
  - already a vanilla holy site for Vaishnava, Shaiva, Shakta and Smarta alike (map check, reading list).

  No other county says "both" so plainly.
- **b_srirangam becomes a holy site,** "where the worn garland went" (*taṉiyaṉ*, Venkatesan p. 223; Govindacharya pp. 51–54). It is not the seat, because temple women were "conspicuous by their absence" there (Orr 2000 pp. 142–143).
- **Other holy sites.**
  - c_cholamandalam: the four hundred.
  - c_madurai: Putuvai's Vaṭapatraśāyī, Tirumāliruñcōlai, Āḻvārtirunagari and Tirukkōḷūr.
  - c_prayaga: Ilā.
  - Optionally a Śaiva site for Tiruvempāvai/Chidambaram country, if the map allows (to check).
- **Risks.** Kanchipuram is also a living pilgrimage city of both sects. Its holy-site text must stay with medieval temple women and donors, and must not present any present-day community as the faith.
