## 1. The argument in brief

- **Thesis.** Three forces shaped Jewish women's status: the Talmudic heritage, the surrounding society, and the economy. The third matters most. Jews became a "bourgeois or petit-bourgeois society", which "exerted a stronger positive influence upon the status of the Jewish woman than any other factor" (Introduction §1; pp. 1–2, per Baumgarten n. 8).
- **Period.** The core is 1000–1300. Spain is followed into the early 14th century. Ashkenazi sources on women fall silent in the 14th century and return only in the late 14th and 15th (Introduction §3).
- **Ten gains, three losses** (ch. 13). The gains include women's economic role and freedom of movement in Christian Europe, a partial relaxation of the ban on teaching women Torah, and women's right to bless over time-bound commandments. Two of the three losses bear on this mod: **"greater strictures in relation to menstrual laws"**, especially in Ashkenaz, and the hostility to women of philosophers in Spain and Provence (ch. 13 §2).
- **"Silencing of creativity."** Grossman finds no medieval Jewish woman of note among the sages and no Jewish woman mystic. Part of his explanation is the fear of a menstruant touching sacred, and especially mystical, books (ch. 13 §3).
- **Pious and rebellious.** "Rebels" include *moredot* who forced divorce, women who refused immersion, Ashkenazi godmothers who defied R. Meir of Rothenburg, and Rabbanit Bruna, who wore *ẓiẓit*. He asks whether Bruna's act was rebellion or piety, and leaves "the definition of 'rebellion'" as "largely subjective" (ch. 13 §4).

## 2. What matters for the design

### 2.1 The women's synagogue and its prayer leader (ch. 8 §4; pp. 180–182, per Roos p. 17 and Baumgarten n. 14)

- **Three layouts.** Grossman distinguishes three: a women's section inside the building, "as a raised gallery or separated … by some other means"; a separate building next door with no direct passage; and a separate building joined to the men's by "a small and narrow opening".
- **The women's prayer leader worked at the opening.** She "stood near that small window, following the prayer of the men's cantor, and repeated the prayer aloud in a musical voice". In this way the women could take part (ch. 8 §4).
- **"The men in their synagogue and the women in their synagogue."** The *Kol Bo*'s rules for Tisha b'Av have men and women reciting dirges apart, by night and by day. Grossman reads "their synagogue" as a separate building (ch. 8 §4). *Kol Bo* is usually held to be a Provençal compilation (*my addition, not from Grossman; verify*). If that holds, it is a Provençal text for a separate women's synagogue, alongside Hildenfinger's Arles deed of 1452.
- **Worms.** The women's synagogue there was built in 1213 (ch. 8 §4).
- **Named women's cantors.**
  - Richenza, "the prayer leader of women", is in the Nürnberg martyr list (ch. 7 §7; ch. 8 §4).
  - Urania of Worms (d. 1275) was a daughter of Abraham, "the leader of the singers". Her gravestone says she "sang the piyyutim for the women with a melodious voice" (ch. 7 §7).
  - Grossman's reading: they "worshipped out loud and with song in the women's section, and those women who did not know how to read Hebrew prayed after them" (ch. 7 §7).
- **Dulca of Worms** (d. 1196). She arrived early and left late, prayed morning and evening, taught other women to pray "and to embellish the prayer with music", and helped supply the synagogue (ch. 8 §4). She also "regularly came to the synagogue on Shabbat to hear her husband's sermon", which needed "substantial knowledge of Torah" (ch. 7 §§5, 7).
- **Bellette** of Le Mans (11th c.) was "a woman of authority and a spiritual leader of the women in the city where she lived". Rashi relied on her tradition about *ẓiẓit* (ch. 7 §5; ch. 8 §10).
- **Women's language.** *Sefer Ḥasidim* tells women to pray "in a language that they understand" (ch. 7 §7) and to learn the commandments that way (ch. 7 §7). R. Mordecai Kimhi says that in 13th-century southern France "even most of the men do not know how to speak Hebrew" (ch. 7 §7).

### 2.2 Women take on observances and the sages ratify them (ch. 1 §6; ch. 8 §§3, 10)

This pattern runs through the whole book, and it matches the sect's own dynamic: women act first, and the sages concede afterwards.

- R. Isaac Halevi of Worms (11th c.) ruled: "if they wish to undertake the yoke of mitzvot they are permitted to do so, and one does not object". Grossman reads this as "a kind of 'concession' to reality" (ch. 8 §3) and "a kind of retroactive agreement" (ch. 1 §6).
- Rabbenu Tam justified the women's blessing because "they were accustomed to do so", and so treated it as a custom (ch. 8 §3).
- Many Tosaphists, and "leading Provençal sages", held that **all** women of their place count as "important women" who must recline at the Seder (ch. 8 §5).
- **Rabbanit Bruna** "always wore a tallit qatan". Asked why he did not protest, Maharil answered: "Perhaps she will not listen to me" (ch. 8 §10). A 13th-century French sage knew "righteous women" who wore *tefillin* with a blessing (ch. 8 §10).
- **The ceiling.** The godmother (*sandeqa'it*) is the counter-case. R. Meir of Rothenburg fought it because the woman "sits in the synagogue among the men … elaborately adorned". He was "only partly successful" (ch. 8 §4). Women could expand their practice inside the women's sphere. When they entered the men's space, the sages pushed back.

### 2.3 The three women's commandments (ch. 1 §5)

- Grossman gives the Midrash's frame for the three: menstrual law, "alongside the commandments of separating hallah and lighting Shabbat candles", is read "as punishment and atonement imposed upon the woman for her role in the primordial sin in Eden" (ch. 1 §5). This is the hostile reading of the Three Lamps that a worthy would use.
- **Separation went far beyond the Talmud.** Raban: "Today women are accustomed not to cook and not to bake during their menstruation". *Or Zarua* cites Rabyah "in the name of the sages of Narbonne" for barring a menstruant from her husband's table. The same passage adds that "there are women who refrain from entering the synagogue or from touching the scroll". It is not clear whether that sentence also goes back to Narbonne (ch. 1 §5).
- **Lamp against days.** In a late Ashkenazi custom, menstruant women did not say blessings, "even … the blessing over Shabbat candles", although "the sages knew well that this custom was opposed to halakhah" (ch. 1 §5). In the strictest practice, then, the days of separation could silence the lamp's blessing.
- **Women made the stringencies.** "Many of these strict customs originated among the women themselves", and passed "from mother to daughter" (ch. 1 §5). Rabyah: "the women were strict with themselves, and separated themselves at the time of their menstruation" (ch. 8 §4). Grossman calls it the women's own "fear of the holy" (ch. 8 §4; pp. 183–184, per Roos p. 17).
- **The turn in the 15th century.** R. Israel Isserlein let menstruants enter on the High Holy Days, "because it saddened their spirits" to stand outside (ch. 8 §4).
- **A woman teaching niddah.** Maharil's wife Schondlein "wrote a halakhic responsum to a woman who asked her a question concerning the laws of menstruation" (ch. 7 §5).

### 2.4 Provence and Spain

- **Provence leans toward women in halakhah.**
  - Meiri read the Yerushalmi's "lazy" women searching for leaven as slow and thorough, and so better than men (ch. 8 §5).
  - Rabad: "I never heard of permission to punish women with sticks". Meiri ruled that hitting a wife is "totally unacceptable under any circumstances" (ch. 10).
  - Rabad held that a childless woman receives her *ketubah* in a first or second marriage, against Maimonides (ch. 11).
  - R. Mordecai Kimhi records edicts that no man divorce his wife "without her agreement, that of her relatives and that of the seven leaders of the city". R. Moshe Nasi renewed them "in twenty-seven communities in our lands" (ch. 4, "The Situation in Provence").
- **Provence also produced harsh philosophers.** Grossman names philosophy, "mostly in Spain and in Provence", as the source of the worst statements about women (ch. 13 §2).
- **Spain.** Women's religious life there is "a great riddle"; almost nothing is known (ch. 7 §8).
  - R. Jonah of Gerona asked women to pray three times a day, mainly for their sons' Torah (ch. 7 §8; ch. 8 §4).
  - He asked them to appoint one of their number to collect alms "to clothe the poor". Grossman ties this to Spain's "mitzvah societies" (ch. 8 §4). This is a **women's charity office inside the confraternity world**, attested for Catalonia in the 13th century.
  - Spanish sages mostly refused women the blessing over time-bound commandments (ch. 8 §3). They allowed a woman to circumcise only when no man was available (ch. 8 §6).
- **Women in commerce.** In Perpignan notarial records, Emery found twenty-five women lending money, most of them widows, against sixty-one loans by Jews in all (cited ch. 5).

### 2.5 Purim and Dinah

- **Purim.** The book's only Purim passage is from the 1507 ordinances of R. Judah Mintz for the Venetian communities. Men may not dance with married women "except on the days of Purim" (ch. 6, "In Italy"). This shows Purim as the licensed exception to the rules on mixing the sexes. It is post-1453, so it is not period evidence.
- **Dinah the cautionary tale.** Genesis 34's Dinah, who "went out", was "a paradigm for numerous homilies in praise of modesty". The 11th-century *Leqaḥ Tov* says it would have been better for Jacob had she never been born (ch. 5 §1). This is the other Dinah, the one the worthies know best. The sect's Dinah is Berakhot 60a's, turned in the womb.

   - But the Arles deed (lower women's synagogue, 1452) and Grossman's separate-building type (with the *Kol Bo* "their synagogue") agree on a **separate room**.
   - This supports Finding 1 of the reading list. Use "the women's synagogue" ("downstairs" at Arles), not "gallery".
2. **The elder "reads the scroll of Esther" (`tm_hipukh.0010.desc`).**
   - Grossman's period model is a women's prayer leader who repeats the men's reader aloud through the opening, and explains in the vernacular.
   - Whether a woman may herself read the Megillah for women is a halakhic question not covered here (*unverified*).
   - Safer wording: she "stands at the little window and gives the reader's words to the women, and explains them in the common tongue". This keeps the existing "explains it in the common tongue", which Grossman supports (§2.1).
3. **"They keep all three" (`tenet_tm_three_lamps_desc`).** Read literally, this is incoherent for a daughter who sees no blood (see §4.1). Proposed rewording is in §4.1.
4. **README, "The line": "A women's ḥevrah."** Grossman documents no women's confraternity. The nearest are R. Jonah's women's alms-collector in Spain and the women's prayer leader in Ashkenaz. This supports the reading list's recommendation: the sect is a **circle** within a mixed confraternity, with a women's office.

## 4. Design implications

### 4.1 Does a re-embodied, infertile daughter keep niddah? An answer in the faith's voice

**The problem.** Niddah is a status that blood triggers. Without blood it does not arise, so a turned woman who sees none has no days of separation. Read literally, the Three Lamps tenet promises something the body cannot give.

**The answer the sources allow.**
- (a) Halakhah already knows women who are fully women and see no blood. The Mishnah counts the *zekenah*, the old woman whose periods have stopped, among the women whose "time suffices" (m. Niddah 1:3–5; *my addition, not from Grossman; verify*). The mod's title for the sect's clergy is **Zekenah** (`tm_zekenah`), so the sect's own title already names a woman with no days of separation.
- (b) Grossman shows that much of medieval niddah *practice* was women's own custom, kept "from mother to daughter". The practice included separate dishes, not baking, staying out of the synagogue, and not looking at the scroll. Women also taught it, as Schondlein did.

**In the faith's voice** (*suggestion*):
- *"The days of separation are kept by the one to whom they come. They come to the young wife and not to the zekenah, and no one says the zekenah is less a woman. The lamp and the dough are hers every week. The days are hers as they are the elder's: she knows them, she counts them for her sisters, she teaches them, and she stands with the women at the door."*

**Proposed tenet line** (`tenet_tm_three_lamps_desc`, *suggestion*):
- *"… The Nehpakhot hold that a woman is made by what she keeps. Each week she keeps the dough and the lamp. She keeps the days as the elder keeps them: if the blood does not come, she keeps the count for her sisters and teaches it to the young."*

**Modifier** (`tm_three_lamps_modifier_desc`, *suggestion*):
- "kept the count of the days, the dough and the Sabbath lamp".

**The hostile answer, for a worthy** (`tm_hipukh.0003` or the *niddui* branch, *suggestion*):
- *"Those three were laid on Eve for her sin. You ask to take on a penance that no court has passed on you."* (ch. 1 §5)
- Possible reply in the faith's voice: *"Then I ask for Eve's share, as Leah asked for Dinah."*

### 4.2 Synagogue access, and a new event, "The Women at the Door"

**Why she is always free to enter.** A daughter who sees no blood is never kept out of the women's synagogue by the days. Her lamp is never silenced by them either (ch. 1 §5).

**Why that gives her a role.**
- From the late 12th century, menstruants in Ashkenaz stood outside on the days, "great sorrow" by Isserlein's account (ch. 8 §4).
- The women's prayer leader stood at the opening and repeated the prayer aloud (ch. 8 §4).

**Proposed event (yearly, autumn, the Days of Awe).** The *zekenah* or the turned daughter stands at the door and gives the prayer to the women outside.

| Option | Effect |
|---|---|
| a. "Stand at the door and give them the prayer" | piety; +opinion from female Jewish courtiers |
| b. "Stand outside with them" | stress −; a small +opinion from the ḥevrah |
| c. "Go in. It is the men's business to rule on this" | nothing |

**Two cautions.**
- The evidence is Ashkenazi. For Provence, the *Or Zarua* line ties "the sages of Narbonne" at least to separation at table. Gate the event to Ashkenazi-heritage courts, or mark it as the sect's own custom.
- Never contrast the turned daughter's "freedom" with born women's "impurity" in narration. The point is that she stands **with** them.

### 4.3 The women's prayer leader: a period office for the ḥevrah

`faith_design.md` §13 says "the ḥevrah's offices wait for a source". Grossman supplies one: **"prayer leader of the women"**, attested for Richenza (Nürnberg) and in substance for Urania (Worms) and Dulca.
- *Suggestion:* the elder's office, in loc, is "prayer leader of the women" (Hebrew title *unverified*). It stays in the existing `tm_zekenah` scope. It sits alongside the existing title and does not replace it.
- **Narrator rule.** The office is attested in Ashkenaz, not in Provence. The narrator says "as the Jewish women of the Rhine had their prayer leaders". The sect may claim it for Arles.
- `tm_hipukh.0020.c`, "Teach the prayer to …", is confirmed: women's leaders taught women to pray (Dulca, ch. 8 §4).

### 4.4 "The worthies had no answer, and let it stand" (`tm_hipukh.0003.a.win`)

- **Confirmed** as the period pattern. Women adopted the practice, and the sages answered "one does not object" (R. Isaac Halevi) or "Perhaps she will not listen to me" (Maharil on Bruna).
- *Suggestion* for the win line: *"The worthies said what the sages of the Rhine said of the women who blessed: 'we do not prevent her.'"*
- **The ceiling (§2.2) gives the loss case its reason.** What draws the ban is entering the men's space, not keeping the women's practice. Option `tm_hipukh.0003.c`, "I am the lady of the house", is the women's sphere and should be the safer claim. Option `.b`, which cites the young men's Purim dress, is the men's sphere and should be the riskier one. *Suggestion for tuning; not checked against the scripted odds.*

### 4.5 Smaller confirmations

- **Spinning by moonlight** (`tm_hipukh.0020.b`, `tm_spinning_circle_modifier`). Confirmed in spirit: the father's wish at a daughter's birth was to raise her "to sew, to spin, and to do good deeds" (ch. 7 §7).
- **The common tongue** (`tm_hipukh.0010.desc`). Confirmed (ch. 7 §7; Kimhi for southern France).
- **The translator daughter** (`tm_hipukh.0030`).
  - Plausible, but Grossman names no Jewish woman translator.
  - The nearest figures are women copyists: Paula, daughter of R. Abraham the Scribe (Rome, late 13th c.), and Miriam, daughter of Benaiah. A Sicilian Jewish widow was licensed to practise medicine in 1376 (ch. 7 §§5, 9).
  - Keep the narrator line as "as the Jews of Provence have done for princes", which is true of men.
- **Jewish lords ruling** (`tm_hipukh.0040`). Grossman shows Ashkenazi sages treating "the fear of her husband" as the reason women were excluded from the ten (ch. 8 §4). It is a period argument a rabbi could turn on a daughter: a woman counts for less. Not needed in the event as built.
- **Infertility and marriage** (re-embodied daughters marry). Barrenness was grounds for divorce after ten years. Rabad (Provence) protected the childless wife's *ketubah* (ch. 11). Possible hook (*suggestion*): a Jewish spouse's kin cite the ten years; a Provençal *beit din* following Rabad lets her keep her *ketubah*.

## 5. Open questions

- The Hebrew for "prayer leader of the women" (Richenza) and Urania's epitaph. Grossman gives English only.
- Is *Kol Bo* Provençal? If so, it is the best Provençal text for the separate women's synagogue.
- Did Provençal women stay out of the synagogue during menstruation? Grossman's evidence is Ashkenazi, apart from the ambiguous *Or Zarua* "sages of Narbonne".
