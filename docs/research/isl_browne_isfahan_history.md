# Browne, "Account of a Rare Manuscript History of Iṣfahán" (JRAS 1901, second part): design reading

Source: Edward G. Browne, "Account of a Rare Manuscript History of Iṣfahán, presented to the Royal Asiatic Society on May 19, 1827, by Sir John Malcolm" (continued from p. 446), *Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society* (October 1901): 661–704. JSTOR 25208350. The first part (pp. 411–446) was not read.

**What was read.** Not the whole part. It was searched for *mukhannath*, effeminate, dancer, singer, eunuch, women, festival, Nawrūz, Zoroastrian, Magian, Jew, Christian, music and drum, and every hit was read in context. Also read in full: Chapter VI on the city's oppressors (pp. 661–668); Chapter VII's winter anecdotes (pp. 672–677); the appendix (pp. 686–687); and the postscript on the Paris manuscript (pp. 690–694). The Persian on pp. 673–674 was read from the page images; the extraction garbles it. Page numbers are the printed ones.

**Relation to existing notes.** `later_starts/mukhannathun_baghdad_1066.md` already cites this article (Sources; §1.2d "Isfahan"; §4; §5 Q2), but described it from a summary. This note reads it directly and corrects that description.

## 0. Framing warning

- **What the manuscript is.** It is a **Persian translation**, with additions, of al-Mafarrūkhī's Arabic *Maḥāsin Iṣfahān*. Browne says al-Mafarrūkhī wrote in the reign of ʿAlāʾ al-Dawla Ibn Kākūya (1007–1041) (p. 667).
- **When the translation was made.** The translator praises his patron, the vizier Ghiyāth al-Dīn Muḥammad, and cites Rashīd al-Dīn (pp. 670, 686). That places the translation in the Ilkhanid period. This is an inference: Browne's own dating is in the unread first part.
- **The translator adds material.**
  - He inserts a letter of al-Ḥajjāj "which the *Maḥāsin* does not contain" (p. 662).
  - He adds his own poems (pp. 668–669, 677, 686).
  - He adds verses by 12th- and 13th-century poets (Mujīr al-Dīn Baylaqānī, Kamāl al-Dīn Ismāʿīl) in the same chapter as the anecdotes (pp. 670–671).
  
  **The *mukhannath* anecdotes therefore cannot be dated to c. 1030 from Browne alone.** Some anecdotes in the block name ʿAlāʾ al-Dawla and Sultan Masʿūd of Ghazna (1030s; pp. 675–676), which fits al-Mafarrūkhī's time. But the block is copied in 14th-century colloquial Persian (p. 672).
- **Browne's own gaze.** He calls the *mukhannathūn* "the degraded class" and leaves out six stories "as objectionable, or pointless, or both" (pp. 672–673). The anecdotes themselves are jokes from outside, like all *mukhannath* material.
- **Translations below are ours.** They are working translations from Browne's printed Persian. Browne does not translate these two stories. Have them checked before quoting them in loc as sourced.

## 1. Findings

### 1.1 The anecdote block (pp. 672–673)

- **Where it sits.** In the chapter on the seasons, the winter's "fireside delights" lead to anecdotes on the wit of "women, *mukhannathān*, and fools" (p. 672).
- **What it holds.** There are fourteen stories (p. 672):
  - Nos. 1–6 have a *mukhannath* as hero: **Dukhandī** in Nos. 1–4, and **Ruzuh(?) Jashmī** in No. 5 (the name is uncertain in Browne).
  - Nos. 7–8 are about unnamed women.
  - Nos. 9–14 are about fools.
- **The scribe copied the block twice** (p. 672).
- **What Browne gives** (p. 673):
  - the Persian text of Nos. 3, 5 and 9;
  - English summaries of Nos. 10–14 (all fools);
  - he omits six. **Nos. 1, 2, 4 and 6 (four of the six *mukhannath* stories) are not in Browne at all.**

### 1.2 No. 3, "Dukhandī and the Jester of Kirmān" (pp. 673–674; our translation, summarised)

- **The visit.** "This *mukhannath*'s" sayings became famous everywhere. Some jesters (*maskharagān*) of Kirmān resolved to visit him for his wit and repartee.
- **The trap.** At Isfahan they asked a notable to summon him, to put to him a question that would shame him.
- **The question.** The Kirmānī asks: when an ell of old cotton cloth sells for two dirhams, what does a cuckold's shroud cost?
- **The answer.** Dukhandī answers at once. You bore heat, cold, hunger and a long road to solve this, when someone at home knows better than I do: you should have asked your whore of a mother what she paid for your cuckold father's shroud.
- **The ending.** The Kirmānī went home ashamed.

### 1.3 No. 5, "The *mukhannath* who wanted a fine shroud" (p. 674; our translation, summarised)

- **The will.** On his deathbed, a *mukhannath* willed that his shroud be cut from costly garments:
  - Rūmī *miqrāḍī* cloth;
  - Baghdadi *bahāʾī*;
  - a turban of gold-worked *qaṣab* linen;
  - Egyptian *dabīqī*.
- **The objection.** They told him to be quiet: a shroud is white cotton, or Yemeni *burd*.
- **His reply.** "God forbid! For sixty years I sat with people in silk, brocade and fine linen. Now I go before the Creator in worthless cloth?"

### 1.4 Other items

- **A shaming parade.** An official who proposed a ruinous tax-farm was mounted backwards on a cow and paraded while a herald cried his offence (p. 667). The narrator was al-Mafarrūkhī's maternal grandfather.
- **Isfahan's learned men** include "Yūsuf the Jew", "Yaʿqūb the Jew" and "Sahl the Jew" among the older philosophers and physicians (p. 664).
- **The only Magian.** A Magian of Isfahan was al-Ḥajjāj's secretary (p. 662).
- **An old woman** talks an invading king out of his attack (Paris MS, p. 693).
- **Negatives.** Nothing on Nawrūz or other festivals, Zoroastrian practice, Christians, eunuchs, dancers or singers was found in this part.

## 2. Cross-checks

- **Correction to `later_starts/mukhannathun_baghdad_1066.md`, Sources.**
  - The note says "Browne summarises the anecdotes and prints them in Persian". **He does neither for most of them.** He prints the Persian of two *mukhannath* stories (Nos. 3 and 5), summarises none, and omits four (p. 673).
  - "c. 1030" rests on Browne p. 667 (ʿAlāʾ al-Dawla's reign). But the text in hand is a later Persian translation with interpolations (§0).
- **Same note, §1.2d.** "*Unverified* beyond Browne's summary" can become: **Nos. 3 and 5 read in Browne's Persian** (pp. 673–674; working translation). Nos. 1, 2, 4 and 6 are unread.
- **Same note, §5 Q2** ("Translate the six anecdotes Browne summarises"). Two are now translated. The other four need the manuscript (RAS; or the Paris copy, Supplément persan 1573, a modern copy made in 1897, pp. 690–691) or an edition of the Arabic original. No edition was identified here.
- **The mother insult.** No. 3's retort turns a question back on the questioner's mother. This is the same weapon as "I will act out your mother in a skit" (Rowson 2003 p. 62; Moreh p. 89; Baghdad note §2.3, Shakar). A fourth case, from Isfahan.
- **Fine dress at death.** Hirsch summarises burial rules: a *mukhannath* is buried under women's rules where these are not forbidden to men (`isl_mukhannath_recent_studies.md`, Hirsch table, "Burial"). No. 5 is about luxury, not about women's dress. **Do not read it as a burial-in-women's-clothes case.**
- ***Dabīqī.*** The fine linen of Dabīq near Tinnīs (Cairo note §1.3, the "linen town") was a luxury known in Isfahan. That is a small thread between the Baghdad and Cairo sets.
- **The shaming parade.** The cow ridden backwards (p. 667) adds an Iranian *tashhīr* to the donkey parades of Baghdad note §2.6 event 2 and Rowson 2003 pp. 64–65.
