## 1. The argument, with what matters for the Way

### 1.1 Religion opened space that formal institutions closed
- **Thesis.** "The more formalized and systematic an organization became, the most [*sic*] likely that men would dominate it… religion allowed women autonomy and creative space" (p. 71).
- **Goddesses.** The Queen Mother of the West "became the most important Daoist goddess of the time". "The Queen Mother did not represent ordinary womanhood. Instead she embodied alluring alternatives to quotidian existence, thereby legitimizing female creativity and independence" (pp. 73–74).
- **The Sage Mother (Shengmu)** of Shanxi was a regional maternal goddess with "many identities" (p. 72).
- **The Mysterious Woman of the Nine Heavens** was recast by Daoists as a transmitter of scripture (p. 73).
- **Why women turned to Daoism** (p. 75):
  - after widowhood, when it was "the foundation of an independent social identity";
  - for upward mobility;
  - for healing;
  - for "free and easy wandering" as "a liberating alternative to restrictive social conventions".
- **The body as the woman's renunciation.** Men renounced wealth and office; women "often manipulated their bodies to express religious commitment" (p. 76, after Cahill). This fits the Jade Embryo: the gate is a bodily work.
- **Death of an adept.** Perfected women rose to heaven; "lesser female saints appeared to die and leave a body behind"; the lowest served "as an official in the Daoist bureaucracy" in a grotto (p. 77).

### 1.2 Nüguan: how one became one, and what it gave
- **Who joined.** Becoming "a Daoist nun or priestess (*nüguan, nüshi*)": "Every kind of woman entered the clergy, from prostitutes to princesses" (p. 77).
- **Ties to family.** Unlike Buddhists, Daoist nuns kept family ties. Some "might even periodically return home to look after" children. "Daoist nuns could not marry", but vows "did not have to be permanent" (p. 77).
- **Nuns at home.** "There are more epitaphs for these sorts of nuns than for those who resided in temples". Convents had too little money to take everyone who asked (p. 77).
- **Procedure** (p. 78):
  1. consent of a parent or responsible kinsman;
  2. a period alone in a hermitage or with a female teacher, purifying "body and mind";
  3. entry to a larger institution for training;
  4. formal vows "when she felt sufficiently prepared".
- **Ranks.** "Comparable systems of ranks that were theoretically equal"; "only senior nuns had the authority to perform the most important religious rites" (p. 78). In practice women took part in fewer ceremonies and rarely wrote scripture (p. 78).
- **The travel certificate.** On vows a woman "received a certificate that allowed her to travel freely so that she could visit academic and cultural centers" (p. 79).
- **Hu Yin** (fl. 848) wrote on Daoist longevity techniques and medical theory and influenced internal alchemy (p. 79; see the Jia note).
- **Li Ye** (d. 784): poems of independent desire; Song critics reclassified her as a courtesan (p. 80).
- **Land and tax.** Under the equal-field system, Daoist and Buddhist nuns each received **20 mu** of farmland against monks' 30, and "nuns were exempt from taxation" (p. 87).

### 1.3 Palace women, princesses and the guan
- **Expulsion and the cloister.** At an emperor's death his concubines had to leave the palace, because incest taboos barred his successor from them. Most went home; "others became Buddhist and Daoist nuns" (p. 42). Old concubines also left.
- **The special link.** "Daoist nuns had a special link to the palace, as harem women often became nuns. Imperial consorts often entered a nunnery when they became old or when a new emperor expelled them" (p. 78).
- **Princess-nuns.** "Of the 210 known Tang princesses, although none became Buddhist nuns, at least seventeen took Daoist vows" (p. 78). *Jia counts about 28* (`cn_jia_daoist_priestesses.md`, p. 18). Hinsch's n. 36 says the sources conflict. Use "about two dozen" in loc, or cite Jia.
- **Late-Tang motive.** "Princesses seem to have joined the clergy as a way to evade marriage. As the Tang declined… [emperors] increasingly married their daughters to warlords and foreign leaders… Not coincidentally, most princess nuns came from the later part of the dynasty" (pp. 78–79). This falls inside or just before the game window.
- **Refuge for losers.** "Convents served as refuges for those involved in failed factions and plots" (p. 79).
- **Households followed.** "When a princess became a Daoist nun, her retainers also took vows so that they could continue to serve her", including former palace singers and dancers (p. 79).
- **Mansions into convents.** Princesses converted their mansions (p. 79).
- **The mixing.** Princess Jinxian, a Daoist nun, funded the Buddhist stone sutras at Yunju (p. 82).
- **Female palace officials.** Women officials ran much of the palace, and some concubines held both a consort rank and an official title. The "female scribes" (*nüshi* 女史) had their own titles (p. 41). Wet nurses could rise "from the bottom rungs of society to the uppermost elite"; Tang rewards "went solely to the women themselves, not their kinsmen" (p. 43).
- **Absconding.** When Zhongzong let thousands of palace women out to see the New Year lanterns, "many of them absconded" (p. 42).

### 1.4 Family law that bears on the Temple Societies and the adoptive matriline
- **Tang law recognised many mothers.** It describes a person as having "three fathers and eight mothers", granting "parental status to the main authority figures who guided or nurtured a child". Law and ritual treated the stepmother "as equal to biological mothers in almost every way" (p. 33).
- **Concubines' children** legally "were to regard their father's official wife as their mother", and had full inheritance rights (p. 27).
- **Mother's authority.** A widowed mother acted as de facto head and managed the estate. While either parent lived, the estate could not be divided, and registers show no such divisions (pp. 31, 61). A mother could sue a disobedient son (p. 58).
- **"Big woman" (*danü* 大女).** Turpan documents name adult women who headed households without men; they were "not uncommon" on the frontier (pp. 58, 61).
- **Extinct households (*hujue* 戶絕), Tang rules** (pp. 60–61):
  - in the early Tang, unmarried daughters inherited all of a sonless household;
  - if all daughters had married, the father's kin divided it;
  - the 780 tax reform made women taxable, and an **836 edict** gave married daughters a share where they were the only heirs;
  - a sonless widow was "custodian… not its owner": the estate "would revert to her husband's kinsmen after her death" or on remarriage.
- **Clerics stood outside the birth family in law.** "The law did not recognize Buddhist clerics as members of the family of their birth. If a nun's kinsman committed a crime so serious that his family members shared the punishment, she was exempted from the collective penalty" (p. 88). Hinsch states this for Buddhist clergy. Whether Daoist clergy shared it is **not stated**; check the Tang Code before relying on it.
- **Registered and private nuns.** Registered nuns (*guaji* 挂籍) lived at home but were on the rolls. "Private" nuns (*sidu* 私度) were unregistered and illegal but "not uncommon"; their numbers surged in the late Tang (p. 88).
- **Dunhuang.** A novice formally bade farewell to her **mother**, thanking her for nursing and raising her. "Her father is not mentioned" (p. 88).
- **Nun numbers.** "Documents from Turpan" show nuns rising from 171 to 693 between 788 and 895 and falling to 450 by 936 (p. 87). **The endnote (n. 86) cites Hao Chunwen's *Dunhuang* monograph, so the place is probably Dunhuang, not Turpan.** Check against `cn_hao_monks_nuns_dunhuang.md` before citing.
- **Divorce.** Of the seven grounds for divorce, **barrenness** is one. A wife could not be divorced if "she had no family to return to" (p. 24). With one exception, "the law did not allow a woman to divorce an unwilling spouse". The exception was righteous separation (*yijue*), an official annulment used mainly after domestic violence (p. 25). A wife who left without a divorce had "absconded" (p. 25).
- **Women's confraternities at Dunhuang** pooled funds, cared for the sick and organised funerals (p. 86). These are covered more deeply elsewhere.
- **Burial apart.** Devout Buddhist widows chose burial apart from their husbands, in a pagoda (p. 86).

### 1.5 The backlash and the scholar-officials' weapons
- **Why Tang women had power** (pp. 46–47): steppe custom; weak ritual; heaven = emperor and earth = his mother; no primogeniture; aristocratic empresses who could "browbeat a minister from far more humble origins".
- **Comparisons that cut both ways.** Thinkers likened consorts to ministers, which "implicitly sanctioned female authority". Writers cited female rulers elsewhere, such as **Lady Qiaoguo** (Lady Xian) in Guangdong (p. 46).
- **Backlash after 755.** Yang Guifei was made a scapegoat. "Thereafter, no women wielded much political influence for the remainder of the dynasty", and "most of the remaining emperors did not even dare to formally name an empress" (p. 54).
- **Pictures.** "Portraits of emperors tended to depict good rulers surrounded by men, while evil ones have female attendants" (p. 55).
- **Portent theory.** "An excess of the feminine yin element in the palace will disrupt the balance of the entire cosmos and bring widespread disaster"; "Tang officials sometimes demanded that rulers expel excess women from the palace" (p. 56).
- **Dynastic-cycle story.** "Eventually a foolish ruler in concert with a dissolute female accomplice bring down the dynasty" (p. 56).
- **Song and Yuan writers** attacked Wu in "nationalistic language… as culturally alien and unorthodox" (p. 55).

### 1.6 Points to flag
- **Lotus dragon girl.** Hinsch says the sutra tells of a dragon's daughter "who became a buddha", which "legitimized the idea that, like men, a devoted woman could achieve enlightenment" (p. 90). He **omits that she first becomes male**. This is the same simplification the reading list flagged; see `cn_levering_dragon_girl.md` and `cn_lotus_sutra_dragon_girl.md`. Do not cite Hinsch for the dragon girl.
- **Glossary conflation.** The glossary gives "nüguan 女冠，女官" as one entry (p. 153). These are two words: 女冠 is the Daoist priestess, 女官 the palace female official (see `cn_mcmahon_imperial_wives.md` row 7). The text means 女冠 at p. 77. Hinsch's "*nüshi*" for a Daoist nun is 女師 ("woman master"); the palace scribe *nüshi* is 女史.
- **Self-immolation** among Lotus-reading nuns (p. 90) is suicide content. Exclude it.

**Contradictions flagged.**
- Hinsch's dragon girl "became a buddha" omits the change to male (p. 90). Do not use him as a source for it.
- His glossary merges 女冠 and 女官 (p. 153).
- His Turpan nun counts cite a Dunhuang book (p. 87, n. 86).
- He counts 17 princess-nuns; Jia counts about 28.

**Usable primary or quasi-primary cases.** The Dunhuang novice's farewell to her mother; the 836 inheritance edict; Zhongzong's lantern night, when palace women absconded; Hu Yin (848); Li Ye; Princess Jinxian and the Yunju sutras; "three fathers and eight mothers".
