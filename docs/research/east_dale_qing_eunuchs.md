# Dale, *Inside the World of the Eunuch* (HKU Press 2018) — research notes for the Adelphai Dorleyaiou chain

**PERIOD FLAG (read first).** Dale covers the Qing palace eunuch system, 1644–1924, with a coda on survivors into the 1980s. Every item below is **four to ten centuries after the CK3 end date (1453)** and from a Manchu-ruled Beijing court, not Anatolia. Nothing here is a *precedent* for an 867 Anatolian heresy. Its value is as a **social-history template**: the only book-length account of what a castrate corps's lived life looked like from the inside — recruitment, the knife, recovery, apprenticeship, discipline, flight, suicide, marriage, old age, burial, and self-understanding — documented from archival confessions, oral histories and memoirs rather than from hostile scholar-official polemic. Earlier passes already covered Tang (Hoeckelmann), Ming (Tsai ×2), Chiang and Sommer; this file cross-references those where Dale leans on them (Tsai pp. 15, 18–19, 40, 46; Sommer pp. 55, 57–58) and does not re-summarise them.

Source text: `scratchpad/books/east_11.txt`. Page numbers below are the printed HKU pagination recovered from running headers in the text file (Intro 1–13; ch.1 14–27; ch.2 28–47; ch.3 48–65; ch.4 66–88; ch.5 89–106; ch.6 107–125; ch.7 126–144; ch.8 145–169; ch.9 170–193; Conclusion 194–200; Appendices 201–204).

Dale's own terminology choices matter for the mod: she uses **"emasculation"** (removal of penis, scrotum and testes — the Qing requirement) rather than "castration" (testes only), and translates *daozijiang* 刀子匠 as "knife experts" rather than Stent's "knifers" (Conventions, p. xi). The Dorley rite as designed is closer to the Qing total excision than to Byzantine/Ottoman testicular castration, so Dale's medical and recovery detail is unusually transferable.

## 1. Book map

**Dale's thesis** (Intro pp. 1–13; Conclusion pp. 194–200). Histories of Chinese eunuchs have been written about a handful of notorious political eunuchs of the Tang and Ming; Dale redirects "the scholarly gaze away from a small number of powerful eunuchs and onto the majority of palace eunuchs who never rose to the heights of eunuch power" (p. 4). Her core claims: (1) Qing eunuchs were "servants but not slaves" (p. 6), occupying an unfree status "on the continuum in between" free and unfree (p. 11); (2) the system was built on an assumption — that emasculation severs family ties and produces a dependent, loyal servant — that "did not meet realities" (p. 16): eunuchs kept contact with families, married, adopted, formed cliques, ran away, and killed themselves; (3) they built "a parallel realm alongside that of the emperor" (p. 5) of mutual aid, gambling, drink and opium, most visible after nightfall; (4) on gender, labelling eunuchs a "third sex" is "an example of presentism that does not reflect Qing categorizations of gender and sexuality. As Jennifer Jay's 1993 article revealed, eunuchs identified as male" (p. 6) — though Han Confucian society read them as yin/feminine and Westerners read them as simply female; (5) labour relations were "defined by a management style reliant on corporal punishment and the use of harsh punishments such as exile and manual labor" (p. 6), with collective responsibility binding master to apprentice and roommate to roommate.

**Sources** (pp. 7–8). Imperial Household Department (Neiwufu 內務府) edicts, memorials, depositions and runaway/suicide case files in Beijing and Taipei archives; confessions of captured runaways; oral histories of the last eunuchs (Ma Deqing et al. 1982; Xin Xiuming; Sun Yaoting's biography; Yu Chunhe's memoir in French translation as Dan Shi, *Mémoires d'un Eunuque*); palace maidservant He Rong'er's recollections (Jin Yi & Shen Yiling, *Gongnü tan wanglu*); Pu Yi's autobiography; Westerners Stent (1877), Morache (1869), Matignon (1899), Katherine Carl (1906), Blofeld. Dale stresses that "eunuch history recorded by eunuchs is extremely rare" because the court deliberately kept them illiterate (p. 7).

## 2. Life-course dossier

All page refs to Dale 2018; where she quotes a primary source I name it. **Qing, 1644–1924 throughout.**

### 2a. Recruitment: poverty, the family decision, the sold boy, the self-cutter

- **Volunteers, not prisoners.** "During the Qing dynasty (1644–1911), volunteers rather than prisoners supplied the imperial court with the majority of candidates for service as palace eunuchs" (p. 2). But "'voluntary' did not necessarily equate to individual choice in the matter" (p. 28): within "a patriarchal and hierarchically organized society such as the Qing, the wishes of elders and the good of the family took precedence over individual concerns, especially those of minors. In the case of emasculation, this resulted in the complete negation of a boy's rights over his own body" (p. 29).
- **Poverty as the screened-for motive.** "Again and again throughout the historical record, poverty appears as the primary motivating factor behind emasculation" (p. 30). The court actively screened for it: "The court screened applicants to ensure that poverty had been the primary motivation for their emasculation... Those who had ulterior motivations such as desire for power and political influence could become problematic" (p. 30). A 1646 statute even encouraged it: "if a commoner family had more than four or five sons, then one should be sent to apply to become an emasculate" (p. 30).
- **The candidate's own voice (Blofeld's informant, late Qing):** "Why not? It seemed a little thing to give up one pleasure for so many. My parents were poor, yet by suffering that small change I could be sure of an easy life in surroundings of beauty and magnificence; I could aspire to intimate companionship with lovely women unmarred by their fear or distrust of me. I could even hope for power and wealth of my own. With good fortune and diligence, I might grow more rich and powerful than some of the greatest officials, in the empire" (p. 30).
- **The failed self-cutter (Stent, 1877):** "a newly-married young man expressed a strong desire to become a eunuch, as he said he should then have nothing to do but eat, drink, and sleep. His relatives endeavored to dissuade him from so insane an idea, and refused to become surety for him; whereupon he attempted to perform the operation on himself, but did it so clumsily that no one would employ him. He, therefore, had mutilated himself for life without gratifying his desire of living the easy life of a eunuch" (p. 30). Note the *surety* requirement — kin must stand guarantor.
- **Daozijiang as recruiters/brokers.** "Daozijiang preyed upon the desperation of the poor and sold them a glorified picture of the eunuch lifestyle" (p. 29). They pre-screened "for qualities such as 'appearance, conversation, intelligence and cleverness, and strength'"; Yu Chunhe recalled Bi "looked Yu over from head to toe and then used his hands to examine between Yu's thighs" (p. 32).
- **Kidnapping and sale.** 1793: nine-year-old Manchu boy Li Zhu "had been drugged and forced to become emasculated" and was simply assigned to the inner court (p. 32). 1836: "two discharged eunuchs were arrested for luring over ten young boys from the road, forcing them to become emasculated by a local daozijiang, and selling them as emasculates. At the time of the arrest, seven of the boys had already been emasculated and sold, while four others had been rescued" (p. 32). Parents sold seven- and eight-year-olds "for a sum of money that would only feed the family for a few months" (pp. 32–33); unlike daughters sold to brothels, "the sales of boys to a daozijiang were final" (p. 33).
- **Double bondage.** The daozijiang "required their 'adopted sons' to turn over their entire salaries from their employment as eunuchs to them. As a result, eunuchs from these daozijiang 'families' found themselves held in bondage by two masters, their imperial employers and their daozijiang 'fathers'" (p. 33).
- **Yu Chunhe's coerced "consent" (age 17).** Lured by "Uncle Qian" with promise of work, sold to Bi; price of freedom 100 taels plus threat of a fraud charge; "After refusing to eat, drink, or even speak for two days and two nights, Yu lost the will to fight and informed Bi that he could perform the emasculation whenever he desired" (p. 33). Qing law "prohibited young boys and men from being emasculated against their will or from being deceived" but "did little to protect young boys from their own family members" (p. 33). Candidates signed "a waiver stating that the procedure was voluntary" (p. 33).
- **Self-emasculation (*zigong* 自宮) — Ma Deqing, age nine, 1905:** "my father called me in, held me down in the shop, and emasculated me with his own hands . . . Please remember at this time, there weren't any anesthetics, injections, or things to stop the bleeding . . . To forcibly hold down a child who is kicking and screaming and cut off his reproductive organs . . . That child is in so much pain!" (pp. 39–40). Dressings of "white wax (baila 白蠟), sesame oil, and powder from the Chinese prickly ash" (p. 40). Early Qing punished self-emasculation with decapitation and family exile (p. 40); Qianlong "excused self-emasculations that had been motivated by poverty," which "had the adverse effect of encouraging future acts of genital mutilation performed by fathers on sons or by men upon themselves" (p. 40).
- **Age.** Preference for pre-pubertal cutting; limits swung with supply (Yongzheng: 17, then 26, then none). After eunuch rebels aided the 1813 Eight Trigrams entry into the palace, Jiaqing decreed "only applicants sixteen and under who had never been married or fathered children" (p. 43). Rebel eunuch Mu Qi 穆七 "had been married and fathered two sons before undergoing emasculation"; but Yu Jiqing 于吉慶 had been cut at age ten and rebelled anyway (p. 43).
- **Penal emasculation (rare, demand-driven).** Qianlong "called for sons of rebels who had killed more than three or four members in a family to be emasculated"; 1789: thirty-seven sons of Taiwan bandits, "many of whom were under the age of nine, some as young as four years old," sent to Beijing to be cut and set to sweeping (pp. 34–35). Dale: these cases "appear to be motivated by the palace's need for eunuchs rather than the desire to teach rebels or murderers a lesson" (p. 35).

### 2b. The knifers, the question, the procedure

- **The guild.** *Daozijiang* were "Granted monopolies by the government until they were revoked in 1900," enrolled "in one of the three banners of the Imperial Household Department and carried the rank of the seventh grade" (p. 31); also called a *yahang* 牙行 guild (p. 31 n.10). A 1793 runaway "described himself as having been emasculated by a fourth-generation daozijiang" (p. 31). Late Qing: **Bi Wu 畢五 ("Bi the Fifth")** on Nanchang Street and **Xiaodao Liu 小刀劉 ("Pocket Knife" Liu)** outside Di'an Gate; Yu Chunhe's memoir adds "'Fine Blade' Liu" on Fangzhuang Street (pp. 31–32). "four times a year (spring, summer, winter, and fall), Bi and Liu would each supply the palace with at least forty emasculates... 160 new eunuch recruits each year" (p. 32).
- **The consent ritual.** "Determining their son's and by extension the family's fate, parents rather than the soon-to-be-emasculate offered the final consent just prior to the excision of the child's sexual organs. When asked houhui bu houhui (後悔不後悔 Will you regret it?), the parent's or sponsor's negative response would serve as the only permission necessary for the daozijiang to proceed" (p. 29). Stent's version has the knifer ask the candidate: "The operating 'knifer' [daozijiang] then stands in front of the man—with his knife in his hand—and enquires if he will ever repent. If the man at the last moment demurs in the slightest, the 'knifer' will not perform the operation, but if he still expresses his willingness, with one sweep of the knife he is made a eunuch" (p. 38).
- **The egg.** Yu Chunhe's bitter postscript: "before starting the procedure, you had told me you were going to give me some medicine. Instead, you forced an egg into my mouth and asked me if I would have any regrets." Bi said the egg kept his airway open; "As for the question, 'Will you regret it?' Daozijiang Bi remarked that this was merely a way to get Yu to open his mouth so that he could insert the egg" — yet "technically, no one could challenge Bi on the point that he had not asked for Yu's consent" (p. 34).
- **Fees and offerings.** "Playing the role of the eunuch's master, the daozijiang required that his 'pupils' present him with offerings such as a pig or a chicken or a bottle of baijiu 白酒. The actual cost of the procedure was determined on a sliding scale depending upon the financial situation of the applicant's family. Those unable to pay... could arrange to pay off their debt after they entered the palace" (p. 34). Stent: six taels for cutting plus recovery care (p. 38). Recovery supplies the applicant had to bring: "(1) thirty jin (斤) of millet (the equivalent of a month's worth of rations), (2) several big corn cobs for use in heating the kang (炕 raised heated bed), (3) several stalks of sesame (to be burnt into an ash for placement under the body for purification purposes), and (4) fifty sheets of window paper to keep the wind out" (p. 34).
- **The procedure (Stent, 1877, pp. 38–39):** candidate reclining on a *kang*, waist held by one man, legs held apart by two; "white ligatures or bandages are bound tightly round the lower part of the belly and the upper part of the thighs, to prevent too much hemorrhage. The parts about to be operated on are then bathed three times with hot pepper-water... the whole—both the testes and penis—are cut off as closely as possible with a small curved knife, something in the shape of a sickle. The emasculation being effected, a pewter needle or spigot is carefully thrust into the main orifice at the root of the penis; the wound is then covered with paper saturated in cold water and is carefully bound up. After the wound is dressed the patient is made to walk about the room supported by two of the 'knifers,' for two or three hours, when he is allowed to lie down." Morache's version: cold bath to numb, genitals wrapped "in a silk-like packet and excised with one pass of the knife," styptic powder (p. 37). Later physicians record "'sturdy scissors' or three knives varying in shape and size (from a sickle shape, to a small hatchet, to a long cleaver)" and plugs of "wood, lead, pewter, and tin" (p. 38). The craft "was a closely guarded secret due to the hereditary nature of the profession" (p. 37).

### 2c. Recovery, mortality, the *bao*

- **The three days.** "For three days following the surgery, the candidate was prohibited from drinking anything since he was unable to urinate during this period. The surgery would be deemed a success if, when the spigot was removed (three days after the surgery), the emasculate could urinate" (p. 38). Recovery needed "not wearing clothing below the waist," so late spring/early summer cutting was preferred (low flies/mosquitoes); "The new eunuch would be considered totally healed in about one hundred days" (p. 38) — the "avoid the wind for one hundred days" convention (p. 41).
- **Mortality.** "death resulting from the actual surgery (often caused by the inability to urinate) was quite rare and occurred in 'about two out of a hundred cases'" (Stent, p. 39). But re-cutting of "impure" eunuchs was far deadlier: "those who underwent emasculation a second time, or had their genitals 'swept clean' (saojing 掃淨), were often 'swept dead' (saosi 掃死)" (p. 58).
- **Incontinence.** "Imprecision by the daozijiang led some eunuchs to suffer from urinary incontinence, making it necessary for them to wear a large handkerchief wrapped around their loins" (p. 39). Late-Qing eunuchs with complications "often sought the help of foreign doctors"; catheterisation "undoubtedly saved the lives of many eunuchs" (p. 39).
- **The *bao* 寶 ("treasure").** "The emasculate's severed genitalia, known as bao (寶 treasure) were preserved in a 'pint measure' and kept high on a shelf. Since the possession of bao was required for eunuch promotions and needed to be placed in the coffin at the time of burial, bao were highly prized. Moreover, if the emasculate or his family failed to ask for these after the procedure, the daozijiang could make a considerable profit by selling or renting bao to eunuchs for use in burials or promotions. Stent cites that eunuchs in the late Qing would often pay as much as fifty taels to purchase a set of bao" (p. 39). Because bao could be "rented, borrowed, bought, and sold," the palace relied on physical examination rather than bao as proof (pp. 41–42). *(Burial beliefs proper are in ch. 8 — see §2h below.)*
- **The physical and the "impure."** From 1661 an elderly eunuch under two officials verified every applicant's cut (p. 41). Failures were "rejected on the grounds that their bodies were 'impure' (shenzi bu jing 身子不淨), terminology that suggests that the male genitalia had not been totally excised, and sent back to their native place" (p. 42). Zhao Ben 照本 was rejected under Yongzheng, rejected again under Qianlong at a marquis's household, and by 1767 was "a sixty-five-year-old itinerant monk arrested for getting into a fight with a shopkeeper"; he had been cut "finding himself in abject poverty after the death of his wife during childbirth" (p. 46 & n.103). Biological (congenital) eunuchs were also refused — "inauspicious omens" (Furth, p. 42). Palace rejects "banded together and even turned to lifestyles removed from the mainstream of society, such as monasticism" (p. 46); in the late Ming "unemployed emasculates earned a living in bathhouses serving their employed brethren" (p. 46, citing Tsai).
- **Biannual re-inspection.** "Twice a year, once in the spring and once in the fall, the Imperial Household Department examined eunuchs" to ensure they were still "clean" (p. 57), driven by fear of pseudo-eunuchs and of "regeneration" of excised genitalia; "some eunuchs underwent emasculation two or three times to ensure no genitalia remained extant and that the wound was completely flat" (p. 58).

### 2d. The new name, the training, the *shifu* bond

- **Rebirth and renaming.** Recruits "would be given new names that symbolically represented the end of their former lives" (p. 66); batch lists show every applicant sharing a first character — *lian* 連 ("to connect") in one list of forty, *jin* 進 ("to enter") in another of twenty-three (p. 67); common name characters were *xin* 信, *zhong* 忠, *fu* 福, *xi* 喜 (p. 67 n.4). Dale links this to monastic renaming: the colloquial term for both becoming a eunuch and becoming a monk is *chu jia* 出家 "leaving home" (pp. 1, 60, 67). "These first characters also indicated peer groups within the palace eunuch population" (p. 67). Once assigned, "the emasculate's rebirth was now complete" (p. 71).
- **Basic training.** Several days to two weeks at the Shenxingsi 慎刑司 (Judicial Department): palace rules, forms of address, taboo words (eunuchs whose names clashed with the emperor's had to rename again), how to receive an order ("Asking a superior to repeat or explain an instruction was not allowed," p. 69), kneeling and *ketou*. Yu Chunhe: "a eunuch's life was spent on his knees" (p. 69). Carrying "at the level of one's eyebrows," serving tea, lighting pipes, dressing a master (p. 70).
- **Why "shifu".** "Since eunuchs did not like to be called taijian, superiors were to be addressed as shifu (師父 teacher) and those in the same generation were to be called by their surnames followed by ye (爺 term of respect)" (p. 68).
- **Selection audience.** Recruits in apprentice dress ("a robe [paozi 袍子], a pair of black boots, and a belt") led by a *zongguan taijian* to the Yangxindian to kneel before the court; wooden slips with name, age, native place; Cixi chose first, "favoring recruits who were young, intelligent, and literate," then emperor, empress, concubines; the rest dispersed to forty-eight departments (pp. 70–71).
- **The apprenticeship itself (pp. 72).** Master = older *zongguan* or *shouling taijian*. "New recruits did not receive systematic training; instead, they learned by following their superiors around... three eunuchs from the late Qing noted, '[we] had to rely on [our] own devices, the master never gave explicit demands, and very rarely instructed [us], wanting us to teach ourselves.'" **Apprentices were their masters' servants:** "Eunuchs would wait on their superiors—drawing water for their bath, helping them dress—and only retiring for the evening after their master's wants and needs had been satisfied. One recruit recalls, 'I would prepare his tea, I would go to find his breakfast at the kitchens and return to wake him up. I would help him get dressed, I would serve him his meal, then while he was attending to his work, I would tidy up . . . In the evening when he was tired, I would massage his legs.'" **Discipline inside the bond:** "The threat of collective punishment for infractions committed by their disciples ensured that masters closely supervised their subordinates. If a master noticed an error, his apprentice would receive a harsh reprimand, a slap on the face for repeated minor offenses, or a beating for errors deemed more serious. Apprentices who learned quickly and performed their duties well earned the favor of their masters and increased their chances of promotion" (p. 72). Dale's gloss: "eunuchs quickly discovered that the emperor was not their only master" (p. 87).
- **The bond as faction-seed.** "Throughout his period of service within the palace, a master would train numerous apprentices and in the process create his own social network" (p. 95). "This 'mutual helpfulness' could often be limited to apprentices trained under the same master, since masters often promoted their individual interests as they endeavored to advance their disciples beyond those of other eunuch masters... the apprentice–master system as a teaching tool had the unintended effect of creating and cementing eunuch bonds and may have even contributed to the formation of eunuch cliques" (p. 96). In the theatre bureau the lineage was formalised: "Each of the different roles customary to Chinese drama had a master, a disciple, an elder master, and a grandson disciple (tusun 徒孫)" — making transfer out "a difficult if not impossible task" (p. 82).
- **Daozijiang as first master.** The knifer too styled himself master: his "pupils" owed offerings (p. 34) and sold boys became his "adopted sons" owing salary (p. 33). Princely-household service functioned as "a kind of probationary apprenticeship for the more advantageous post at court" (Mann, p. 36); Stent: "Every fifth year each prince is required to furnish, for the use of the palace, eight young eunuchs... inspected and guaranteed to be pure eunuchs—i.e., properly emasculated, clean in person, and free from all disease," paid 250 taels each (p. 37).

### 2e. Hierarchy and discipline (pre-runaway material; ch. 6–7 added below)

- **Ranks (p. 75).** Chief eunuch (*Gongdian jiandu lingshi taijian* 宮殿監督領事太監 / *Qianqinggong zongguan* 乾清宮總管 / *dazongguan* 大總管) → *zongguan taijian* 總管太監 (assistant chiefs) → *shouling taijian* 首領太監 (supervisory, responsible for their department's eunuchs) → *fushouling taijian* 副首領太監 → unranked *taijian*. "During the early and mid-Qing, eunuch rank could not surpass grade four... the majority of eunuchs, who served as palace attendants, did not even possess rank." Only ranked eunuchs could wear hats; hat colour (red → blues → white → gold) marked grades two through eight; embroidered bird on the robe marked rank; the *magua* 馬褂 riding jacket was reserved for *zongguan* and *shouling* (p. 77).
- **Inner vs outer.** *Neigong taijian* 內宮太監 (inner, ~400–500, 20–25%) vs *waiwei taijian* 外圍太監 (outer, ~75%, housed outside the Forbidden City); Mann likens the prestige gap to "house and field work in American slavery" (p. 77). Outer-court eunuchs "were not subject to many of the strict rules and etiquette requirements imposed upon eunuchs of the inner court" (p. 78).
- **Quarters, pay, messes.** Eunuchs lived in *tatan* 他坦 — those of the Presence "in the narrow passageways between the walls... adjacent to the sewage drainage system" (p. 84); aged eunuchs clustered in a *hutong* east of Beihai (p. 84). Pay 2–8 taels/month plus rice by rank (p. 85); eunuchs "pool their resources... 'form[ing] messes'" (p. 86); eunuch-only restaurants Siheyi 四合義 and Daheyi 大合義 by the Shenwu Gate (p. 86).
- **Eunuch clergy and the deformity bar (pp. 79–81).** Eunuch Buddhist monks, Daoist priests and lamas served the palace women; Daoguang dismissed the Buddhist and Daoist eunuch clergy, leaving lama eunuchs (3 *shouling lama*, 12 *lama taijian*, 2 students at the Zhongzhengdian) who chanted daily "expiating the sins of the dead (chaodu 超度)" and "substituting for the emperor at the Yonghegong"; eight more served the imperial tombs (p. 80). Stent: eunuchs "were allowed to enter temples to burn incense and to fast but were prohibited from mounting the platform to confess and be absolved by a priest... 'In this, however, they are not worse off than many others, for no cripple or deformed person, no one short of an eye, finger, or any portion of the body, nor any woman in her courses, etc., can ascend the dais'" (p. 81). Dale: eunuch religious leadership despite this bar is "an apparent contradiction" needing more research (p. 81).
- **Eunuchs as floggers.** Pu Yi's list of eunuch duties includes "flogging offending eunuchs and serving women" (p. 79) — discipline was administered by eunuchs on eunuchs.
- **Punishment schedule (ch. 5).** Gambling: mid-1700s sixty blows of the *ban* 板 (light bamboo); 1704 four eunuchs caught gambling on watch got two months' cangue in the city plus one hundred lashes (pp. 98–99). Mid-1800s regulations: first offence three months' cangue, forty blows, exile to the Eastern/Western Tombs; second offence strangulation; 1851 commuted to three years as an official's slave at Dasheng Wula (p. 99). Running a den: cangue, forty blows, Heilongjiang military slavery; promoting mass gambling: strangulation on first offence (p. 99). **Collective responsibility:** supervisors fined two months' to two years' pay; fellow eunuchs who suspected but did not report: two months' cangue and forty blows (p. 99). Drinking by day: sixty blows and the *shouling* fined six months' pay (p. 100). Opium in the inner court: strangulation even for first offence; "The eunuch's family would be exiled... to Xinjiang, to serve as slaves for the military. The eunuchs' superior, regardless if he knew of the eunuch's activity or not, was also exiled... to Heilongjiang... Even the eunuch's roommate received punishment and was exiled" (p. 103). Eunuchs got "fewer blows from the lighter bamboo rod" than commoners (p. 99).
- **Violence among eunuchs.** 1722 Jin Tinglin 金廷林 drunk near the emperor's quarters, cursed superiors, brandished a sword: two months' cangue, 100 lashes (p. 100); later roster shows a 41-year-old Jin sent to cut grass at Weng Mountain 甕山 after cangue and whip (p. 102). 1739 theatre eunuch Du Ming 杜明 stabbed Liu Erqi 劉爾奇 three times in the legs outside the palace after being made to *ketou* and fined by his supervisor; sentence per Kangxi precedent: cutting grass at Weng Mountain (pp. 101–102). Kutcher's Yuanmingyuan crab-culvert murder: confession "filled with territorial sentiment" (p. 102). Dale: "Eunuchs fighting and stabbing each other with knives stands in stark contrast to traditional understandings of eunuch behavior" (pp. 102–103).
- **Opium underworld.** Stent 1877: "Most of the eunuchs smoke opium, and to enable them to indulge in this habit without quitting the palace, seven or eight opium dens are opened in its precincts... it is considered policy to shut the eyes—officially—to their existence" (p. 104). Sun Yaoting "became the accountant (bookie) for an opium dealer (another eunuch) inside the palace" (p. 104). Cixi's eunuchs kept "special linen clothes, which they put on for smoking" (Carl, p. 105).
- **The ruling formula.** Regulations repeat "taijian bu shou guiju (太監不守規矩 eunuchs aren't observing with the rules)" (p. 106).

### 2f. Bodies, health, voice — and how eunuchs described their own gender

- **Physical sequelae (pp. 50–52).** Pre-pubertal cuts: taller, "shorter torsos and longer legs and noticeably broader pelvises," bowed legs, kyphosis in 20 of Wagenseil's 31 subjects (1933), "wide hips and gynecomastia," obesity, sparse brows/beard/body hair, more facial wrinkles. Adult cuts: "difficult to distinguish from unemasculated males"; one cut at 36 *sui*: "Because I was castrated in middle age, my face could grow a beard and people couldn't tell that I was a eunuch" (p. 51). **Voice:** "a 'special timber'" (Morache), valued for palace opera female roles (pp. 51, 81). **Gait:** "lean slightly forward, take short steps, keep the legs close, [while] at the same time turning the toes outward" (Stent, p. 52); "indescribable je-ne-sais quoi" (p. 52). **Psychology:** hot flashes, mood swings; described as "'hysterical' and 'easily moved to violent anger' but also 'easily appeased, readily amused, depressed, timid, honest, and charitable'" (p. 52). **Resentment of fathers:** Ma Deqing "claims that the day his father cut off his sexual organs was the day that he disassociated himself from his family"; Matignon "found that feelings of hatred toward their fathers were common among his eunuch patients" (p. 52). **Libido persists:** "emasculation... did not remove sexual desire in all eunuchs" (p. 60); Zhang Delang, "a late Qing eunuch with three wives," frequented a Tianjin prostitute, and his servant Yu Chunhe watching was "burning with fever and desire" (p. 61). Dorn's "sexless and frustrated": Dale accepts "frustrated," rejects "sexless" (p. 61).

- **Self-view: men.** This is the book's firmest gender finding. "In sharp contrast to Confucian society's labeling of eunuch gender as feminine, eunuchs themselves continued to self-identify as gendered male" (p. 55). Quoting Jay: "The writings by eunuchs give no indication of even a slight change in gender identity after castration. Even their sexuality, or rumours of it, remained male-oriented" (p. 56); eunuchs "remained unquestionably male in gender identity and role . . . They . . . married, adopted children, ran the households as male heads of the family when off duty or when retired from the palaces" (p. 56). Dale: "gender identity was a non-issue for eunuchs (they were simply boys and men who had undergone emasculation)" (p. 56); "Eunuchs were not homosexuals or those with sexual anomalies such as hermaphrodites or transsexuals looking for sexual reassignment through emasculation" (p. 56); "For eunuchs, emasculation removed their genitalia but not their identity as male" (p. 65). Dale explicitly rejects the "third sex" label (Wang Yude's *Shenmi de di san xing*) as "presentism" (pp. 6, 53).

- **Others' view 1 — Han Confucian society: yin/feminine.** Furth: with "sexual action or initiative . . . a male attribute," "hermaphrodites and eunuchs were viewed as sexually female"; "Chinese society did not allow for an intermediate status between the sexes" (p. 53). Du Wanyan describes castration as "turning [a man] into 'half woman'" (p. 16). Inner court gendered yin, outer court yang; scholar-officials "expressed their resentment... by stigmatizing and feminizing eunuchs," denying them the two masculine routes (*wen* examination learning, *wu* arms) (pp. 53–55). Sommer's penetration logic: inability "to assume the social role of penetrator... resulted in the reading of the eunuch body as feminine" (p. 55). Confucian shorthand: "servile, feminized inhabitants of the inner quarters" (p. 55). Kangxi 1689: eunuchs are "yin 陰 with a disposition that was not like ordinary men. When older they are already feeble and their speech and movements are like babies. Outwardly, they appear respectful and virtuous. Inwardly, they are really unfathomable" (p. 56). Stent on purity grades: "All eunuchs are considered pure (chên [zhen] 真 or ch'ing [qing] 清,) but boys who are made eunuchs when under ten years of age are termed 'thoroughly pure' (t'ung-chên [tongzhen] 通真). These are specially prized, and are employed by the ladies of the palace with as much freedom as if they were girls; performing such offices as ought only be done by women" (p. 57). Yet the "thoroughly pure" aged out and were removed from the ladies' apartments (p. 60).

- **Others' view 2 — the Manchu court: anxious, but in practice "men without genitalia".** Biannual inspections, re-cutting, prohibition of *duishi* 對食 bonds with maidservants ("Maidservants who addressed eunuchs in familial terms, who referred to eunuchs as boshu (伯叔 uncle) or xiongdi (兄弟 brother), faced punishment and expulsion and her family faced exile to Xinjiang," p. 59), use of maidservants rather than eunuchs for bathing/dressing/sleeping watch (p. 60) — all signal doubt that the cut neutered desire. But "Palace employment of eunuchs as palace guards and regulations against eunuchs wielding knives in the palace suggest that, despite their impotence, they were considered able to perform roles that female maidservants could not... the Manchu imperial court suspected that eunuchs were simply men without genitalia" (p. 61); Yongzheng ordered 200 outer-court eunuchs trained in archery and firearms as *suwei* 宿衛 guards (p. 54). Verdict: the court came "to view eunuchs as more yang than yin" (p. 65).

- **Others' view 3 — Westerners: female, full stop.** "To the Western observer and reader, there was no confusion about how to read the eunuch body. It was simply and undeniably gendered female" (p. 61). Stent: "young eunuchs are often very handsome and feminine in appearance; indeed, one can almost imagine some of them to be young women dressed in men's clothing"; older ones "bear a resemblance to old women, who forgetting their age and sex, are masquerading in male attire"; recognisable "by the voice, the want of hair on the face, cringing manner, hangdog, bloated appearance"; "face is bare as a billiard ball" (p. 63). Blofeld (1930s) took a eunuch's "soft, feminine voice" for a woman's in the dark (p. 64). The one Western woman observer, Katherine Carl, described Li Lianying's "massive lean jaw" and Savonarola head without feminising him (p. 64).

- **Society's slur on eunuch marriage.** Wealthy eunuchs who "buy a wife, build a house, and 'establish some semblance of family'" "were labeled 'fake old cocks [roosters]' (jia laogong 假老公), a term perhaps even more derogatory than laogong (老公 old rooster), since it implied more than simply the eunuch's inability to reproduce but also society's unwillingness to accept his family as 'real'" (p. 94). 1923 Dagongbao headline: "Must Escape. Why would a eunuch want a wife?" — society "viewed eunuch marriage as not only a farce but a form of bondage" (p. 94). Stent on An Dehai: "He also had a wife—an unnecessary appendage to a eunuch" (p. 94).

### 2g. Marriage, concubines, adopted sons, families (ch. 5; old age and burial in ch. 8 below)

- **Families kept.** "many eunuchs still retained some measure of contact with their natal families... eunuchs regularly sent money home, spent leave with their families, and attended their parents' funerals" (p. 90). Monthly visits on *chu'er* 初二 at a secluded spot west of the Shenwu Gate 神武門 where they could "remit to them part or all of their monthly salaries" (p. 90). Bereavement leave sixteen days (ranked) or twelve (p. 91); three days for a male relative's wedding, with Zongwufu officials checking that eunuchs on leave "did not travel freely, attend the theater, fraternize with other eunuchs, gamble" (p. 92). 1771: eleven-year-old Yuanmingyuan eunuch Liu Jinxi 劉進喜 drowned; "Liu's father had routinely picked his son up every night from the palace" (p. 91). c.1817 Wang Fu 王幅 dictated five letters home after his elder brother was murdered; "he was unable to get leave from the palace and, since his mother was elderly, he didn't know how she was doing and he could not be at peace" (p. 92).
- **Marriage.** "Eunuch marriage was not a rarity, particularly among eunuchs who had undergone emasculation after puberty... Eunuchs even married after emasculation and while serving in the palace... marriages between [eunuchs and maidservants] were not uncommon. Eunuchs also married women who already had children, an often ideal situation for the infertile eunuch" (p. 93). Cixi, told a thirty-year-old eunuch had a wife and son: "Oh, I see. You have a wife and a son, and still wanted to serve as a eunuch. Not bad" (p. 93). Cixi "even arranged marriages between palace eunuchs and Manchu maidservants": an eighteen-*sui* maidservant to eunuch Liu, "the informally adopted son (gan erzi 乾兒子) of the Empress's favorite, Li Lianying" (p. 93). Late-Qing eunuchs with outside families could "go out and spend a few days with them occasionally" (Stent, p. 93). Zhang Delang had three wives (p. 61).
- **Adoption.** "Eunuchs also created their own families by adopting children. As described by Jay, eunuchs such as Li Lianying 李連英 (1848–1911), adopted biological nephews and nieces, a practice customary among Ming and Qing eunuchs" (p. 93; cross-ref Tsai/Chen on Ming eunuch kinship). The *gan erzi* (dry/informal son) relation also ran eunuch-to-eunuch (Li Lianying → Liu, p. 93).
- **Homosexuality.** Dale is agnostic: "the suggestion that even a small percentage of eunuchs engaged in homosexual activities in not unrealistic," given "unmarried and impoverished bachelors" in "an all male environment," but "the final word... may never be more than speculation" (pp. 94–95).
- **Esprit de corps.** Carl noticed "a 'feeling of 'esprit de corps' among them [the eunuchs]—a spirit of mutual helpfulness'"; "native place associations, such as that formed by eunuchs from Shandong, assisted young emasculates in entering the palace eunuch system" (pp. 95–96). Dogs as companionship; eunuchs' dogs "usually inferior breeds" by class rule (p. 97).

### 2h. The runaway problem (ch. 6, pp. 107–125)

- **Why they ran.** Service was for life; "if a eunuch was unhappy with his work environment, he rarely had the option of quitting or leaving the system" (p. 107). "Faced with impending beatings for often minor infractions, for eunuchs, flight from the system represented an act of agency in a life filled with rules and regulations set by others" (p. 108). Interrogators in 1818 asked eunuch Jihua 紀華 "was it that his shouling taijian had cursed at [him], or that he had gotten into a fight with eunuchs or others, or that he had habitually broken the rules against drinking alcohol and gambling" (p. 121) — the court knew the causes. Dale rejects Kutcher's view that confessions were "formulaic": "eunuch-professed motives... reveal that eunuchs were running away due to problems endemic to the eunuch system" (p. 121). Li Guoqin 李國秦 (1716, Rehe): "Since my leg was hurting I requested a leave of absence from my zongguan taijian... My shouling taijian, Wang Taiping 王泰平, urged me to return [to work] and rudely insulted and cursed at me. I got in an argument with him. Fearing being beaten by him . . . I ran away" (p. 122). Fifteen-year-old Zhao Rong 趙榮, struck twenty-nine times with the *ban* by his *shouling*, "became depressed" and joined two co-workers' escape (pp. 122–123). Gao Jingrui fled "due to losing his eunuch supervisor's dog" (p. 123). Yang Dehai 楊得海 fled grass-cutting because his "daily grain ration was not enough and he was suffering" (p. 122). Cui Zilu 崔子路 (1714) dropped his flute, got forty blows, then fled "fearing that he would receive a beating for not studying well" (p. 115). Pu Jie's family eunuch who brought soup (*tāng*) instead of sugar (*táng*) got "several tens of blows from the large flattened bamboo (daban 大板)... he could not sit down on a stool for several days" (p. 114).
- **Palace gods against beatings.** Pu Yi: ordinary eunuchs made offerings to "the palace gods (snakes, foxes, weasels, and hedgehogs), the protectors of eunuchs, in hopes 'that the palace gods would protect them from the beatings and other forms of ill-treatment from which they often suffered'" (p. 124; offerings "of eggs, dried bean curd, spirits, and cake," p. 133 n.27).
- **Escape routes.** Errands ("going to buy vegetables or wontons"), the barber outside the walls (the queue needed regular shaving; and "A visit to the barber outside the palace would have facilitated a eunuch's attempt to disguise himself as a monk," p. 109 n.7), and leave — Jiaqing required escorts to and from leave destinations (p. 109). Zhao De 趙德 (1763) "was so bold as to escape by horse" (p. 110).
- **The body on the wanted poster.** Notices listed clothing and skin ("a yellow face, and wearing a spring hat, a blue cotton robe, a green cloth unlined outer coat, and green cloth boots," p. 110) rather than features — because "eunuch physical characteristics were a given" (p. 110). Bao Desheng 鮑的生, fleeing exile in 1842, was stopped at Shanhai Pass because he "'looked like a eunuch' (xing si taijian 形似太監)" (p. 111). Young runaways "could and often did pass as non-emasculated males" (p. 110).
- **Monk disguise.** "One disguise that did prove successful... was to pretend they were monks by shaving their heads and donning religious attire. Since eunuchs often retreated to monasteries after retirement, the presence of eunuchs at temples... would not have aroused the suspicions" (p. 111). Zhu Xiang 朱祥, Imperial Library eunuch, "tried to hide so that he would not have to endure a beating... cut off his queue, took the alias of Li Fang 李芳, and joined the staff of the Wanshandian... as a monk" (p. 111). Itinerant monks could also beg (p. 112).
- **Surviving outside.** Five-time runaway Sun Yong'an 孫勇安 (1819, age 44) could not afford a saddle for the hunt and "could not borrow [the money] because I am usually not on friendly terms with my co-workers"; on the run he moved daily, "lied and said that I was a eunuch on leave from an outer palace," and "used up the money [I obtained] from selling the clothes off my back to buy food," then turned himself in (p. 112). Fifteen-year-olds Zhao Rong and Zhao Yu hired out "cutting bramble, bundling sorghum, chopping firewood, and cutting grass" (pp. 112–113). Others hid with kin — "one eunuch stayed with his adopted son's daughter" (p. 113) — or re-hired under aliases in princely households (Hu Jinyu 胡進玉, 1802; Ma Jinzhong 馬進忠, 1763, as "Hao Fu"). Kutcher: in eight years the Fanyichu 番役處 "arrested seventy-five runaway eunuchs and were in 'hot pursuit' of twenty-two others" (p. 112 n.23). Stent's "Palace Squad (Neidaban'er 內大班兒)": "not eunuchs themselves, but know all the eunuchs of the palace... adepts at disguises... spread themselves all over the city, and speedily recapture the deserter" (p. 115). Travel pass *luyin* 路引 and ID *zhizhao* 執照 required (p. 115).
- **Punishment schedule (QDGZXXZL, pp. 116–117).** First offence, self-surrender: sixty blows of the *ban*, one year's pay cut. First offence caught / second surrendered: sixty blows, one year cutting grass at Wudian 吳甸 (horse fodder for the Manchus). Second caught both times: one hundred blows, two years. Third/fourth/fifth: one hundred blows and three/four/five years — then re-employed "but restricted to positions in the outer court" (p. 117). Fleeing Wudian or re-fleeing after five years: "cangue in perpetuity"; four-time Wudian escapers: slaves to soldiers at Dasheng Wula 大牲烏拉 for three years (p. 117). Fleeing to Manchuria/Shandong/Henan/Shanxi: cangue one year plus Heilongjiang slavery, no amnesty (p. 117). Fleeing >500 *li* into Zhili: cangue six months plus Dasheng Wula three years (p. 117). Li Fang 李芳 (1715) "broke free from his iron chains" at Weng Mountain; recommended a fifty-*jin* cangue, emperor sent him back to the mountain (pp. 117–118). But leniency when supply was short: six-time runaways Li Shoutong 李壽通 and Zhang Fujun 張福均 discharged after only four years' cangue; three-timers Wu Degui and Jia Defu reduced to one hundred blows and returned (pp. 118–119). An 1810 decree complained exiled eunuchs "lived in freedom after paying their ransoms... deliberately recommitted crimes in order to be sent back to exile... encouraged them to look upon the land of exile as paradise" (p. 119).
- **Theft and flight.** Ye Fu 葉福 (1770) spent 2,000 *wen* of the kennel budget, sold his *shouling*'s silk coat to cover it, fled (p. 121). Ma Yu 馬玉 pawned silver vessels → Heilongjiang; "Ma's family was forced to pay for the items" (p. 122).
- **Status verdict.** "the punishment of banishment to the frontiers of the empire to serve as a slave was considered more restrictive than the life of a palace eunuch" (p. 120); "To the Qing, eunuchs occupied a type of unfree status outside slavery, as that classification was reserved as a punishment for repeat runaway offenders" (p. 125). "the Qing's reliance on collective responsibility created a culture in which eunuch supervisors abused their subordinates" (p. 123). He Rong'er: "In reality, there was no happiness within the palace . . . People were just like wooden people, going along according to the rules, nobody being allow to make a mistake or differ" (p. 123).

### 2i. Suicide (ch. 7, pp. 126–144; App. 2, p. 202)

- **Opening case.** 1808: Li Guangyu 李光玉 hanged himself in the Imperial Garden; "the Imperial Household Department dumped Li's body in the wilderness and exiled his relatives to Yili to serve as slaves to soldiers" — mother, younger brother, his wife, two nephews and a niece (pp. 126, 135). "a eunuch's suicide posed a risk to the imperial court, his family members, and his fellow eunuchs" (p. 126). Sixty-six digitised cases in the First Historical Archives (p. 126 n.2).
- **Contrast with commoners.** Among the populace suicide "was not regarded as deviant . . . what was deviant was not the suicide act, but the pressuring of a person into suicide" (Hsieh & Spence, p. 128); the question was "Who?" not "Why?" (p. 128). For eunuchs, "Strikingly absent are those questions... such as 'Who drove this person to commit suicide?' Undoubtedly, the Qing did not ask such questions in eunuch suicides simply because the guilty party might well have been members of the Imperial Household and the eunuch management system" (p. 139).
- **The four statutes (App. 2, p. 202).** "All eunuchs and women who use a knife to harm themselves within the palace shall be beheaded immediately. [Those] who are saved from committing suicide shall be [sentenced to] strangulation after the assizes." / "All eunuchs and women who are saved while attempting to commit suicide in the gardens shall be sent to Yili to serve as slaves for soldiers. According to the law, women are authorized to be redeemed." / "[As for] eunuchs and women who commit suicide within the palace, their corpses shall be discarded in the wilderness and their relatives sent to Yili to be given to soldiers to serve as slaves." / "[As for] eunuchs and women who commit suicide in the gardens, their corpses will be spared from being cast out into the wilderness [but] their relatives shall be sent to Urumqi." Eunuchs and *nüzi* 女子 are grouped "due to their shared classification as subordinated groups" (p. 130).
- **Why so harsh.** "Eunuchs were not simply being punished for attempting to decide when and where to dispose of their bodies but also for possessing weapons within the palace, disrupting the Forbidden City, and potentially polluting the imperial residence" (p. 131). Strangulation preserved "somatic integrity" for the spirit's return; the knife "would also have led to blood being spilled" (p. 132). A suicide's *po* 魄 "resembling carbonized wheat-bran" lies under the spot and "unless eradicated, a repetition of the incident is sure to occur" (Bencao gangmu via De Groot, pp. 132–133); ghosts of suicides "did not rest until they had caused another to kill him or herself in a similar manner" (p. 133).
- **Decontamination (Wang Bing 王炳, 1765).** Body carried out; coroner (*wu* 忤) of the Shenxingsi examines; "workers were sent to pull down the now inauspicious building's tiles and the wall next to the rafter. Inside, the floor was removed down three feet (chi 尺), replaced with new soil, and allowed to dry for three days. Thereafter, the wall and tiles were replaced. This process culminated with a lama 喇嘛 being called in to read sutras and sweep the floor with wine" (p. 132).
- **Corpse-dumping as worse than felony.** Even those executed by a thousand cuts got anonymous burial; "a eunuch who committed suicide was considered lower than a criminal" (p. 135). Huang Liu-hung: "Why should I value the body bequeathed to someone by his parents if he does not value it himself?" (p. 135).
- **Collective responsibility.** Zhou Baoshi 周寶石's *shouling* fined six months' pay, fellow eunuchs thirty blows, *zongguan* three months' pay (p. 137); Zheng Furui 鄭福瑞 (1799) found in a well — six eunuchs lost one to two years' pay and food (p. 137). Wang Bing (1770): his eunuch "brothers" (*baoxiong* 胞兄) got two months' cangue, forty blows, and were sent home — "effectively removed from the eunuch system" (p. 140 n.53). Dale: punishment of kin "may have also stemmed from the idea of punishing the eunuch's guarantor" (pp. 136–137).
- **Motives recovered from witnesses.** Ma Feng 馬鳳 (Imperial Kitchen): "fearful that he would receive a beating from his superior for wearing disheveled clothes, he slit his own throat," survived, sentenced "to cut grass in perpetuity at Weng Mountain" (pp. 139–140). Wang Bing (1770, 57 *sui*, serving a princess): drank, "would return home from visiting his family visibly agitated," asked time off "to settle his debts," found hanging from scaffolding (p. 140). Zhou Baoshi, a new recruit who "looked sick and walked very slowly... had not recovered well after his emasculation," found hanging from the *tatan* door; "the coroner's findings, Zhou's emasculation wound had not healed well" (p. 140) — possible "depression brought on by emasculation" (pp. 140–141). Li Chao 李朝 (1758) came to a colleague "crying, asking to borrow money because during a visit with his family his parents had taken his fur gown... his outer jacket... and his padded gown" (p. 138); Gao Yuwang 高玉枉 (1759) found throat slit after running away, identified by mother and brother from a dog-bite scar (p. 138).
- **The knife as statement.** Chinese preferred bloodless means; eunuch statutes single out the knife (*jin dao* 金刀). "Having already violated this precept by becoming emasculated, eunuchs may have considered the possibility of being considered unfilial a moot point... a eunuch's life was essentially defined by the knife... It is conceivable that some eunuchs may have looked to the knife to terminate their existence as such" (pp. 142–143). Suicide as "an act of aggression" (Martin), "the ultimate rebellion" (Wolf), "purposeful suicide" (Hsieh & Spence) — "directed at their employers, the Qing government" (p. 141). Buddhist reincarnation may have made it "merely a gateway into the next life" (p. 141).
- **1923.** After the expulsion "some eunuchs even committed suicide by jumping into the Tongzi River" (the moat) (p. 180).

### 2j. Sickness, retirement, the temple, the grave (ch. 8, pp. 145–169; App. 3–4)

- **Three authorized exits:** "sick leave, retirement due to old age or illness, and natural death"; discharge made one a *weimin taijian* 為民太監 "commoner eunuch," but "eunuch reintegration into society proved to be more of an ideal than a reality" (p. 145).
- **Sick leave.** Seriously ill eunuchs were *required* to go home (pp. 147–148); smallpox quarantine 1674 (p. 147). Kangxi ordered the Board of Rites to "'adopt' (shouyang 收養)" inner-court eunuchs too old or sick to travel: "Every four were to be given a house, and each was to receive a monetary payment of .5 liang... and 1 hu of rice and when the eunuch died he was to be buried in Wucheng" (p. 148) — compare the Ming Wanyiju 浣衣局 nursing bureau (Tsai). Illnesses: pulmonary abscess, spitting blood, dysentery, typhoid, *tan* 痰 phlegm, *tanqi* 痰氣 (p. 147); urological blockages and incontinence, "a nuisance and an embarrassment more than an illness" (p. 150). Fraud: Su Chang 蘇常 (1780) feigned disability → cangue, beating, slave to Oirat Mongols at Yili; his approving superior Li Yu "committed suicide before being officially charged" (p. 150). 1809 decree: ministers must "personally investigate," bedridden eunuchs inspected by a secretary; *zongguan* who co-sign false claims punished (p. 152). Recovered dischargees who did not report back: three years' grass-cutting (p. 151). Seriously ill eunuchs could recuperate "at the Xinglongsi (興隆寺 Temple of Prosperity)" (p. 154); Stent: the Gangbing miao 剛秉廟 and a Guandi temple "were used as wards" (p. 157).
- **Yongzheng's compassion and its limit.** 1726: eunuchs who had served "twenty and thirty years, now that they were old and sick, were suddenly finding themselves ordered to leave the palace. 'If dogs and horses are supported, why not eunuchs?'" — stipend of one *liang*/month and one or two rooms in Neiwufu housing, two *shouling* to manage them (p. 156). 1727: discharged eunuchs in the capital "without a guarantor (baoliu 保留) were to be sought out and returned to their native place"; guarantors "held collectively responsible for the crimes of their guarantees" (pp. 156–157).
- **Retirement at 65 *sui*** "was not automatic; the eunuch also had to prove that he was no longer able to serve" (p. 158). Society's reception: "eunuchs would be cursed as laogong (Old Roosters), or when referring to a foul odor, people might say, 'He stinks like a eunuch' . . . [or refer to] them as 'stinking eunuchs'" (p. 158, the smell = incontinence); "To society, eunuchs were lowly freaks and the subject of ridicule"; "some relatives, fearful of losing face for their association with the disfigured relative, even joined in the castigation" (pp. 158–159). Kidnapped boys "might not even remember where they had come from and thus not know where to return to" (p. 159). Stent's claim of a universal pension (two to six taels, rent-free temples) is "perhaps" true for some; Chang Yonggui 常永貴's pension plus gifts "ended up making just as much as his original salary," but "the government fully expected the majority of eunuchs in the lower echelons... to be self-supporting" (pp. 159–160). "The majority, however, were left with no one but themselves and other eunuchs to rely on after retirement" (p. 160).
- **Buying a place: the temple *shifu*.** "Some retirees would spend their money on land; others chose to donate money to a temple where they became the disciples of a shifu (師父 master) or helped fund the repair of a temple. After leaving the eunuch system, the eunuch could then move to the temple to live out the remainder of his years, 'enjoying both peace and stability.' A small number of powerful and wealthy eunuchs, such as one of the Empress Dowager Cixi's eunuchs, Liu Duosheng 劉多生, even became abbots" (p. 160). Hard workers received *enjiayin* 恩加銀 "gracious reward money" for this purpose (p. 160).
- **Retirement associations.** "eunuch retirement associations housed in these eunuch temples provided retirees with the companionship and peace of living among their own kind without discrimination, as well as the assurance of receiving room and board"; unlike Ming isolation in the Wanyiju "to prevent the revelation of court secrets," "Qing eunuchs spent their later years in self-imposed segregation" (p. 160). Beiping Eunuch Retirement Association (Beiping de Taijian Yanglaoyihui 北平的太監養老義會): fee of 180 *kuai*, "Three years after joining the association, eunuchs would be entitled to retire at the temple and receive free room and board"; some required "an introduction" (pp. 160–161). Those without money "often became itinerant and eventually froze or starved to death" (p. 161). Some temples waived the wait in 1911 (p. 173). Twenty-six eunuch temples around the capital (p. 161; App. 3). "Eunuch tenants tilled fields, raising grains, vegetables, and fruit trees for the market" (p. 162).
- **Gang Tie Miao 剛鐵廟.** Visited by Starrett 1934, "about one hour outside the walls of Beijing near the village of Xiazhuang"; "home to more than thirty eunuchs who supported themselves by growing grains and vegetables for the Beijing market and tobacco" (p. 162); "refuge for old eunuchs" of "more than seventeen acres" (p. 187). Legend (Arlington & Lewisohn, p. 162 n.68): Gang Tie / Gang Bing, a Yongle general left in charge of the palace, "Concerned that his enemies might attempt to harm him by fabricating stories of his relations with the palace women, Gang castrated himself... Gang defended himself by revealing to the emperor his now castrated genitalia, stored in his saddle. Impressed by Gang's loyalty, the emperor made him his chief eunuch and deified him as the patron saint of eunuchs." These temples "resulted in a self-imposed segregation of eunuchs from the rest of society" (p. 162).
- **Burial and the *bao*.** "ancestral cemeteries often denied burial to eunuchs, due to their physical disfigurement. While eunuchs attempted to fool the King of Hades (Yanwang 閻王) into thinking they were complete males by being buried with their bao (寶 treasure), apparently clan members were not always willing to risk upsetting the spirits" (pp. 162–163); Stent: the King of Hades "would transform eunuchs into 'she-mules'" (p. 163 n.72). Buying back one's *bao* from the knifer "was known as 'returning one's flesh and blood to the family' (gurou huan jia 骨肉還家)" (p. 163). Families "chose to disassociate themselves from their eunuch relative rather than lose face... he ceased being of use to the family financially or for connections and was often disowned" (p. 163).
- **Enjizhuang 恩濟莊 ("Village of Imperial Favor").** Built 1734–1738 under Qianlong; "some 2,700 eunuchs were buried at Enjizhuang" but only 300 headstones survived to 1958 after desecration by Feng Yuxiang's soldiers (p. 163). Headstones give "title, name, birth and death dates, and the native place... the age he became emasculated and entered the palace, and the different positions he held" (pp. 163–164); mostly *zongguan*/*shouling*; Li Lianying's tablet "more than 800 characters," while "most eunuchs' burial sites consisted of nothing more than a grave" (p. 164). Ten *liang* death money to families 1688–1821 (p. 165).
- **The burial society.** Under Qianlong "eunuchs had joined together to form the Enjizhuang Lifelong Society... two chapters, the first headed by eight eunuchs from [eight departments]; and the second composed of more than one hundred eunuchs from the Yuchafang... the Yushufang... and the Gudongfang"; "each month every member was required to pay fifty wen in dues towards the five thousand wen burial fee" (p. 165). The Jingshifang skimmed its coffers for cemetery repairs — Tang's "government supervised privately run organization (guandu minban 官督民辦)" (p. 165). 1799: rent from land outside Xizhimen "to purchase incense for the Enjizhuang Temple," later doubled (p. 166); Stent: "a yearly sum to enable a certain number of eunuchs to go to the cemeteries in the spring and autumn to burn paper money, and offer sacrifices" (p. 166). The point: "Retirement associations... provided the benefit of a burial site and the assurance that other eunuchs would stand in for the eunuch's kin and offer paper money, incense, etc. in the form of ancestor veneration after he was gone" (p. 166).
- **Post-1911 coda.** Sun Yaoting, cut by his own father, entered under a dead man's name, was expelled at 22 and used Empress Wanrong's twenty-*liang* gift to pay the association deposit (pp. 177, 187); he "died at the age of 93 at his home in a temple in Beijing, in December 1996" (p. 174 n.18). Blofeld's guide: "Discriminating Chinese gentlemen arriving from the provinces prefer to obtain their guides through the Palace Eunuchs' Mutual Prosperity Association... I was one of his [Li Lianying's] men" (p. 188).

**Design cautions from Dale.** (1) Do not let the castrate characters uniformly accept the faith's "counted as women" doctrine — Dale's strongest finding is that the men themselves did not; model dissent. (2) The corps was violent and factional, not serene — cliques by master, knife fights, trafficking of boys by ex-eunuchs. (3) Every welfare institution (refuge, burial society) was **self-funded by the castrates**, with the state at best donating land and at worst skimming — good basis for the mod's communal-property doctrine. (4) Dale's medical vocabulary ("emasculation" = total excision) is the right register if the Dorley rite is total; if it is testicular only, the recovery/urination events need toning down.
