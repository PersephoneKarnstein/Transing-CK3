## 0. Framing warnings

- **Webb is mostly late antique.** Her sources run from the second to the seventh century, rural Egypt to Constantinople (p. 120). After the fourth century references to dance "become few and far between" (p. 124). Only Theodore the Stoudite (9th c.), Kekaumenos (11th c.), Zonaras and Balsamon (12th c.) are middle Byzantine.
- **Her women's dance tradition is an inference.** She fills the silence with a model drawn from dance in the modern Middle East (pp. 122–24). She calls her questions "ultimately unanswerable" (p. 125) and her readings "very tentative" (p. 122). Treat any household dance tradition as the mod's reconstruction, not a finding.
- **She warns against the orientalist reflex.** Historians call Byzantine dancers "lascivious" when nothing in the sources warrants it (n. 32, p. 143). The same rule binds our loc.
- **Every voice is male and hostile.** The one exception is a papyrus hiring contract between two women (pp. 129–30), from third-century Egypt.
- **Effeminacy words aim at gesture.** Male dancers were "effeminate", "unbecoming", "unmanly" (pp. 125–26). Writing rule 3 keeps hostile lines on the imagined body, not on gesture or dress, and `faith_design.md` §18 already refused Chrysostom's actor line on these grounds. Do not turn Webb's effeminacy vocabulary on daughters.
- **Out of scope.** James's introduction points to Patlagean's work on women who lived as monks (n. 32, p. xxiv). That is trans-men material; it is not used.

## 1. Webb, "Salome's sisters" (pp. 119–48)

### Words for dancers and dancing
- **Performer terms:** *thumelikai* and *scaenicae* ("women of the stage"); *choreutriai*, *orchestrides* (dancers); *mimades* (mimes). They are used loosely: one woman can be all of them (pp. 120–21).
- ***Lugisma*:** the recurring Greek word for dance. It suggests "fluid, winding movements of the torso" (pp. 122–23). Zonaras uses the verb *lugizō* for men (p. 131). Webb notes that the modern *tsifte teli* "definitely involves *lugismata*" (n. 22, p. 142).
- ***Gurisma*** ("twisting, turning"): Kekaumenos (11th c.) uses it for "the characteristic motion of the female body, which should be kept concealed from strangers' eyes" (p. 123). In Webb it is a word about the female body in general, not a dance step. Her reference: *Strategikon*, ed. Wassiliewsky and Jernstedt, p. 44, ll. 19–22, which she owes to Catia Galatariotou (n. 25, p. 143). Webb does not quote the Greek.

### Dance and gender (pp. 125–28)
- **Both sexes danced in public, and both bore the legal disabilities of performers.** Culturally, though, dance was "feminine" and rhetoric "masculine" (p. 125).
- **Men who danced:** Lucian calls mime "a worthless and womanish affair". Christians and pagans alike called male dancing "unbecoming" (*aprepes*) or "unmanly" (*anandros*) (p. 126).
- **Watching was risky too.** Patristic and Byzantine texts describe spectacles as "feminising" those who see or hear them (p. 126). Balsamon (12th c.) says monks must not watch any spectacle that "renders the sense of sight effeminate" (n. 42, p. 144).
- **Masculinity as a fragile achievement.** It was won by training and self-control (*paideia*). Beardless youths tempt monks as women do, and eunuchs "retained the womanly nature of the young boy" (p. 127).
- **Womanhood as the prior state.** In this model the female was "a state that men left behind through puberty". Men could always fall back into it (p. 127).
- **Artemidoros on dreams** (2nd c.). A man dreaming of dancing at home is a good omen. For a woman, appearing in public at all foretells "great and infamous scandals" (p. 128).

### Public performance (pp. 128–31)
- **Court ceremony.** The Theodosian obelisk base in the Hippodrome shows a chorus line of women dancing before the emperor as he crowns a victor (p. 128).
- **Bound to the stage.** The Theodosian Code held stage women to perform "for the public good". They could leave the stage by converting, and the Code closed the deathbed-sacrament loophole (p. 129).
- **Rich households kept their own troupes,** with women dancers (*orchestrides*): Gregory of Nyssa's list of luxuries (p. 129).
- **The papyrus contract** (3rd-c. Egypt). Artemisia, a woman, hires the *krotala* (castanet) dancer Isidora and one companion for a six-day festival. They get donkeys, and their costumes and gold are kept safe in the hirer's house (pp. 129–30).
- **The canons:**
  - Laodicea (4th c.) bars Christians from dancing at weddings and tells clergy to leave before the entertainers arrive (p. 130).
  - **Trullo canon 62** (692) bans "public dances by women which are able to cause much impurity and harm" (p. 130).
- **Zonaras on canon 62** (12th c.) splits the sexes. For men, swaying (*lugizō*) is unseemly; dancing women corrupt the viewer (p. 131).
- **Pelagia's veil.** Before her repentance, Pelagia's veil lies on her shoulders, not over her head (p. 131).

### Who has the power: viewer or viewed (pp. 131–35)
- **Chrysostom's effect.** The sight of a woman on stage leaves an image (*eidolon*) in the viewer's soul: "wounds in the soul". The man carries it home to his family (p. 133).
- **He also attacks the double standard.** He asks how men can watch on stage what would shock them in the agora (p. 133).
- **Webb's reading.** The male viewer is cast as passive, "a prisoner" who suffers (*paschein*). The woman is credited with active power over him, which is the opposite of modern "gaze" theory (pp. 134–35).

### Salome and the women's art (pp. 135–40)
- **Gregory Nazianzen:** "if you must dance … do not dance like the unseemly daughter of Herodias … but like David" (p. 135).
- **Dance as speech.** Basil of Seleucia (5th c.) makes the dance a rival rhetoric. Her gestures are *schemata*, the word for figures of speech, and she "celebrates" (*panēgurizō*, also "makes a speech") (pp. 136–37).
- **Dance as a mother's teaching.** Basil calls Salome's dance her mother's lessons. Theodore the Stoudite (9th c.) says she was schooled in dance (*pepaideusthai*) by her mother (p. 138). It is a female education set against male *paideia*. Ambrose, in Latin, warns mothers to teach girls prayer, not dance (p. 138).
- **Webb's suggestion:** dance "may, in fact, have had a value quite independent of the word" (p. 138).
- **Women's display as statement.** For Chrysostom, a bride at her wedding and women mourning at a funeral make an *epideixis*: a showing, but also a rhetorical display (p. 138).
- **Dance at home.** Chrysostom complains of women dancing before men at weddings, and of hired entertainers bringing the theatre into the house (p. 139).
- **Dance at the martyrs' tombs.** Basil of Caesarea condemns women who dance and sing there, veils fallen. Webb asks whether this is "women expressing devotion in a way particular to them" (p. 139).

### The insiders' view, by analogy (pp. 123–24)
- **Van Nieuwkerk's interviews** with entertainers in modern Egypt find three views:
  - upper-class informants condemn dancers as immoral;
  - those closer to them in status see "working women";
  - the performers themselves "have their own strict moral codes" (p. 123).
- **"The fault is theirs."** Societies can enjoy and condemn dance at once by putting all the blame on the performer (p. 123; one informant quoted in n. 26, p. 143).
- **Webb's corollary.** Calling performers *pornai* is "moral disapprobation rather than … a statement of fact" (p. 123; also n. 73, p. 146).
- **Dance at home.** Webb also uses the unrecorded dance passed "from mother to daughter and performed privately in the home" (p. 124).

### Notes worth keeping
- **n. 60 (p. 145): the Monomachos crown.** Alan Cameron (1973) suggests that the dancers on the crown of Constantine IX Monomachos (r. 1042–55) reflect court ceremonial dance rather than Arab influence. **Secondhand.**
- **n. 71 (p. 146): the feast of Agathe.** Laiou (1986) on a Constantinopolitan festival "that involved women dancing, possibly in the streets". **Secondhand.**
- **n. 77 (p. 146): a defence of mime.** Chorikios's *Apologia mimorum* (6th c.) defends mime on the grounds that such displays were commonly accepted (n. 86, p. 147).

## 2. Tougher, "Byzantine eunuchs" (pp. 168–84): only what the notes lack

- **Ringrose 1994, as Tougher reports it (p. 169).** In "Living in the shadows" (Herdt, ed., *Third Sex, Third Gender*), Ringrose read secular opinion as treating eunuchs as a distinct third sex. The ecclesiastical view, represented by Theophylact of Ohrid, "undermined the distinct engendering of eunuchs". On this reading Theophylact holds that "eunuchs are neither a third sex nor a third gender; they are simply men" (Ringrose 1994, pp. 102, 107). **Secondhand.** See §5, item 4.
- **Ringrose's list of eunuch occupations** (n. 31, p. 182): monks, priests, bishops, **singers, actors, prostitutes** and teachers (Ringrose 1994, pp. 98–99). **Secondhand.** It is the only link in the volume between eunuchs and performance.
- **Castration.**
  - **Justinian's figure.** Legislation of Justinian says that of ninety people castrated only three survived (pp. 175–76).
  - **Masudi's methods.** He names "cutting" and "dragging" (p. 176).
  - **Penectomy was rare in Byzantium.** Liudprand's *carzimasia* are the example (p. 176).
- **Castration after puberty.** Basil of Ancyra (4th c.) warns virgins that such eunuchs "burn with greater and less restrained desire" (p. 177, via Rousselle).
- **Free Roman families.** Paphlagonian farmers cut their sons, educated them and sent them to the capital (p. 179, Synaxarion). Tougher wonders whether Paphlagonia was "particularly given to the production of eunuchs" (n. 80, p. 184).
- **The return of eunuch power.** Kazhdan notes that powerful court eunuchs reappear under the Angeloi at the end of the twelfth century (p. 172).

## 3. Barber, "*Homo byzantinus*?" (pp. 185–99)

- **Kekaumenos, chapter 101** (1070s; Litavrin ed. pp. 202–4). Do not lodge a foreign friend in your house. If you do, "Do not let your wife, daughter or daughter-in-law leave their rooms and look into the house proper." The guest will learn "their movements, their dress and their eyes" (p. 192).
- **Barber's reading:** the house is where masculinity is made, and the threat is the other man (pp. 192–94). The father is the household's model and judge, and rules through his own self-control (p. 193).
- **Imperial virtue as virility.** Psellos praises Basil II as "averse to all that is delicate" (pp. 190–91). Women on the throne were attacked in gendered terms:
  - Theodora and Irene (p. 191);
  - the iconoclast council of 815 blamed Irene's reign on "female frivolity" (n. 23, p. 198);
  - Blemmydes (13th c.) blamed 1204 on the emperors' effeminacy (p. 191).
- **Theophylact's monk** attacks the eunuch "as a feminised male" (n. 6, p. 196, citing Gautier pp. 291–95). This agrees with `hist_tougher_eunuch_byzantine.md` (p. 108).

## 4. Other chapters (searched)

- **Cameron: the Song of Songs.** In Origen the bride of the Song is the Church, and also "the individual soul" (p. 11). This supports writing rule 26 (the Adelphai Bride is the soul of the Song, after Origen).
- **Cameron: gender reversal in the ascetic tradition** (p. 16, Palladius, *Lausiac History* 34.6, cited in n. 63, p. 23). Women could be "praised for being like men", and some voices held that "men could take on womanly qualities as well as vice versa". The Palladius passage itself was not checked.
- **Cameron: women in church.** From the fourth century women were repeatedly forbidden to "baptise, serve at the altar or sing in church choirs" (p. 17). Male monasteries kept "everything female" out (p. 2).
- **Cameron: God beyond gender.** On p. 15, Gregory Nazianzen argues that God has no gender. A further line, "the Father loved so much that He became as woman", appears without its own source; Cameron's note (n. 57) points to Wesche 1986. **Secondhand, unattributed.**
- **Cormack** quotes the Life of Michael the Synkellos praising the empress Theodora's "manly nobility in feminine garb" (p. 36). Women ascending to manhood: not used.
- **Smythe, Hill, Brubaker, Eastmond:** no material on eunuchs, dance or men in women's roles. Smythe mentions "third sex" in a note, as a possible label for imperial eunuchs or ascetics (n. 1, p. 163).

## 5. Cross-checks

1. **`isl_kapitaikin_palatina_dancers.md` §1, "The spinning dancer" (line 30).** It says Kekaumenos calls the spinning movement *gurisma* "after Webb 1997". **Contradiction.** Webb (p. 123) uses *gurisma* for the female body's motion in general, something to hide from strangers. She does not use it of dance or of spinning. Her dance word is *lugisma*, the sinuous torso (pp. 122–23, 131). **Do not name a spinning dance *gurisma* on Webb's authority.** The Kekaumenos passage itself (ed. W–J p. 44, ll. 19–22) is unread.
2. **`hist_neville_byzantine_gender.md` #34 (line 172).** Neville and Barber (p. 192) cite the same seclusion passage, so they agree. Barber adds its chapter (101) and the fear of the guest. It is unknown whether Webb's *gurisma* line (W–J p. 44) comes from the same chapter.
3. **Trullo canon 62.** `byz_betancourt_intersectionality.md` (line 32) and writing rule 24 use it for "no man should wear feminine attire". Webb (p. 130) shows that the same canon bans women's public dances. **The two agree.** The canon covers dancing, dress and masks together, which gives a hostile event two clauses to choose between (§6, row 2).
4. **Theophylact and the Galenic wing.** `faiths/adelphai-dorleyaiou.md` (line 67) and `byz_tougher_eunuchs_antiquity_beyond.md` (row 12) make *In Defence of Eunuchs* the Galenic wing's book: the "some third thing" reading. Ringrose 1994, as Tougher reports it (p. 169), read Theophylact as saying the opposite: eunuchs are "simply men". That fits Ringrose 2003 ("They are men", p. 37; `byz_ringrose_perfect_servant.md` line 15). It sits oddly with Tougher 2008's summary that Ringrose keyed a positive third gender to Theophylact (`hist_tougher_eunuch_byzantine.md` line 161). **Tension, not a blocker.** The wing can still carry the book, as Mullett's caveats already allow. But the Aristotelian answer gains a line: his book calls the cut "men", not a third thing. Check Ringrose 1994, pp. 102–7, and Gautier's text before writing it.
5. **These agree with existing notes:**
   - Metrios and the Paphlagonian farmers (p. 179): Tougher 2008, p. 61; Ringrose 2003, p. 189; `byz_ringrose_prophet_daniel.md` line 73.
   - Michael I's sons, Theophylact (20) and Niketas (14), the future Ignatios (p. 180): "Ignatios, cut at fourteen" (`faiths/adelphai-dorleyaiou.md` line 189).
   - Leo VI's Novel 26 (eunuchs may adopt) and Novel 98 (eunuchs may not marry) (n. 73, p. 184).
   - The three posts barred to eunuchs (p. 172) and eunuch *patrikioi* ranking above bearded ones (n. 28, pp. 181–82): `byz_bury_kletorologion.md`.
   - Danelis's hundred eunuchs (p. 168), Methodios (p. 176), the *carzimasia* (pp. 176, 178).
6. **Eunuch decline.** The faith page dates the decline from 1057 and 1081 (Guilland, Gaul). Tougher adds Kazhdan's point that court eunuchs **return under the Angeloi** (p. 172). Any "decline" era text should not imply that it was final by 1200.
