# Jia Jinhua, *Gender, Power, and Talent* (2018): Tang Daoist priestesses, design reading

**Citation (checked against the epub copyright page).** Jinhua Jia, *Gender, Power, and Talent: The Journey of Daoist Priestesses in Tang China*. New York: Columbia University Press, 2018. ISBN 978-0-231-18444-1 (cloth); e-ISBN 978-0-231-54549-5; LCCN 2017029657; DOI 10.7312/jia-18444. Page numbers below are the print pages carried in the epub's page markers.

**What was read.** The whole book: introduction, chapters 1–7, conclusion, the appendix on Du Guangting, and the notes to chapters 1–3 and the appendix.
- Chapter 5 (Hu Yin's medicine) and chapter 6 (poetry) were read for life facts and gender content. Their technical and literary analysis was skimmed.

**Chapters, one line each:**

- Ch. 1, The rise of the priestesses (pp. 1–17): state Daoism, monasticism, the seven-rank ordination, land allotment, Tang gender relations. **Design-relevant.**
- Ch. 2, Ordained royal women (pp. 18–49): the 28 princesses, palace women, Jinxian and Yuzhen. **Design-relevant.**
- Ch. 3, Leadership, practice and ritual (pp. 50–78): 53 priestesses tabulated; abbesses, preachers, Dunhuang copyists, and the palace convent Yuchen guan. **Design-relevant.**
- Ch. 4, Liu Moran (773–840) (pp. 79–99): the short *Zuowang lun*, three-stage refinement, the Xue Yuanjun inscription. **Design-relevant (doctrine).**

- Ch. 6, The *Yaochi ji* and three priestess-poets (pp. 133–163): Li Jilan, Yuan Chun, Cui Zhongrong.

## 1. The argument, by design question

### A distinct, named, legal estate
- **Numbers.** In the Kaiyuan period (713–741), "550 of the total of 1,687 Daoist abbeys (32.6 percent) were convents" (p. 50). About a third of the Daoist clergy were women.
- **Titles.** Three titles were used (p. 13):
  - *nüguan* 女官, "female official";
  - *nüguan* 女冠, "female headdress" (n. 62 suggests the headdress may come from the Queen Mother's *sheng* crown);
  - *nüdaoshi* 女道士, "female Daoist priest".
- **Legal status.** Law named them as one of "four distinct, gendered religious groups": 道士, 僧, 尼, 女冠 (p. 14). For part of the period they were judged under the *Daosengge* code, not secular law (p. 14).
- **Dress and song.** They had their own vestments, more feminine than nuns' robes, and kept their hair (pp. 13, 28). A tune, "Nüguan zi", was composed in their honour (p. 13).

### Ordination and rank
- **Seven ranks** (pp. 4–5), from lowest to highest: Zhengyi, Gaoxuan, Dongshen, Shengxuan, Dongxuan, Dongzhen, Dadong (also called Bidao).
  - The top title was "Disciple of the Great Cavern and Three Radiances".
- **In practice, about three investitures,** often on three different mountains. Wang Xuming (792–859) received (p. 5):
  - Zhengyi at Luoyang;
  - Dongshen and Dongxuan at Mount Song;
  - Shangqing on Mount Magu.
- **Women's standing.** Women were "essentially equal to priests in procedures of transmission and ordination" (p. 15). Thirteen of the 53 in Jia's table reached the top two ranks (p. 51).
- **A new name.** Ordination gave a Daoist name.
  - Yu Xuanji was probably born Huilan and ordained as Xuanji (p. 166).
  - Princess Xicheng became Jinxian ("Golden Immortal") on ordination, with the Daoist name Wushangdao (pp. 32–33).
- **Marriage.**
  - Zhengyi novices could marry.
  - From the second rank up, celibacy was the rule, but it was not "strictly observed" (p. 10).
  - Yuzhen held the top rank, married after ordination and had two sons (p. 42).
- **Age at ordination** varied widely.
  - Yin Zhiqing of Dunhuang received Zhengyi at 11 (p. 61).
  - Others were ordained as widows, or after their children were grown.
  - Some girls "vowed rather to die than to marry" until their parents gave way (p. 62).

### Who transmitted the registers?
- In every case Jia documents, the registers came **from male masters**:
  - Wang Xuming (p. 5);
  - Liu Moran, who received a text together with her daughter from a priest named Xu (p. 82);
  - Han Ziming and Xie Ziran, ordained by Cheng Taixu (p. 70);
  - the princesses, ordained by Shi Chongxuan (pp. 35–36).
- **Women did have disciples.**
  - Huang Lingwei's disciple was Li Qiongxian.
  - A disciple of Cheng Wuwei signed her stele (n. 12).
  - Women preached in public and ran rites without men.
- **No source in the book shows a woman conferring registers on another woman.** A female-only line of transmission is the Way's own choice, not period practice.

### Housing and property
- **Land.**
  - The rule read: "Daoist priests are allotted thirty mu of land, and Daoist priestesses are allotted twenty mu" (p. 14).
  - A priestess qualified if she could recite the *Daode jing* and was registered in a convent.
  - The land was allotted to the abbey, not to the person (p. 4).
  - The allotment ended with the two-tax law of 780 (p. 14).
- **Wealth after 780.** Convents grew rich from grants, gifts and "astute management" (p. 14).
  - Feng Deyi of the Wutong convent in Chang'an built a mill "as a common estate", against the objections of "everyone" (p. 65).
  - The term is *changzhu* 常住, "permanent property shared by all monastics" (n. 14).
- **Mansions into convents.** Princesses turned their mansions into convents: Jiuhua, Yongmu, Tangchang, Xinchang and Xianyi (p. 25).
- **Lodgers.** Convents rented rooms to scholars. Bai Juyi lived in the Huayang convent in 804–805 (p. 29).
- **Residence.** Most priestesses lived in convents. Six of 53 stayed at home or moved between home and convent (p. 61).

### Kin, fostering and lineage
Jia has nothing on formal adoption, but three patterns bear on the matriline.
- **Recruitment ran through women** (table 3.1):
  - daughters followed their mother (both of Liu Moran's daughters took orders);
  - nieces followed an aunt (Wang Xuming's two nieces);
  - a mother followed her daughter (Priestess Chang, after Lü Xuanhe).
- **Fostering.**
  - Han Ziming raised her dead brother's orphans and saw them married. She sold a mountain villa to pay his debt "to a eunuch". She was summoned to the palace for this virtue (pp. 70–71).
  - Zhi Zhijian, a Buddhist novice who converted to Daoism in 845, took in her dying brother's wife and daughter (p. 72).
- **Female genealogy as gender awareness.**
  - Huang Lingwei restored Lady Wei Huacun's shrines, and Li Qiongxian set Huang's statue beside Wei's (pp. 63–64).
  - Liu Moran signed herself the "female disciple" of Xue Yuanjun (p. 96).

### Political power: Yuzhen and Jinxian
- **The princesses.** About 28 princesses were ordained. There is "no record that any princess ever became a Buddhist nun" (p. 18).
- **Why princesses were ordained** (pp. 26–28):
  - To make merit for their ancestors. No prince was ever ordained for this; Jia calls it the "rulers' gender agenda" (p. 26).
  - Illness.
  - Widowhood.
  - To refuse a foreign marriage. Taiping refused a Tibetan prince in 681. Yong'an refused the Uighur qaghan in 821 and returned the betrothal gift (pp. 26–27).

**Jinxian (689–732) and Yuzhen (691–762):**
- **Shelter from purges.**
  - Their mother was murdered on Empress Wu's order in 692 (pp. 33–34).
  - Their first investiture, in 706, sheltered them during the palace purges (pp. 34–35).
- **Two investitures.**
  - The Cavern-Mystery investiture of 711 was held at Guizhen abbey, inside the palace.
  - The Shangqing investiture of 712 raised both to the Great Cavern rank (pp. 35–36).
- **Opposition to the cost.**
  - Officials sent eight memorials (710–712) against the cost of their two convents in Fuxing ward.
  - The emperor halted the work in name, but it went on (pp. 36–38).
- **Jinxian's endowment.** In 730 Jinxian endowed the Fangshan Buddhist stone canon with sutras, an estate, a wheat farm, an orchard and forests (pp. 39–41).
- **Yuzhen's convents and rites.**
  - She ran three convents: Yuzhen, Anguo in Luoyang, and Lingdu on Mount Wangwu (742).
  - She held the Golden Register fast for the emperor and threw dragon tablets (pp. 43–44).
  - At Wangwu her rain prayer was remembered as "the rain of the princess" (p. 44).
- **Yuzhen's patronage and rescues.**
  - She got the poet Li Bai summoned to court (742).
  - She saved Li Yixun's fief (724).
  - With the eunuch Gao Lishi she saved Prince Xin'an (727) (pp. 45–46).
- **Yuzhen's later years.**
  - In 744 she gave back her princess title and fief (p. 33).
  - In 761 the eunuch Li Fuguo moved the retired Xuanzong out of his palace, and she was sent back to her convent (p. 46).

**Inside the game window:**
- In 877 Emperor Xizong "ordered five of them to return to the palace", because they were "disturbing people outside". The five were Ankang, Yongxing, Tianchang, Ningguo and Xingtang (p. 28; table 2.1 note).
- From 829, ordained princesses received 700 bolts of cloth a year on top of their stipends (p. 28).

### Religious authority in public
- **Preaching.** Women preached in public.
  - Of Tian Yuansu: "listeners filled the hall … spectators [gathered round] like a wall" (p. 67).
  - In Han Yu's poem, the "Maiden of Mount Hua" emptied the Buddhist temples (p. 67).
- **Teaching emperors.** Tian Yuansu taught four emperors at the palace convent Yuchen guan 玉晨觀, which stood behind the Zichen hall (pp. 68–69).
- **The Yuchen guan's palace rites.** Its priestesses held the court's rites without men (pp. 74–77):
  - the Upper Prime festival and the Beginning of Spring;
  - imperial birthdays and death days;
  - **rain prayers, recorded by Dugu Lin, who served in the Hanlin academy in 862–869**;
  - merit services.
  - In 830 Hou Qiongzhen and others "establish[ed] an altar … to present the registers" (p. 75).
- **Released palace women.** Aging palace women were released into convents.
  - A poet wrote of them: "Half of them used to be singers and dancers in the palace" (p. 31).
  - He Youjing died aged 108 and was buried in 864 by imperial order. Two palace eunuchs supervised the funeral, and the tomb had a keeper (pp. 31–32).

### Sites
Jia's 53 priestesses were spread across Chang'an 10, Luoyang 6, Jiangxi 6, Sichuan 5, Henan 5, Gansu 5 and Shanxi 4 (p. 62).
- **Dunhuang.**
  - The Chongxu convent 沖虛觀 had an abbess, Song Miaoxian. She was called to the capital to copy the canon (c. 675) and died there (p. 72).
  - Three Dunhuang priestesses copied the *Benji jing* in fine regular script (pp. 72–73).
  - There were **37 priestesses at Dunhuang in 758** (P. 4072; p. 73).
- **Chang'an:** Zhide, Taiping, Jiayou, Wutong, Yongmu, Xianyi (the aristocratic convent), and the palace Yuchen guan.
- **Luoyang:** Anguo, Kaiyuan.
- **Mountains:**
  - Wangwu: the Lingdu convent, and Yangtai, where Liu Moran's stele of 829 was re-cut in 1094.
  - Song: Jiao Jingzhen; the Dongqing convent.
  - Magu: Wang Xuming's highest ordination; Pang Dezu, summoned to the palace from here in 837.
  - Heng: Xue Yuanjun; Lu Meiniang.
  - Lu: Li Tengkong and Cai Xunzhen, who received a local cult after death.
  - Also Hua; Taibai (Hu Yin); Zhongtiao; Mao (Liu Zhirou); Longhe in Sichuan, where Cheng Wuwei planted "more than ten thousand" trees; Qianqing in Hangzhou (Wang Fengxian).
- **Not in Jia:** Longhu and Wudang. Qingcheng appears only through Du Guangting's Shu context.

### Doctrine of the body (Liu Moran, ch. 4)
- **The short *Zuowang lun*.** Liu had it cut in stone in 829.
  - It says "the body-vessel is the storehouse of the inner nature".
  - To nurture the spirit without the body "is like destroying the house and dwelling outdoors" (p. 93).
  - It ends: "then yin and yang are controlled by myself" (陰陽為我所制, p. 94).
  - Hu Yin says the same: "That which generates all things in turn is controlled by me" (p. 189).
- **Liu's eulogy of Xue Yuanjun.**
  - It opens: "The highest male immortal is called perfected man, and the highest female immortal is called primal mistress" (p. 95).
  - It describes a woman's refinement: "the yin sediment subsides" and "the yang essence is verified" (p. 96).
  - Jia calls it a "germ of later female alchemy", whose direction runs from yin to yang (p. 97). This is the same yang terminus flagged in the Pregadio and Valussi notes.
- **Place in neidan's history.**
  - Liu's three stages (body to qi, qi to spirit, spirit to Dao) foreshadow neidan's Song formula (p. 91).
  - Her text is "among the first" to call the result *jindan* (p. 94).

### Sex change
- The only case in the book is **editorial**.
  - In the hagiography of Wang Fajin, the Sovereign on High predicts Wang will become an "attendant lad" in heaven, yet Du Guangting's *Jixian lu* lists Wang among the female immortals.
  - Jia suggests Du may have changed Wang's sex "to add one more virtuous priestess-immortal to the Wang family" of the Shu rulers (pp. 207–208).
- This is a scholar's conjecture about a compiler's motive. It is **not** a transition story.
- Nothing else in the book bears on male-to-female change.

### The Song turn
- **The system dissolved.** After the Tang, the state-run, integrated Daoist system "dissolved", replaced by local cults and sects (p. 188).
- **Song contempt for Tang women.**
  - Song Neo-Confucians dismissed Tang women's freedom: "The Tang [ruling house] originated from non-Han people, so they did not see women's violation against ritual regulations as abnormal" (p. 6).
  - Zhu Xi called preaching priestesses "indecent women" (*shixing furen* 失行婦人) (p. 68).
  - From the Song to the Qing, critics relabelled them "courtesans" (pp. xxi, 191).
- **A new model woman.** Du Guangting wrote an ideal Daoist woman c. 923.
  - She is beautiful, of the Queen Mother's lineage, compassionate, filial and loyal.
  - The model quietly disapproves of women's public roles (p. 190).
  - It was "followed by female Daoists from the Song dynasty onward", such as Sun Bu'er and the Quanzhen women (p. 191).

### Hostile precedents inside or near the window

**Yu Xuanji** (c. 843–868), of the Xianyi convent:
- **Ordination.** Li Yi, whose concubine she was, abandoned her, and she was ordained in 866 (p. 169).
- **Death.**
  - In 868 she beat her maid Lüqiao to death and was executed by the Metropolitan Governor.
  - Jia notes that elite masters who killed maids were rarely executed. Her death therefore shows that "the social status of a Daoist priestess was still very limited" (p. 172).
- **Her poem at the Chongzhen abbey:** "I hate the gauze gown / which covers my verses … I envy the names on the list" (自恨羅衣掩詩句, 舉頭空羨榜中名, p. 185).

**Li Jilan** (d. 784):
- She was summoned to the Yuchen guan in 783.
- During Zhu Ci's revolt the rebels took her and forced her to write him a poem.
- When Emperor Dezong returned, he had her beaten to death (pp. 150–151).

**Wang Fengxian** (d. c. 887):
- **In the histories.**
  - She was a wandering adept, called "sorceress" and "evil nun".
  - In Yangzhou in 887 she told the rebel generals that "a great man must die". Gao Pian and his kin were then killed.
  - Afterwards "all rewards and punishments … were decided by her" (pp. 201–203).
- **In Du Guangting.** He rewrote her as a saint who died on Mount Qianqing, Hangzhou, in 886, aged 48 (p. 202).

**Du Guangting** (850–933):
- **Career.** He failed the examination in the Xiantong era and was ordained on Mount Tiantai. He served Emperor Xizong, then the Former Shu.
- **The Shu court.**
  - In 923 he ordained the Shu ruler Wang Yan.
  - Wang Yan's court "dressed themselves and other palace ladies and maids like Daoists and immortals" (p. 197).
- **The Walled City.** His *Jixian lu* makes the Queen Mother of the West "the ancestress and head of the clan of female immortals", ruling in the Walled City, Yongcheng 墉城 (p. 197).
