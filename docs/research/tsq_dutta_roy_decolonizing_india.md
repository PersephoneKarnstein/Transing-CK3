# Dutta and Roy, "Decolonizing Transgender in India" (TSQ 2014): design reading

**Citation.** Aniruddha Dutta and Raina Roy, "Decolonizing Transgender in India: Some Reflections," *TSQ: Transgender Studies Quarterly* 1, no. 3 (August 2014): 320–337. DOI 10.1215/23289252-2685615. In the special issue "Decolonizing the Transgender Imaginary" (see `tsq_decolonizing_issue_2014.md`).

**What was read.** The whole essay, references included. Page numbers are the journal's.

**Authors.** Dutta is an ethnographer in US academia (the essay uses zie/hir for Dutta). Raina Roy is an activist in West Bengal and Delhi (she/her). Both write as members, to different degrees, of the kothi-hijra communities they describe (pp. 320, 322).

**Quotes.** Verbatim, each under 25 words. Unnamed people the essay quotes are paraphrased.

**Hard rule.** Kothi and hijra communities are living communities. Nothing here becomes a faith, a rite or an in-game group.

---

## 1. Relation to existing notes

This is the strongest piece of the "decolonial" line on India in the TSQ set, and it is more careful than the work Bhatt attacks. It agrees with her on some things and disagrees on the central one.

**Where it agrees with Bhatt.**
- It rejects the "Westernization" charge against trans and gay identities in India. Calling them inauthentic would "mimic right-wing religious and political viewpoints" (p. 323). Bhatt makes the same point: queer people in the non-West are squeezed between empire and homegrown reaction (`book_bhatt_brown_trans_les.md` §2.5).
- It refuses to rank androgyny above bodily transition: "We do not seek to rehearse the facile critique of transsexuality as conformist and reproducing binary gender" (p. 331). Bhatt calls the same critique "facile, absurd" (`book_bhatt_third_sex.md` §1).
- It condemns respectability. Some NGO leaders told kothis to behave like "women" and not like hijras, and "hijra-like" became an insult inside trans groups (p. 331). Bhatt's "respectability is betrayal" says the same.
- It sees penetrability. The slur for the feminised, penetrated partner is far worse than the word for the man who "plays around with boys" (p. 324). This is Bhatt's ch. 3 theory of penetrability in one example.
- It is suspicious of the state's certificates: the 2014 ministry report and the NALSA ruling would validate gender by committee or psychological test (pp. 329, 334).

**Where it disagrees with Bhatt.**
- Its core claim is that the modern demand for one stable gender is itself a colonising pressure. Under it, "A trans woman, to be respected as such, has to be seen as really and only a woman" (p. 332). It says hijras "may be both women and not-women" (p. 333) and calls for "deontologizing transgender" (p. 334). Bhatt holds the opposite: trans women are women and change sex, and the "not-woman" reading of hijras is the anthropologists' error (Nanda's "I am a lady", `book_bhatt_brown_trans_les.md` §2.9).
- It accepts the hijra self-description as distinct from men and women, citing Reddy and Nanda (p. 322). Bhatt reads that literature as third-sexing.
- It criticises NALSA for fixing identities. Bhatt criticises NALSA for third-sexing hijras. Same target, opposite reasons.
- Bhatt does not cite this essay. But her attack on Western "decolonial" gender studies (ch. 4–5) covers its frame.

**Mod position.** The mod's faiths hold Bhatt's view: daughters are women. This essay does not move that. Its useful parts are the ones where it agrees with her.

## 2. Argument in brief

- Since about 2007, Indian HIV funding has made "transgender" (TG) the official category, first meaning hijras, later an "umbrella" (pp. 326–328).
- The umbrella turns regional words (kothi, dhurani, meti and others) into mere "local" variants, though they span several regions (p. 328). Scale is made in the act of defining, not found (p. 328).
- The umbrella also imposes borders it claims to dissolve: cis/trans and homo/trans. Kothis, once counted as MSM, became contested. At one 2010 consultation, some activists accused others of being men "masquerading as TG to gain funds" (p. 329). Kothi dropped out of official use (p. 329).
- Inside the community, people still hail each other as kothi; with funders they say TG. Only those with English and training can move between the two registers (p. 330).
- New models of trans womanhood bring a hierarchy: some who seek full surgery scorn the older hijra operation as "less", and that scorn makes hijras and non-transsexual kothis seem less than human (p. 331).
- Even plural lists of identities assume each person has one consistent identity (pp. 331–333). Hijras may hold female papers and still mark themselves as hijra in public (p. 333). Multiple ID cards cause real harm in clinics (p. 333).
- Conclusion: keep the category for services, but let people use it without having to identify with a set definition; and dismantle the funding hierarchy that runs from Western metropoles down to small community groups (pp. 334–335).

## 3. What is usable

1. **Resource-gated identity breeds border wars.** When a share, a stipend or a grant depends on being the right kind of person, the community starts policing who is "really" one (p. 329). The mod has such resources: the Garland's temple-street share, the Houses' adoption and inheritance, alms and refuge. This gives a reaction event.
2. **No road is "more complete".** The scorn for the older operation as "less" than full surgery (p. 331) warns against any ranking of roads to womanhood. Bhatt would agree: she rejects ranking in the other direction too. The mod has several roads (the knife; re-embodiment, which a game rule can switch off; the embryo; the vow; the crossing).
3. **Certificates are a regime.** Committees and tests that validate gender (pp. 329, 334) back the mod's existing rule that gates "recognise, they do not test" (Serano revisions, `faith_design.md` §12).
4. **Penetrability, again.** The slur gap (p. 324) supports Bhatt §5 row 7. No new text needed.

## 5. Cautions

- Do not adopt the essay's "both women and not-women" frame for any daughter. It contradicts the mod's premise and Bhatt's.
- Do not use kothi, dhurani, meti or other terms the essay records. They are living community words, some from a guarded argot (p. 325).
- The essay's history starts in the 1990s. It says nothing about the medieval period and supports no in-period claim.
- Respect its own caution against a West/non-West dualism (p. 322). The mod's faiths are not "the authentic local" against a foreign "transgender". They are inventions with modern politics, and the narrator says so.
