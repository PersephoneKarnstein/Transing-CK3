## 1. The argument in brief

- **The historical Kōtai is a silhouette** (pp. 5–6).
  - Her signature verses (*phala śruti*) give only the name Kōtai, a town Putuvai (later identified with Śrīvilliputtūr), and a tie to the poet Viṣṇucittaṉ (Periyāḻvār).
  - In the poems he is "the chief-priest" (*paṭṭar pirāṉ*), not a humble garland maker, and no kinship is stated (p. 142).
  - Periyāḻvār's own poem places him in the 9th century, which makes Āṇṭāḷ "his contemporary" (pp. 5–6; n. 7, p. 43).
  - Dating by the astronomy of the *Tiruppāvai* is "extremely unreliable" (n. 7, p. 43).
- **The two poems are opposites** (pp. 11–14, 23–27).
  - The *Tiruppāvai* is thirty verses, communal, set in Mārkaḻi, in a plural "we" of cowherd girls.
  - The *Nācciyār Tirumoḻi* is 143 verses. It opens in Tai with a solitary vow to Kāma and is mostly a lonely "I", where companions, birds and clouds are useless or hostile (pp. 12, 26).
  - Union comes only in the collective, mythic world of Vṛndāvana or in a dream (pp. 26–27).
- **The goddess is later than the poet** (pp. 6, 10, 225).
  - The Śrīvaiṣṇavas made the Tamil canon revealed text from the 11th century. They then made its poets emanations of Viṣṇu by the 13th, and recast Kōtai as the consort Bhū Devī (p. 6).
  - That apotheosis "began around the late twelfth century" (p. 225).
  - With each retelling she becomes "more divine, and her love more innocent, asexual, and non-threatening" (p. 10).
- **The commentaries are "texts of enjoyment"** (pp. 31–33). Periyavāccāṉ Piḷḷai (b. 1228) was the first to comment on both poems, and set the narrative that joins them into one life (pp. 31–32).

## 2. What matters for the design

### 2.1 The Mārkaḻi vow (pp. 11–12, 17–22, 81–96, 133)

- **The vow.** The *pāvai nōṉpu* was kept by unmarried girls in Mārkaḻi (mid-December to mid-January) "to secure a virtuous husband". In the poem, Kṛṣṇa is the husband (pp. 11–12).
  - The bath is likely adapted from the Caṅkam girls' bath in Tai (*tai nīrāṭal*, Paripāṭal 11) (pp. 17–18).
  - The refrain and genre are shared with Māṇikkavācakar's Śaiva *Tiruvempāvai* and with Jain *pāvai* poems (p. 19; n. 27, p. 46).
- **The rules (TP 2; p. 88).** Rise before dawn and bathe. Abstain from ghee and milk. Do not adorn yourself. Do not gossip. Give alms. Sing his names.
  - The bath is at *brahmamuhūrta*, about two hours before sunrise (p. 86).
  - The rewards are rain, harvest and cattle for the whole country, as well as union (pp. 18, 89).
- **The companions.** TP 6–15 are the "waking-up" verses. The girls rouse each friend at her threshold, never entering her house (pp. 20, 94). The poem insists that god is reached only "in the company of like-minded beings" (p. 86), and each reader becomes "a gopī who has slept too late" (p. 20).
- **The twist (TP 29).** The girls refuse the *paṟai*-drum they came for and ask only to belong to him (pp. 19, 22–23).
- **Men as the sleeping girls.** Later commentators (Vanamamalai Jīyar "and other later Śrīvaiṣṇava commentators") identify each sleeping girl of TP 6–15 with a male Āḻvār, for example Nammāḻvār in TP 8 or 14. Venkatesan calls this "anachronistic" (table 2, p. 94; p. 81). No date is given for these commentators.

### 2.2 The worn garland: date and source (pp. 4–5, 43, 142, 223–225)

- **The story** (Venkatesan's retelling, p. 4). Viṣṇucittaṉ finds the baby under a *tulasī* plant. Kōtai secretly wears the god's garland. Her father catches her and withholds the garland. Viṣṇu tells him in a dream that he prefers the worn one, hence *cūṭikkoṭuttavaḷ*, "she who gave what she had worn".
  - The retelling is "an amalgamation of several oral and written hagiographic accounts" (n. 2, p. 43).
  - "Recorded in oral and written hagiographies since the eleventh century" (p. 5).
  - **It is not in her poems.** The poems never tell it.
- **The earliest witness is the *taṉiyaṉ*.** The Tamil laudatory verse to the *Tiruppāvai* is attributed to Uyyakoṇṭār (c. mid-10th century, a disciple of Nāthamuni). It praises her as the one "who gave the lord of Araṅkam ... the garland of flowers she had worn" (p. 223).
  - The attribution is traditional; Venkatesan does not test it.
  - The Sanskrit *taṉiyaṉ* of Parāśara Bhaṭṭar also has the garland: "she bound him with the garland she had worn" (p. 224). Venkatesan dates Bhaṭṭar inconsistently: 11th century (p. 143), "late 12th century" (p. 224), "twelfth-century" (glossary, p. 242).
  - An anonymous *Nācciyār Tirumoḻi* *taṉiyaṉ* has her adorn her hair with the garland meant for the lord of Araṅkam (p. 225).
- **Grade.**
  - The garland legend is *attested in period as tradition*: at least the 11th–12th century, and the mid-10th century if Uyyakoṇṭār's attribution holds.
  - The early verses tie the garland to **Srirangam** (Araṅkam), which supports the seat.
  - The "hair in the garland" detail in the reading list is not in Venkatesan. Her version has the father catch Kōtai wearing it (p. 4). Drop the hair, or source it elsewhere.
- **The later frame.**
  - Viṣṇucittaṉ as the "humble garland maker" is the hagiography's image, not the poems' (p. 142). Orr also suggests the garland-maker image may reflect late-Chola temple practice (Orr, ch. 4 n. 35, pp. 239–240).
  - The Śrīvilliputtūr Āṇṭāḷ temple was "probably built around 1571" under the Madurai Nāyakas (n. 8, p. 43), which is **post-period**. Her 9th-century Putuvai temple is the Vaṭapatraśāyi (Viṣṇu) temple (pp. 4, 133).

### 2.3 The dreamed wedding (NT 6; pp. 12, 25–27, 33, 163–165, 204–206)

- **NT 6, "Vāraṇam āyiram"**, is the dream of the full wedding rite, told to a friend in every refrain ("I dreamed it, friend") (pp. 163–165). Her brothers lead her to the fire; Durgā as the god's sister drapes the bridal cloth (6.3, 6.9; p. 205).
- **The dream is not the end.** In NT 7 the heroine wakes to find "her grand wedding ... was but a cruel deception" (p. 27). For Periyavāccāṉ Piḷḷai, a dream is "like lightning" and cannot last (p. 206). The god sent it because she was too alone to bear the wait (p. 205).
- **The poem has no secure ending.** It closes open-endedly in Vṛndāvana (pp. 23–24, 27). The hagiography supplies the ending: at Srirangam she climbs onto the image and "simply disappeared" (p. 5).
- **A living liturgy.** NT 6 is sung today at Śrīvaiṣṇava Brahmin weddings, where the bride is dressed as Āṇṭāḷ (pp. 33, 204). That is living community practice: do not quote it as liturgy.
- **Refusal of a mortal groom.** The line is in NT 1.5 of the translation (p. 148), though the introduction's note says 1.4 (n. 1, p. 43). The same verse continues "I cannot live", which is excluded by the no-suicide rule.

### 2.4 Male voices taking her words (pp. 6–7, 32, 34–36, 38, 133, 199–200)

- **Commentary (13th century, in period).** Periyavāccāṉ Piḷḷai opens his *Tiruppāvai* commentary by saying it is natural for a woman to love a man but "difficult for a man to love another man". That is why the other Āḻvārs "had to assume the guise of women" (p. 32). This is the in-period theological statement closest to the faith's premise. It treats the man's womanhood as a *guise*, and the faith inverts that.
- ***Araiyar*.** A "hereditary ritual performance tradition performed by a patrilineal community of Brahmin men". It survives at Srirangam, Śrīvilliputtūr and Āḻvār Tirunakari, and combines gesture with recitation (p. 35).
  - In the Mārkaḻi Festival of Recitation, they perform the pearl divination (*muttukkuṟi*) using NT 4. At Srirangam and Āḻvār Tirunakari the heroine is a generic lovesick girl; at Śrīvilliputtūr she is Āṇṭāḷ (pp. 35–36, 199–200).
  - At Śrīvilliputtūr's Mārkaḻi bathing festival, "the Araiyar ... assumes Āṇṭāḷ's identity" and recites TP 26 at the god's gate (p. 133). That festival dates "probably ... since the mid-seventeenth century" (p. 34), which is **post-period**.
  - Venkatesan gives no medieval date for the *araiyar*. Their origin with Nāthamuni is the tradition's own claim (reading list, item 9).
- **The caution.** Today "only Brahmin men are allowed to recite the āḻvār songs, including the Tiruppāvai and Nācciyār Tirumoḻi" in temple worship (p. 38). The male voice in her words is also an *exclusion of women*. Orr shows the same for the Chola-period *Tiruvāymoḻi* (Orr, p. 108). Do not romanticise the *araiyar*.
- **Srirangam's festival.** At Srirangam's Mārkaḻi Festival of Recitation, Āṇṭāḷ's bronze is absent from the row of Āḻvārs because she is counted as consort, while her poems open the recitation (pp. 6–7). This is modern practice.

## 3. Corrections to the candidate (gate and line)

1. **The vow is grounded. The thirty dawns are a fair abstraction.** The poem is one dawn's quest told in thirty verses. Daily dawn bathing through the month is how the tradition and the festival read it (pp. 20, 34–35, 86). Use "thirty dawns", but count kept dawns rather than requiring a perfect thirty.
2. **The companion step is the most grounded part.** Use the poem's own form: she must *wake* a friend at her threshold, and the friend must *wake* another (pp. 20, 94). Add the abstentions (no feasting, no adornment, no gossip, give alms) as the stress costs (p. 88).
3. **The worn garland: change the discovery.** The temple does not find a hair. Her guardian or the garland keeper catches her wearing it, and the god's preference is revealed in someone else's *dream* (p. 4). The garland goes to the lord of Araṅkam in the earliest verse (p. 223). Mark it as hagiography: "the tradition says", 10th–12th-century witnesses.
4. **The dream must not be the success.** In the poem the dream is followed by waking and loss (p. 27). The hagiography's success is disappearance into the image (p. 5). Orr shows this is the only model South Indian myth offers for a woman marrying God (Orr, ch. 6 n. 11, pp. 258–259). Disappearance reads as death, and the brief forbids it.
   - Make the dream the *sign* (step 4).
   - Make success the morning after: she wakes and the body has followed the dream, a reconstruction in the faith's voice.
   - The god's garland is given *to her* (compare the 15th-century honor, Orr p. 175).
   - She stays in the world, owns and gives.
5. **The order of months.** The *Tiruppāvai* is Mārkaḻi and communal. The *Nācciyār Tirumoḻi* begins in Tai and is solitary (p. 12). An optional ordering:
   - Mārkaḻi: vow, companions, garland.
   - Tai: the Kāma vow (NT 1) and the dream.

   That gives one hard solitary stretch after the communal one.
6. **"Daughter of God" and the bride are different strands.** Āṇṭāḷ supplies the bride; the temple women supplied property, not brides (see the Orr note). The line text should not call the street's women brides.
7. **The *araiyar* as "Marked" court position.** Keep it as a *man who performs the girl's part*. Grade it as tradition with an undated origin. Note that it is a Brahmin men's right that excludes women (pp. 35, 38). Never present the *araiyar* as the faith's institution.
8. **Holy sites.**
   - Śrīvilliputtūr's Āṇṭāḷ temple is c. 1571. In period, the c_madurai site should name Putuvai's Vaṭapatraśāyi temple, or simply "Putuvai, where the girl wove her songs".
   - Her poems also name Vēṅkaṭam (NT 8) and Tirumāliruñcōlai near Madurai (NT 9) (p. 25). Tirumāliruñcōlai strengthens c_madurai.

## 4. Design implications

**Gate: "Keep the Mārkaḻi Vow" (revised)**
1. **Wake a friend** (TP 6–15). At the start of Mārkaḻi, choose at least one friend, lover or sister. Each companion who also keeps the vow raises the odds. A companion who "sleeps late" can be roused by a scheme or event. Possible title: "She who woke the others".
2. **The dawns** (TP 1–2). Kept dawns are counted. War, travel and illness cost dawns. The abstentions bring stress for gluttonous or vain characters and a piety bonus for giving alms.
3. **Not for the drum** (TP 29). At the end of the month she is offered a reward: gold, a title, or the temple's drum honor. Refusing it ("we did not come for the drum") is the hidden best choice and raises the final odds.
4. **The worn garland.** She wears the god's garland first.
   - She is caught by her guardian or the garland keeper, who withholds it from the god.
   - That night someone dreams that the god wants the worn garland.
   - Choice: own it, which risks the temple's censure but gains the faith's prestige, or hide it.
5. **The dream, told to a friend** (NT 6). She dreams the wedding and must tell it to a companion ("I dreamed it, friend"). A dream told to no one does not count, which ties back to step 1.
   - On waking, success: the body has followed, a reconstruction in the faith's voice.
   - Or failure: "the dream was lightning; next Mārkaḻi", with the odds raised.

   No disappearance. The god sends her the garland in the morning.

**Short lines (mod's own renderings, for loc).**
- "Wake, girl, the birds are already singing." (from TP 6)
- "We did not come for the drum." (TP 29)
- "I dreamed it, friend." (NT 6 refrain)
- "His conch is dear to him; are my bangles not as dear to me?" (NT 11.1)
- "Those who love me, I love in return": words Kōtai gives to the lord of Araṅkam (NT 11.10, p. 5). Use for the Srirangam holy site.
- "She who gave the garland she had worn." (epithet; *taṉiyaṉ*, p. 223)

Avoid NT 1.5's "I cannot live" and NT 13.8, where she threatens to tear out her breasts. Both breach the brief's rules on suicide and self-harm.

**Doctrine (faith's voice).**
- From Piḷḷai: "for the other poets the girl was a guise" (p. 32). The Garland answers: "the guise was the truth, and the Lord kept the garland."
- From the later commentaries: the sleeping girls are the poets themselves (p. 94). The Garland says: "every Āḻvār was a girl woken for the vow." This claim is in the faith's voice. The narrator says the identification is a later commentarial reading.

**Hostile and dissenting reaction lines.**
- A Śrīvaiṣṇava teacher: "She was Bhū Devī. No man becomes what a goddess was born." This uses the 12th–13th-century apotheosis (pp. 6, 205).
- A Garland dissenter: "One Lord and every soul a bride: we have moved the court into heaven." (reading-list risk)
- A woman reciter, barred from the temple: "Your men sing her words in the temple, and I may not." (p. 38)

**Living communities.**
- Āṇṭāḷ is a goddess and a saint in a living tradition. Śrīvilliputtūr, the *araiyar*, the Godā Maṇḍali women's singing circles (p. 38) and NT 6 at weddings are all living practice.
- The faith venerates Āṇṭāḷ; she is never its founder and never trans. She was a woman.
- No Śrīvaiṣṇava institution is presented as the Garland's.
