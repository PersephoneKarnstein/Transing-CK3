# Damico, *Beowulf's Wealhtheow and the Valkyrie Tradition* (1984), design reading

**Source:** Helen Damico, *Beowulf's Wealhtheow and the Valkyrie Tradition*. Madison: University of Wisconsin Press, 1984. xiii + 271 pp. ISBN 0-299-09500-2 (13-digit 978-0-299-09500-0). Reading list item 39.
**Read:** Preface, chapters 1–4 (pp. 3–86) and the chapter 3 notes (pp. 200–205) in full; the Afterword (pp. 179–80) in full. Chapters 5–7 (Helgi's valkyrie-brides, the saga queens, Yrsa) read for the *dís* = sister passage (pp. 92–93), Yrsa's hall-guard (pp. 143–45), the second cup (pp. 166–67) and the altar-ring (pp. 169–70). Page numbers are the book's. Quotations are verbatim and short; Old English translations are Damico's.
**Serves:** the Bædling faith (`tm_baeddel*`): the Anglo-Saxon side, Óðinn's host, the planned flyting (`norse_clover_unferth_flyting.md` row 9, "Pour the Cup"), and `tm_pour_the_mead_interaction` (`norse_acker_larrington_poetic_edda_essays.md` row 12).
**Health warning:** the book is forty years old, and its etymological readings are explicitly speculative. Damico herself calls one key reading (*hos* = "troop of warriors") "untenable" (p. 74), and she keeps her Wealhtheow-Yrsa identification "provisional" (p. 180). Use it for the Old English evidence and the cup ritual. Do not treat its thesis (Wealhtheow is a valkyrie) as established fact.

## 1. The argument in brief

**Wealhtheow in the poem (ch. 1).**
- The queen enters right after the Unferth flyting is over: "the altercation between Beowulf and Unferth has ended" (p. 10). She carries the cup first to Hrothgar and then to Beowulf, and Beowulf makes his vow over it (lines 611–41).
- Damico reads her as "both a parallel figure to Unferth and a contrasting one" (p. 8). Like the king's spokesman, she is a counsellor who can stir dissension, but she works in the opposite register.
- She lists the roles packed into the queen: "the queen-mother prophetess, the exemplary hostess, the queen-politician, the erotic bride, and the warrior-woman" (p. 7).
- Her last line to Beowulf claims the warriors' obedience: *druncne dryhtguman doð swa ic bidde*, "the warriors flushed with drink do as I bid" (1231; pp. 6, 144).

**The cup is a hall office (ch. 2).**
- *Maxims I* (81–92) gives the rule. At the mead-ceremony the queen "first she shall greet the protector of the princes, quickly shall offer the first cup to the hand of the lord, and know good counsel" (pp. 21–22).
- Ordinary serving was someone else's job: "The serving of drink ... is a function of official cup-bearers" (p. 22). The *byrelas* poured the wine (1161) and a thane bore the ale-cup (494–96).
- The queen's cup "is ceremonial and not to be interpreted as a quotidian activity" (p. 22). So her office is the **first cup and the counsel**, not pouring.

**Women's public power.**
- Tacitus, *Germania* 18: the bride and groom exchange arms at the wedding. Under *Germania* 8, women came to the field and urged on the fighters, and seeresses were heeded (p. 24).
- Damico finds "no prohibition of or aversion to female rule among the Anglo-Saxons" (p. 25). Her examples: Cynethryth, Eadburh, Æthelflæd, Emma.

**Women of war in Old English (chs. 2–3).**
- Elene is *guðcwen*, "warrior-queen" (p. 27). Wealhtheow's *folccwen* matches the martial *folccyning* (pp. 27–28).
- *beahhroden*, "ring-adorned", may mean "shield-adorned" (pp. 33–34). Damico ties it to Saxo's shield-maidens at Brávellir under Wisna and to the ringed figure on the Möne collar.
- She notes "There are no intimate relationships between females in Anglo-Saxon poetry, not even between mother and daughter" (p. 20).

**The Old English *wælcyrge* is a monster, not a bride (ch. 3, pp. 44–45, 53).**
- Glosses of the 8th to 11th centuries render the word as the Erinyes, Allecto, Tisiphone, the Parcae and Bellona (p. 44, n. 12). Aldhelm's glossator also puts *wælcyrge* for *veneris*, with "goddess" as a synonym (p. 48).
- Wulfstan's *Sermo Lupi* (1014) lists "witches and valkyries" with harlots, infanticides, fornicators and robbers as the ruin of England (p. 53). Cnut's letter puts the *wælcyrian* in a "similar censurable context" (p. 44).
- *The Wonders of the East* says valkyries were begotten in Gorgoneus and gives a beast "the eyes of the valkyrie" (p. 44).
- Two charms. In "For a Sudden Stitch", "the mighty women made ready their strength, and they sent forth screaming spears" (p. 45). The bee-charm's *sigewif*, "victorious women", is "the one possible exception to the prevailing view of the valkyries in Anglo-Saxon literature as baneful" (p. 45).
- In the poems the grim type is Grendel's mother (p. 46) and Modthrytho (pp. 46–49).
- **Flag:** Damico calls Grendel's mother "ambisexual": she has *idese onlicnes*, is called *sinnigne secg*, and takes masculine pronouns (p. 46). She also quotes Saxo's shield-maidens with "bodies of women ... [but] the souls of men" (p. 46). Both run against the rule that a daughter's courage is never "manly". Do not use either.

**The oldest evidence in Britain (p. 52, n. 26–27).**
- The Housesteads altars on Hadrian's Wall (AD 222–235) were raised by Frisian and Germanic soldiers from Twenthe. They are dedicated to Mars Thincsus and the paired Alaisiagae: Beda and Fimmilena, Baudihillia ("Ruler of Battle") and Friagabis ("Giver of Freedom").
- Whether these are valkyries is "a matter of controversy" (n. 27). One school reads Thincsus as god of the *þing* and Beda and Fimmilena as "personifications of legal processes".

**Óðinn's companions were not always women (p. 53).**
- "the gender of Odin's emissaries on the battlefield had changed. His female companions had been displaced by dancing youths". The evidence is the Sutton Hoo helmet and the Torslunda dies.
- After that come the memorial stones and pendants with the cup-bearing woman at Valhalla's door.

**The *full* is an oath-cup (pp. 54–56).**
- Old Norse *full* is the vessel of the *blót*, "over which sacred oaths were pledged" (p. 54). In *Beowulf*, *ful* appears only in the two feast scenes. Four of its six uses for Wealhtheow's vessel are explicitly an oath-cup.
- *Hákonar saga góða* ch. 14 gives the order of toasts: Óðinn's *full* for victory, then Njǫrðr's and Freyr's for harvest and peace, then the *bragarfull* (p. 55).
- *Ynglinga saga* ch. 36: the heir stands, vows over the *bragarfull*, drains it, and is "led to the high seat that had been his father's. He had then come into his entire patrimony" (p. 55).
- Vows over it are unbreakable and "sealed in the gods" (Grønbech, p. 55).
- Damico's key admission: "What is missing from the Old Norse sequences, and is very prominent in the Beowulfian, is the female figure" (p. 56). In the Norse texts the chieftain blesses the cup. Only *Beowulf* gives it to a woman.

**The name and its words (ch. 4).**
- *Wealhþeow* reads first as "foreign slave". *Wealh* is the Briton or Welshman, whose wergild was about half an Englishman's (p. 59).
- Gordon's alternative is "chosen servant", servant of a god (pp. 64–65). Damico calls the resulting composite portrait (*wealh*, *val-*, "valkyrie") a reading (p. 66).
- Valkyries are *nunnur Herjans*, "nuns of Herjan", and *óskmeyjar*, "'adopted or chosen' maids" (p. 38).
- OE *ides* matches ON *dís* (p. 69). The *dís* has a cult at the *hǫrgr*: Freyja's stone is worn glassy with offerings (p. 70).
- Wealhtheow's *mægþa hos*, "troop of maidens" (924), may echo the valkyrie troop (pp. 72–74). Damico then retracts the strong reading (p. 74).
- *friðusibb*, "pledge of peace": Germanic *frið* means "introducing a peace-power among the disputants", an armed peace, not disarmament (Grønbech, p. 85).

**Sister, hall-guard and the dísarsalr (chs. 5–7).**
- The primary sense of *dís* is "sister". Skaldic diction calls sisters *dís* and *jóðdís* (*Skáldskaparmál* 68, p. 92). OE *ides* can mean "daughter" in birth and inheritance contexts (p. 93).
- In *Svipdags þáttr* Queen Yrsa has her own hall and her own troop. She ends the quarrel between Svipdag and the berserkers: "the queen now made peace between them" (pp. 143–44).
- The neck-ring may be a *stallahringr*, an altar-ring for oaths (Magoun, pp. 169–70). Aðils dies in the *dísarsalr* during the *dísablót* at Uppsala (*Ynglinga saga* ch. 29), and Yrsa may have been its priestess (p. 170).

**Contradictions flagged.**
- The README's narrator line on Óðinn's host (row 2).
- The current Clover note frames Pour the Cup as only a peace-maker. Damico shows the Old English and Norse cup is above all a **vow** instrument (rows 3–5).
- Acker & Larrington's "valkyries serve mead" is Norse. In English sources the cup-woman is a queen, not a *wælcyrge* (row 1).
