# Charles River Editors, *Mansa Musa and Timbuktu* (n.d.): design reading

Source: Charles River Editors, *Mansa Musa und Timbuktu: Die Geschichte des westafrikanischen Herrschers und der sagenumwobensten Stadt des mittelalterlichen Afrikas* (German translation of *Mansa Musa and Timbuktu*; Charles River Editors, self-published Kindle e-book, n.d., c. 2019 to judge from its web citations). No ISBN or page numbers.
Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo. **The copy is the German translation**, so the quotes below are my English renderings.

**What was read.** The whole booklet was skimmed: Introduction; "The Sahara's predecessors"; "Sundiata"; "Mansa Musa as leader"; "Trade"; "The greatest hajj"; "Timbuktu"; and the bibliography. I searched it for eunuchs, women, wives, queens, slaves and Ibn Baṭṭūṭa.

**Relation to existing notes.** `comparanda_reading_list.md`, Northwest Africa, finding 4 and "Sub-regions judged a poor fit": the Sahel has no male-to-female evidence before 1453. This booklet changes nothing there.

## Verdict: not a scholarly source

- It is a popular booklet from a content publisher, with **no footnotes**. Quotations carry no references, and modern commentators (an NPR writer, a Northwestern anthropologist) are quoted next to legend with nothing to mark which is which.
- Its bibliography does point to real sources:
  - Levtzion and Hopkins (eds.), *Corpus of Early Arabic Sources for West African History* (1981; 2000);
  - Levtzion, "The Thirteenth- and Fourteenth-Century Kings of Mali", *JAH* 4 (1963);
  - Levtzion, *Ancient Ghana and Mali* (1973);
  - Hunwick, *Timbuktu and the Songhay Empire* (1999);
  - Bell, "The Age of Mansa Musa of Mali", *IJAHS* 5 (1972).
  - The *Corpus* is already in the reading list (item 10). The others could be added as P3 if the Sahel is ever revisited.
- **Do not cite the booklet in the README bibliography.**

## Gender material found (all unsourced in the booklet; *unverified*)

- **Musa's wife on the hajj.** "His favourite wife, Inari Kunate, accompanied him with her own retinue of 500 servants" ("Der größte Hadsch"). No source is given. The name is common in popular accounts; its primary basis is *unverified*.
- **Women sent home by river.** On the return, at Kabara, "most of the women" took boats up the Niger while the rest went overland. A boat carrying the Quraysh families was seized by the lord of Dienne and released at "Shinshin". This is probably from the Timbuktu chronicles (*Ta'rikh al-fattash* / *Ta'rikh al-Sudan*), but no source is given.
- **Slave dancers.** Musa's entry to Cairo included "slave dancers dressed in feathers and little else". The source is not given.
- **Origin legends.**
  - Timbuktu was named for a slave woman, "Tinbuktu", or Buktu, left to keep a well.
  - The Tuareg matriarch Tin Hinan is said to have been crowned "first queen" in the 4th c.
  - The Wagadu serpent Bida demanded a yearly virgin sacrifice.
  - All are legends, presented as half-history.
- **Court protocol.** Petitioners kissed the ground and threw dust on their heads, and the king spoke only through an interpreter. Al-'Umari's account of Musa refusing to kiss the ground before al-Nasir Muhammad is quoted without a page; it is in the *Corpus*.
- **No eunuchs** appear anywhere, and there is **nothing on gender crossing** at the Mali court.

## Design implications

- **None for the faiths.** The reaction line stays "West African traditional religions and Sahelian courts: no evidence either way before 1453; keep neutral and avoid inventing".
- If a Mali flavour event is ever wanted (a daughter at Niani, or a Malian hajj passing through Cairo in 1324), build it from al-'Umari and Ibn Baṭṭūṭa in the *Corpus*, not from this booklet.
- For Mamluk reaction the useful link is **al-Nasir Muhammad**: Musa's 1324 audience falls in the reign Rowson discusses (`isl_islamicate_sexualities.md`).
