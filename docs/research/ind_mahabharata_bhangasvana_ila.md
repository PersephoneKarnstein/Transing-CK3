## 1. The texts in brief

### Bhaṅgāśvana (Anuśāsana XII)

- **Frame.** Yudhiṣṭhira asks Bhīṣma which partner, man or woman, gets "the greater pleasure" from union. The story is told as the answer. Pleasure is the question the whole section is built to settle; it is not an afterthought.
- **The cause.** A righteous, sonless king performs the Agniṣṭuta for sons. Indra resents a rite that honours Agni alone and looks for a fault to punish. Finding none, he "stupefied the monarch" on a hunt. Lost and thirsty, the king bathes in a lake: "To his amazement he found that he was changed, by virtue of the waters, into a woman."
- **First reaction: shame.** The king "became overpowered with shame", recites what "Rishis" say (mildness and softness are women's attributes, hardness and energy men's), and laments: "Alas, my manliness has disappeared." These are the received ideas of the man who has not yet chosen.
- **The abdication.** She rides home, tells the court plainly what happened, gives the kingdom to her hundred sons and goes to the forest. There she bears "a century of sons" to an ascetic.
- **The joint kingdom.** She brings the second hundred home: "Ye are the children of my loins while I was a man." She commands both sets to rule "together, like brothers born of the same parents", and they do.
- **Indra's frustration.** "By transforming this royal sage into a woman I have, it seems, done him good instead of an injury." He then turns the brothers against each other (sons of the king versus sons of an ascetic) and they kill one another.
- **The first boon.** Indra reveals himself; she asks pardon; he offers to revive one set of sons. She chooses: "O Vasava, let those sons of mine come to life that were borne by me as a woman." Her reason: "The affection that is entertained by a woman is much greater than that which is entertained by a man."
- **Indra revives both sets.** "Let all thy children come back into life." The reading list implies only the woman-born sons return; in the text all two hundred do.
- **The second boon, the choice.** Indra: "do thou take from me whatever status thou choosest, that of woman or of man." She: "I desire to remain a woman, O Sakra." Then: "In fact, I do not wish to be restored to the status of manhood, O Vasava."
- **Her stated reason for staying** is the pleasure argument, and only that: in congress women's pleasure "is always much greater". She closes: "I am quite content with this status of womanhood that I now have." And: "Do thou leave me now, O lord of heaven." Indra says "So be it" and leaves.
- **Address after the change.** Indra still calls her "foremost of kings", "puissant one" and "of excellent vows". The narrator calls her "the ascetic lady" and "that foremost of monarchs transformed into a woman". She is never given a woman's name; she stays "Bhangaswana" in the narration.

### Ilā (Ādi LXXV and Anuśāsana)

- **Ādi LXXV** lists Manu's ten children with "a daughter named Ila" as the eighth. Then: "The learned Pururavas was born of Ila." And the one line that carries the whole story: "It hath been heard by us that Ila was both his mother and father."
- **No grove, no curse, no Śiva, no Pārvatī, no alternating months** appear in the Mahābhārata passages. Ilā's change is implied by "mother and father", never narrated.
- **Anuśāsana CXLVII** lists Manu's "daughter named Ila and a son to be named Sudyumna" as two children, with Ilā married to Budha ("Vudha"). In Purāṇic versions Sudyumna and Ilā are one person; this list splits them.
- **Anuśāsana CL** names Purūravas "the son of Ila" and "that dear son of Vudha"; Ganguli writes "of the Solar race", which is a slip or a variant (Purūravas founds the Lunar line). **Anuśāsana VI**: Purūravas, "the descendant of Ila", reached heaven by the Brahmins' intercession.
- **Purūravas's record is mixed.** Ādi LXXV: he ruled "thirteen islands of the sea", brought the three sacred fires and Urvaśī from the Gandharvas, but robbed Brahmins and "was immediately destroyed by their curse". Ayus, Nahuṣa and Yayāti follow; the Yādavas and Kurus descend from this line.

### Other episodes found in the two volumes

- **Śikhaṇḍin (female-to-male; reaction data only).** Ādi LXIII: Drupada's "daughter Sikhandin who was afterwards transformed into a male child", changed by the Yakṣa Sthūṇa "from the desire of doing her good". Ādi LXVII: Śikhaṇḍin, "at first a female", was a Rākṣasa incarnate. The full story is now read: see section 5. Note the shared formula: both changes are said to *do good* to the one changed.
- **Arjuna and Urvaśī (Vana XLVI).** Arjuna refuses the apsaras as a mother of his race; she curses him to live "among females unregarded, and as a dancer, and destitute of manhood and scorned as a eunuch". Indra turns the curse to use: one year "as a dancer without manhood" during the exile in disguise, after which he will "regain thy power". This is the epic's model of a man among women as **curse, disguise and loss**, temporary and reversed. See section 6 for the Virāṭa year itself.
- **Pañcacūḍā (Anuśāsana XXXVIII)** is a long discourse on the faults of women's nature, put in an apsaras's mouth. Located, not read; a likely source of hostile lines.

## 2. What matters for the design

1. **The choice is explicit and offered by a god.** "Whatever status thou choosest, that of woman or of man" (Anu. XII). The text frames the change as a punishment that became a good (Indra's own words), and then as a free choice made with full knowledge of both lives. This is the strongest pre-period text on the subcontinent for a woman who chose to stay a woman.
2. **The order is curse → lake → shame → children → choice → pleasure.** The lake is accidental and hostile; the staying is deliberate. A faith can keep the lake as the place and drop the curse as the cause.
3. **The text's own reason to remain is pleasure.** The frame question (Yudhiṣṭhira's) is male curiosity about women's pleasure, and her answer serves it. The faith may quote the pleasure line, but should lead with the lines that are hers alone: "I desire to remain a woman", "I am quite content", "Do thou leave me now".
4. **Two families, one mother.** The sons of her two lives rule "like brothers born of the same parents" until a god sows division. This is a ready CK3 mechanic: children from before and after the change, a shared inheritance, and an outside agent (rival faith, schemer) who splits them by appeal to paternity.
5. **She keeps her royal address.** Indra calls her "foremost of kings" after the change. The faith can say a queen is not a demoted king.
6. **Ilā gives a genealogy, not a story.** "Ila was both his mother and father" (Ādi LXXV) is enough for the faith's dynastic claim ("half the kings of India descend from a woman who was a man"). The grove, Śiva's curse and the monthly alternation must be sourced from the Rāmāyaṇa Uttarakāṇḍa or the Purāṇas before the narrator uses them.

## 3. Corrections to the candidate

- **Ilā in MBh 1.75 is not the grove story.** The reading list attributes the grove, the curse and Pārvatī's alternating months to "Rāmāyaṇa, Uttarakāṇḍa; Mahābhārata 1.75". The Mahābhārata gives only the genealogy and the "mother and father" line. Source the grove in Goldman's Uttarakāṇḍa or Doniger 1999 (item 13).
- **The Mahābhārata splits Ilā and Sudyumna** into a daughter and a son (Anu. CXLVII). Any text on Ilā should not depend on their identity without the Purāṇic source.
- **Indra revives all two hundred sons,** not only those she bore as a woman. Her *choice* favours the woman-born; the god's *gift* restores both.
- **Her reason for staying is only pleasure.** The children argument answers a different question (which sons to revive). The reading list says this correctly; flag it in any loc that blends them.
- **Section numbers are Ganguli's (Vulgate).** "MBh 1.75" and "13.12" are Ganguli/Vulgate numbers. The Critical Edition numbers differ for Ādi (probably 1.70; *unverified*). Cite as "Ganguli, Ādi Parva sec. LXXV".
- **Purūravas is not a model king** in the Mahābhārata: he dies by the Brahmins' curse for robbing them (Ādi LXXV). Holy-site or ancestor text should not praise him uncritically.

## 4. Design implications

- **Creed line (faith's own voice):** "I desire to remain a woman." (Anu. XII.) Second creed line for doctrine screens: "I am quite content with this status of womanhood that I now have."
- **Gate step, the dawn bath:** Bhaṅgāśvana's lake is the textual anchor for the bath step of the Mārkaḻi vow. Flavour: "changed, by virtue of the waters". The faith claims the lake; the narrator never says the bath works.
- **Gate success event:** a dream offer framed on Indra's boon ("whatever status thou choosest"), with the option text "I desire to remain a woman." A second option ("Do thou leave me now") could dismiss a hostile god-figure or priest.
- **Event, "Two Hundred Sons":** a converted ruler with children from both lives; a rival (Indra's role) whispers about whose sons are truly hers. Outcomes: the joint inheritance holds (piety, dynasty prestige) or breaks (a claimant war). Avoid killing the children on screen.
- **Modifier name:** "Content in Womanhood" (from "I am quite content").
- **Reaction lines (hostile):** Indra's "done him good instead of an injury" works as a frustrated enemy's line; Arjuna's curse ("destitute of manhood and scorned as a eunuch", Vana XLVI) is the hostile Hindu reading that the faith's critics would apply to her. Use the curse frame only in hostile mouths.
- **Doctrine against the third thing:** Arjuna's year "without manhood" is the epic's temporary, shameful in-between; the faith's polemic can set Bhaṅgāśvana's permanent, chosen womanhood against it. Keep Pārvatī's alternation (the "cage of the grove") until the Rāmāyaṇa source is read.
- **Dynastic claim (faith's voice only):** "Ilā was both his mother and father." Lunar-descent houses gain the faith's favour; the narrator presents this as the faith's reading.
- **Holy site c_prayaga:** text should rest on the genealogy and Purūravas as founder; the Pratiṣṭhāna placement is not in these passages and needs another source.
- **Do not use:** Śikhaṇḍin as a trans woman (the text runs female to male); Bhaṅgāśvana's shame speech as the faith's view of womanhood (it is the king's pre-change prejudice).

## 5. Śikhaṇḍin and Sthūṇākarṇa (Udyoga CLXXXIX–CXCV)

Ganguli vol. 2 (#15475). Bhīṣma tells the story to Duryodhana to explain why he will not fight Śikhaṇḍin.

### The text in brief

- **Ambā's vow (CLXXXIX–CXC).** Paraśurāma cannot defeat Bhīṣma for her (CLXXXIX). Ambā, princess of Kāśī, turns to extreme austerities to bring about Bhīṣma's death. To the ascetics she names her state: Bhīṣma is the one "for whom I have become neither woman nor man" (CXC). "As a woman, I have no longer any desire. I am, however, resolved to obtain manhood" (CXC), and only for revenge. Śiva grants it: "Thou shalt slay Bhishma, and even obtain manhood" in a new birth, remembering this life (CXC). Gaṅgā, by contrast, curses her to become half a crooked seasonal river (CLXXXIX).
- **Her death ends CXC by her own act on a pyre.** Hard rule: no suicide content. This scene is not usable in any form.
- **Birth (CXCI).** Drupada asks Śiva for a son to kill Bhīṣma; Śiva answers that his child "will be a female and male". A daughter is born; the queen announces a son, and Drupada performs a son's rites and names the child Śikhaṇḍin. The concealment is the queen's, "from fear of my co-wives" (CXCIV).
- **The marriage (CXCII–CXCIII).** Raised in arts and arms (a pupil of Droṇa), Śikhaṇḍin is married to the daughter of Hiraṇyavarman of Daśārṇa. The bride finds Śikhaṇḍin "a woman like herself" and tells her nurses; her father calls it "deception" and marches on Drupada to kill him "with Sikhandin" (CXCIII).
- **Despair (CXCIV).** Śikhaṇḍinī, "filled with shame" at her parents' danger, goes into a forest meaning to end her life. **Not usable** (same rule).
- **The exchange (CXCIV–CXCV).** The yakṣa Sthūṇākarṇa offers help. Śikhaṇḍinī asks to "become a perfect man" until the invader leaves. The yakṣa's terms: "For a certain period I will give thee my manhood" and "I will bear thy womanhood" (CXCV). They make a covenant and "imparted unto each other's body their sexes" (CXCV). The Daśārṇa king sends young women to examine Śikhaṇḍin; they report a man; the war is called off.
- **Sthūṇā becomes a woman, and is punished for it (CXCV).** Kubera finds the yakṣa absent from court and learns that Sthūṇā "stayeth within his abode having become a woman" and does not come "in shame". Kubera's curse: "let the femininity of the wretch remain as it is!" and "from this day, thou shalt remain a woman and she shall remain a man!" Kubera's charge is that Sthūṇā has done "what hath never been done by anybody". The yakṣas plead; the limit set is Śikhaṇḍin's death.
- **Honour kept (CXCV).** Śikhaṇḍin returns as promised; Sthūṇā says "I am pleased with thee!" and releases him: "live happily amongst men as thou choosest".
- **Bhīṣma's vow (CXCV).** He will not shoot "a woman, or one that was a woman before or one bearing a feminine name, or one whose form resembleth a woman's". He counts Śikhaṇḍin as a woman and so will not fight him.

### How the text frames the change

- Śikhaṇḍin's change (female to male) is **revenge carried across a rebirth**, foretold by Śiva and completed by an exchange. It is reaction and contrast data only.
- Sthūṇākarṇa's change (**male to female**) is the passage that matters for the Garland. It is **a gift**, freely offered ("I will bear thy womanhood"), then made permanent by a lord as **punishment**, and lived in **shame** (the yakṣa will not appear at court). It is lifted only by another's death. No one in the text asks what Sthūṇā wants.
- Ambā's "neither woman nor man" is the epic's own phrase for the in-between state of grievance. It is the "third thing" the faith refuses, and here it is a wound, not an identity.
- Bhīṣma's vow is an ancient list of who counts as a woman, by past, name or form. It treats a woman who was once a man, and a man who was once a woman, alike as not to be fought.

### Design use for the Worn Garland

- **Sthūṇākarṇa, claimed in the faith's voice:** "The yakṣa bore a woman's body for a friend's sake and kept it." The faith can turn Kubera's curse ("let the femininity ... remain as it is") into the blessing it was not meant to be, as Bhaṅgāśvana's lake was turned. The narrator does not say Sthūṇā chose it; the text does not.
- **Hostile line (Kubera's frame):** "Thou hast done what hath never been done by anybody." Good for a lord or rival priest reacting to a convert's change. The shame of hiding from court can be a stress event the Garland's companions answer.
- **Event, "The Bride's Nurses":** a marriage alliance where the spouse's household sends women to examine (CXCV's examination). Handle off screen. Outcomes: the alliance holds, or the bride's father calls it deception and sends a casus belli (CXCII–CXCIII). Do not resolve it by a body swap; the Garland's change is its own.
- **Reaction, "Bhīṣma's Vow":** a chivalrous hostile character may refuse to duel a Garland daughter "who was once a man" (CXCV). The faith may read it as recognition; the narrator presents it as the knight's rule.
- **Do not use:** Ambā's pyre or Śikhaṇḍinī's despair (no suicide content); Śikhaṇḍin as a trans woman (the change runs female to male); Ambā's "neither woman nor man" as anything the faith affirms.

## 6. Arjuna as Bṛhannalā (Virāṭa II–LXXII)

Ganguli vol. 2 (#15475). The Pāṇḍavas must pass the thirteenth year of exile unrecognised.

### The text in brief

- **The plan (II).** "I will declare myself as one of the neuter sex." He will cover his bow-scarred arms with bangles, wear earrings and a braid, and "appear as one of the third sex, Vrihannala by name". "And living as a female I shall (always) entertain the king and the inmates of the inner apartments". He will teach the palace women song, dance and instruments.
- **Arrival (XI).** He comes "decked in the ornaments of women". King Virāṭa doubts it: "such a person as thou can never be of the neuter sex". Arjuna's self-account: "Know me, O king of men, to be Vrihannala, a son or daughter without father or mother." He asks to be "dancing-master to the royal maiden" Uttarā. The king has him "examined by women" and, told the condition is permanent, sends him to the maidens' apartments, where he wins "their good graces".
- **Draupadī's grief (XIX).** She mourns the hero "now wearing braids ending in unsightly curls" and serving "gladdened women with his songs" in "a guise that is despaired by all".
- **A moment of shared sorrow (XXIV).** Taunted by Draupadī (as Sairandhrī) that she lives "happily in the apartments of the girls", Bṛhannalā answers in the feminine: "Vrihannala also hath unparalleled sorrows of her own." This is the only place in the episode where Ganguli gives Bṛhannalā a woman's pronoun in her own speech.
- **Unmasking (XXXVIII–XLV).** Drafted as Uttara's charioteer, she chases the fleeing prince, "his own long braid and pure red garments fluttering". The Kurus: "He is partly a man and partly a woman." To Uttara, Arjuna explains: "I am not truly one of the neuter sex, but I have adopted this vow of eunuchism from subservience to another's will and from desire of religious merit" (XLV). Uttara: "such a person as thou, O best of men, cannot be of the neuter sex" (XLV).
- **Contempt (LXVII–LXVIII).** Virāṭa strikes Yudhiṣṭhira for praising "one of the neuter sex" over his son.
- **Purity (LXXII).** Offered Uttarā as wife, Arjuna takes her as a daughter-in-law instead: she "trusted me as her father", and a marriage would invite "slanderous and false accusations".
- **Urvaśī's curse is not mentioned in the Virāṭa Parva** in Ganguli. The link is made only in Vana XLVI, where Indra says the curse will be served "as a dancer without manhood" for the one year (section 1 above). In Virāṭa Arjuna himself calls it a vow taken at his elder brother's behest.

### How the text frames it

- A **disguise** chosen for concealment, "like fire concealed in ashes" (II; XXXVIII), lasting **one year**, ended on schedule.
- The category is "neuter" / "third sex" / "eunuch", the very *klība* frame Bhatt warns against. Every character who sees the hero's body says he "can never be" one.
- It is lived with **real intimacy with women**: teacher of the maidens, trusted "as her father", sharer of Draupadī's sorrow. The women's quarters are the place of safety, the battlefield the place of unmasking.

### Design use for the Worn Garland

- **Precedent the faith may claim, with care:** the dancing-master of the women's quarters. In the faith's voice: "Even the greatest archer lived a year among the women, and taught them, and was trusted." This supports the *araiyar*-style court position (a man who performs the girl's part) as the first Marking of a seeker. It is not a precedent for becoming a woman: the text insists he does not.
- **The faith's correction of the story:** the Garland's polemic should say Bṛhannalā is the cage, not the door: a year of "neuter" guise, then back to arms. "The archer wore the braid for a year; the bride wears the garland for life." This keeps the faith on Bhatt's side: no third thing.
- **Usable lines in the faith's voice:** "Vrihannala also hath unparalleled sorrows of her own" (XXIV), as a daughter's answer to a sister who thinks her life easy; "a son or daughter without father or mother" (XI), as a seeker's answer to a hostile lord, with care that it is not read as a third category.
- **Hostile lines:** Virāṭa's "Thou wretch of a Brahmana, dost thou compare one of the neuter sex with my son!" (LXVII); the Kurus' "partly a man and partly a woman" (XXXVIII); Draupadī's "a guise that is despaired by all" (XIX). Use only in hostile or grieving mouths.
- **Event idea, "The Charioteer":** a Garland daughter, a former warrior, is pressed into a battle by a frightened prince. Options keep her vow (stay off the field) or take the reins (prowess, risk to the vow). Never frame her courage as "manly"; the text's own "not the practice of the brave" speech belongs to Arjuna, not to her.
- **Do not:** present Bṛhannalā as a trans woman in narration; use "eunuch", "neuter" or "third sex" in the faith's own voice except to refuse them.
