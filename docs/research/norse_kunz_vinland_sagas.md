## 1. Relevant passages

### Eiríks saga ch. 4: Thorbjorg at Herjolfsnes (the key text)
- **Setting.** A lean winter in Greenland: the hunters bring back little and some do not come back. Thorkel, the leading farmer at Herjolfsnes, is expected to find out when the hard times will end.
- **Who she is.** Thorbjorg, "called the 'Little Prophetess'" (*lítilvölva*). She "was one of ten sisters, all of whom had the gift of prophecy", and the only one still alive. **She belongs to a sisterhood of seeresses, and she is its last member.**
- **How she works.** She spends each winter going from farm to farm by invitation. People ask about their own futures or the coming year. A man is sent to fetch her, and escorts pass her on from one farm to the next.
- **The high seat.** "A high seat was set for her, complete with cushion. This was to be stuffed with chicken feathers." The glossary defines *öndvegi* as a seat higher in honour, "not necessarily higher in elevation". **The high seat is the honour given to a guest. It is not where the rite happens.**
- **Costume (paraphrased, in the saga's order).**
  - A **black** mantle with a strap, set with stones right down to the hem. Kunz translates the Old Norse *blár* as "black".
  - A string of glass beads.
  - A hood of black lambskin lined with white catskin.
  - A staff with a brass-mounted knob set with stones.
  - A linked charm belt with a large purse holding "the charms which she needed for her predictions".
  - Fur-lined calfskin boots with long laces and pewter knobs.
  - "gloves of catskin, white and lined with fur".
- **Reception.** Everyone must greet her, and she answers each person according to how they please her. Thorkel leads her to her seat by the hand and asks her to look over his flock, servants and buildings. She says little about any of it.
- **The meal.** She is given "A porridge of kid's milk" and "the hearts of all the animals available there". She eats with a brass spoon and a knife with an ivory haft bound with bronze, **"the point of which had broken off"**.
- **The wait.** She will not answer until she has spent the night. The rite takes place late the following day.
- **The singers are scarce.** She asks for women who know the chants, "called warlock songs" (*varðlokkur*), and none can be found. Gudrid alone knows them, because **her foster-mother Halldis taught her** in Iceland.
- **Gudrid refuses and gives way.** Gudrid refuses at first: "I am a Christian woman." Thorbjorg answers that she could help the people here, "and you'd be no worse a woman for that". Gudrid sings because Thorkel urges her.
- **The rite.** "The women formed a warding ring around the platform raised for sorcery, with Thorbjorg perched atop it." The platform is the *seiðhjallr*. Gudrid sings alone; the ring of women does not sing. Her voice was fairer than anyone there had heard.
- **The result.** The spirits came because the chant was fair. Before that they "wished to turn their backs on us and refused to do our bidding". Thorbjorg foretells three things:
  - the famine ends with spring;
  - the sickness eases;
  - Gudrid's whole future: a great match, a move to Iceland, and a line over which "a bright ray will shine".
  Then each person asks her a question in turn, and "little that she predicted did not occur".
- **Christian absence.** Gudrid's father Thorbjorn had left the farm rather than stay "while such heathen practices were going on". An escort takes Thorbjorg on to the next farm.

### Kunz's note 8 on *varðlokkur*
- Kunz derives the word from Scots "warlock". Most scholars instead read it as *varð-* plus *lokka*, "ward-lures", songs that draw the guarding spirits. The Penguin gloss is a minority view. **The mod should not translate the term as "warlock songs".** "Warding songs" is the safer gloss and matches "warding ring".

### Glossary (Gísli Sigurðsson)
- ***seiður*:** "originally only practised by women". Men who practised it, Óðinn among them, were "almost always looked down on as having engaged in an 'effeminate' activity". It had two uses: influencing people and weather, and finding out the future.
- ***völva*:** the glossary adds *útiseta*, "sitting outside" at night on graves or at crossroads.

### Introduction (Gísli Sigurðsson)
- The two sagas were written down independently in the early thirteenth century from shared oral tradition. *Eiríks saga* survives in Hauksbók (early fourteenth century) and Skálholtsbók (early fifteenth).
- The sagas frame paganism against Christianity. The Christian Gudrid "agrees to take part in sorcery to help people in need". Her name is read as containing *Guð*, God. Freydis's name contains Frey, so the saga ties her outrages to heathendom.
- Gudrid's piety exists to honour her bishop descendants, the saga's patrons. **The prophecy of a "bright ray" is a heathen seeress foretelling Christian bishops.**

### Eiríks saga, other chapters
- **Ch. 1, Aud the Deep-minded.** A widowed matriarch has a ship built in secret, marries off her granddaughter, claims land and frees her bondsman Vifil, Gudrid's grandfather. She is a model of a woman who holds a house and its land.
- **Ch. 5.** Thjodhild converts, has a church built, and "refused to sleep with Eirik". In Greenland, conversion splits households along sex lines.
- **Ch. 5, Thorgunna.** A Hebridean woman who "knew a thing or two". Her son Thorgils later "was also thought to have something preternatural about him". Uncanny knowledge passes down the mother's side.
- **Ch. 6.** The ghost of Thorstein Eiriksson prophesies Gudrid's fate in Christian terms.
- **Ch. 8, Thorhall the Huntsman.** A pagan devotee of Thor. Searchers find him on a cliff edge staring skyward, mouth, nostrils and eyes wide open, scratching and pinching himself and mumbling. It reads like *útiseta* or a poem-trance. Thorhall then credits Thor ("Old Redbeard") for a beached whale, and the Christians throw it away. **A man doing a pagan trance is called surly, never effeminate.** The contempt in the sources is for seiðr in particular, not for trance in general.
- **Ch. 11, Freydis at Hop.** She is pregnant and the men are fleeing. She jeers at them: "Had I a weapon I'm sure I would fight better than any of you". She takes a dead man's sword, bares a breast and slaps the blade with it, and the attackers flee. **This is the nearest thing to a shield-maiden in either saga, and it is a woman's body used as the weapon.**

### Grænlendinga saga
- **Ch. 5, Grimhild.** "a very large woman, with the strength of a man", who rises after death. Thorstein's corpse sits up to prophesy Gudrid's life, ending as a nun.
- **Ch. 7, the second Gudrid.** At Vinland a pale woman with huge eyes enters and asks Gudrid her name. Asked her own, she answers "My name is Gudrid" and vanishes. No one else saw her.
- **Ch. 7, Freydis's voyage.**
  - She goes barefoot in her husband's cape to provoke a massacre.
  - When the men will not kill the five women, she says "Hand me an axe" and kills them herself.
  - She threatens death to anyone who tells.
  - Leif condemns her, but will not punish his sister himself.
- **Ch. 9.** As a widow Gudrid runs the household with her son, goes on pilgrimage, and becomes a nun and anchoress.
