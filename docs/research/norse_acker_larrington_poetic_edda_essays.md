## 1. The argument in brief

### The völva's voice (Lönnroth, Quinn)
- **A voice, not a body.** The völva's "presence is imagined almost exclusively as voice" (Quinn p. 248). She never names herself. Her identity is left to "the declarations and conjectures of her interlocutors" (p. 248).
- **She is journeyed to.** "The vǫlva never travels, she is always journeyed to, with the site of interrogation always being on her home turf" (p. 256). In the sagas the völva goes on a circuit (Þorbjǫrg in *Eiríks saga*, Lönnroth pp. 9–10). The myth and the saga disagree on who travels.
- **Desired and disavowed.** Vǫlur, wizards and seiðr-workers "represent the arts of magic and sorcery that the Æsir simultaneously desire access to and disavow" (p. 250). To the gods she embodies "the feminine, death, and the disorderly world of wolves, forests, and the future" (p. 257).
- **Óðinn's interview ends in abuse.** In *Baldrs draumar*, Óðinn raises a dead völva with a corpse-spell, "compromising his masculinity" (p. 260), then tricks her with a riddle and calls her "mother of three ogres". Quinn reads this as his need to "re-establish his credentials as both áss (god) and male" (p. 260). She answers "in the mode of a senna": "Ride home, Óðinn, and be proud of yourself!" (p. 259).
- **In *Vǫluspá* she has "all the confidence of a court poet"** (p. 261). Óðinn pays her in rings and necklaces. She warns him that Gullveig, burned three times in his hall, "yet she lives still" (p. 261).
- **The seiðr nexus.** "The practice of seiðr forms a nexus between the vǫlva, Freyja and Óðinn" (p. 268). Freyja taught seiðr to the Æsir (*Ynglinga saga*). Gullveig/Heiðr may be hypostases of Freyja. "None of the vǫlur in the eddic poems are represented as performing seiðr themselves" (p. 268).
- **The völva as genealogist.** In *Hyndluljóð* Freyja needs Hyndla to recite Óttarr's lineage, because his "patrimony" and "legal right of succession" turn on it (p. 265). Freyja calls her "sister". Hyndla answers with a senna and the she-goat taunt, and "her insinuation of friendship is thrown back at her" (p. 267).
- **Her audience is everyone.** "Hear me, all you holy kindred" addresses "both the high and the low" (Lönnroth p. 13). Lönnroth also shows the voice serving power: the recitation tells the hall that the golden age returns "provided one accepts the hierarchy of worth that prevails in the chieftain's banquet hall" (p. 22). Christian readers mapped Ragnarǫk onto the Last Judgment and Gimlé onto the New Jerusalem (pp. 21–22).

### Lokasenna and flyting (Anderson, Frakes, Clover)
- **The charges are gendered.** Loki "accuses the gods of unmanliness, or cowardice and sexual indecency, and he accuses the goddesses of unwomanliness" (Anderson p. 145).
- **Galdralag as magic.** Loki's four seven-line stanzas "represent attempts by Loki to use magic" (p. 151). Óðinn tries to outdo him with an eight-line stanza and fails. "Loki succeeds with his much simpler stanza in doing what Odin could not" (p. 153). Only the threat of Þórr's hammer ends the senna.
- **Óðinn's seiðr.** *Ls* 24: "you once practised seid on Samsey, and you beat on the drum as witches do ... and I thought that the hallmark of a pervert" (Frakes p. 167). Óðinn's counter-charge (*Ls* 23) is that Loki spent "eight winters ... beneath the earth, a woman milking cows, and there you bore children" (Anderson p. 152). Frakes calls Óðinn, like Loki, "a part-time transsexual" (p. 167). That is the scholar's 1987 term, not a period one.
- **Loki is the margin.** "External to the system but essential to its function" (Frakes p. 166). "It is only by means of the margin that the center is defined" (p. 171).
- **Hárbarðsljóð.** Óðinn, as Hárbarðr, boasts of slave girls, seduced witches ("night-riders") and a wand won from a giant, and sets these against Þórr's giant-killing. "The opposition is traditional, but the bias is exactly reversed ... generic heresy" (Clover p. 105). Þórr's only insult is "a frayed *ergi* formula" (p. 108). *Þrymskviða* 17 shows the same fixation: "The Æsir might call me a fag" (p. 108, the volume's translation).

### Þrymskviða and cross-dressing (Clunies Ross)
- **The hammer is the phallus.** Þrymr's theft is "a symbolic castration of Þórr himself ... The god is ragr" (p. 188). The bride's lap is hallowed with the hammer, so Þórr recovers it "at the very moment of his ritual feminisation" (p. 188).
- **Þórr is "an inept drag queen".** Loki's "successful impersonation of a quick-witted maid underlines his androgynous character" (p. 182). Loki is "successfully bisexual, whereas Þórr's masculine identity is compromised but not obliterated" (p. 189).
- **The poem restores order.** Its ending is "satisfyingly positive for proponents of the social status quo", "reinforcing norms associated with male dominance" (pp. 181–82). It "insists that Þórr himself had never really lost his manliness" (p. 189). In the sagas, by contrast, "the price of humiliation is usually dishonour" (p. 189).
- **Notes.** Loki's child-bearing is "a mythological reflex of the Old Icelandic legal concept of ýki" (n. 8, p. 193). In a woman, *ergi* meant "inordinate sexual desire", the sense of Skírnir's curse (n. 9). Loki uses the neuter plural *tvau*, the form for a mixed-sex pair, for himself and the veiled Þórr (n. 16, p. 194).

### Óðinn, women's magic and valkyries (McKinnell, Svava, Harris)
- **Fear of women's magic.** The *Vǫlundarkviða* poet shows "a fear of the magical powers of women" in "a culture which regarded seiðr as a characteristically female art, treated it with consistent fear and loathing, and made it subject to severe legal penalties" (McKinnell p. 207). Völundr's hamstringing and lost sword read as "a symbolic castration" (p. 208).
- **Swan maidens choose.** "It is the women who take the initiative in the marriages", which "must have been very striking" to the first audience (p. 202). They leave of their own will.
- **An English poem.** Old English vocabulary points to a poet "composing in England", most likely Yorkshire, c. 900–1050 (p. 200). The only Scandinavian Völundr stone is Ardre VIII on Gotland (p. 200). Old English named the male sex *wæpnedcynn* (p. 208).
- **Sovereignty and the cup.** Svava reads Gunnlǫð on "the golden chair" (p. 32) as the goddess who confers kingship with mead and embrace. The valkyries inherited her role: "supernatural beings who accompanied the king and heroes", who governed fate (p. 44). "The goddess's support meant health and good fortune for the king, but her abandonment meant defeat and certain death" (p. 45). "She is the one who directs the course of events and decides whether the king is worthy" (p. 47). **Caution:** the editors note that not all scholars accept a Norse great goddess (n. 13, citing Davidson: "vague and suggestive merely"). Use this as flavour, not as fact.
- **Rebirth from the earth.** Foster-brotherhood was sworn by going under raised turf and mixing blood with soil (*Gísla saga*). Svava calls the earth there "the ceremony's frame, the shrine itself, the actual womb of a mother" (p. 40).
- **A shared English–Norse curse.** Skírnir's thistle simile matches OE Metrical Charm 9 ("May he quite waste away ... be just as fragile as a thistle"). Harris argues for "an old, perhaps common Germanic, tradition of cursing with the thistle" (p. 85).

### Gender generally
Across the volume the Eddic gender system has three parts. Men are measured by honour, which is lost through *ergi*. Women are measured by sexual constancy, which is lost through promiscuity (Anderson; Clunies Ross pp. 186–87). Female magic is feared (McKinnell). Sacral female authority survives only in remnants: the völva the gods need, the goddess on the golden chair, the valkyrie who chooses (Quinn, Svava). Crossings occur three ways: as insult (Lokasenna), as temporary disguise that restores the norm (Þrymskviða), and as Loki's and Óðinn's shape-changing, which the texts both use and condemn.
