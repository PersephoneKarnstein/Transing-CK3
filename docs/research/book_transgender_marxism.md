## 2. Core beliefs / claims of transgender Marxism as this book presents them

Each entry: paragraph explanation, then exact quote(s) with location.

### 2.1 Trans liberation cannot be achieved through "rights"; civic emancipation is a pyrrhic victory
The editors' framing claim. Formal equality (self-ID, streamlined legal sex change) leaves "everyday torments" untouched; the mainstream of trans activism treats domination by "state bureaucracy, by landlords and employers" as a given to be softened with sensitivity training. Marx's "On the Jewish Question" is invoked: the state "abolishes, in its own way, distinctions" while presupposing them.

> "But what if the emancipation of trans people cannot be won through the securing of 'rights'? What if, however, smoothed-over the process of state validation were to become, a meaningful liberation remained out of view?" — Intro (Gleeson & O'Rourke), l.211

> "Transgender rights provide a hollow target. Civic emancipation is a pyrrhic victory. Private households and workplaces alike offer a continual churn of oppression and developmental damage. This wounding across generations will not find itself mitigated through any institutional shift, or any movement that falls short of the commune. To emancipate trans people requires, above all else, overturning class divisions, reversing our separation from the means of production, and developing new forms for nurture beyond the family." — Intro, l.377

> "The 'mainstream' of trans activism has focused on smoothing over the process of transition as it runs through institutions, including those belonging to the state. Our domination by state bureaucracy, by landlords and employers, is often enough treated as a given, to be reclad with sensitivity training workshops and pronoun go-rounds." — Intro, l.209

### 2.2 The family / household is a unit of capitalist governance; family abolition
The *oikos* is where capital reproduces itself across generations; the "traditional family" is not a refuge from the market but "the means by which this alienation persists". The family is also the place that expels trans people. Raha recovers Third World Gay Revolution and STAR's communes; Doyle Griffiths notes the "classic but notorious communist call for family abolition"; Rosenberg ends with "repair of the rift as a kind of family abolition".

> "There is no thoroughly anti-capitalist politics that does not include a critique of the household as a social unit of capitalist governance. There is no critique of value that succeeds without becoming queer." — Intro, l.289

> "Capitalist families are never the answer to the separation to which proletarians are subject. They are exactly the means by which this alienation persists, one generation after the next." — Intro, l.329

> "To transition is to renege on agreements that were previously assumed, albeit never actually signed for." — Intro, l.379

> "Third World Gay Revolution (TWGR), formed in New York, USA, in the summer of 1970, argued that the family was a central site for the perpetuation of heterosexist and homophobic 'sex roles' […] The idea that such socially reproductive labour should be collectivised through the formation of communes was common" — Ch.5 (Raha), l.1111

> "Trans people's exclusion from the heterosexual family unit is central to our economic disempowerment and our difficulties with social reproduction." — Ch.10 (Belinsky), l.2027

> "If the white family is the inauthentic (but frenetically propped up) 'solution' to the metabolic rift, a people's epidemiology, pace Du Bois's Brown, offers the horizon of repairing that rift. […] Rather, repair of the rift as a kind of family abolition: the refusal to sacralise the reproduction of whiteness. Protection is offered outward, rather than hoarded." — Afterword (Rosenberg), l.3003

### 2.3 Transition is social reproduction; trans identity is produced in community
Transition is not a solitary medical event but the product of unpaid reproductive labour by trans people for one another (mentoring, "reciprocal recognition", shared knowledge, modelling). Zazanis: trans communities provide the "models, enacted experiences, and sources of tuition" that cis society forecloses; Gleeson: communities "always have to be actively cultivated".

> "We transition through the exercise of individual and collective agency. This occurs in community with other trans people, and through everyday acts of reproduction – each of which influences social cognition." — Ch.1 (Zazanis), l.485

> "Through 'support, mentoring, and reciprocal recognition', as well as the curation and dissemination of 'shared knowledge' and 'practical wisdom', trans people ourselves facilitate the development of transgender identities in community with one another." — Ch.1, l.509

> "In this way, trans people actively participate in the reproduction of trans identity – through serving as models to new generations of transitioners." — Ch.1, l.531

> "Communities are never to be assumed as unified, or taken for granted. Trans communities, like any other kind, always have to be actively cultivated, and sustained, across time." — Ch.4 (Gleeson), l.851

> "One indispensable service offered by trans communities to newcomers is presenting them with the fact that much of what they had considered their most freakish features are, within trans circles, so familiar as to be articles of cliché." — Ch.4, l.861

> "The guiding thread of this book is that 'transgender' is not a staid ontology, or an abstract, regulative identity imposed from without, but a practical truth. Our every identification is realised by the conscious, patient, and collective action of intentional communities which ground and give meaning to it." — Intro, l.373

### 2.4 Gender is labour ("gender labour"); gender bears hidden labour like the commodity
Raha (via Jane Ward) defines gender labour as "giving gender to others"; it falls most heavily on feminised subjects. Dickson draws the analogy to commodity fetishism: gender appears effortless, trans people make the work visible and therefore need "the means to produce ours".

> "To describe the affective and bodily efforts invested in giving gender to others, or actively suspending self-focus in the service of helping others achieve the varied forms of gender recognition they long for. Gender labour is the work of bolstering someone's gender authenticity, but it is also the work of co-producing someone's gender irony, transgression, or exceptionality." — Ch.5 (Raha quoting Ward), l.1073

> "[G]ender labour, like other forms of caring, weighs down most heavily on feminine subjects, the people for whom labours of love are naturalised, expected or forced" — Ch.5, l.1077

> "the loving and sexual pleasure, cooking and feeding and housing, resting and rearing, cleaning and washing and dressing, the emotional and psychological support, transition advice and specialised counselling, healthcare support, the work of creating our genders, performances or expressions, the very fabrication of us." — Ch.5, l.1135

> "Commodities are imbued with a mythical relationship to one another that bears no trace of the labour of human beings. In just the same way, gender is imagined as having an explanation that bears no trace of human effort. […] Tools are not used to make gender, but to reflect it." — Ch.12 (Dickson), l.2297

> "Since gender does clearly require work, transgender people need access to the means to produce ours." — Ch.12, l.2303

> "As transgender people, we must work to prove that our genders pre-exist the work we do to produce them and make them legible to others." — Ch.12, l.2329

### 2.5 Critique of medical gatekeeping; the clinic as an "oikonomic institution"; DIY / black-market remedies
The clinic is "not only a sinister force, but an absurdity"; it administers sexual difference on behalf of the state, imposing a "checkbox list" and a teleological binary transition. Trans people perform bad-faith compliance to get hormones; dispossession from medicine parallels dispossession from knowledge (insulin deaths, black-market hormones, fish antibiotics). Communities provide what clinicians refuse.

> "Let's accept that the clinic is not only a sinister force, but an absurdity. The pathological view of transition has never provided a true remedy for it. We have neither been cured, nor suppressed. Where clinicians have refused to proffer treatment, communities have provided for each other." — Intro, l.287

> "We can begin to see how the clinic functions as an oikonomic institution. At the clinic, stewarding patient wellbeing is a subordinate concern to the propertarian interest of the state in administering and managing sexual difference itself." — Intro, l.365

> "trans people passing through Gender Identity Clinics often wear more overtly gender-coded clothes than they would on a day-to-day basis, and provide an account of their daily lives and gender histories which they know to match the preferred model of 'transsexuality', in order to ensure access to hormones." — Ch.4 (Gleeson), l.843

> "To use them, we must first submit our reality for inspection and garner the approval of a number of friends, partners, parents, judges, psychiatrists, clerks, nations, doctors, and more. We receive epistemological licences, with restrictions. Break the rules, lose the licence and the access that it affords." — Ch.12 (Dickson), l.2301

> "If you want access to hormones, you must submit to diagnosis; if you want access to surgical care of any kind, you must produce a resume for your gender as a pathology necessitating intervention." — Ch.12, l.2303

> "We must ask permission to use medicine, and it is assumed that we will make bad choices without paternalistic management. […] Our dispossession in both regards is everywhere visible, in the death of diabetics who cannot obtain insulin, the black market in hormones for gender transition, the use of fish antibiotics for the treatment of human infections" — Ch.12, l.2349

> "The main solace we can offer is that trans culture seems set to produce subject positions faster than clinicians can update their disapproval." — Intro, l.361

### 2.6 Hormones and endocrine needs are "no more natural than capitalism, and no more sinister than … food"
Against the left tendency to treat endocrinology as "a corporate plot", the editors insist transition is a response to "its own form of hunger"; the body is reshaped by capital but "transitions never belong to capitalism."

> "Too often, what passes for revolutionary thought on sex has done little better: endocrinology is reduced to a corporate plot. […] For us, the flows of hormones which can condemn or revive us are no more natural than capitalism, and no more sinister than filling our bellies with food." — Intro, l.241

> "But transitions never belong to capitalism. Even as they are always routed through it, they also run against it. The transgender revolutionary is one who can neither deny their cravings, nor curse themselves for their untoward identity. We resent the society that birthed us, just as we refuse to set aside the tools it has offered us." — Intro, l.243

### 2.7 Mutual aid, chosen family, communal houses (STAR House, ball houses, Q3C)
Concrete economies of survival outside family and state: Black and Latinx trans women's mutual-aid networks and ball houses; STAR House funded by sex work and "liberated" food; Cohen & Monk's "queer household"; Dickson's Queer Communist Community Center.

> "Largely without the access to funding and medicalised legitimacy held by white-led NGOs, trans people of colour (particularly Black and Brown trans women) have developed extensive grassroots mutual aid networks and chosen familial structures." — Ch.1 (Zazanis), l.539

> "Renting a building from the Mafia and paying the $200 rent through sex work, STAR provided shelter, food, clothing, friends, and political solidarity, primarily for Latinx street queens. STAR House consisted of four rooms, housing up to 25 street queens and homeless gays and lesbians at once." — Ch.5 (Raha), l.1121

> "STAR House functioned with a division of labour: the older group members selling sex to shield the younger queens from the dangers of working on the street, while the younger queens 'liberated food'." — Ch.5, l.1123

> "She describes STAR's work as including babysitting for locals, and feeding 'half of the neighborhood because we had an abundance of food the kids liberated'. Rivera described such solidarity as 'a revolutionary thing'. […] '[t]here was always food in the house and everyone had fun'." — Ch.5, l.1125

> "[Y]our life as a working class or poor and/or sex working and/or disabled and/or Black or brown femme person has taught you that the only damn way you or anybody survives is by helping each other. No institutions exist to help us survive – we survive because of each other. Your life is maintained by a complex, non-monetary economy of shared, reciprocal care …" — Ch.5 (Raha quoting Piepzna-Samarasinha), l.1037

> "It's common that in response to this, queers and other similarly displaced people form alternative arrangements, today most commonly referred to as 'chosen family'. These are social relations which are simultaneously more free and open to creative adaptations, among more relatively equal individuals. But they are also more precarious, pressed, and less stable for precisely those same reasons." — Ch.7 (Doyle Griffiths), l.1573

> "the Queer Communist Community Center, or Q3C. It's a large warm building in a state of bad repair, brimming with transgender communists, all of us some combination of poor, disabled, or mentally ill. Much of our time is spent attempting to build a language and way of relating to one another." — Ch.12 (Dickson), l.2355

### 2.8 Unwaged queer/trans reproductive labour should be counted and recognised (Wages Due Lesbians; "wages for transition")
Raha argues the "emotional housework of surviving as lesbian women" is already productive labour; the demand for wages demystifies it. Harry Josephine Giles' "Wages for Transition" is cited as a successor.

> "the particular physical and emotional housework of surviving as lesbian women in a hostile and prejudiced society" — Ch.5 (Raha quoting Wages Due Lesbians), l.975

> "Wages Due Lesbians argued that wages for lesbian emotional work would provide queer women 'the economic power to afford sexual choices and [such that we] can come out in millions', building a life away from the heterosexual family." — Ch.5, l.1021

> "'Homosexuality and heterosexuality are both working conditions … but homosexuality is the workers' control of production'" — Ch.5 (Raha quoting Federici's *Wages Against Housework*), l.1033

> "We must recognise and affirm the necessary work for our survival within racial capitalist society as work. Work is required to flourish, when our lives are marked for destruction within racial capitalist nation-states" — Ch.5, l.1139

> "Harry Josephine Giles' 'Wages for Transition' manifesto makes political demands in the context of the collective, communised labour of gender transition." — Ch.5 note 21, l.1191

### 2.9 Work imposes gender discipline on everyone; employment is "an institution of gender violence"
O'Brien: trans unemployability reveals that all jobs demand gendered comportment. Trans work struggles therefore open onto a general working-class fight "over rights to self-determine one's body".

> "Trans people have so much trouble finding work because work itself imposes gendered expectations on everyone." — Ch.2 (O'Brien), l.677

> "Like many people's experience of the family, employment is an institution of gender violence and everyday coercion." — Ch.2, l.683

> "Trans people fighting for dignity in formal employment, and the right to survive outside of work, are the leading edge for working-class people to discover the power and pleasure in refusing the gender expectations imposed on everyone." — Ch.2, l.689

> "Rather than identity politics being arbitrarily opposed to labour struggles, in trans-work organising they are inseparable." — Ch.2, l.691

### 2.10 Queer/trans workers as a "vanguard-in-itself" / militant minority at "chokepoints of social reproduction"
Doyle Griffiths: queers are over-represented in paid social reproduction and logistics, dispossessed from the family, and so bridge workplace, community and socialist organisation.

> "I argue that trans and queer experiences can be seen as offering us additional access to an often neglected aspect of the fragility of capitalist relations. Namely, chokepoints of social reproduction." — Ch.7 (Doyle Griffiths), l.1527

> "Or perhaps be considered as a kind of vanguard-in-itself. One of several vanguards, but representing that potential as an actually existing social force, a basis of militant minority organising across and between workplace and community. […] Queer/ trans workforces are the revenge of the margins." — Ch.7, l.1555

> "Far from being an obstacle to be overcome in the class war, trans and queer workers are and can be organised as its leading edge." — Ch.7, l.1613

> "The left is not only unusually 'tolerant' of queers and trans people: it also consists of us." — Ch.7, l.1551

### 2.11 "To each according to her need": particular needs are class demands; need vs desire is a capitalist distinction
Trans healthcare refuses "a one-size-fits-all profit-driven standard of what constitutes the bare minimum necessity"; Gabriel (via Rosenberg) frames this as "a demand upon our own pleasure".

> "Here, the lived experience of a small group of people recalls and exemplifies that original demand central to the socialist or communist vision – that 'to each according to [her] need' entails a recognition of different individual and particular needs. And that the distinction between need and desire is as much an artificial product of capitalist logic as the division between politics and economy, or between public and private." — Ch.7, l.1565

> "Our struggle to secure social reproduction leads us to refuse to surrender ourselves to a one-size-fits-all profit-driven standard of what constitutes the bare minimum necessity for human survival." — Ch.7, l.1563

> "The implications of this goes beyond the politics of 'bread and butter', to one of bread and roses." — Ch.7, l.1539

> "'transsexual demands to exercise autonomy, however, enabled by social or medical interventions, over our configurations of embodiment and sexuality pertain to a category of high-stakes desires and pleasures, whose utopian core is the demand for the body to be a site of affirmation over and against routine abjection'." — Afterword (Rosenberg quoting Kay Gabriel), l.2905

### 2.12 Against the state; policing as gender enforcement; abolition
The state "literally polices gender"; Stonewall's lesson is that "the police and the capitalist state are enemies of the oppressed"; Angela Davis: challenging the gender binary teaches that prisons can be resisted.

> "In this context, Marxism can offer explanations that prevent inevitable burnout from backsliding into mere cynicism. It can reorient us away from liberal optimism, and the predictable shocks that follow on from it. It directs us against the state, and the naturalisation of human exploitation." — Intro, l.225

> "What's more, they are enforced through the violent regulatory practices of the state, which literally polices gender through the criminalisation of sex work and through targeted police violence against and incarceration of Black and Brown trans people." — Ch.1 (Zazanis), l.521

> "That movement received support from the feminist movement, the Black Panther Party, and other leftists and taught us a powerful lesson: the police and the capitalist state are enemies of the oppressed and the struggle against exploitation." — Ch.6 (Guitzel), l.1425

> "'[T]he trans community taught us that it is possible to effectively challenge that which is considered the very foundation of our sense of normalcy. So if it is possible to challenge the gender binary, then we can certainly, effectively, resist prisons, and jails, and police'." — Afterword (Rosenberg quoting Angela Davis), l.2887

> "Each of us struggles to pass through the eye of the state, to subsist as best we can." — Intro, l.355

### 2.13 Sexual liberation is twinned with class struggle; sexual repression is a mechanism of class rule
Guitzel's Trotskyist framing: heteronormativity and the binary serve "a specific economic system"; the body is a terrain of class struggle (FHAR, Perlongher). Metcalfe: "Post-capitalism will come only after the phallus."

> "Control over others' bodies, the imposition of binary genders pre-defined by genitalia, heteronormative sexuality, and cisgender conformity are not 'natural'. These norms are at the service of a specific economic system, which favours a specific social class that organises society (and consequently its morality) to serve its own class interests. […] We should embark on a sexual revolution capable of destroying repressive sexual morality." — Ch.6 (Guitzel), l.1395

> "Sexual liberation is twinned with the liberation of workers from the condition of wage slavery." — Ch.6, l.1445

> "For us, the class struggle also passes through the body. Which means that our refusal to withstand the dictatorship of the bourgeoisie is freeing the body of this prison in which it has been systematically bound for 2,000 years of sexual repression, of alienated labor, and economic oppression." — Ch.6 (quoting FHAR, 1971), l.1429

## 5. Historical / premodern hooks (anything lending a medieval or pre-capitalist veneer)

The book is resolutely modern (nothing on medieval Europe, guilds, monasticism, peasant movements or heresies by name — grep returned zero for "medieval", "guild", "monast", "feudal", "peasant" [except Shonkwiler's "peasantism"], "heresy"/"heretic" [except Flower's bio: "heretical tendencies of the twentieth century", l.3229]). ### 5.1 Marx's "old mole" vs the imperial eagle (Bataille) — chthonic vs celestial
> "The eagle's hooked beak, which cuts all that enters into competition with it and cannot be cut, suggests its sovereign virility...Politically the eagle is identified with imperialism […] Revolutionary idealism tends to make of the revolution an eagle above eagles, a supereagle striking down authoritarian imperialism." — Intro quoting Bataille, l.233

> "Meanwhile, brought back to the subterranean action of economic facts, the 'old-mole' revolution hollows out chambers in a decomposed soil repugnant to the delicate nose of the utopians. […] Marx's point of departure has nothing to do with the heavens, preferred station of the imperialist eagle as of Christian or revolutionary utopias. He begins with the bowels of the earth, as in the materialist bowels of proletarians." — Intro quoting Bataille, l.235

> "Following the mole's tracks, Transgender Marxism unearths the base needs of trans proles and brings them above ground, into clearer view." — Intro, l.237

### 5.2 The *oikos* and Aristotle; the sovereign patriarch of the estate
> "So to grasp capitalism as it plays out in our lives is to grasp the oikos (household) as it shapes history – as households replenish themselves, our personal histories mingle with the fate of our governing mode of production." — Intro, l.311
