## 1. The argument in brief

- **Functional, not essential.** Classical rabbinic law "cannot be described as an ontological or teleological essentialism"; it institutes "a functional gender duality, anchored in the need of reproduction of the Jewish collective body" (2006, p. 82).
  - Essentialism about women's nature "entered the halakhic discourse only later, as is the case with Maimonides's Aristotelianism and Neoplatonic traditions" (2006, p. 82).
  - Ibn Ezra on Deut. 22:5 is her example of the later view that women were created for reproduction (2006, p. 82 n. 5).
- **The law still enforces the match of body and gender.** Halakha "aims to enforce and normativize congruence between sexed bodies and gendered identities" (2006, p. 82). Her examples:
  - the cross-dressing ban (Deut. 22:5);
  - sex acts coded male or female;
  - the "ritualization of the sexed body": circumcision "inscribes" the male body, niddah "circumscribes" the female (2006, p. 83).
- **The rabbis appeal to tradition, not nature.** They "do not legitimate their overt gender dimorphism by taking recourse to nature" but to biblical law under divine authority, so Butler's critique "hardly appl[ies]" (2007, p. 272).
- **Sex is read from the genitals.** "One circumcises the baby at the place from which it can be recognized whether he is male or female" (t. Shabbat 15:9; 2007, p. 274; also 2006, p. 84, citing Gen. Rab. 46).
- **The male body passes as "human".** *'Adam* can be read as the male body, "but never by the female body"; women have four limbs beyond the 248 (b. Bekhorot 45a; 2007, pp. 279–80; 2006, pp. 85–86).
- **Saris and aylonit are not genders.** They are "men or women who cannot reproduce", like minors: categories that change obligations "but not their sexual identity" (2006, p. 93). R. Eliezer calls them "the male" and "the female" (m. Niddah 5:9; 2006, p. 92). *Saris* "would better be translated as a 'sterile' or 'infertile' man" (2006, p. 87).
- **Signs, but no physiognomy.** The baraita of signs (t. Yevamot 10:6–7) shares its signs with Greco-Roman physiognomy (Polemo, Dio Chrysostom, Clement). Its aim differs. The physiognomists hunt hidden effeminacy; the rabbis "infer a potential incapacity to reproduce before marriage is contracted" (2006, p. 92). There is "no rabbinic equivalent for the discipline of physiognomy, nor ... for the *kinaidos*" (2006, p. 104).
- **The androginos is male by default.** The penis has "greater signifying force than the female organ": the androginos dresses as a man and "may take a wife, but cannot be taken as a wife" (2006, p. 100; 2007, p. 281). When a decision is needed, "the default sex of the *androginos* is the male" (2006, p. 104).
  - R. El'azar's minority view (t. Yevamot 10:2) exempts vaginal intercourse with an androginos from stoning. That view "would indeed maintain the hybridity ... as a valid legal subject position" (2007, p. 282).
  - The androginos baby is not circumcised on the Sabbath: "not man enough to justify superseding the laws of the Sabbath" (M. Shabbat 19:3; 2007, p. 284).
- **The androginos never changes; the tumtum may.** "Nowhere in rabbinic literature" is the androginos expected to change or be changed. The *tumtum* is "a not-yet sexed person", who may be opened surgically and turn out a man, "or perhaps even a saris" (2006, p. 94).
- **R. Yose is a marginal opinion.** The "creature in his own right" opinion (R. Meir in the editio princeps) is "entirely marginalized in the tannaitic discourse" (2006, p. 99 n. 56). Had the rabbis adopted it, they would have denied the androginos "any legal subject position" (2007, p. 282). Roman practice is the contrast: drowning such infants, or Pliny's "entertainments" (2007, p. 282, n. 33).
- **No denunciation of the person.** The rabbis do not "denounce the person or character of the *androginos*. Rather, the devil lies in the sexual act itself" (2007, p. 287). Hermaphrodites "are not only human but also Jews" (2007, p. 288).
- **The thesis.** "Sex is variable but gender is not" (2007, p. 289). The aim was "to demonstrate that the Torah ... could absorb everything under its mantle" (2007, p. 289). In 2006 she puts it this way: the system "manages to maintain its fundamental gender binarism in Jewish law" (2006, p. 104).

## 2. What matters for the design

### 2.1 Cross-dressing and the Purim revel

- Deut. 22:5 "presumes self-evident categories" of men's and women's apparel (2006, pp. 82–83). The Talmud reads men's apparel as weapons (b. Nazir 59a), and women's apparel as plucking hair (b. Nazir 59a, b. Shabbat 94b, b. Makkot 20b) or as women's jewellery. Perfuming is contested (b. Berakhot 43b) (2006, p. 83).
- Via Satlow, the Talmud's editorial voice (b. Nazir 59a) explains the ban as fear that a man dressed as a woman "will slip disguised among women" (2006, p. 83 n. 7).
- **Confirms** the built `tm_hipukh.0011.desc`: young men in "women's anklets and necklaces" are breaking the jewellery reading of Deut. 22:5. It also supports `tm_hipukh.0003.desc`, where the worthies cite the law against a man adorning himself as a woman. Fonrobert does not discuss lashes, so the lashes stay sourced to Maimonides (see `jud_rabbinic_primary_texts.md`).
- **Do not put the b. Nazir 59a rationale on screen.** It is the "man in women's spaces" argument, which Strassfeld (*Trans Talmud* ch. 2) traces in today's anti-trans "bathroom bills". A worthy can cite the verse. He should not voice its rationale.

### 2.2 The Three Lamps

- Fonrobert's "ritualization of the sexed body" (2006, p. 83) is the scholarly form of what the tenet `tenet_tm_three_lamps_desc` says in the faith's voice: "a woman is made by what she keeps". The rabbis made women's gender through niddah. The sect turns that into a claim: keep it and you are one. This is a good fit, and it should stay in the faith's voice.
- **A predictable rabbinic objection.** Niddah is triggered by menstrual blood, and the "chamber" metaphors of m. Niddah 2:5 locate it in the womb (2007, pp. 275–76). A rabbi can say the days of separation are meaningless without blood. A rival-faith reaction or an elder's dispute line could use this. *Suggestion:* *"Separation from what? There is no blood."* The reply in the faith's voice: *"There is a calendar."*
- **Obligation.** The androginos "is obligated with regard to all the commandments mentioned in the Torah, like men" (t. Bikkurim 2:4; 2006, p. 97; 2007, p. 292 n. 36). Men are always obligated and women only sometimes (m. Kiddushin 1:7; 2006, pp. 80–81).
  - A rabbi will say a Turned woman stays bound to the time-bound commandments: tefillin, tallit, the quorum.
  - That makes "keeping only the women's commandments" a sin of omission in rabbinic eyes, not a neutral choice. It is good material for the *niddui* (shunning) branch of `tm_hipukh.0003`.

### 2.3 "What Came Forth Male"

- The opinion name (`tm_jewish_daughter_opinion`) and the rabbis' line in `tm_hipukh.0040` match Fonrobert's reading exactly. Sex is fixed at circumcision by the genitals (t. Shabbat 15:9; 2007, p. 274). Categories such as saris change obligations, not sexual identity (2006, p. 93). **Confirmed; no change.**
- "A body made by human hands is still a man's" (`tm_hipukh.0040.desc`) is also consistent. The *saris adam* is a man who "becomes incapable of reproduction by human interference" (2006, p. 88).

### 2.4 The aylonit: Fonrobert's additions

- **"Masculine woman".** In 2007 Fonrobert glosses *aylonit* as "the masculine woman" (2007, p. 280). The category concerns reproduction, "much more ... than ... differentiating between sexual identities" (2007, p. 280).
- **The etymology** (2006, p. 87 n. 18). b. Ketubbot 11a derives the word from *ayil*, "ram-like" woman. The **Arukh** (Nathan ben Yeḥiel of Rome, 11th c.; *dating mine*) rejects this and derives it from *ilan*, a "wood-like" or "tree-like" woman, vocalised *ilonit*.
  - This is real **medieval reception**, and a usable one. The hard limits forbid animal imagery, so the ram is out. The tree is in.
  - It also meets Isaiah 56:3, "let not the eunuch say, I am a withered tree", which the sect already claims (`jud_strassfeld_classically_queer.md` §2.4 no. 4).
- **Breasts are not a sign of the saris.** Breasts are missing from the saris's signs (2007, p. 292 n. 24). A priest with breasts "hanging like those of a woman" has a blemish that bars him from the altar, but he is not an androginos (m. Bekhorot 7:5; 2006, p. 95 n. 42; 2007, pp. 280–81). The rabbis did not read breasts on a man as a change of sex.

### 2.5 Medieval commentators in these chapters

These are the only medieval readers Fonrobert cites. All of them are useful for period-true hostile or neutral lines.
- **Rashi** (Troyes, d. 1105) on b. Yevamot 80b glosses the saris's smooth flesh "smooth as women's flesh. The norm of men's flesh is to be hairy, rough" (2006, p. 88 n. 22). He also reads "his hair is soft" (n. 22), the foaming urine (n. 23) and the *aylonit*'s raised mons (n. 26).
- **Rashi** on b. Bekhorot 41a explains R. Ishmael's view that the doubly-sexed firstborn animal has the greatest blemish: "because the place of femininity is like a blemish" (2006, p. 102). Fonrobert: overall the system gives the male organ "greater signifying force".
- **Tosafot** on b. Shabbat 136b (the androginos's circumcision) and on b. Bekhorot 42a (2006, p. 101 n. 61; p. 102 n. 63). Not quoted.
- **Maimonides and Ibn Ezra** as the entry of essentialism (2006, p. 82 and n. 5).
- **The Arukh** on *aylonit* (see §2.4).

## 4. Design implications

1. **A Rashi line for the worthies** (`tm_hipukh.0003` or `tm_hipukh.0040`). Rashi is a near neighbour in time and place (Troyes, d. 1105), and Provençal scholars knew his commentaries well. *Suggestion:* a worthy reading the signs: *"Rashi says a man's flesh is rough and hairy by nature. Hers is smooth. That makes her a saris, not a woman."* This is accurate to 2006, p. 88 n. 22. It lets the opposition sound learned rather than hateful, which matches the "dry, legal" tone rule.
2. **The tree, not the ram.** If an elder ever glosses *aylonit*, use the Arukh's *ilonit*, "tree-like", tied to Isaiah 56:3. *Suggestion* for `tm_hipukh.0020` or the Three Lamps tenet's flavour: *"The Arukh reads aylonit as ilonit, a woman like a tree. Let no one say, I am a withered tree."* Never use the ram (hard limit on animal imagery).
3. **The Maimonidean worthies.** Fonrobert dates gender essentialism in halakha to Maimonides' Aristotelianism (2006, p. 82). Arles in the 1300s sits inside the Provençal controversy over Maimonides and philosophy (*context mine; not in Fonrobert*). The worthies who call the sect before them could be split along that line. A philosophical worthy says a woman's nature is matter and receptivity. A traditionalist worthy says only that the law counts genitals. Each is a different "lose" flavour for `tm_hipukh.0003.a.lose`. *Design suggestion; the Provençal controversy needs its own source.*
4. **Obligation as the real charge.** Strengthen the *niddui* branch with t. Bikkurim 2:4's "obligated ... like men" and m. Kiddushin 1:7. Her sin in the worthies' eyes is not the dress (Purim allows that for one day) but dropping men's time-bound commandments. This fits the built "Purim is over, and I am still wearing the dress" and makes the dispute about law, not disgust.
5. **Narrator rule, reinforced.** For any encyclopedia or faith-description text: the rabbis admitted bodies that were both, neither, or sterile into the law, and "integrated them into the system" (2007, p. 288), so that the law stayed two-gendered. Use "sex is variable but gender is not" (with credit) rather than any count of genders.
