## 1. The argument in brief (Rowson, ch. 6)

- **The frame: Edward II and William of Adam.** Rowson opens with Edward II and Gaveston (pp. 204-205). He then turns to the Dominican William of Adam, "writing about 1318" (p. 205), who claims that among the Saracens any sexual act is "not only not forbidden, but permitted and praised" (p. 205).
  - William also attacks effeminate men who shave their beards, wear women's finery, sell themselves to men and "cohabit as husband and wife" (p. 205).
  - He attacks eastern Christians who fatten their sons for sale to Saracens (p. 205).
  - Rowson reads this as polemic, but polemic that knew two real things: the *mukhannath*, "to whom Muslim societies accorded a recognized, if not universally approved, role", and the Mamluk practice of buying slave boys (p. 205).
  - Rowson takes William from Michael Uebel's chapter in Farmer and Pasternack (eds.), *Gender and Difference in the Middle Ages* (2003), pp. 244-245 and n. 63 (n. 2). That is the same volume as Rowson 2003, which the README already lists.
- **The one sentence on the Mamluk *mukhannath*.** "Beard-shaving, cross-dressing, and passive homosexuality are all very well attested for the mukhannaths in this period; cohabitation is not" (n. 3, p. 230). William's "husband and wife" is therefore the claim with no Arabic support.
- **Ahmad b. al-Nasir Muhammad (r. 1342).** An heir disgraced over a young man, al-Shuhayb, at Karak (pp. 205-208). Rowson thinks the young man's unsuitability was probably class, not sex: he was "just inappropriately plebeian" (p. 208).
- **The Fatimid caliph al-Zafir (r. 1149-54).** Rumour held that the caliph did with Nasr b. 'Abbas "what is done with women" (p. 209). Usama b. Munqidh quipped that a district's revenues were "Hardly an excessive bride-price for such as you!" (p. 210). Goaded by this, Nasr killed the caliph.
  - Rowson's point: "it was Nasr, as the presumed passive partner, whose honor was at stake" (p. 210). The caliph's own honour was not ruined.
- **The rule of the period.** Homoerotic feeling expressed in public, above all in poetry, "was fully sanctioned", while "too-public, too-sexual homosexual behavior was not" (pp. 212-213). Chaste love (*'udhri*) was the accepted way to say it (pp. 224-225). Al-Saruji (d. 1294) is the example: chaste and pious, and buried in his beloved's grave by the boy's own father (p. 224).
- **Prosecutions.**
  - Sodomy charges were a weapon against scholars. The vizier Ibn Sal'us suborned a young man to accuse the jurist's son 'Abd al-Rahman, who was humiliated and jailed without proof (p. 213).
  - The jurist Ibn al-Baqaqi was charged in 1287 with "declaring illicit things licit" (*istihlal al-muharramat*), which was "legally understood to imply apostasy and thus justify the death penalty". He had taught that wine and *liwat* (sex between men) were permitted, and was beheaded in central Cairo in 1302 (pp. 213-214).
  - Anti-vice campaigns came in waves: Baybars, Lajin (1296-99), and Barsbay at Damietta in 1428. They never lasted, partly because tavern and brothel taxes were lost (p. 215).
  - A Christian, Ibn al-Mallah, tried before the sultan himself for public sodomy, "promptly converted to Islam" and was forgiven (pp. 215-216).
- **Castration as a punishment (post-1453).** Qaytbay had a Turk castrated for sodomy with his own mamluks, "apparently a then unprecedented punishment" (p. 216).
  - Rowson notes that castration "flew in the face of Islamic law" but could be justified as *siyasa* (state interest) (n. 36).
  - In Cairo, eunuchs were "theoretically" all castrated outside Islamic territory, by non-Muslims (n. 36).
  - Note 36 also names the religion of an operator. **Do not use that detail** (hard rule on Jewish slave and eunuch-trade content).
- **Contemporaries blamed the rise in sodomy on someone else.**
  - Al-Maqrizi blamed the sultan Barquq (r. 1382-99) (pp. 216-217).
  - Ibn Taghri Birdi answered that the "scourge" was "very old". He dated it to Abu Muslim's Khurasanians in 749-50, and pointed out that al-Nasir Muhammad had promoted beardless favourites to the highest command and married them to his daughters (pp. 218-219).
  - Rowson: "the idea that they 'got' it from someone else seems to be well nigh universal" (p. 221). He calls the importation theory "clearly invalid" (p. 229).
- **Women imitating men, as moralists saw it.** Under Faraj (r. 1399-1412), "love of males" was so common among the elite that "their wives decided to imitate males", wearing the "Circassian cap", and the city's women followed (al-Maqrizi, p. 217). Prostitutes "were obliged to imitate boys" (n. 39). (Context only; women dressing as men is out of scope as precedent.)
- **The barracks and the eunuchs who ran them.**
  - Al-Maqrizi recalls the eunuch *muqaddam al-mamalik* checking a boy's underwear when the boy performed the major ablution (p. 227).
  - Al-Subki (d. 1370) says such eunuch officers "have now become notorious for pimping their charges" (p. 227).
  - Al-Subki also writes that the *jamdariyya* (valets, mostly beardless boys) "outdo women in adorning themselves". He rules that it is forbidden for any of them "to imitate women in what they were created for" (p. 227).
- **Ghazan and the envoy.** Ghazan asked why Mamluk amirs "abandon women and have recourse to young men". The envoy al-Mujiri blamed Turghay's Oirats (p. 222).
  - Al-Maqrizi's own account of the Oirats says the amirs wanted their children "according to their tastes for females or males". It "does not here imply" any rise in homoeroticism (pp. 223-224).
- **Rowson's conclusion.** Interest in adolescent males was widespread and taken for granted; acts were "widely deplored". A prince who "lost his head over a young soldier" was "neither insane nor hopelessly perverted" (p. 228). Prosecutions may have increased (p. 229).

## 2. Other chapters (pre-1500 material only)

- **Lagrange, ch. 5 (Buyid Iran, 10th c.).** The subject is al-Tawhidi's (d. c. 1010) libel of the vizier al-Sahib Ibn 'Abbad.
  - He gives the vocabulary of male irregularity: *mukhannath* ("effeminate, sometimes transvestite male"), *luti* (active), *ma'bun* or *halaqi* (passive), *mu'ajar* (male prostitute) and *amrad* (beardless page) (p. 163).
  - The insult image is the *mukhannath ashmat*, "the 'old queen'", used to mock a minister's lack of self-control (p. 182).
  - The ninth-century poet Jahshawayh "flaunted his status as a catamite", but anthologists "refuse to listen". They called it a joke and said he was really the active partner (p. 177).
  - Lagrange disputes Rowson's claim that the *mukhannath*'s gender inversion rationalised his sexual role, at least for the tenth century (n. 38, pp. 200-201).
- **Amer, ch. 3, and Epps, ch. 4 (FtM; reaction data only).** In the *Nights* tale of Qamar al-Zaman and Budur, Budur rules as king in men's clothes and marries a princess.
  - Amer argues that in the Arabic tale this cross-dressing causes no "category crisis", because Budur keeps her royal rank throughout (pp. 94-95).
  - Epps (pp. 150-152) picks out the wedding-night joke. Finding no "minaret", Qamar takes the king to be "neither man nor woman but a white eunuch" (p. 150). An unexpected body is read as a eunuch.
  - The date of the tale's recension is *unverified* and probably late.
- **al-Kassim, Epilogue.** She repeats Lagrange's list of terms (p. 323). There is nothing new.

## 4. Design implications in brief

- **Mamluk reaction is mockery plus prosecution, not tolerance.** Use a pulse: a Mamluk ruler's opinion of the faith worsens during anti-vice campaigns. A random "Anti-Vice Campaign" event (Baybars type) gives a temporary hostile modifier that ends when tax income is missed (p. 215). It suits a court event for Egypt and Syria after 1250.
- **Eunuch officers as moral police.** The *muqaddam al-mamalik* (p. 227) gives a Mamluk court a natural hostile inspector: the eunuch who polices boys would police a daughter. This links to Adelphai Dorleyaiou's line on the eunuch estate ("the court ... buys them as boys").
- **Nothing here supports a fourth faith.** No Mamluk male-to-female institution appears. The chapter's value is reaction text, plus correction #1 to built loc.
