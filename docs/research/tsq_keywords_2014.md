## 1. The introduction (Stryker and Currah, pp. 1–18)

- The editors present trans studies as the study of the power that names and manages gender-variant people, not only of those people. Trans people are to be "subjects of knowledge as well as objects of knowledge" (p. 9).
- They warn that "transgender", stretched over the world, can become "a kind of Cartesian grid imposed on the globe" (p. 8). Linking it to mahu, hijra, eunuch, berdache and the rest has often been "epistemological violence" (p. 7).
- They allow "transgender" as a way to ask questions of the past, but say it is anachronistic as an identity label for earlier periods (p. 8).
- The rest is about funding the journal and the field's place in the academy.

**Usable.** The warning against projecting one category onto every culture backs the existing rules: no "third gender" framing, no hijra link, each faith its own words. It agrees with Bhatt on the point that matters here. It does not make Bhatt's further point that non-Western patriarchy is real and must not be romanticised; keep both.

## 2. Essays read in full: gist and what is usable

### 2.1 History, religion and region

**Whittington, *Medieval* (pp. 125–129).** Medieval Europe (500–1400) is neither a dark age nor a model to copy. Holy women who "became male" (Greek *apandros*) were admired, but "any instances of men becoming women were condemned or met with confusion" (p. 126). Medical writers described intersex bodies in neutral terms (the "seven-celled uterus", whose middle cell made intersex children; masculine women and effeminate men from the balance of seed). Jurists and theologians wanted a fixed sex. Intersex people could sometimes choose their sex and marital role, but the choice could not be unmade. The *Roman de Silence* shows "what concepts of transsexuality remain when the surgeon and clinician are eliminated" (p. 127, quoting Lurkhur). Alchemy used the hermaphrodite as a figure of change.
*Usable.* (a) The one-way ladder: women may climb to manhood, men may not climb down. This is Bhatt's "the binary has always been a hierarchy", in period form, and it is exactly how Byzantine and Latin clergy would see the Sisters. (b) The rule that a chosen sex is binding once chosen gives vanilla jurists a period voice on re-embodiment. (c) The seed and seven-cell theories feed the Aristotelian and Galenic dispute. **Verify** before use: the seven-celled uterus is a Latin Western text of the 12th–13th centuries (Cadden), not Byzantine.

**Altınay, *Islam and Islamophobia* (pp. 115–118).** The Qur'an's one reference to non-normative gender is 24:31: women need not keep modesty before male attendants who are free of desire and use women's codes. Some hadith curse cross-dressers. Acceptance goes to a desexualised trans person; one who seems to have desire, especially toward men, is stigmatised. Modern debates on surgery split Sunni and Shi'i jurists. Altınay also attacks Western Islamophobia that paints Islam as uniquely transphobic, and notes conservatism inside Muslim LGBT circles.
*Usable.* The verse is a period hook for how an Islamic household might admit a daughter: as an attendant "without desire", which is the eunuch's cage, not womanhood. That is third-sexing by admission, in Bhatt's sense. The hadith gives hostile text (attributed, §4). The surgery fatwas are 20th-century and out of period. Islamophobia caution: see §6.

**Chiang, *Sinophone* (pp. 184–187).** The first reported "Chinese transsexual" was Xie Jianshun in Taiwan in 1953, a soldier first reported as intersex. The press headline: "Christine Will Not Be America's Exclusive: Soldier Destined to Become a Lady" (p. 185). The Nationalist state used its mastery of Western sex medicine to show it was modern.
*Usable, weakly.* The state proves its power by deciding a body's sex. That fits the Yutai Dao's palace and its edict ordinations (`tm_ordain_by_edict_interaction`): recognition granted to display the throne's reach, and so revocable. Nothing from the 20th-century case itself.

**Salah, *Subaltern* (pp. 200–205).** From Gramsci to Spivak. Spivak: a resistant woman caught between imperial feminism and native nationalism cannot be heard. Stone: the medical model made transsexuals erase their pasts; "It is difficult to articulate a counter-discourse if one is programmed to disappear" (p. 201). Namaste, Spade and others: the rights-bearing trans subject is built by excluding sex workers, prisoners, the poor and the racialised.
*Usable.* (a) Spivak's double bind is Bhatt's "trapped between two forms of violence" (her ch. 6); it confirms Bhatt note row 14 for Ha-Nehpakhot. (b) Stone's "programmed to disappear" is the cost of the veil (H25) and the argument for the counter-record (Bhatt note row 6). (c) The excluded sex worker is the street daughter the Houses must not drop (§3 row 5).

**Wieringa, *Symbolic Subversion* (pp. 210–213).** Heteronormativity is enforced on a sliding scale of violence; resistance runs on a matching scale, from open defiance to quiet accommodation (James Scott's "hidden transcripts"). Based on research with widows, divorced women, sex workers and lesbians in India and Indonesia. "There is often a thin line between defiance and defeat" (p. 211).
*Checked for Badenya: there is no African woman-marriage material in this essay, and a full-text search of all 86 Keywords finds none (Africa appears only in passing: South Africa, Caster Semenya, activist networks).* The essay is South and Southeast Asian. What it gives Badenya, and every faith whose daughters live under another faith's lord, is the point that accommodation is also resistance. That matches Bhatt ch. 7 (do not demand that the abject be subversive). One passage concerns a double suicide; it is excluded (§5).

**Muñoz, *Tatume* (pp. 213–216).** A squash that is both "summer" and "winter" becomes a figure for transing categories, diaspora, food sovereignty and life on Haudenosaunee land. "Saving seeds, not souls" (p. 214).
*Not usable.* It is about living Indigenous communities and present-day land politics.

### 2.2 Hostility and its forms

**Bettcher, *Transphobia* (pp. 249–252).** Transphobia is not irrational; it has "a kind of rationality" inside a system that rewards it (p. 249). Do not define it as hostility to people who violate gender norms: a trans woman may see herself as a woman inside the binary, and such a definition "perversely enacts a verbal hostility that it fails to explain" (p. 250). Hostility has at least two shapes: unpersoning ("it") and exposure ("really a man disguised as a woman"). It differs by culture and setting, and it blends with misogyny, racism and contempt for sex workers.
*Usable.* A clean split for hostile text: the unpersoning voice (the "thing", the "third thing" of Galen) and the exposing voice (the deceiver). Bettcher's "within the binary" point agrees with Bhatt and the mod: daughters are women, not a third thing.

**Phillips, *Abjection* (pp. 19–21).** Kristeva's abjection: the self guards its borders by casting out what is mixed or unclassifiable. Stryker's reclaiming: "I am a transsexual, and therefore I am a monster" (p. 20). Halperin: normalised gay life sheds its abject members.
*Usable.* The casting-out logic is the hostile faiths' logic. The normalisation point matches Bhatt's "respectability is betrayal". Reclaiming belongs only to the speaker (B5).

**Koch-Rein, *Monster* (pp. 134–136).** Daly and Raymond used Frankenstein's creature for trans women's bodies; films and crime shows repeat it. Trans writers reclaim the monster, but must fight in the attacker's metaphor.
*Usable.* Hostile clergy and physicians may call the knife's work a botched, stitched body (attributed). Reclaiming is the daughters' right, never the narrator's.

**Seid, *Reveal* (pp. 176–178).** The "reveal" forces a trans person's body into public as the "truth" of her. Films about trans women often show genitals to provoke revulsion. The usual outcome is "that's really a man" (p. 177).
*Usable as a rule.* No event uses a body reveal as a plot twist or a joke. This is Bhatt's "Have you had the surgery?" from the other side (§3 row 8).

**Beauchamp, *Surveillance* (pp. 208–210).** The category was built by surveillance: clinic checklists, the demand to pass, paper trails. Trans people are suspect because they are "culturally constructed as concealing something" (p. 209). The system's contradictions show that its categories are made, not natural.
*Usable.* Confirms the "gates recognise, they do not test" rule and Bhatt note rows 4–5. Hostile officials read a daughter as a person hiding something.

**Vitulli, *Prison-Industrial Complex in the United States* (pp. 162–165).** Trans women of colour are policed as presumed sex workers ("walking while trans") and jailed by genitals in men's prisons. The essay opens with the case of a named living woman; that case is not used.
*Usable.* The general pattern supports Spring the Cell and the Rescue the Daughters CB: a captive daughter is held among men. See §3 row 15.

**Lennon and Mistler, *Cisgenderism* (pp. 63–65).** "Cisgenderism" names the ideology, not a fear.
*Usable, minor.* Supports writing hostility as doctrine of the vanilla faiths, not as personal fear.

### 2.3 Bodies, medicine, categories

**van Anders, *Bio/logics* (pp. 33–35).** Three ways of reasoning sex from "biology". Interior: the deepest part (genes, then gonads) is the truest. Newborn: sex is what a doctor sees at birth, so changing it needs surgery for a second inspection. Trace: what was once there still counts; "born-penises are meant to penetrate, born-vaginas to be penetrated (and born-vulvas to be ignored)" (p. 34).
*Usable, high.* A typology for every hostile line in the mod (§3 row 1). Trace logic is Bhatt's penetrability argument in a sentence. It also explains why re-embodiment never silences a hostile faith: trace logic survives any change of body.

**Morland, *Intersex* (pp. 111–114).** Infant genital surgery treats a social norm as a bodily fault: "ambiguity is an interpretation, not a trait; and one cannot do surgery on a norm" (p. 113). Shame the one who looks, not the child. Secrecy makes the strangeness it claims to remove.
*Usable.* A9 (forced gendering of intersex infants is sin) gets an event (§3 row 11). Intersex stays distinct from the Marked trait.

**Cotten, *Surgery* (pp. 205–208).** "Redrawing the body's sex contours affirms the feminist mantra that biology is not destiny" (p. 205). Surgery is agency and also a state tool when required for papers or tied to forced sterilisation.
*Usable.* The knife is chosen, never required for recognition (§3 row 4).

**Suess, Espineira and Walters, *Depathologization* (pp. 73–77).** Against classing transition as mental illness. Legal recognition often demands diagnosis, hormones and sometimes "genital surgery, sterilization, and divorce" (p. 74).
*Usable.* Forced divorce is a period-plausible demand by vanilla canon law (§3 row 20). A physician who calls her mad is a hostile voice.

**Howard, *Error* (pp. 82–84).** Medicine framed trans bodies as errors; artists embrace "error". A film title quotes the old taunt that a trans woman will never be a woman.
*Usable.* Only the taunt, attributed (§4).

**Engdahl, *Wrong Body* (pp. 267–270).** Critiques the "wrong body" story as gatekeeping and essentialist; prefers a phenomenological "lived body" in which wrongness is relative to situations of misreading.
*Caution.* Bhatt defends bodily transition against those who rank pure gender-play above it, and quotes Revathi wanting to be rid of the male form. The faiths keep that voice. Do not adopt Engdahl's relativising as doctrine (§5).

**Carter, *Transition* (pp. 235–237).** "Transition" replaced "sex change" and "the operation"; it implies duration, not an event. "Transition is a misnomer because you were here, like this, all along" (p. 236). If unlucky in kin, we may be buried under another's idea of our gender. One line describes self-harm; it is excluded (§5).
*Usable.* H39 ("the rite recognises, it does not create") in a line. The burial point gives a death event (§3 row 7).

**Castañeda, *Childhood* (pp. 59–61).** "The child is always already seen as incomplete, as not yet fully formed" (p. 59), so her word about her gender is doubted and her body is thought correctable.
*Usable.* Confirms: a Marked child may be named, but no bodily gate before adulthood (§3 row 19).

**Puar, *Disability* (pp. 77–82).** Trans and disability movements both turned "debility" into capacity, often by proving productive. "While the exceptional disabled body can overcome its limits, the trans body can potentially rehabilitate itself" (p. 77).
*Usable, minor.* Supports B6 and H32: a daughter's welcome does not depend on her being useful. Puar's "trans as motion, not identity" is set aside (§5).

**Rand, *Hips* (p. 98).** Hormones "shape gender in the bone"; money buys the right belts and clothes.
*Usable, minor.* Portrait work for re-embodiment.

**Sullivan, *Somatechnics* (pp. 187–191).** Body and technique are never separate; all bodies are modified.
*Usable, minor.* Supports F3, "the Mother's art over Nature".

### 2.4 State, law, rights, economy

**Stryker, *Biopolitics* (pp. 38–42).** "Gender, rather, is an apparatus within which all bodies are taken up" (p. 39), through records from birth to death. Trans people sit at the edge of what the system can count, which exposes them to death and sometimes, now, to managed inclusion.
*Usable.* The registers already in the mod (the palace's eunuch register, the clergy roll, the name-panel) are this apparatus in period form.

**Currah, *The State* (pp. 197–200).** No single state defines sex; each office does it its own way, so one person's sex can switch between offices. The liberal aim is to fix the "right" definition. The left aim is "to get the state out of the business of defining sex in the first place" (p. 198). Look at what a sex marker does, not what it is.
*Usable, high.* "Many ledgers" (§3 row 4): bishop, tax roll, levy, court office and House may each count her differently. Byzantine court offices already split daughters of the knife from other women. Caution on Bhatt: §6.

**Stanley, *Gender Self-Determination* (pp. 89–92).** A collective practice, tied to anticolonial, Black Power and prison abolition movements. Against hate-crime laws that grow prisons and against "X" passports as managed categories. Miss Major: "becoming liberated as we speak" (p. 92).
*Usable.* Supports H11 (no dungeons) and D7. Gives pressure on the H1 crime doctrine (§3 row 15).

**Irving, *Capital* (pp. 50–53).** Middle-class trans professionals came to speak for the movement and erased the labour of racialised trans women, many of them sex workers. Neoliberal life makes everyone an "entrepreneur of the self". Surplus people are called "worth-less, unproductive, unfit, backward, risky, and/or inflexible" (p. 52).
*Usable.* The Houses must honour the street daughters who did the work (§3 row 5). The essay also mentions two-spirit people; that term stays out of the design.

**Balzer/LaGata, *Human Rights* (pp. 99–103).** The shift from a medical to a human-rights frame (Yogyakarta, 2006; Argentina, 2012). Spivak's warning that rights can be an alibi for intervention. A backlash in the name of "traditional values" (p. 102).
*Usable, minor.* The "traditional values" backlash is the era-decay pattern the mod already has.

**González, *La Loca* (pp. 123–125).** *Loca*, a slur for effeminate gay men, reclaimed. Lemebel feared "a triumph of capitalist homosexuality" (p. 124) that would erase femininity.
*Usable, minor.* Agrees with Bhatt ch. 4: gay assimilation trades away gender variance. *Loca* names a living community; not used.

**Cano, *Revolution* (pp. 178–181).** Trans men; out of scope (§0).

### 2.5 Words and categories

**Singer, *Umbrella* (pp. 259–262).** The "transgender umbrella" diagram shelters many terms but also erases. Culturally specific terms like hijra or waria are folded in as "the transgender native", "a reassuring fantasy that gendered utopias exist elsewhere" (p. 261).
*Usable, high.* The strongest Bhatt-compatible essay in the issue. It backs "no third gender", "no hijra link" and "each faith its own words" (§3 row 16).

**Enke, *Translation* (pp. 241–245).** Stone's story from the Stanford clinic in 1972: a trans woman with a clay pipe says, "I am a woman. This is my pipe. Therefore, this is a woman's pipe" (p. 242). Translators of Japanese manga strip out self-gendering pronouns. Translation is power.
*Usable, high.* The pipe line is a ready model for the faiths' answer to "manly" taunts (§4.2). The pronoun point fits H22 and Ha-Nehpakhot's translation event (§3 row 12).

**Stone, *Guerrilla* (pp. 92–96).** "Reading oneself aloud" is not just coming out; it is writing oneself into the discourses that write you. "Speaking yourself disrupts both society's and culture's stupendous drive to speak you" (p. 95). Warns that movements decay into careers once they win a place.
*Usable.* E3 (Read Aloud) takes its name from Stone; this sharpens what it should reward (§3 row 6). The decay warning gives an event for old Houses (§3 row 5).

**Rawson, *Archive* (pp. 24–26).** An archive is a "technology of identity". It also refuses to let anyone be forgotten: transitioned people may feel betrayed by "the archive's stubborn and insistent refusal to forget" (p. 25).
*Usable.* A real tension inside E10 (keep the archive) and H28 (the old name buried as a corpse) (§3 row 7).

**Williams, *Transgender* (pp. 232–235).** The word's history; it was used as an umbrella from the 1970s, not coined by Virginia Prince.
*Not usable.*

**Aultman, *Cisgender* (pp. 61–63).** "Cis" marks the unmarked; Enke worries it naturalises birth sex.
*Usable, minor.* Bears on H3 (two estates) vs H38 (cis is not a caste). No change.

**Hines, *Feminism* (pp. 84–86).** Raymond's radical feminism was hostile; Butler and queer theory opened the way; trans activists and "third wave" transfeminism (Koyama) followed.
*Caution.* The essay treats radical feminism as the problem and queer theory as the cure. Bhatt is a radical feminist who reconciles Crenshaw with MacKinnon and who rejects deconstruction as the answer (§5).

**Amin, *Temporality* (pp. 219–222).** Transition stories follow "chrononormativity", a set time-line, which fits some lives and distorts others.
*Usable, minor.* Confirms delays that never reset a gate (the embryo rule; Ha-Nehpakhot's patience).

**Elliot, *Psychoanalysis* (pp. 165–169), and Carlson, *Psychoanalytic* (pp. 169–172).** Two overviews of trans-affirming Lacanian work (Gherovici's *sinthome*, Gozlan's scar, Carlson's hysteric). Covered in depth in `tsq_transpsychoanalytics_2017.md`.
*Not usable here* beyond what that note says.

## 5. Cautions

**Where essays conflict with Bhatt (the mod follows Bhatt).**
- *Hines, Feminism*: casts radical feminism as the enemy and queer deconstruction as the cure. Bhatt is a radical feminist who keeps the category "woman" and puts daughters in it. Do not let H37 speak for any main faith.
- *Engdahl, Wrong Body*: relativises bodily wrongness to "situations of misreading". Bhatt defends bodily transition as a real need and attacks ranking gender-play above it. The faiths keep the voice that wants to be rid of the male form.
- *Puar, Disability*, and the Deleuzian essays she draws on: "trans as a motion, not an identity". Bhatt's daughters are women, and they change sex. Do not use.
- *Currah, The State*, and *Stanley*: the goal of getting the state out of sex classification sits beside Bhatt's insistence that trans women change sex, not just papers. Keep both: the faiths' own law records daughters as women (A6), and no gate depends on the state.
- *Wieringa*: her frame is "heteronormativity". Bhatt's frame is male supremacy and the reproductive Nation. Wieringa's practical point (accommodation is resistance) agrees with Bhatt ch. 7; use only that.
- *Altınay*: his attack on Western Islamophobia agrees with Bhatt only in part. Bhatt also says that refusing to criticise a culture is wrong (her ch. 6). So: Islamic hostility is shown, attributed and never heavier than Christian or Jewish hostility, and Islamic courts are never written as uniquely cruel.
- *Medieval*: Laqueur's one-sex model is contested (Cadden; Park and Nye). The repo's `byz_king_one_sex_body.md` already treats it with care.

**No suicide or self-harm content.** Excluded: the double-suicide passage in *Symbolic Subversion* (p. 211); the self-harm line in *Transition* (p. 236); *Sick* (triaged) is about illness and medication. Nothing from these reaches loc, even paraphrased.

**Never a living community as a faith.** The essays name or describe: hijra and waria (Singer), two-spirit people (Irving), the Haudenosaunee and Boricua diaspora (Muñoz), Chilean *locas* (González), present-day Muslim trans people in Turkey and Iran (Altınay), and named living women (Vitulli's opening case; Miss Major in Stanley). None is used as a faith, a precedent or a model for an institution. Only general patterns are used.

**Trans men out of scope.** *Revolution* (read, not used), *Brown Bois* and *Transbutch* (listed). *Surgery*'s trans-masculine history is not used.

**Children.** Only the rule in §3 row 19. No child body content of any kind.

**Period checks before use.** The seven-celled uterus (Latin West, 12th–13th c.; Cadden); a Byzantine Greek word for "monster" (row 17); the Q 24:31 rendering and the hadith numbering (row 10).

**Antisemitism.** Row 12 is about a copyist's grammar inside the Jewish community, handled by the women of the lamp; it must not turn into a Christian lord "protecting" her from Jews (Bhatt note row 14).
