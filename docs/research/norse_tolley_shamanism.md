## 1. The argument

### Ch. 6, "Purposes" (Norse, pp. 135-142)
- **Seiðr is mostly divination, then spells.** Most cases are divinatory; a large minority are spells: weather, killing, impotence, fish into a fjord, shape-exchange (pp. 135, 141). There is "no evidence that seiðr was involved with healing", apart from Þorbjǫrg's fungus belt, a text he discounts (p. 135).
- **Divinatory seiðr is women's; *útiseta* is both sexes.** "Sitting out" at night was done "by both men and women (unlike divinatory seiðr, practised only by women)" (p. 136). The laws put it beside "waking up a troll".
- **The völva only reveals the harvest.** The questions concern the seasons and the year's produce. Nothing says the rite changed the harvest, "only that it revealed what they would be" (p. 137).
- **Verdict.** "The functional correspondence between seiðr and shamanism is minimal" (p. 142). The seiðkona is "a manipulator of the supernatural world" who more often increases conflict than resolves it (p. 142). He leaves an opening: the prose picture "may very well be a distortion" of something older and closer to shamanism, but the case "remains rather weak" (p. 142).

### Ch. 7, "Community and gender" (Norse, pp. 149-166)
- **The saga seiðr is a literary picture.** Seiðr in the sagas is "almost certainly largely the creation of the saga writers". The laws record "what people believed was happening around them, not what actually was" (p. 149). The social picture is "far more a picture of literary motifs than an ethnographic presentation" (p. 150).
- **Respected, feared, rejected.** The seiðkona is welcomed so that her forecast stays kind, as with Þorbjǫrg, and Oddbjǫrg in *Víga-Glúms saga* ch. 12, whom housewives had to welcome well (p. 150). The refusers (Þórhallr, Guðríðr, Örvar-Oddr) serve the saga's Christian or heroic theme (p. 150).
- **Völuspá's bargain.** The völva taunts Óðinn ("What do you ask me? Why do you try me?") until he pays her in jewellery, "buying her far-sighted mind in the way one might buy a whore's body" (pp. 150-151).
- **The platform and choir set her apart.** The *seiðhjallr* and the choir point to "separation rather than integration between audience and volva". The choir travels with her in *Örvar-Odds saga* ch. 3, so it is her own company, not the household (p. 151). **Key caution:** this arrangement may be modelled on church ritual, priest, clergy and choir set apart at the altar, imagined back onto pagans "without any basis in real tradition" (p. 151).
- **No resident völva.** She is "a mere communicator of information", not a negotiator. There is no district seeress like a clan shaman; she is a wanderer "very much on the edge of society", like "the gypsy fortune-teller of later days" (pp. 151-152). The wandering is a motif that makes her "other" (p. 152 n. 3). Settled magic-workers exist: Þórdís at Spákonufell, Þrándr at Gata (p. 152).
- **Women, and once central.** Divination was "indeed in the hands mainly of women" across the Germanic world, including Old English verse (p. 152). The ancient seeresses (Veleda, the Cimbrian priestesses) were political counsellors, not marginal. The sagas may show "an attenuation of their role" under Christianity (p. 152). Þorgerðr Hǫlgabrúðr, Hákon jarl's guardian *dís* and "bride", is the Norse echo (p. 153).
- **Women's house-cult against the æsir.** Following Dronke on *Völuspá* 21-24: Heiðr's house-based women's cult "threatens to undermine the world of the æsir" (p. 153). Tolley adds that a women's home cult is not naturally a threat (Korea shows the reverse). The opposition may reflect Christian-era politics (*Völsa þáttr*, c. 1380) (pp. 153-154).
- **Male seiðmenn are accusations.** Divinatory seiðr is ascribed only to women, though magicians in general are evenly split (Dillmann) (p. 154). Rǫgnvaldr réttilbeini was "simply a political threat" to his brother Eiríkr. Even calling his men *seiðmenn* "may be no more than accusation spread by his enemies". The only safe inference is that male seiðr was distasteful enough to be used for "political discreditation" (p. 154).
- **Possession, not soul-journey.** Using Bourguignon's split between visionary trance (men, hunters) and possession trance (women, farmers), the seiðkona "remained still (in a chair, for example)". She did not send her soul out ("for which, indeed, there is no evidence in seiðr") but "accepted spirits into or to herself" (p. 155).
- ***Ergi* defined.** "Opening oneself up for sexual penetration by an inappropriate person". For a man: by anyone, "a change in his essential nature". For a woman: outside a licit relationship, a matter "merely of the degree of her sexual openness" (p. 158). Women can be *org* ("nymphomaniac"; Venus in Hauksbók, p. 156), but rarely. *Ergi* also means loss of virility with age: "everyone grows argr as he grows older" (*Hrafnkels saga* ch. 8, p. 158).
- **Snorri's two traditions.** Ynglinga saga ch. 7 ("such great ergi accompanies this magic ... taught to the priestesses") fuses Freyja's ownership of seiðr with Loki's taunt in *Lokasenna* 24 (p. 155). Snorri ties *ergi* to seiðr absolutely, yet implies it was fine for priestesses (p. 156).
- **Gulaþing 196** (p. 156 n. 7) lists the *fullréttisorð*, including "has given birth to children". This is already in `norse_meulengracht_sorensen_unmanly_man.md`.
- **Against Clover, partly.** Clover's one-sex, one-gender model is "valid, to a point" but genre-bound: a 13th-century literary motif (p. 157). Women were seen as inferior, "but it would be going too far to deduce that women were shameful by nature" (p. 158).
- **Why seiðr is *argr*: relationality.** Clunies Ross's "penetrated by a spirit" is "somewhat too crude"; the sources never say spirits entered her (p. 158). Tolley's reading: seiðr asks the worker to lower her resistance and depend on spirits. The male hero is an individual; women, the old and the medium are defined by relationship. "Once individuality is set up as the favoured focus of aspiration, expressions of relationality come to be despised" (pp. 159-160). So *ergi* attaches to the medium, not to sitting-out or to sending spirits out (p. 160).
- **The vanir thrive on the Other.** "The vulnerability, indeed penetrability, of the seiðr practitioner was the key to her strength" (p. 163). In *Völuspá* 21-24 the vanir accept "the Other ... within their very bodies", and this makes them reborn "in a yet more vigorous state". The way to kill them is isolation, as in Skírnir's curse on Gerðr (p. 164).
- **Seiðr and heirs.** Two products of seiðr: knowledge of the future and heirs, "one abstract, the other physical" (p. 163). Óðinn wins Rindr by seiðr to beget an avenger (Kormákr, *Sigurðardrápa* 3); Signý swaps shape with a seiðkona to beget Sinfjǫtli (p. 160). A *farandi kona*, travelling woman, "was typically viewed as both a witch and a whore" (p. 161).
- **Óðinn crosses.** Practitioners "no doubt identified with the patron goddess". Men gained "a feminine sexuality" thought to give power "by virtue of overstepping a limen". This is his reading of Óðinn's motive in *Lokasenna*, like Siberian shamans who change sex (p. 164). He proposes emending *vitka* to *vitku* (fem.) in *Lokasenna* 24 to point at Saxo's Wecha (p. 160). **Note:** both the Rindr seduction and Saxo's Wecha are the rape the design already rejects as precedent.
- **Þrymskviða.** Þórr's *ergi* is the loss of the hammer, not the dress; ergi "opens no powers for Þórr" (p. 163). Already excluded as precedent.
- **Salutary note.** "We have no evidence for the integration of sexual activity into the human practice of seiðr" (p. 164). Practices "could be carried out ... by various sorts of practitioner" (p. 164).
- **Haddingjar and Uppsala.** *Haddingjar* comes from *haddr*, a woman's hairstyle. Óðinn is *fallhaddr*, "loose-haired" (*Berudrápa*). Loose hair marked witches in the West Gotland law (p. 165). Tacitus's Naharvali priest *muliebri ornatu*; Dumézil's transvestite priest of the divine pair; Saxo's Starkaðr disgusted at the effeminate rites at Uppsala (p. 165). Kroesen's objection: long hair can be a king's vow (p. 165 n. 19).
- **Conclusion of ch. 7.** Seiðr may be "a sort of outlier" of a European women's shamanism, like that of agrarian societies across Eurasia (p. 166). Against DuBois, he denies a Sámi origin: Sámi shamanism is male, so it cannot underlie seiðr. Saga Sámi magic is "nothing more than a use of the motif of the Sámi as outsiders" (p. 166 n. 20).
- **Sámi women singers (Eurasian section).** While the noaidi lies "spiritless", a woman, "if possible a maiden", must yoik without stopping, to remind him of his errand and bring him back to life (Jens Kildal; Isaac Olsen; p. 148). Sámi women magic-workers (*guobas*) did divination and yoik but not sacrifice (pp. 147-148).

### Ch. 17, "Performance" (Norse, pp. 470-517): the core of the skeptical case
- **Trance, but not a soul journey.** "Evidence does not ... support the notion that seiðr involved the soul visiting other worlds" (p. 471). He downplays trance as a test of shamanism, since it "is impossible to define" (p. 471). The likely Norse type is on-the-spot information from summoned spirits, marked by yawning: the seiðkona in *Hrólfs saga* ch. 3, Þrándr in *Færeyinga saga* ch. 41, Heiðr who "sat by the fire and yawned greatly" in *Hauks þáttr* (p. 472).
- **Guðríðr is no Sámi rouser.** She is "a lone helper by default", because the requested choir could not be found. The "artistic reason" is to put the spotlight on the heroine (p. 472). In so far as the scene keeps any tradition, seiðr "was at least sometimes believed" to need a group of professional helpers (p. 472).
- **Women's innate foresight.** Tacitus, *Germania* 8: Germans think "there is something holy and foresighted in them". Tolley reads this as a force *in* women, specific to foresight (p. 474). Prophetic verse in the sagas comes spontaneously from women; men produce "the more mundane *vísa*" (Quinn, p. 474 n. 8).
- ***Völuspá*** (pp. 474-486). A fictional frame, not a rite. The tree is remembered, not climbed (p. 475). "A vǫlva is likely to have carried out her magic on her own: it is not a social activity" (p. 475). The Norse practitioner "appears to be marginalised to a greater extent than in any shamanic society" (p. 476). Heiðr is *leikin*, "played upon", likely a wild, light trance or possession, not the death-like Sámi trance (pp. 477-478). Heiðr goes house to house, "gathering a cult among women" (p. 478), like a new Nanai shaman visiting every house to be accepted (p. 480). The "I"/"she" voices may be several völur speaking through one (Dronke). If genuine, this would mean more complexity in seiðr "than our later Norse sources usually recognise" (p. 482). The frame borrows from the witch of Endor (1 Samuel 28) and the sibylline oracles (pp. 483-486).
- ***Eiríks saga* ch. 4: Tolley's reading** (pp. 487-507). This is the passage our gate rests on.
  - **The text.** The S text (c. 1420) is closer to the lost early 13th-century original than Hauksbók (H, 1306-8), which was revised (p. 487). Dillmann already "rightly rejects any shamanic element": no trance is mentioned (p. 487). Tolley goes further: appeals to genuine pagan tradition "underestimate the inventive ingenuity of the thirteenth-century author" (p. 487).
  - **Verisimilitude.** The author also describes a monopod (ch. 12) "no less well crafted" (p. 487). The saga is "almost fundamentalist" Christian polemic: a whale sent by Þórr poisons the eaters, and a dead husband rises to object to pagan customs (p. 488).
  - **Guðríðr is the point.** She is a Ruth and a Mary figure, foremother of three bishops (pp. 488-489). Þorbjǫrg's finery and fussiness are "a foil" to Guðríðr's humility. "Without Guðríðr the soothsaying session would not have taken place": prophecy lies "only with those who serve the true God" (p. 489).
  - **The warding song is the author's device.** "We cannot infer from this that it was a general prerequisite for a seiðr performance to have a particular song performed" (p. 489). Nor that seiðr singing was thought beautiful: the beauty is the faith's (p. 489).
  - **Bishop and anti-bishop.** Þorbjǫrg is Gabriel and the Magi, testifying to the coming bishops (p. 490). She is also "a sort of inversion - female and pagan - of the male, Christian bishop": her circuit is an episcopal visitation, the high seat a cathedra, the staff a crozier, cloak and hood a mitre and robes, and the ring of women a "*chorus virginum*" (p. 491). She is "a sort of anti-bishop of paganism (or perhaps 'ante-bishop')" (p. 492). He places it beside the Feast of Fools and the Boy Bishop: Christmas-season misrule with staff, chant, rich gloves and white fur, and cross-dressing (pp. 492-495). Girls also elected a Girl Abbess (p. 493).
  - **A folk memorate behind it.** Pykly, a dreaded Finnish wandering sorceress recalled in 1955, was fed and given gifts so she would not work magic. Another seeress was received "as if she were a bishop in the parsonage" (1938) (pp. 495-496). The type is real; the details are not: "it is inconceivable that such precision and particularity could survive in oral tradition over a couple of centuries" (p. 497). Norway or Scotland, not Iceland, is the likely source of such figures (p. 497).
  - **Pagan elements the author does use.** The words *seiðr*, *seiðhjallr*, *vǫlva*. "It does not follow that anything in his description beyond these well-recognised terms also belongs to that tradition" (p. 498). The meal of kids' milk and hearts echoes Deuteronomy 14:21 (p. 498).
  - ***Náttúrur* are not spirits.** "Spirits" for *náttúrur* occurs "nowhere else in Old Norse" (p. 500). In S the völva thanks Guðríðr. Tolley renders: "She had brought out here many natural strengths ... who previously wanted to turn away from us and offer no obedience" (p. 501). The one who wanted to turn away is **Guðríðr**, not the spirits. "Spirits play no part in it" (p. 501).
  - **The ring of women is invented.** The author read *varðlokur* as "women-guard" plus "lock". "As this scene appears to be the result of the author's inference of the meaning of the word *varðlokur*, the account may be dismissed as affording any evidence for actual practices" (p. 502). The aim is to banish spirits, "whom he could scarcely regard as anything other than devils" (p. 502).
  - **But *varðlokkur* itself is genuine.** It is "unlikely (but cannot altogether be dismissed) that the author invented" the word (p. 503). Tolley keeps the song's place before the rite: "dismissing one element after another of the account will result in the whole matter disappearing into thin air" (p. 503). His reading: the H form *-lokkur* comes from *lokka*, "entice". The Norwegian *lokk* is a herding call "mainly sung by women" (p. 506). The *vǫrðr* is the völva's own protective power, like the Finnish *luonto*, readied before the encounter because she will be vulnerable (pp. 504, 507). "The need for an assistant to oversee the *varðlokur* certainly implies that the vǫlva would be in a state where she could not maintain this protection by herself", which suggests "some sort of trance" (p. 504). Women perhaps needed "to be receptive to the spirits than to be able to resist them" (p. 504). Gróa's "Urðar lokur" in *Grógaldr* 7 are also women's (p. 505 n. 34).
- ***Ynglinga saga* ch. 7 is Snorri's composite** (pp. 507-513). Óðinn's body "as if asleep or dead" while he travels in animal shape is "unique among Norse sources" (p. 508). Snorri built it from Norse poems, vision literature and Sámi practice, most likely via *Historia Norwegie* (pp. 510-513). Snorri euhemerises Óðinn, giving him a process "elsewhere only undergone by humans" (p. 510).
- **Minor sources.** Night-time seiðr is a Christian commonplace (p. 514). Mǫndull turns seiðr back on the seiðmenn in *Göngu-Hrólfs saga* ch. 28, "a particular vulnerability while in the process of performing seiðr", but the saga is late fantasy (p. 514). *Örvar-Odds saga*'s Heiðr, who travels with fifteen boys and fifteen girls as a choir, is "derivative" of *Eiríks saga*, c. 1270 (pp. 514-515).
- **Chapter verdict.** *Eiríks saga* yields "merely a few scraps of arguably genuine pagan lore ... frozen within it, as if in amber; the most important of these is *varðlokkur*" (p. 517). "The seiðkona would enter a trance, almost certainly of a light sort, during which she no doubt obtained information from the spirits" (p. 517). There is no evidence for sending out the free soul. Whether she called the spirits to her or took them in is uncertain, though yawning suggests the latter (p. 517).

### Ch. 16, "Vocation and initiation" (Norse, pp. 419-462): Óðinn as "shaman"
- **The ancestors of the völur.** *Hyndluljóð* 33 gives Viðólfr, the ancestor of völur ("Woodwolf", perhaps "outlaw"), and Svarthǫfði, the ancestor of the *seiðberendr*. *Seiðberendr* just means "practitioners". Strömbäck's obscene reading is "wholly inappropriate" (p. 420). This bears on Price row 6. The stanza shows only "social exclusion": the völva "was afforded any recognition only grudgingly" (p. 421).
- **Freyja taught it.** Freyja first taught seiðr among the æsir (Ynglinga saga ch. 4). Heiðr is Freyja come "among womankind to teach this art" (p. 422). Gullveig and Heiðr make up "a double subversion, against both the æsir and against husbands". The seiðr is "something powerful ... only accepted grudgingly", whose practitioners "were almost social pariahs" (p. 422). That negative picture may reflect early Christian influence (p. 423).
- **No vocation, no initiation.** "We do not hear of any vocation to seiðr in Old Norse" (p. 423). Learning magic is a literary motif: Gunnhildr learns from two Sámi men, and Guðríðr learns "the ritual song from her foster-mother Halldís" (Mitchell). Non-literary sources show individual spells being learned (p. 423).
- **Óðinn's ordeals.** The hanging on the tree is "on balance ... reasonable, just" to class as parallel to a shamanic initiation, but not as an example of one (p. 433). Chapter verdict: the four ordeals (tree, eye, mead, fires) "lack many of the typical elements of shamanic initiation". Although Óðinn practised seiðr, "this ostensibly shamanic magic practice is not linked to his 'initiatory' feats", and nothing shows what initiation, if any, made a seiðr worker (p. 462).
- ***Grímnismál*** is "a series of allusions to initiation", not a rite (pp. 460-461). Tolley compares the *Bacchae*. Dionysus's followers are "very largely women", like the cult of seiðr Heiðr gathers among women. Dionysus's effeminacy and feminine hair parallel Óðinn *fallhaddr* (p. 460 n.).
- **Freyja's *farandi kona*.** As a travelling woman searching for Óðr, Freyja "would have appeared as a witch". Heizmann shows *farandi kona* meant "more or less synonymous with a whore", and also a herbal healer (pp. 451-452). Óðr may be Freyja's externalised prophetic inspiration, like a shaman's spirit mate (p. 452).

### Ch. 18, "Accoutrements" (Norse, pp. 527-550)
- **Þorbjǫrg's costume is "of very little value".** Only the staff is probable, "and possibly that they were believed to have some sort of distinctive outfit (though even this might be questioned)" (pp. 527-528). The items line up with a bishop's dress: mantle with bands for the dalmatic, white gloves for white gloves, the hood for the mitre, beads for the pectoral cross, the staff for the crozier (p. 529). The catskin (Freyja's cat) and the belt (Brísingamen) "may" be pagan motifs, but caution is needed (pp. 529-530). A real shaman's costume is functional; this one is "arbitrary" (p. 530). The nearest shamanic dress is far east of the Sámi (p. 531).
- **The belt** carries strength and birth. Strabo's Cimbrian priestesses wear bronze belts, "perhaps" the ancestors of Freyja's girdle (pp. 532-533). The belt of the dead Óláfr Geirstaðaálfr is laid on the labouring mother of Óláfr Haraldsson (p. 532).
- **The *vétt*.** Loki: Óðinn "beat on a vétt like völur" (*Lokasenna* 24). It is perhaps a lid used as a drum, as Sámi diviners used a bucket lid. "We do have some evidence for a sort of drumming in connection with völur and seiðr", perhaps to summon and send spirits (pp. 534-536, 549). The Cimbrian priestesses drummed on their wagon skins in battle (p. 534).
- **The staff.** The etymology *vǫlva* < *vǫlr* "staff" is "questionable". *Vǫlr* is prosaic, and "a vǫlva is never said to possess a vǫlr". Tolley proposes IE *\*wel-* "see", as in Veleda, for an original sense "seeress" (pp. 536-537). Only two texts give völur staffs: *Eiríks saga*, and *Laxdæla* ch. 76, the *seiðstafr* in the grave, which he reads as a parody of exhuming a bishop (pp. 537-538). Þórdís lends her staff to a man (*Vatnsdæla* ch. 44): a wand that "differs from" a shaman's (p. 538). Norwegian law bans staffs at home with roots and the *vétt* (p. 539). On the graves: Price's readings are "at best, speculative", and henbane "could equally well indicate a folk healer". But "since the staffs occur in opulent female graves, they formed part of an array of objects symbolising female authority" (p. 539).
- **The staff and *ergi*.** Skírnir's *gambanteinn* and Þórr's staff from Gríðr give "power over ergi": to impose it (on Gerðr) or to fend it off (Þórr against the giantesses). Both users are male and not *argr* (pp. 540-544, 549). The Bergen rune-stick sends "the she-wolf's *ergi*" (p. 542).
- **The *seiðhjallr*** has "only the vaguest parallels" in shamanism. It sets the völva apart and implies sight over the worlds, not travel through them. "Over all, it is a distinct mark of the non-shamanic nature of seiðr" (pp. 545, 586). Raised seeresses: Veleda in her tower; Auðr (Otta) prophesying from the altar at Clonmacnoise, c. 838-845, "the earliest written evidence of Norse fortune-telling"; the Cimbrian priestesses on a ladder or platform; a sorcerer of pagan Sussex in 666 on a hill (p. 545). The platform belongs with Hliðskjálf, the high seat and sitting on a mound (pp. 545-548).

### Ch. 21, "Conclusion" (pp. 581-589)
- **The verdict.** "Little grounds for proposing the presence of shamanism", if classic Siberian shamanism is meant. But there is "some ritual and belief of a broadly (but not classically) shamanic nature" (p. 581). Most of what the literary sources yield is literary: "The distance between our sources and lived experience is greater than is often allowed for" (p. 581).
- **Against Price.** Tolley is "essentially at odds" with Price (2002), who uses seiðr as "catch-all". He sees "scant evidence for a particularly military aspect to seiðr" (pp. 581-582). Archaeology and late texts are "one flimsy card against the other" (p. 582).
- **Seiðr's differences from shamanism.** No soul retrieval, no healing, no sending out of the soul. Divination is central and ancient. It is women's practice, unlike Sámi shamanism. It always carried "the onus of social rejection" (pp. 583-584).
- **Social position may be late.** The völva is "in but not of" her society, like the later witch. But "the same is clearly not true of the classical seeresses", so the outsider status "may well be the result of later literary tradition" (p. 584).
- ***Ergi* summary** (p. 584). For men, "a loss of the strength which defined their virility", possibly penetration by spirits, "and may also have involved a feminisation, as the practitioners of seiðr identified with the goddess Freyja ... (such identification is nowhere directly indicated, though it is implied in the case of ... Heiðr)". For women, promiscuity. Seiðr "is likely to have been concerned with birth, and rebirth".
- **The *vǫrðr*** song "relies, admittedly, on just one word, of the greatest interpretative difficulty, surviving in an otherwise unreliable source" (p. 585).
- **No initiation, and that is not a mark against it.** "Seiðr is not said to involve any initiation". Women's shamanism (the Sora) also lacked one, and seiðr "may well not have involved such essentially male warrior imagery" (p. 586).
- **The closest model is Japan, not the Sámi.** The *itako*: wandering women mediums, welcomed into houses, answering questions about crops from a set-apart space (p. 587). Saga sorcerers who behave like Sámi shamans are explicitly called *Finnar* (p. 587).
- **The final word on *Eiríks saga*.** It is "almost wholly unreliable, and ... reflect[s] primarily Christian concerns" (p. 589). The practice it stands for, "contact with the spirits", "could be described as broadly shamanic", like much of Europe's (p. 588).

### Finnic and Sámi material (skimmed)
- **Sámi shamans were male.** Women took subsidiary roles, singing, yoiking and divining, and were barred from the drum and from sacrifice (pp. 77, 147-148). Later Sámi drum-women were thought of as "of the shadow side" (p. 147). In the *Historia Norwegie* kamlanie, the hostess who falls into trance too early may be the woman assistant whose job is to lead the noaidi back (p. 261). Women's singing as support for the one in trance is real Sámi practice. But Tolley warns against making Guðríðr a Sámi "rouser" (p. 472).
- **Finnish *luonto*.** The innate force a *tietäjä* raises before an encounter. Women's was weaker, so they were rarely *tietäjät* (p. 474). Tolley uses *luonto* to explain the *vǫrðr* and the *varðlokkur* (p. 504), and the Norse reversal: women are the receptive ones and therefore the practitioners (p. 474).
- **Sámi as "Other".** The Norse ascribed their own uncomfortable practices to the *Finnar*, so the ascription "may thus tell us rather that the practices were regarded as alien than that they actually were" (p. 65). Rǫgnvaldr réttilbeini, son of the Sámi Snæfríðr, burned by Eiríkr with his eighty seiðmenn, is part of this "attempt to deny or expunge" the dynasty's Sámi link (p. 61).

## 1a. Answers to the brief's questions

**What is attested, and what is literary construction?** Tolley's own sorting:
- **Attested (as far as anything is):**
  - Divination was mainly women's work across the Germanic world (Tacitus, Strabo, Veleda, Old English verse).
  - *Seiðr* was chiefly divinatory and done by women, and was shameful (*ergi*) for men (*Lokasenna* 24; Snorri).
  - The words *seiðr*, *seiðhjallr*, *vǫlva* and *varðlokkur*.
  - A raised platform, attested in many accounts and paralleled by Veleda's tower, Auðr at Clonmacnoise and the Cimbrian priestesses.
  - A light trance or possession: Heiðr *leikin*; the seeresses yawning.
  - A staff, likely though poorly evidenced.
  - A drum-lid (*vétt*).
  - Heiðr going house to house among women.
  - A wandering seeress welcomed out of fear. The type is real (Finnish Pykly); the details are not.
- **Construction:**
  - Þorbjǫrg's costume (a bishop parody) and the meal (Deuteronomy).
  - The ring of women (built from the word *varðlokur*).
  - The plot need for Guðríðr's song, and its beauty.
  - Spirits drawn by the song (*náttúrur* means natural powers).
  - The whole social choreography.
  - *Örvar-Odds saga*'s copy of the scene.
  - Snorri's soul-journeying Óðinn (from Sámi sources via *Historia Norwegie*).
  - Night-time performance.
  - Any sexual act inside human seiðr.

**Does Tolley undercut the three-part rite?** He undercuts its details, not its skeleton.
- **(1) The völva comes, with high seat and feast.** This is the weakest part: the high seat is a cathedra and the feast is biblical. But a wandering seeress welcomed in fear is a real folk type (pp. 495-497), and "Custom says" in `tm_seidr.0010` already hedges.
- **(2) The warding songs.** Paradoxically this is the best-supported part. *Varðlokkur* is the one "scrap ... frozen ... as if in amber" (p. 517). Tolley keeps its place *before* the rite (p. 503). His reading makes it a song that readies the völva's own guard for a vulnerable state (p. 504). This is better grounds for "failure is the song's, not hers" than the saga's plot.
- **(3) The völva in trance on the platform.** Supported: "almost certainly of a light sort" (p. 517), and the platform is widely attested.
- **What must go:** the claim that the spirits come for the song's beauty, and any soul-journey language.
- **Hedging.** Put the rite's claims in the faith's or a character's voice, never in the narrator's. Event text is already in the first person. The places where the narrator asserts the rite are `tm_baeddel_desc`, `tm_seidr.0011.desc` and `trait_tm_daughter_of_seidr_desc`. Do not put scholarly hedges ("historians doubt") into game text. Attribute instead ("By Bædling custom ...", "The Bædlings hold ...").

**Gender, *ergi*, Óðinn.**
- Divinatory seiðr is women's. The male seiðmenn are mostly accusations; Rǫgnvaldr is "simply a political threat" (p. 154).
- *Ergi* is penetrability. For a man it is a change of nature; for a woman it is a matter of degree (p. 158). Its root is relationality versus individuality: "the vulnerability ... of the seiðr practitioner was the key to her strength" (pp. 159-164).
- Óðinn's seiðr is Loki's insult, a men's boundary-crossing that Tolley likens to Siberian sex-changed shamans (p. 164). This liminality reading is exactly the "third thing" the design rejects (Bhatt). His proposed *vitku* emendation (p. 160) ties *Lokasenna* 24 to Saxo's Wecha, which is excluded as precedent.
- The "Óðinn as shaman" case is minimal. His ordeals are not linked to seiðr (p. 462), and *Ynglinga saga* ch. 7 is Snorri's composite (p. 513).
