This note checks what Catalan and Aragonese Jewish evidence can give as reaction data to a turned woman, and what it cannot.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **Iberian Jews were unusually lax.** Iberian Jewry took on the sexual mores of both Christian and Muslim neighbours. It shows "un relaxament dels costums sexuals" not found in other medieval Jewish communities (p. 11). Modern scholars who idealised the Jewish family, Assis says, explained away the evidence that did not fit (p. 12).
- **The sources** are Hebrew responsa (Solomon ibn Adret of Barcelona above all; Asher ben Yeḥiel; Nissim of Girona; Isaac ben Sheshet Perfet), the poetry of Todros ha-Levi Abulafia of Toledo, and Latin registers of the Crown of Aragon (ACA, Régné's regesta) (pp. 7–8, 47).
- **Topics**, each with cases:
  - courtship and fraudulent betrothal (pp. 11–15);
  - husbands and wives (pp. 15–24): bigamy, wife-beating, divorce sought by women;
  - Muslim concubines (pp. 24–30);
  - premarital and extramarital sex, including Jewish–Christian relations (pp. 30–36);
  - prostitution (pp. 36–37);
  - adultery (pp. 38–41);
  - forbidden marriages and incest (pp. 41–42);
  - rape (pp. 42–43);
  - "Homosexuals" (pp. 43–45).
- **Conclusion.** Every sexual behaviour found among Iberian Jews is found among their neighbours. Laxity was worst among courtiers, diplomats and financiers in royal service (pp. 45–46).

## 2. What matters for the design

### 2.1 Sodomy: the Catalan-Aragonese cases (pp. 43–45)

Assis sets out the cases as follows.
- **General rule.** In the Crown of Catalonia-Aragon, men accused of homosexual relations "eren jutjats i tot sovint se'ls condonava el càstig per una suma de diners" (were tried, and their punishment was often remitted for a sum of money) (p. 44).
- **Lleida, 1263.** The king pardoned Alaçar, grandson of Vives de Lomoux. **Two Jews** had accused him. Two days later the king voided every statement against him, but David Avendayan and others kept trying to incriminate him (p. 44; ACA reg. 12, ff. 91, 93v).
- **Alfandech, 1274.** Vives son of Jucef Abenvives, the *batlle* (bailiff) of Alfandech and Marines, was accused "de corrupció i sodomia" by **a group of Jews and a Saracen** (p. 44; ACA reg. 19, f. 156). No outcome is given.
- **Alagón, 1328.** Alfonso III had three Jews accused of sodomy released (p. 44; ACA CR Alfons III, caixa 2, no. 155). No payment is mentioned.
- **1374.** A Jew was condemned to death by fire for homosexual relations (p. 44; ACA Reial Patrimoni reg. 1682, f. 83v, a reference Assis had from J. Riera i Sans). No place is given, and no record that the sentence was carried out.
- **A cantor.** A responsum from Iberia (*Teshuvot geone mizraḥ u-ma'arav* no. 71) tells of a *ḥazzan* who was a *kohen* and had illicit relations with gentile prostitutes, Jewish women and a boy. He was dismissed (p. 44).
- **Two boys.** In a responsum of Ibn Adret (V:176), about twenty Christian witnesses accused two boys aged eleven and twelve of sexual relations with each other (pp. 44–45). Do not use this case: they are children.
- **Poetry.** Love of boys appears in the Hebrew poetry of al-Andalus and in Todros of Toledo, who praises boys over women (p. 44 and n. 182, citing N. Roth 1982).

**Pattern.** The accusers named are Jews, sometimes with a Muslim. The tribunal is the king's. The usual end is a pardon, a release or a fine. Burning is recorded once, a century after the first case. No *beit din* trial for sodomy is recorded here.

### 2.2 How the king's justice worked (pp. 33–36)

- For a Jew with a Christian woman the law prescribed death. In practice nearly every case ended in a paid royal pardon, whether the man was guilty or not (pp. 33–36). One innocent man still paid 3,000 *sous* (p. 34).
- **The only known death sentence** in the Crown for a Jewish man and a Christian woman was in 1381, and it involved a nun (p. 35).
- **Assis's reading:** the kings did not enforce capital penalties "perquè el perdó era beneficiós econòmicament" (because the pardon paid) (p. 36).
- A Jew of Lleida, 1286, complained that the people of Albesa broke into his house and brought in a Christian woman to frame him. The count of Urgell then extorted 5,000 *sous* from him (p. 35). Accusation as extortion is part of the picture.

### 2.3 Jewish courts and the "worthies" (pp. 33, 38–39, 42–43)

- **Berurim.** In late 14th-century Alzira the *berurim* (elected judges) heard a married woman's complaint against a man who harassed her. They had warned him six years earlier about another man's wife (p. 38; Perfet 265). In Lleida the *berurim* "menaven una campanya de reformes morals i religioses" (were leading a campaign of moral and religious reform) and questioned a husband about his wife (p. 39; Adret I:1187).
- **Harsher than the king.** Rabbinic courts had their own procedure for rapists, harsher than the Christian courts', "ja que podia utilitzar la tortura i altres formes de càstig corporal" (p. 43). In Huesca in 1281 Peter III asked whether the Jewish penalty for Assach Alcutavi, a rapist, was harsher than the *fuero*'s, and ordered the harsher one applied (p. 43).
- **Zealots.** Castilian rabbis who could not try sexual offenders "donaven carta blanca a fanàtics defensors de la moral" (gave free rein to zealots of morals) who attacked offenders caught in the act (p. 33; *Zikron Yehuda* 63).
- **Courts as women's refuge.** Rabbinic courts generally sided with beaten wives and with women who wanted a divorce from gamblers: "hom no pot permetre que les dones israelites siguin captives de llurs marits" (Israelite women may not be made captives of their husbands) (p. 23; Yom Tov ben Abraham of Seville, 122). Women who had been forced into marriage were heard with special sympathy (p. 23).

### 2.4 Cross-dressing and effeminacy: nothing

The article has **no case of cross-dressing, no Purim custom, and no word on effeminate men**. Its "Homosexuals" section is about acts, not about gender. For the sect this is a negative result. Catalonia, where *Even Boḥan* was written, gives no Jewish evidence for or against men in women's dress. Kalonymus remains the only source for the Purim custom (see `jud_kalonymus_even_bohan.md`). Rosen likewise found no evidence of medieval Jewish transvestism (`jud_rosen_circumcised_cinderella.md`, R2003 p. 151).

### 2.5 Other material of use

- **Wedding songs.** Judeo-Catalan wedding songs, probably 14th century, from northern Catalonia or Roussillon, give frank advice to bride and groom. One, the *Piyyut na'e*, is a near-obscene satire on an old husband and a very young bride (p. 20; Lazar 1970, Riera i Sans 1974). Baricci 2014 (p. 226 n. 16) lists the same three Hebrew-Romance epithalamia among Judeo-Provençal texts, so the genre crosses the Provence–Catalonia line. This is flavour for a wedding of a Daughter of the Turning.
- **Courtiers at risk.** Menahem ben Zeraḥ wanted to write a compendium of the commandments for Jewish courtiers, because life at court "comportava tota mena de riscos" (p. 46). This fits the sect's translators and physicians.
- **Penitence after political falls.** The Toledo ordinance of 1281 against sexual immorality followed the fall of the Jewish courtiers in 1279–80. Preachers read that fall as God's anger at their morals (pp. 27–28). This is a model for a moral panic that targets the sect after a patron's fall.
- **Isaac Arama** (a generation before 1492) wanted offenders tolerated with the community's connivance to be "escapçats, cremats i lapidats" (p. 37). That is post-1453 and far from practice. Do not use it.

## 3. Corrections to current docs

1. **`judaism_reading_list.md` finding 3** reads "men accused of sodomy were tried. They were often pardoned for a payment (Lleida 1263; Alfandech 1274; Alagón 1328)". Assis's general statement supports "tried and often let off for money" (p. 44). But of the three cases, only 1263 is a pardon. 1274 is an accusation with no outcome, and 1328 is a release with no payment named. Suggested wording: "were tried by the king's officers and often let off for money; at Lleida (1263) the king pardoned a man two Jews had accused, at Alagón (1328) three accused men were released, and in 1374 one was condemned to be burned (Assis 2001, p. 44)."
2. **Item 8's *unverified* mark on the English citation can go.** The *Tamid* colophon (p. 47) confirms Rapoport-Albert and Zipperstein (eds.), London: Peter Halban, 1988, pp. 25–59.
3. **No change to the built loc is required by Assis.** Nothing in it contradicts `tm_hipukh.0003.desc`. "The law sets lashes for a man who adorns himself as a woman" is rabbinic law (in `jud_rabbinic_primary_texts.md`). Assis shows that Iberian Jewish courts did impose corporal punishment, in a rape case (p. 43). That supports the threat as plausible in Catalonia, not as attested for dress.

## 4. Design implications

- **Who accuses.** In every named Catalan case, the accusers of a Jew for sodomy are other Jews (p. 44), often rivals. The 1263 case shows the accuser persisting after the king had voided the charge. For `tm_hipukh.0003` (the worthies) and any follow-up, the threat to a Daughter of the Turning should come from a **rival inside the community** who carries the matter to the lord's court, not from the rabbis alone.
  - Suggested mechanic: if a Daughter has a rival of her own faith, `0003` gains a branch in which the rival "takes it to the bailiff".
  - Outcome: gold paid to the liege, or her patron's protection (`tm_translation_patron_modifier`, already used for *niddui*) quashes it.
  - This matches the 1263 pattern and the existing rule that the lord licenses the ban (Shatzmiller pp. 87, 89).
- **Penalties: fines, not fire.** For Catalonia and Aragon the model is a royal fine or pardon (pp. 34, 36, 44). Keep burning off-screen and do not attach it to a Daughter. It is recorded once, in 1374, for homosexual acts, not for dress or gender, and a turned woman is not that case. Never show a *beit din* executing anyone (existing hard limit). The harsh courts in Assis use flogging, which the loc already names.
- **The worthies' reform campaign.** The Lleida *berurim* "leading a campaign of moral and religious reform" (p. 39) give `tm_hipukh.0003` a named office for its "worthies". Optional loc tweak for a Catalan or Aragonese location: "the *berurim*, who are cleaning up the street this year". Arles and Provence keep "worthies".
- **A woman's court.** "Israelite women may not be made captives of their husbands" (p. 23) is a real Iberian rabbinic principle. It could be a line for a Daughter married against her will, or one seeking a divorce: she goes to the *beit din*, not away from it. That avoids casting Jewish law only as an enemy. Use it in the sect's or the judge's voice, with the attribution (Yom Tov of Seville) in the README, not in loc.
- **Moral panic after a fall.** On the death or disgrace of the sect's patron (the lord holding `tm_translation_patron_modifier`), fire a one-off "the preachers say God is angry" event that raises the chance of `0003` for a year. Model: Toledo 1281 (pp. 27–28). Set it in Castile or Aragon only if the player is there; otherwise keep it generic.
- **Catalan wedding songs.** A marriage involving a Daughter could fire a short flavour event: the guests sing the bawdy Catalan songs, and the old women advise the bride how to keep her husband's eye (p. 20). Keep it light and non-explicit.
- **Do not use:**
  - the two boys (children);
  - Isaac Arama's call for stoning (post-1453, no practice);
  - Todros's poems on boys;
  - the Muslim slave-concubine material (pp. 24–30). Jewish ownership of slaves breaks the hard rule against Jewish slave-trade content.
