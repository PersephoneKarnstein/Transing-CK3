**Scope and bias.** An archaeologist's argument about northern Sharq al-Andalus, the coast around Tortosa and the Ebro mouth, from c. 800 to the 10th century, with some later documents (1097, 1304, 1327, 1610–11). It argues that ribāṭ was first a **practice** carried out by volunteers in a network of drystone hillforts and coastal enclosures. Only later was it a **place**, the Ribāṭ Kashkī. Much rests on Negre's own excavations at Tossal de la Vila and on forthcoming work. The reading of the hillforts as places of ribāṭ is his hypothesis, which he states with confidence. Treat it as one scholar's case. Ifrīqiya (Monastir, Sousse) appears only through citations to Hassen, El Bahi, Fenwick and Van Staëvel, and through the Aghlabid conquest of Sicily.

**Relation to other notes.** Pairs with `isl_mortel_ribats_mecca.md`: Negre gives the armed frontier phase, Mortel the hospice phase. Compare `byz_sophoulis_banditry.md` for bands turned landholders and rulers co-opting irregulars. The raids on Arles (842, 850) touch Ha-Nehpakhot's seat (README, Ha-Nehpakhot section).

**Relevance:** **high** for how volunteer and seafaring groups formed, served a frontier, broke with the ruler, went abroad, came back and took land, and were absorbed when the state centralised. **Moderate** for a frontier post growing into a lordship with villages, fisheries and salt. **Nil** for women, eunuchs and gender crossing.

## 1. What ribāṭ meant here

- **Practice before place.** Ribāṭ and jihad were linked "models of praxis" uniting religion and its armed defence. Ribāṭ was "usually passive" defence and watch on the frontier from the 8th century (pp. 1–2). "It was only at a later stage" that the word meant a place where ribāṭ was performed (p. 2, after Morriss).
- **Ascetic defenders.** The Andalusi jurist Ibn Abī Zamanīn (d. 1008) describes the *murābiṭūn* as ascetic defenders of the conquered lands (p. 2).
- **No single building type.** Cities, forts and watchtowers counted as places of ribāṭ when the practice went on there (p. 3). Pavón concluded there was no common model in al-Andalus (p. 4). The criteria proposed are defences, a place of worship, regular living quarters round an open space, written mention of ribāṭ, toponymy, and a location on contested routes (p. 4).
- **Many functions.** Watch over roads and sea, staging posts on trade routes controlling goods and taxes, cores of new peasant settlement (some became cities), and in the east places for **exchanging prisoners**, "beyond the reach of state intervention" (p. 3).
- **Atlantic Maghreb (after Cressier).** Ribāṭs there (9th–10th c.) answered Norman raids, heterodox groups such as the Barghawāṭa, and preachers. Their spiritual and commercial side (worship, periodic fairs) is clear; their military role is "practically imperceptible" (pp. 3–4).
- **Guardamar** (Alicante): built in the late 9th century by seafarers from Pechina as an occasional trading post. Under the Caliphate it became a centre of prayer and retreat with cells and oratories round a mosque, and lodging for pilgrims (p. 4, after Azuar).

## 2. Who did ribāṭ: four kinds of volunteer

- **Murābiṭūn, mujāhidūn, muṭṭawwiʿa, baḥriyyūn** were "just some of the faces of the same multifaceted reality" (p. 10). Negre also allows "less devout and more materialistic motives" (p. 10). The Ribāṭ Kashkī was a chance "to make money from military campaigns" (p. 17).
- **Baḥriyyūn** ("people of the sea"). Sailors of the old Tarraconensis coast. Al-Ḥimyarī says they were of "the lowest strata of society", lived mostly around Tortosa, carried people and goods (luxuries included), and plundered infidel coasts and ships (p. 7). They belonged to an old guild of the coast that had never stopped working, and they took in newcomers (p. 7). They carried "new forms of spirituality, such as ribāṭ itself" between the Maghreb and al-Andalus (p. 7). Some sources call their crews *mujāhidūn* (p. 7). Once the emirate had its own navy they gave up war for trade (p. 9).
- **Muṭṭawwiʿa.** Irregulars "not paid a public salary", sent to the frontier for raids (p. 9). At Tortosa, during the last Carolingian attack (808–809), they served under the governors of Zaragoza and Tortosa before Córdoba's army came (pp. 6, 9). Ulama and believers "willing to sacrifice themselves" were among them (p. 9).
- **Named scholars.** At least seven *fuqahāʾ* were based at Tortosa in the 9th century. Three died fighting: Ṭāhir b. Ḥazm and Abū Zakariyyāʾ Yaḥyā, kinsmen killed on a raid, and Abū al-Muṭarrif ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Muʿāwiya with two of his sons at Barcelona (p. 9).
- **Not hermits.** Volunteers worked with local authorities and used stable infrastructure (p. 9). The bulk of armies was still border troops and townsmen (p. 9).

## 3. How rulers related to them

- **Partnership.** Big expeditions needed volunteers who sailed or paid, *baḥriyyūn* to crew the ships, and the emir's or governor's approval (p. 8).
- **The 903 Mallorca model.** Ibn Khaldūn: Emir ʿAbd Allāh, in deep crisis, and a wealthy devout private man, ʿIṣām al-Khawlānī, both commissioned ships and recruited volunteers. ʿIṣām alone led. On success the emir **made him governor of the island** (p. 8).
- **Breaking the rules.** Before 875 sailors of Tortosa, Elvira and Tudmīr joined forces, broke the emirs' maritime rules and attacked the Andalusi city of Marchena. Unsafe at home, they sailed to North Africa and helped turn the fortress of **Tenes** into a small medina, "the main centre of gathering and exchange" for local Berbers (pp. 8–9).
- **Coming home as lords.** Around 884 a large group of *baḥriyyūn* from Tenes, many from Tortosa, came back during the revolts. They expelled the Arab governors of **Pechina** and took the territory "after gaining the emir's approval" (p. 9).
- **The governor's letter (c. 850–851).** The governor of Tortosa told Emir ʿAbd al-Raḥmān II that with his district's taxes he could rebuild his fortresses (*ḥuṣūn*) and **ransom captives**. After the campaigns he had sent the Córdoban cavalry to the coastal enclosures (*marābiṭ*) to keep watch (pp. 15, 21). Negre reads this as provincial autonomy and close ties with the volunteers (p. 21).
- **Heresy dealt with locally.** In 851–52 the same governor, after consulting the ulama, crucified a teacher (*muʿallim*) who claimed to be a prophet (p. 16).
- **Statisation.** After c. 920 (al-Nāṣir) and the Caliphate, Tortosa was ruled by *quwwād* sent from Córdoba. In 942 Mundhir b. Saʿīd al-Ballūṭī became supreme frontier authority, supervising judges and governors and inspecting all arrivals from Christian lands (p. 21). A new governor built the arsenal, shipyard and walls (p. 21). "Volunteers practically disappeared" from the campaigns (p. 21). The caliph "did not want impregnable barracks in remote, inaccessible places", so the hilltop posts were abandoned (pp. 21–22).

## 4. The places: hillforts, enclosures, and the Ribāṭ Kashkī

- **Tossal de la Vila** (954 m, Castellón): built late 8th–early 9th c., used to the early 10th, abandoned "in a planned and calculated way" c. 925 (pp. 12–13). A 2–3 m wall with towers. Simple rooms against the wall facing an open space. One better-built house whose occupants ate better, which Negre reads as an officer, so "the community mimicked the behaviour of regular troops" (p. 13). A 60 m² mosque, one of the earliest rural mosques in Iberia, with **two miḥrābs**, "perhaps" to divide the room into two prayer spaces with a movable screen. **Negre does not say the division was by sex** (p. 13). No farming: firewood, a few sheep, goats and chickens. About 50 occupants at a time, staying for intermittent periods of unknown length (p. 13).
- **Ḥuṣūn and marābiṭ.** Negre distinguishes high fortresses with living quarters (semi-permanent garrisons) from walled hilltop enclosures without internal buildings (*marābiṭ*), staging posts for mobile coastal patrols (pp. 15–16).
- **Ribāṭ Kashkī** (Ebro mouth, near La Ràpita). Mentioned by ʿAbd al-Malik b. Ḥabīb (d. 852), so founded before c. 850 (p. 16). Al-Zuhrī tells of a well that never dropped though thousands drank, a marvel story (*ʿajāʾib*) giving the place blessing (p. 16). Al-Idrīsī (12th c.) calls it the *Rābiṭa Kashṭālī*, a strong walled enclosure by a flourishing settlement and a main anchorage (pp. 16–17). Negre credits its founding to the *baḥriyyūn*, a hypothesis (pp. 16–17). Functions: river and sea watch, coordinating volunteer patrols, harbour for expeditions, and trade "including trading booty and prisoners captured during raids" (p. 17).
- **From ribāṭ to rābiṭa to lordship.** Peasant settlements grew round it from the 9th century, with strong growth under the Caliphate and Taifas (p. 18). A document of **1097**, an inventory of its goods drawn up during a failed feudal attempt on Tortosa, gives it **29 villages** (*villulae*), many with family names (Benifallim, Ventalles), a stopping place (Codair/Alcozer), salt rights (Marmortua) and fishing rights. Ramon Berenguer III gave the fishing rights to the monastery of Sant Cugat in his failed donation of 1097. The Hospitallers of Amposta later disputed them (p. 18). "Gradually, the ribāṭ became a rābiṭa" and kept its villages and jurisdiction under the Caliphate. It became an administrative and judicial centre round a religious institution and a place of pilgrimage and retreat (pp. 20–22).
- **After the conquest.** At Guardamar a document of **1304** ordered that "pilgrimage to the place to where Muslims were accustomed should stop immediately". The order was **repealed in 1327** (p. 17). In 1610–11 the Ebro mouth was the port from which no fewer than 40,000 Moriscos were deported (p. 17).

## 5. Women, eunuchs, gender crossing: findings and absences

- **Women: none.** No woman appears anywhere: not among volunteers, sailors, founders, settlers or pilgrims. The 1097 villages have family names but no people.
- **The two miḥrābs** at Tossal de la Vila are the only hint of divided worship, and Negre gives no reason for the division (p. 13). Do not cite it as a women's prayer space.
- **Eunuchs and gender crossing: none.**
- **Design consequence.** Negre's material serves the **bands** side of the mod (formation, service, breach, exile, return, settlement, absorption), not the Houses. Women in these bands are the mod's invention.

## 8. Risks and cautions

- **Jihad framing.** Ribāṭ here is armed devotion against Christians. Never give a non-Muslim faith "ribāṭ" or "jihad" as its own practice. Hired watch, service, exile and return are the borrowable parts.
- **Al-Ḥimyarī's "lowest strata"** (p. 7) is a class label from a hostile source. Don't turn sailors into a criminal people. The Marchena attack is one group's act.
- **Prisoners and booty** (p. 17): ransom and freedom only. No event sells anyone.
- **Ha-Nehpakhot finance guardrail** for proposal 2: the sponsor role must never be Jewish.
- **Negre's hypothesis.** The hillforts as volunteer ribāṭ posts and the baḥriyyūn as founders of Kashkī are his arguments, partly from forthcoming work. Say "it is thought" in any text that uses them.
- **Garbled pages** 6, 11 and 19: one 9th-century passage on p. 19 was not readable. Nothing here depends on it.
