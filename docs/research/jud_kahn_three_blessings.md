## 1. The argument in brief

- **Kahn.** The three "Menahot blessings" (who did not make me a gentile, a slave, a woman) began as a Jewish version of a Greek saying (K pp. 9–11; Tosefta Berakhot 6:18, K p. 10). Their history is a history of boundaries: gentile softened under censorship (ch. 5), woman answered by women's blessings (ch. 6), and in Kabbalah the boundary moved inside the soul, which "is in nightly danger of being transmigrated" into the other (K p. 6; ch. 7).
- **Baricci 2014.** Medieval Jewish women's literature survives mostly as voice: songs and prayers in the mother tongue. The Judeo-Provençal women's siddur preserves one such prayer: a woman thanks God for making her a woman, probably a formula women made before any scribe wrote it down (B2014 pp. 233–234, 236).
- **Baricci 2023.** Roth 32 is the only known Judeo-Provençal siddur, a wedding gift copied near Avignon at the end of the 15th century. Its translation is a school calque of the Hebrew, with rubrics closer to spoken Provençal (B2023 pp. 64–65, 75, 78).

## 2. The blessing: exact wording, manuscript, date

### 2.1 The manuscript

| | |
|---|---|
| Shelfmark | Leeds, Brotherton Library (University of Leeds), MS Roth 32 (B2014 p. 233 n. 34; B2023 title) |
| Contents | A full siddur, daily and festival, 164 folios, translated into Judeo-Provençal in Hebrew script, vocalised; some passages carry the Hebrew beside the translation (B2023 pp. 66–67) |

| Date | End of the 15th century (B2023 p. 64, after Lazar 1970 and Frojmovič 1997). B2014 says 15th century (p. 233; its n. 16 has the misprint "XIV-XIV"). Kahn says only "fourteenth or fifteenth centuries" for the three women's books together (K p. 70) |
| Owner | A bride. The title page, in gold square letters, adapts Gen 24:60: "my sister, may you become thousands of myriads" (B2014 p. 233; B2023 p. 65). The Bible has "our sister"; Baricci reads the change as a brother's gift (B2023 p. 65) |

### 2.2 The wording: three transcriptions

The manuscript is in Hebrew letters, so every Latin-letter form is a modern transcription. The three studies disagree on spelling.

| Source | Third blessing | Page |
|---|---|---|
| B2014 | "Bendig tu, sant benez[et] nostre Dieu rei del segle qui fis mi fenna" | p. 236 |
| B2014 title | "Bendig tu…qui fis me fenna" | p. 221 |
| B2023 | "Benezeg tu, sant benez[et] nostre Dieu rei del segle qui fis me fenna" | p. 66 |
| K (from Jochnowitz 1981) | "Benedich Tu Sant Benezet nostre Diew rey dal segle ke fis mi fena" | p. 70 |

- **Sequence.** In B2014 and K the women's set runs "qui non fis mi serventa" (maidservant), "qui non fis mi goyah" (gentile woman), "qui fis mi fenna" (woman) (B2014 p. 236; K p. 70). Kahn notes that the feminine forms and the order slave, gentile, woman follow Italian prayer books of the period (K p. 71).
- **Meaning.** "Blessed are You, Holy-Blessed, our God, king of the world, who made me a woman" (Baricci's Italian, B2014 p. 236 n. 46).
- **"Sant Benezet"** is how Roth 32 renders the divine Name throughout. Psalm 135 opens "lauzas nom de Sant Benezet" for "praise the name of the LORD" (B2023 p. 71). So the women's blessing is said in full form, with the Name. That matters: some authorities held that a blessing without Talmudic warrant should be said "without the introductory formula invoking God's name" (K p. 67).
- **Recommendation.** Use "qui fis me fenna" ("who made me a woman"). It is the reading of Baricci's 2023 article, the most recent study by the scholar preparing the edition, and of her 2014 title. Do not use the opening word: Bendig, Benezeg and Benedich are three readings of one word. The vowel of *me/mi* is also uncertain between her two articles. Mark the form *unverified against the manuscript*.

### 2.3 Is it unique? The two Hebrew books copied by Farissol

- Two Hebrew prayer books for women read "who made me a woman and not a man" (שעשיתני אשה ולא איש): New York, JTSA 8255, f. 5v (Ferrara, 1478), and Jerusalem, NLI 8° 5492, ff. 6v–7r (Mantua, 1480) (B2014 p. 237 nn. 48–49; K p. 71, table 6.1).
- Both were copied by Abraham Farissol, a cantor born at Avignon in 1452 (K p. 53; B2014 p. 237). One was commissioned by a man for his wife, the other by the woman herself (K p. 71).
- In JTSA 8255 the woman's blessing comes first in the set (K p. 71).
- Kahn: it was "beyond the pale of what any rabbinic figure of the day could have accepted" (K p. 71). It is the mirror of the Palestinian form "who made me a man and not a woman" (K p. 36; p. 156).
- **Relationship.** Roth 32 is not a translation of either Farissol book. Kahn thinks it was translated word for word from a lost Hebrew original, so other books carried the reading (K pp. 71, 73; B2014 p. 237 n. 50). Baricci, with three witnesses all tied to Provence, is "tempted" to place the blessing's origin there (B2014 p. 237; B2023 p. 66).
- **Not unique.** Jochnowitz (1981) thought the Roth 32 blessing was written "once and only once" for a sister. Baricci answers him with the Farissol books (B2014 p. 237 n. 47).

### 2.4 Before the manuscripts: what women said, and when

| Date | Practice | Source |
|---|---|---|
| c. 840 (Sura) | Women say the morning blessings but skip "who did not make me a woman" | Sar Shalom Gaon (K pp. 66–67; B2014 p. 235) |
| c. 1322 (Catalonia) | Earliest allusion to a women's alternative: Kalonymus, wishing to be a woman, says the men's blessing in a faint voice | *Even Boḥan* (K p. 67 and p. 164 n. 33; B2014 p. 235 n. 43, p. 238) |
| by c. 1340 (Toledo) | "Women customarily bless 'who made me according to His will'"; Jacob ben Asher glosses it as accepting "the justice of the harsh [decree]" | *Arba'ah Turim*, Oraḥ Ḥayyim 46 (K p. 67) |
| 14th c. (Seville) | Abudarham repeats the gloss | (K p. 67) |
| c. 1450–80 (Bavaria) | Some women say "who did not make me a beast" instead; Isserlein's mother did | *Leket Yosher* (K p. 69) |
| 1478–80 (Ferrara, Mantua) | "who made me a woman and not a man" | Farissol (K p. 71) |
| late 15th c. (near Avignon) | "qui fis me fenna" | Roth 32 (B2023 pp. 64, 66) |
| 16th c. | "According to His will" becomes universal through the *Shulḥan Arukh* | (K p. 67) |

- **The "harsh decree".** The phrase is the one used for *tziduk ha-din*, the blessing said on hearing of a death. Later commentators wrote "like one who accepts", Kahn says, because they could not bring themselves to equate womanhood with death (K p. 167 n. 59).
- **Women as authors.** Both Kahn and Baricci argue that the sources call "according to His will" a custom of women, not an instruction to women. So women may have made it themselves (K p. 70; B2014 p. 236). Baricci adds that women probably said such blessings aloud while following an ordinary siddur, long before any scribe wrote one down (B2014 p. 236). Kahn: "Can we not also imagine that women may have had their own oral traditions" (K p. 73).

### 2.5 Synagogue or home?

No source says where Roth 32's owner said her blessing. What the sources do say:
- The morning blessings began as blessings said at home on waking (K pp. 80, 83–84). By the 10th–12th centuries many communities, in Spain and Egypt among them, said them aloud in the synagogue "before the Pesukei D'Zimra" (K p. 82). Maimonides and his son objected (K pp. 80–83).
- Women did not come to the daily service. Cordovero calls the synagogue the place "where women customarily do not come on a daily basis" (K p. 88; 16th century).
- Women's prayer books were made for private use. A 15th-century Judeo-Spanish one "was intended for the private use of a woman at home" (K p. 55). Kahn guesses that the variant blessings went into books "designed for women's private use", which men or rabbis would not read (K p. 73).
- Roth 32 itself was also used in synagogue (B2023 p. 90 n. 67).
- **Conclusion.** The women's blessing was most likely said at home on waking, or quietly from her own book in the women's place. It was never the congregation's blessing. Baricci's note that the blessing belongs "durante la 'amidah" (B2023 p. 66 n. 5) is very likely a slip: the morning blessings come before the psalms of the morning service, not inside the Amidah (K p. 82).

## 3. What else matters for the design

- **Purim was for women's ears.** Crescas de Caylar's Provençal *Roman d'Esther* (Avignon, c. 1327) was written, by his own account, "in the vernacular of child and women", to be recited on Purim day before the community, beside a Hebrew poem for the learned (B2014 p. 228). The rule that the Megillah may be explained to "women and the unlearned" goes back to a ruling quoted in *Shibbolei ha-Leqet* (B2014 p. 228 and n. 22). In the Provençal version Vashti's long refusal to be shown naked to the court is told with relish. Baricci thinks wives in the audience enjoyed it (B2014 pp. 230–231).
- **Women's books were wedding gifts.** Roth 32 is one. A 1279 Judeo-French glossary ends with a verse for the copyist's bride (B2014 p. 232). Owning a siddur "in Latin" (in the vernacular) was a women's luxury and later almost a "status symbol" (B2014 pp. 232–233; B2023 p. 90).
- **Rubrics in the vernacular.** The copyist's instructions are in plain Provençal, e.g. "e clin om la cara e dis om taḥana" ("one bows the face and says the *taḥanun*") (B2023 p. 67 n. 11), and "cantas" ("sing") (n. 11). They are good flavour for loc.
- **Kabbalah in period (reaction data).**
  - Menahem Recanati (Italy, d. c. 1290) hinted that the blessing "who did not make me a beast" is about souls passing into beasts after forbidden sexual relations (K pp. 67–68).
  - Joshua ibn Shuab (Spain, c. 1310) ranked souls: Israel above the nations, and male souls above "even" Jewish women's (K p. 85).
  - The Zohar has the soul rise each night and be judged (K p. 86).
  - From this the kabbalists made the daily blessing a daily renewal: being made as one is becomes "a daily affirmative act of renewal" (K p. 69).
- **Kabbalah after 1453 (not in period).** Ibn Gabbai (1480–c. 1540) and Cordovero (1522–1570) say a man's soul can be transmigrated "as a woman a number of times", so that his "deeds change—sometimes in the qualities of women" (K p. 90). This is the sharpest hostile reading of a turned woman anywhere in these sources, but it is 16th-century Safed. Use it only as background.
- **Censorship (ch. 5).** Christian censorship of Hebrew books began after the Barcelona disputation of 1263 (K pp. 46–47). Jews removed "not a gentile" themselves long before the censors asked (K p. 49). This is relevant only if the mod ever has a book of the sect seized. The existing rule that gentiles are not shown as tormentors argues against doing so.
- **Leads, not read.**
  - Kahn cites Israel Isserlein's "comparison to the androgynous and the hermaphrodite" in connection with the women's blessing (K p. 164 n. 35, "Pitchai teshuvah, 22"). Isserlein (1390–1460) is in period. The passage itself is *unverified*.
  - A 15th-century Catalan siddur (Riera i Sans, *El Siddur en català*) orders the blessings slave, woman, idolater (K p. 157 n. 47).

## 4. Corrections to current docs

1. **Kahn's note on the manuscript is wrong.** K p. 166 n. 50 identifies the Judeo-Provençal siddur as "Manuscript JTSA 8255". That is Farissol's Hebrew book of 1478 (his own table 6.1, p. 71). The Judeo-Provençal book is Leeds, Roth 32 (B2014 p. 233 n. 34; B2023). Do not repeat Kahn's shelfmark.
2. **Every witness postdates the game.** Roth 32 is late 15th century; the Farissol books are 1478 and 1480. CK3 ends in 1453. README §Ha-Nehpakhot ("from a 15th-century Judeo-Provençal prayer book") and `faith_design.md` revision 7 ("Rosen citing Tabory") make the narrator date the sect's blessing by a book the game never reaches. Suggested README wording: "The women teach her their blessing, 'who made me a woman'. Women's prayer books from Provence and Italy wrote it down only at the end of the fifteenth century; the Nehpakhot say their mothers said it long before." Update revision 7's source to Baricci 2014, 2023 and Kahn 2011.
3. **Page numbers in `judaism_reading_list.md` finding 3** are one page early. The Roth 32 text is on B2014 p. 236, but the Farissol manuscripts (nn. 47–50) are on p. 237. Kalonymus is at p. 235 n. 43 and pp. 237–238. The title-page dedication is on p. 233. The finding quotes "qui fis mi fenna" correctly for B2014, but B2023 reads "me".
4. **Rosen note §6.** The date split it proposes ("before c. 1400 use *she-asani kirtsono*") misplaces the evidence. Before c. 1320 the attested women's practice is to skip the line (Sar Shalom). "According to His will" is attested c. 1322–40. "Who made me a woman" is attested only after 1478. The Tabory report is now confirmed and needs no *unverified* mark: Roth 32 and the Italian Hebrew books exist as Rosen described.
5. **Kalonymus in the blessing history.** Both Kahn and Baricci treat *Even Boḥan* as the earliest allusion to a women's blessing (K p. 164 n. 33; B2014 p. 238). This supports `tm_hipukh.0001.desc_touchstone` ("He said the men's blessing afterwards in a low voice").

## 6. Design implications

- **Rewrite `tm_hipukh.0002.desc` with the Provençal words, behind a trigger.** The event fires for any successful Turning, anywhere. Use a `first_valid` desc:
  - For a character in Provence or the Occitan lands (or of a Provençal or Occitan culture; exact region and culture keys to be checked in the files), use `tm_hipukh.0002.desc_provence`: "The feast runs into a second day, as the scroll allows. In the morning the women teach me their blessing in the tongue of Provence, and I say it with them, aloud and with the Name: not "who did not make me a woman", but "qui fis me fenna", who made me a woman. Now they say a turned woman needs a name of her own, and they are waiting to hear what they should call me."
  - Elsewhere, keep the current line, adding "aloud and with the Name".
  - Leave out the maidservant and gentile-woman lines. The third line is the whole point, and a "not a gentile" line in loc would read as chauvinism the narrator cannot gloss.
- **A brother's gift (new event, family acceptance).** Roth 32's title page has a brother give his sister a prayer book blessed with Rebecca's blessing, "my sister, may you become thousands of myriads" (Gen 24:60, adapted; B2023 p. 65). Suggested `tm_hipukh.0021`: a brother or close kinsman of a Daughter of the Turning who is not hostile to her gives her a vernacular siddur. Its title page reads "my sister", in English in the loc (the manuscript's line is Hebrew).
  - Effects: +opinion both ways, a small piety gain, and an artifact "A Prayer Book in the Tongue of Provence" (court-artifact flavour: small, gilt title page, red penwork).
  - This is the faith's warmest available hook: acceptance by a man of her own family, in the period's own gesture. Narrator loc may state as fact that such books were given as wedding gifts. The brother's gift is fiction.
- **A Rabbinic opponent's line (reaction data, in period).** Jacob ben Asher's gloss gives a Rabbinic elder a real argument: "Our women say 'according to His will', as one accepts a harsh decree; you make a thanksgiving of it." Suggested use: a Rabbinic-faith reaction option in `tm_hipukh.0003` or a Rabbinic opinion modifier description. The sect's answer: "She thanks Him for what He made." Do not quote the *tziduk ha-din* comparison to death in loc. Kahn himself notes the commentators recoiled from it (K p. 167 n. 59).
- **Kabbalist opponent (after c. 1300, Iberia and Italy).** Ibn Shuab ranks male souls above women's, and the Zohar's soul is judged nightly (K pp. 85–86). A Rabbinic character with a mystic or kabbalist trait (if the mod has a hook for it) can read the Daughter as "a soul set in the woman's place for its sins". The sect turns the same doctrine round, in its own voice: each morning He makes her again, and each morning she thanks Him who made her a woman (after K p. 69). This could be a line in `tenet_tm_three_lamps_desc` or in a `tm_hipukh.0020` elder speech. **Do not** use Cordovero's "as a woman a number of times" (16th century) in loc.
- **Purim: the women's Esther.** On the Turning feast, add flavour that the women hear the scroll explained in the vernacular, as Crescas wrote his for "child and women" (B2014 p. 228). For an option in `tm_hipukh.0001`, use Vashti's refusal to be shown before the court (B2014 pp. 230–231): the women laugh at the drunken king. It gives the sect a Purim heroine besides Esther, and one who says no.
- **Rubric flavour.** A short Provençal rubric can head the event, e.g. "e dis om" ("and one says"), from the siddur's own instructions (B2023 p. 67 n. 11; p. 79 n. 34).
- **No change to mechanics.** The blessing is flavour in the sect's voice. It needs no new trait, doctrine or holy site. Roth 32 points to Avignon rather than Arles, but Arles stays the seat. Avignon could be named in a later artifact description ("copied near Avignon").

## 7. Open questions

1. Should the Provençal "qui fis me fenna" appear in loc at all, given three conflicting transcriptions? This note recommends yes, behind a Provence trigger, marked as Baricci's 2023 reading.
2. Should the sect's blessing be date-gated (e.g. "according to His will" before 1300), or is it the sect's own from 867? This note recommends the sect's own, since the narrator makes no date claim in the event.
3. The Isserlein "androgynous and hermaphrodite" reference (K p. 164 n. 35) is worth a look if the Hebrew is available.
