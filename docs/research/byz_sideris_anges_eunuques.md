# Sidéris, "Sur l'origine des anges eunuques à Byzance" (2013): design reading

**Citation (checked against the PDF).** Georges Sidéris, "Sur l'origine des anges eunuques à Byzance", in Constantin Zuckerman (ed.), *Constructing the Seventh Century* (Travaux et Mémoires 17), Paris: Association des Amis du Centre d'Histoire et Civilisation de Byzance, 2013, pp. 539–558. ISBN 978-2-916716-45-9; ISSN 0577-1471. The brief's short title leaves out "à Byzance". The PDF holds the volume's front matter and the whole article. **What was read:** the whole article (sections I–VII and the conclusion). Quotes are my English translations, with the French for key terms in brackets. Page numbers are the printed ones.

**Relevance:** high for the angel line in the faith description and for Phrygian flavour; none for offices or law.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **The angel-eunuch is a Byzantine feature, not a Western one.** The angel "presenting itself under the aspect of a eunuch, and in particular of an imperial eunuch, a cubicularius" is "a remarkable trait of Byzantine civilisation, notably absent in the West" (p. 539).
- **Mango's Attis thesis is rejected.** Cyril Mango (1984) traced the angel-eunuch to a fusion of the Archangel Michael with Attis, the castrated god, in 4th–5th-century Phrygia and Galatia. His evidence was the healing-spring church of St Michael at Germia, "close to Pessinus, principal cult site of Cybele" (p. 541); Chonai/Colossae in Phrygia as "the greatest centre of the cult of St Michael" (p. 541); and a statue near Byzantium that he read as a winged Attis. Sidéris: the identification "rests on hypotheses [hypothèses] and analogies" (p. 542). Jewish and apocryphal angels already wore purple, a diadem and a sceptre (Apocalypse of Abraham, p. 544), and a purely Christian healing-water cult existed at Constantinople (Zotikos, pp. 545–546). So the pagan link is not needed.
- **A Pessinus detail from the footnotes (n. 10, p. 542).** In 532 the praetorian prefect Phokas, said to be a pagan, endowed a church "dedicated to the army [στρατιά] of the angels near Pessinus". There was also a church "of the holy Myriad-Angels [Μυριαγγέλων]" outside Pessinus's walls. Mango read the angel cult as common ground for "Christians, pagans and Jews".
- **The 4th-century heavenly court had no eunuchs.** In the *Vision of Dorotheos* (late 4th c.) the angels hold the ranks of a palace guard regiment (*tiro, biarchus, praepositus, domesticus*). "Neither Gabriel nor the other angels are presented as eunuchs" (p. 547). Angels were soldiers, as in Socrates' story of the angels guarding the palace from Gainas in 400. "We are not yet at all in an analogy between angels and eunuchs" (p. 548).
- **Eunuchs were thought ugly until the 6th century.** Ammianus: eunuchs "livid and hideous with their disjointed limbs" (p. 548). Basil of Caesarea (c. 372) calls them "an infamous race ... neither feminine nor masculine [ni féminine, ni masculine], always in a woman's frenzy" and "condemned to the iron at their very birth" (p. 549). This is the third-sexing slur in its 4th-century form.
- **Beauty arrives under Justinian.** Leontios Scholastikos praises the praepositus Kallinikos's beauty (p. 550). Corippus (565–67) describes the eunuch Narses in angelic terms (tall, golden, "the morning star"), and calls the chamberlains "chaste men" [hommes chastes]. But Corippus "does not take the step of explicitly identifying angels and eunuchs, an idea doubtless still too daring to be expressed within the imperial palace" (p. 551).
- **Ascetics, men and women, were "eunuchs in spirit".** The "angelic life" was a monastic commonplace from the 4th century. Holy nuns such as Marina, Pelagia and Matrona "were taken by the world for real eunuchs" (p. 552). Around 500, the eunuch monk John (Mithradates, a former cubicularius) had a face "like the face of an angel". He earned that as a monk, not as a eunuch (p. 553).
- **The motif is born in early 7th-century saints' Lives.** It appears in the Life of Symeon Stylites the Younger (written c. 602–610): "troops of holy angels like eunuchs [semblables à des eunuques], with garments like snow" (p. 555). Leontios of Neapolis (c. 641–42) uses it twice. When the Fool takes the habit there are "eunuchs bearing torches, and others holding a sceptre" (p. 556). And in the Life of John the Almsgiver a shining eunuch with a golden sceptre says "Come, please. The King of kings asks for you" (p. 556). A Damascene fragment once credited to Theodore Lector, with a "young eunuch in shining dress" sent by the heavenly emperor, is a reworking from no earlier than c. 600 (pp. 553–554).
- **It was a church weapon as much as an imperial one.** By naming a "heavenly cubicularius" as the one who summoned him, John the Almsgiver could put himself beyond the reach of the emperor Heraclius and the patrician Niketas. Niketas, the Life of John says, was John's sworn brother [adelphopoiètos]. "A coup de force and a political act" (p. 557). Leontios "systematised" the theme to put "the Church and the monks in the prolongation of heavenly sovereignty" against temporal power (p. 557).
- **Why the 7th century.** With the empire besieged by Persians and Avars, the angel-eunuch gave it a single *taxis* "unifying palatine hierarchy and heavenly hierarchy". That is "also why the theme ... was well received by the population" (p. 558). After Leontios it becomes a "stereotype" of hagiography and a "norm of sanctity" (pp. 540, 557).

**Net finding for the design.** The angel-eunuch is not court doctrine. It is a 7th-century *hagiographic* topos: angels *appear as* eunuch chamberlains in saints' visions. The palace exploited it but never legislated it. Even the 6th-century court poet stopped short of saying it outright. The direction matters. The sources say "the angel looked like a eunuch", not "the eunuch is an angel". The second phrasing is the later (9th–12th c.) inversion that the existing notes report (NV p. 54, "that's no eunuch, that's an angel!").

## 3. In-world lines for loc

- "The bishops say the angel at Germia was always Michael. The Sisters say he had another name, at Pessinus, before he had wings." (Dorleyaian holy-site text; a claim by the faith, not the narrator)
- "The monks saw angels standing at the throne and called them eunuchs. We saw the same and called them daughters." (Sisters' sermon)
- "A race neither feminine nor masculine, condemned to the iron at birth." (hostile Orthodox reaction text, after Basil of Caesarea, *Letter* 115)
- "Come, please. The King of kings asks for you." (a death-summons line for an elderly Mother of a House; after the Life of John the Almsgiver)

## 4. Flags

- **Contradiction risk (mild):** beliefs.md G (c) cites "Court theology: angels look like eunuchs". Sidéris locates the origin in saints' Lives, which the court used later. The angels-as-eunuchs image is real for 867. Calling it "court theology" overstates it.
- **Primary-source cases usable as events:** the Rhodes vision of John the Almsgiver (a heavenly chamberlain lets a churchman refuse an emperor's summons), which suits a Mother of Mothers refusing a Byzantine summons; the Vision of Dorotheos (a palace guard flogged in heaven, then baptised "Andreas", the manly name), possibly for an ironic Dorley-path event.
