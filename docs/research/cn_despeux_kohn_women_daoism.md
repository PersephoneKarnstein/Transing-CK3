## 1. The argument, by design question

### Tang ordination, rank and status (ch. 5)
- **Equal ranks.** "Women underwent these ceremonies in much the same way as men and acquired the same ritual status and power" (p. 120).
  - The *Fengdao kejie* says *daoshi nüguan* for "all Daoists, whether male or female" (p. 121).
  - Two differences persisted: dress (the *guan* headdress, after the Queen Mother's *sheng* crown) and ritual sequence (pp. 121–122). Women began rites on the right side, men on the left (p. 108).
  - **A rite led by women needs two women officiants,** "since yang numbers are odd and yin numbers are even" (p. 122).
- **How an ordination worked** (p. 120):
  - training under a master, with "several sponsors from the community";
  - seclusion and purification;
  - a three-tiered altar;
  - receiving scriptures, precepts and methods, together with "a new religious name and a set of formal vestments";
  - the ordinand gave pledges in return.
- **The princesses' altar of 711.** Each table carried "a scarlet kerchief and a blue-green cover, substitutes for smearing the lips with blood and cutting off a lock of hair" in the old blood covenant (p. 127).
- **Women could take orders from women.** A fifth-century Celestial Master code lists **five categories of women** eligible for registers (pp. 111–112):
  1. unmarried daughters living at home;
  2. daughters who leave home rather than marry, who should study "preferably with an unmarried woman master but definitely not with a male master living alone";
  3. married women;
  4. single women, "including widows who have vowed not to remarry, virtuous or wealthy spinsters, older women without sons, or those too poor or sick to be married";
  5. daughters returned home as widows or divorcees, or because their horoscopes clashed.
  - The vow of an unmarried daughter says: "I embolden myself to advance, and now take refuge at the master's gate" (p. 112).
  - A renunciant lived with a teacher "who was either another woman or a lay, married priest". Ordination then gave her "the ability to perform rituals, set up her own convent, and attain senior rank" (p. 113).
- **Female masters and abbots.**
  - *Nüshi* ("female masters") taught the women of the early Celestial Master parishes (p. 105).
  - On Mount Mao, "among fifty-seven abbots there were eighteen women" (p. 118).
- **Tang numbers.** In the eighth century there were 1,687 abbeys, "1137 for men and 550 for women" (p. 119).
- **Freedom and its critics.**
  - Women "could also run businesses and build or remodel convents". Moralists condemned the "free manners of 'female hats'" (p. 122).
  - Convents also served "to hold women otherwise unfit for society": courtesans, divorcees, widows, impoverished aristocrats, "other female misfits" (p. 123).
  - The court used convents as expedients. Taiping was placed in one in 670. Yang Guifei was parked in one as Taizhen so that she could be separated from the emperor's son (p. 123).
- **Conflict with Jia.**
  - D&K say Yu Xuanji "made a living as a courtesan for a while" (p. 122). Jia (`cn_jia_daoist_priestesses.md`, pp. 166–168) shows the claim rests on a nineteenth-century emendation, and that Yu was a concubine, never a courtesan. **Follow Jia.**
  - D&K give Wang Fengxian only from the hagiography (ca. 835–885, a saint; p. 125). Jia shows her as the rebels' oracle at Yangzhou in 887.

### Refusing marriage (ch. 5 and conclusion)
- **Song Yuxian** (*Daoxue zhuan*): at her own wedding "she changed into her Daoist clothes—a yellow linen skirt and coarse woolen cloak—took her magpie-tail incense burner, and refused to play the wife's part". She was sent home and became a recluse (pp. 114–115).
- **Zhou Ziliang's aunt:** in 504 "government restrictions on Daoist practice forced her to take a husband, if only in name" (p. 118).
- **Bian Dongxuan** "refused to marry and was expelled by her family". She worked as a weaver to buy elixir ingredients (p. 200).
- **The stock hagiographic plot:** the father pressures the daughter into marriage, "often resorting to physical violence", and fasting is her weapon (p. 247).

### Self-gestation, and a male god in a woman's shape (ch. 2)
- The *Santian neijie jing* (c. 420) says that Lord Lao, reborn under the Shang, "transformed his body of pure emptiness into the shape of Mother Li. Then he took refuge in his own womb. There was never a real Mother Li" (pp. 51–52, from Bokenkamp's translation).
- This is the clearest canonical case in either book of **a male-gendered divinity taking a woman's shape to gestate himself.**
  - The text treats the pregnancy as the god's own act and the mother as his appearance. D&K note that "her physical pregnancy has to remain an illusion" (p. 52).
- **A title that changed gender.** *Yuanjun* "was a common appellation for female deities by the late middle ages, but in earlier texts refers to a male instructor", namely Laozi's teacher in Ge Hong (p. 57).

### Inner alchemy: men must become pregnant (chs. 8–10)

**The key passage (from Qing manuals, especially the *Nü jindan* of 1892).**
- "both sexes must fully realize the female or yin-quality body. This is the purpose of the second stage, when men achieve the state of pregnancy and women return to womanhood" (p. 234).
- The embryo's birth through the crown is "the adept's rebirth on a new level and in a new yin body". Only the third stage turns that into "a body of pure yang" (p. 223).
- The conclusion generalises the point. Women were admired "for their natural ability of pregnancy, a state men needed to attain in a spiritual way while pursuing immortality" (p. 251).

**Older anchors (inside the game window or earlier).**
- **Tang:** the *Neiguan jing* gives a month-by-month gestation (pp. 232–233):
  - month 3: "yang shen arouses the three spirit souls";
  - month 4: the seven material souls arrive;
  - month 7: the orifices open "to let in cosmic light";
  - month 10: "the qi is strong enough to complete the body".
- **Tang:** a hagiography of Pei Xuanren (*Yunji qiqian* 105) says men and women "can equally practice". Women begin at the nipples and heart, and "the elixir forms equally in either" (p. 190).
- **1169:** Xue Shi's preface says "in women it is called the refinement of the body" (p. 191).
- **1227:** the *Duren jing neiyi* speaks of "the holy womb" and "the immortal embryo" (p. 187).

**The birth (Qing manuals).**
- Before the birth the adept lies as if dead for up to six days. "She needs a helper at this time who watches over her day and night"; afterwards, "One can call out to her in a low voice" (pp. 237–238).
- The birth itself is "deliverance from the womb" (*tuotai*). There is "a deep inner rumbling, like a clap of thunder", and then "the celestial gate at the top of her head bursts free" (p. 238).
- The five colours rise through the head in a different order for women than for men (p. 238).

**Women's centre of practice.**
- In women, blood replaces *jing*, and "the holy enterprise begins with the middle cinnabar field in the breasts" (p. 189).
- The "Cavern of Qi" between the breasts is a Qing formula (p. 191).
- Women are "more adept than men at meditation" (p. 224), and their spirit exits "more easily and much sooner" (p. 240).
- **But** at the end, "since women are part of cosmic yin, unlike men who at this stage attain the diamond body of pure yang, they are still partly yin" (p. 243).

**How the embryo's sex is set (medical lore).** The *Chushi yishu*, attributed to the medieval physician Chu Cheng, explains it by which arrives first (pp. 195–196, citing Furth):
- if yin blood comes first and semen enters it, "a male child is formed";
- if yang qi comes first and blood "enters to make the foundation, … a female child is formed".

**Dating caution.** Nearly all the detailed women's texts date from 1745 into the twentieth century (p. 202). Only the Tang *Neiguan jing*, the Pei Xuanren hagiography, the 1169 preface and the 1227 diagram fall inside the game's 867–1453 window.

### The Song change (chs. 3, 6, 9)
- **Collapse after the Tang.** "With the end of the Tang dynasty, the integrated ordination structure and state-supported institutions of Daoism collapsed" (p. 129). Practitioners became fee-taking ritual masters (*fashi*).
- **Women's share of the clergy.** Song registers give women "about three to four percent of the total Daoist population" (p. 201):

  | Year | Men | Women |
  |---|---|---|
  | 1019 | 7,081 | 89 |
  | 1021 | 19,606 | 731 |
  | 1033 | 19,548 | 588 |
  | 1042 | 19,680 | 502 |
  | 1068 | 18,746 | 738 |
  | 1077 | 18,513 | 708 |

  This is "a great reduction" from the Tang third. Women rose again only with Complete Perfection (p. 201).
- **Song women in general** were "more disenfranchised than under the Tang, bound by tighter moral codes, and hampered by the newly developing custom of foot-binding" (p. 65). Yet many of the new popular cults were "either founded or led by women" (p. 130).
- **Women founders inside the window:**
  - **Zu Shu** (fl. 889–904), from Lingling, founded the Way of Pure Subtlety (Qingwei). She received talismans from the Holy Mother of Numinous Radiance, and her methods passed down to a ninth patriarch (pp. 132–133).
  - **Mother Chen**, preceptor of Xu Xun's Jingming school, founded the Huangtang guan near Nanjing by oracle. She threw a straw and said: "Wherever it lands, there I shall build a sanctuary" (pp. 131–132).
  - **Cao Wenyi** (fl. 1119–1125) was summoned by Huizong and given a title. Her *Daode jing* commentary is the only woman's among the twenty in Peng Helin's collection (pp. 133–134).
  - **Sun Bu'er** (1119–1182) of Ninghai, Shandong (pp. 140–148):
    - She joined Wang Chongyang's movement in 1167–69 and later formally separated from her husband, Ma Danyang.
    - From 1179 she practised at Lady Feng's grotto in Luoyang, "symbolically inherit[ing] … a female lineage".
    - She died there in 1182.
    - She appears among the Seven Perfected only from 1241. Kubilai gave her a title in 1269, and Wuzong another in 1310 (p. 147).

### Jin and Yuan women's houses (ch. 7)
- **Scale.** Under the Yuan there were 20,000 registered Daoists, "many of them women". Women were about a third of the Complete Perfection clergy (pp. 152, 157).
- **The Shenxiao guan at Ren (Jining), Shandong** (p. 152):
  - the nun Miaoqing rebuilt it in 1259, with leave from the local military leader;
  - it passed to "her senior disciple Shouqing, from whom it went to Liu Huixiu";
  - in 1290 Liu was made overseer of the area.
- **The Qiyun guan in Kaifeng.** Li Miaoyuan "followed her mother into the monastic life" after 1263. Both studied under a **woman**, Ji Zhizhen, and were "granted three acres of land to erect their own convent" (p. 152).
- **The Jizhen guan in Henan.** Yang Shouhe was ordained in 1253. "After her death … her daughter took over the temple" (p. 154).
- **Other houses.**
  - Zuo Shouzhen (1202–1290), widowed in a raid in 1240, ran her own hermitage at Zhangde (p. 154).
  - Zhang Shouwei rebuilt the Xiuzhen guan at Yizhou from 1241 (p. 155).
- **The pattern.** Widows of the Mongol raids, "often joined by a daughter", became abbots and created "family-based lineages" (pp. 155, 172).
- **Generation names.** Each lineage has a poem whose characters serve as generation markers in members' names (p. 157). Sun Bu'er's Qingjing lineage is the women's school.

### Ming tightening (ch. 7)
- Ordination was allowed only at designated monasteries, once every three, five or ten years (p. 156).
- "Women were not permitted to become nuns when still of child-bearing age" (p. 156).
- Private temples were curtailed (p. 156).
- Adopting a son "from a family with a different surname" was prohibited (p. 156).

### Holy sites
- **Longhu.** Zhang Daoling's wife, Lady Sun, "cultivated the Dao on Mount Longhu" and ruled beside him as a female master. She was titled Lady of the Eastern Peak (p. 107 n. 3, from Du Guangting).
- **Qingcheng.**
  - In 1984 the Jieyin dian housed women, "continuing the ancient Qingdu guan … where the Tang princess Jade Perfected had made her religious home" (pp. 169–170).
  - **Flag:** Jia places Yuzhen's houses in Chang'an, Luoyang and on Mount Wangwu, not on Qingcheng. This looks like a site tradition reported on a visit; verify it before use.
  - Qingcheng was also a twentieth-century centre for collecting women's alchemy texts (p. 207).
- **Heng and Hua.** Xiwangmu's Tang cult ran from Mount Heng to Mount Hua (p. 41). She was "protector of female outsiders—actresses, prostitutes, widows, and nuns" (p. 29).
- **Magu.** On Mount Magu (Jiangxi), Yan Zhenqing recorded a stele in 768. Magu and Lady Wei shared a hall on Mount Heng (p. 97).
- **Wudang.** It appears only as the source of a Qing Longmen sub-branch (p. 207).
