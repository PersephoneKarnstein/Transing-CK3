**What kind of book it is.** A 1980 economic history built on temple inscriptions, with a strong thesis: each *nāḍu* (agrarian district) had one *nagaram* (market-town assembly), the itinerant guilds grew into supra-regional bodies, and merchants with hired troops became part of the drift to "warlord" rule in the 12th–13th centuries (pp. 187, 205–206). Hall says himself the sources are fragmentary and his market hierarchy is a model, not a proof (p. 161 n. 65). Some readings are his alone (p. 156 n. 2). Treat his interpretations as his, and the inscriptions he cites as the evidence.

**Relation to other notes.** Read with `ind_orr_temple_women.md` (temple women as donors), `ind_tanjavur_inscription.md` (*SII* 2 no. 66), `docs/stage2/worn-garland.md` (shares and gifts) and `later_starts/region_south-asia-maritime-sea.md` (the 1178 start, Takkolam). `docs/silk_and_silver.md` already names Hall and the Five Hundred as the Garland's merchant hook, and asks that temple women never be made trade goods or "dancing girls". Hall uses "dancing girls" throughout and "temple prostitutes" once (p. 7). Orr's book exists to correct that picture; this note keeps Hall's words only in quotation.

**Relevance:** high for the armed caravan guard (a landless band's job), for gifts made *through* a market assembly (a landless woman's way to give), for the market town as the place where a band could settle, and for dated port and diplomatic events. Low for women: there is no woman in any guild record Hall cites (§8).

## 1. The guilds: who they were and how one joined

- **The Five Hundred.** The leading itinerant body was the *ticaiyāyirattaiññūṟṟuvar*, "the five hundred merchants of the thousand directions", with inscriptions in northern Sumatra and Pagan (p. 141). By the 12th century it had absorbed the other itinerant groups (p. 150). Its Kannada form was the Ayyāvoḷe (pp. 151–54).
- **Its territory, in its own formula:** "the eighteen *paṭṭiṇam*", "the thirty-two *vaḷarpuram*" (coastal towns) and "the sixty-four *kaṭigaittāvaḷam*" (periodic markets), from the Piranmalai inscription, *SII* 8 no. 442 (pp. 141–42). Hall thinks the numbers symbolic (p. 157 n. 25).
- **Membership was individual, not a partnership.** A *samayam* (guild) took members "on an individual basis"; there was no shared investment and no sharing of profits or losses; members could make their own partnerships inside or outside it (p. 147).
- **Entry by oath.** Candidates "swore by a code of mercantile conduct", the *samaya-dharma*, which set them apart from other itinerants (p. 147). This is the nearest thing to an initiation in the book. No rite is described.
- **Who belonged.** An early list names "cettis, cettiputras, kavares, kandalis, bhadrakas, gavunda-svamins" and others (p. 147). Members were classed as native (*svadēśi*), foreign (*paradēśi*) and itinerant (*nānādēśi*), and an executive council of all of them was the *ubhaya-nānādēśi* (p. 147).
- **Local towns joined too.** Whole *nagaram* became members or formal trade partners from the late 11th century (pp. 154, 203). Hall's main reason: the guild's hired troops (p. 203).
- **Branches.** The *maṇigrāmam* was a branch with a home market of its own (Koḍumbāḷūr, Uṟaiyūr, Valikandapuram) and an assigned zone, not a rival guild (p. 150). The *vaḷañjiyar* were members found mostly in the far south (p. 161 n. 63). A Malabar body, the *añjuvaṇṇam*, shared Kollam's customs with the *maṇigrāmam* (pp. 171–72).
- **Honours.** Rich members became "merchant princes" with the right to umbrellas, elephants and many-storeyed houses, and monopolies on some goods (p. 147). The Kottayam plates of 1320 list the honours of one such man: festive clothing, house pillars, five instruments and a conch to announce him, lamps by day, cloth spread on his path, a litter, a royal umbrella, a drum, an ornamented arch (p. 148). They also give him the town's merchants and five artisan groups as his "slaves" (p. 148). Do not use that clause.
- **Family firms.** Hall finds no evidence that partnership or individual trade was the norm. Merchant families gave to temples as families: "fathers, sons, daughters, and wives" (p. 158 n. 34). At Tiruvaḍandai twelve families "who made their livelihood through the marketing of woven goods" jointly funded a seven-day festival for Rājarāja I (p. 158 n. 34; p. 170).

## 2. The *nagaram*: the market town's assembly

- **Place and assembly.** *Nagaram* meant both the market quarter and the assembly that ran it. *Nagarattār* were its members; *nagarattōm*, "we the members of the nagaram", is the voice of its decisions (p. 52).
- **Who decided.** The quarter held weavers, oil-pressers, fishers, artisans and even shepherds, but "only the merchants constituted the nagaram" as the deciding body (pp. 52–53).
- **Officers.** A standing committee (*nagaravāriyam*), a chief merchant (*nagaram-āḷvān*), an annual supervision committee, a clerk and an accountant (pp. 55–56, 92). At Tillaistanam the clerk and accountant were fined for misusing temple funds (p. 56).
- **Kanchipuram's *mānagaram*.** Only two "great" *nagaram* are attested under the Pallavas: Kanchipuram and its port Mamallapuram (pp. 51, 188). In Uttama Cōḷa's time the Kanchipuram *nagaram* was the residents of two named quarters; other commercial quarters were not part of it (p. 53).
- **Takkolam had a *nagaram*** with two classes of member: general merchants with the *saṅkarappāḍi*, and the cloth-merchant *cāliya-nagarattōm* (p. 54). Takkolam is b_takkaloma in c_kanchipuram, a *tēvaṉār makaḷ* cluster in Orr (Orr 2000, p. 58).
- **Towns of the hymns.** The *Dēvāram* names about ten *nagaram*, among them Tiruppamburam and Tirukkacci (Kanchipuram) (p. 51). Tiruppamburam is, I take it, Orr's Tirupampuram, where a temple woman received a verse of the *Tiruvempāvai* in 1213 (Orr 2000, pp. 17–18). The identification is mine.
- **Founding a *nagaram*.** By assigning, buying or leasing land and house sites, with a tax due or goods owed (p. 57). A late Cōḷa king fixed a new *nagaram*'s tax at 23,000 *kalam* of paddy and 1,000 *kāsu* (p. 57).
- **Leaving as leverage.** Merchants and weavers who found demands too heavy left; officials had to invite them back with remissions (pp. 74–75, 96, 198; p. 212 n. 47). In Kanchipuram, a quarter that "had dwindled" and stopped supplying oil and rice to a temple was resettled tax-free "in consideration of their poverty" (Madras Museum Plates of Uttama Cōḷa; pp. 74, 91).

## 3. Merchants and temples (finance framing kept narrow)

- **The *nagaram* as trustee of a gift.** Temple funds and private gifts were deposited with a *nagaram*, which "became the collective agent of the donor" (pp. 76–77). Money or animals went in; the return came out not as coin but as paddy, seed, ghee, curds or lamp oil (p. 77). One deed of 60 *pon* required the ghee "even if they were to leave this village and settle elsewhere" (p. 77).
- **Royal gifts in market towns went to the *nagaram*, not the priests,** which agreed to administer them (p. 78).
- **Hall's money framing.** He writes of "investment" and "interest" (pp. 76–77). Spencer's study of the Thanjavur temple found that of its loans 95.4% went to village assemblies and 4.6% to merchants (p. 72). Hall also says Kanchipuram merchants "seem even to have helped finance Cōḷa military expeditions" and cites a loan to an officer of Rājendra I (p. 191; p. 207 n. 4). For the mod, use only the *perpetual endowment*: gold or animals placed with the assembly, oil or ghee to the god for ever. Do not build a lending mechanic on this, and never let it touch Ha-Nehpakhot (§9).
- **Kanchipuram's merchants ran their own temple.** Under the Madras Museum Plates (Uttama Cōḷa, r. 970–85, reign dates general knowledge) the *nagaram* supervised the Viṣṇu temple of Ulagaḷanda Perumāḷ ("Uragam"). Its chief merchant and annual committee audited the accounts yearly; the weavers of royal garments inspected them monthly (pp. 90–92). Gold was placed with four weavers' quarters, three Śaiva quarters and four outside villages to keep the festivals and lamps (p. 91). Note: Śaiva quarters kept a lamp in a Viṣṇu temple (p. 91, Hall's own "mind you").
- **Their own worship.** The king allowed Vedic Brahmans at the *mānagaram*'s temple only when a non-Brahman Vaiṣṇava ritualist was not available (p. 93). Hall reads this as the merchants defending their own worship against Śaiva royal pressure (pp. 93–94).
- **Piety as status.** Merchants built whole temples, tanks and images; 12th-c. inscriptions name many *dharma-ceṭṭi*, "righteous merchants" (pp. 82–83). The guild's eulogies call them protectors of religion (pp. 83, 154–55).
- **Guild gifts.** A temple *maṇḍapa* at Tiruviḍaimarudūr was named for the Five Hundred (p. 144). A guild and local merchants co-managed a lamp endowment at Valikandapuram, and split a temple's oil two-thirds to one-third at Jambai (p. 144). The *vaḷañjiyar aiññūṟṟuvar* gave gold in 870 for a tank called "the Five Hundred's tank" (Municandai, p. 161 n. 63).
- **Where merchants lost the temple.** At Thanjavur and Srirangam, royal temples grew rich enough to swallow the local *nagaram*; Srirangam's late Cōḷa records thank merchants but give them no say (pp. 80–81, 200–201). Kanchipuram is the opposite case: its *nagaram* was "too deeply entrenched" to be pushed aside (p. 86).

## 4. The armed merchants: an armed-band angle

- **Origins in self-protection.** Hall thinks the guilds began as "small groups of expeditionary merchants" who banded together "for their mutual protection" in poor or isolated country (p. 151).
- **"Heroes of the road."** An inland emporium under a guild's protection was an *erivīrapaṭṭiṇam*, "a place where the heroes of the road conduct trade" (p. 143). Such towns lay "far from the heartlands" of the Pallava and Cōḷa kingdoms, in frontier areas where royal protection was "more symbolic than practical" (pp. 151, 188).
- **How a town was made one.** At Kaṭṭūr (Chingleput) the Five Hundred laid down rules for the residents: they were freed of communal dues and got "twice the privileges" they had before; the guild would protect the town (p. 144). At Basinkonda the guild, "including those of the nāḍu, nagaram, and nānādēśi", met to convert a village into an *erivīrapaṭṭiṇam* (p. 147).
- **Armed merchants as a threat.** The Kaṭṭūr text promised the new town would never hold merchants who "demanded taxes or tolls by threatening people with drawn swords or by capturing them" (256 of 1912; pp. 151, 193). Hall reads this as proof that some armed merchant bands "turned to banditry" (p. 193). He also says merchants' troops threatened "townspeople as well as brigands" (p. 189).
- **The Ayyāvoḷe eulogy** (Shikarpur 118, dated 1150; p. 160 n. 56): "born to be wanderers over many countries", "a sharp sword as their wood knife" (p. 152). They punish a burst sack, a runaway ass, "a cart that has been seized", "a cry of looting"; they take "the head of an intruder as the tassel" (p. 152). And: "like the elephant, they attack and kill; like the cow, they stand and kill" (pp. 152–53, 193). "Gambling they will not allow" (p. 152).
- **Commanders and regiments.** Inscriptions name the *vaḷañjiyar-sēnāpati*, "the army commander of the vaḷañjiyar merchants", and the *nānādēśi-daṇḍanāyaka* (p. 154; from the unpublished Vahalkada inscription in Sri Lanka as translated by Indrapala, p. 161 n. 63). A regiment, the Kaikkōḷa-perumbaḍai, "the armed mercenaries of the itinerant merchants", paid for part of the Five Hundred's *maṇḍapa* at Tiruviḍaimarudūr (pp. 154, 192; 253 of 1907). Kaikkōḷa regiments appear with *nagaram* at Tillaistanam, Kīḻūr and the market of Jambai (p. 208 n. 12).
- **The *vēḷaikkārar*.** A regiment class the Cōḷas also used. Fourteen *valaṅgai vēḷaikkārar* regiments are among thirty-one royal regiments; Hall warns that not all were merchants' troops (p. 208 n. 14). One was stationed at Nagapattinam and may have hired out to caravans going inland (pp. 167–68).
- **Polonnaruwa: a sworn bond between soldiers and merchants.** After Vijayabāhu I drove out the Cōḷas, the Tooth Relic temple was entrusted to the *vēḷaikkārar* (pp. 191–92). Their inscription: "We of the Mahatantra … having called together the Valañjiyar … and the Nagarattar and others, who always accompany us" (p. 192). Nilakanta Sastri reads its term *mitadaigal* as a "spiritual or constitutional relationship" between the troops and the merchants (p. 207 n. 9); Hall calls it "almost a kinship type bond" (p. 193). This is the book's nearest case to sworn brotherhood.
- **Hero memorials.** A 10th-c. Five Hundred inscription at Valikandapuram honours Vīravaḷañjiyaṉār, "an armed 'hero of the road'", who died after killing another *vaḷañjiyan*, "a traitor to the organization" (308 of 1964–65; p. 159 n. 40, p. 160 n. 55, pp. 208–209 n. 22). At Salukki a man of the *nagaram* is honoured who fell "in a fight among merchants" (472 of 1920; pp. 160–61 n. 55). The guild acquired "five hundred *vīra-śāsanas*" (hero edicts) by its own boast (p. 153).
- **Merchants on campaign.** Merchants lived in the camps of royal troops (p. 208 n. 17); merchant regiments went on royal expeditions and shared the plunder (pp. 192–93). Hall sees the guild troops as a sign of the Cōḷa state's "basic flaw": it could not guarantee safe roads (p. 193).
- **From caravan guard to local power.** Hall's ch. 8 argument: *nagaram* "began to form their own military units from troops which were initially hired to police the local market-place and the overland trade routes" (p. 81). Under weak kings these troops could turn to banditry (p. 193). In the 13th c. merchants and their armies backed men seeking power (p. 206).

## 5. A market town that tried to break away: Valikandapuram

The book's one full case of a merchant body becoming a local power, and of its fall (pp. 193–98).
- **10th c.** A *nagaram* and a *maṇigrāmam* both call Valikandapuram home (p. 194; Āditya I 871–907 and Parāntaka I 907–55). The *nagaram* administers gifts to the temple of the Rāma–Vāli meeting, mostly from outsiders. "The daughter of a local political figure (chief ?) made a gift of a lamp using the nagaram members as her intermediaries" (p. 194; 241 of 1943–44).
- **Late 12th c.** A regional guild, the "*vāṇiya-nagarattār* of eighteen lands", claims the town as its *nagarakkāṇi* (tenure), names it a *śūradaḷam*, "a fortified commercial center" (Hall's query), and endows 145 festival lamps and a temple car (pp. 194–95). A member supervises the temple (p. 195).
- **Early 13th c.** The guild allies with a military chief, "the lord of Vaṇṇāḍu", against the Cōḷa king. Under Rājarāja III (1216–60) two Hoysaḷa generals put the "rebel chief" down and redraw three *nāḍu* into two (p. 195). Three years later worship is restored "as originally arranged by the nāṭṭār" (p. 195).

## 6. Overseas: ports, Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia

- **Kollam.** The *añjuvaṇṇam* and *maṇigrāmam* held *kārāṇmai*, a perpetual tenure, over the city's trade: customs were assessed only in their presence, land in the city was set apart for them, they could suspend customs payments if wronged, and they alone judged their own members' faults (pp. 171–72; inscription of Sthāṇu Ravi, which Hall places "in the period of Āditya I"). This is the closest the book comes to a trading post held by charter.
- **Nagapattinam**, the Cōḷa port, had no *nagaram*; merchants lived in their own quarters (p. 167). The king of Srivijaya built a Buddhist *vihāra* there; Rājarāja I endowed it in 1006 and Kulōttuṅga I renewed the grant in 1090, evicting squatters (pp. 168–69, 174). The Srivijaya king's agents gave a jewel in 1014–15 and 60¾ and 87¾ *kaḻañju* of "gold of China" in 1018–19 to a Nagapattinam temple (pp. 169–70). Rājendra I raided Srivijaya's ports in 1025 (p. 170); Hall follows Spencer that the Cōḷas saw them as "legitimate objects of plunder" (p. 170).
- **A merchants' temple abroad.** Takuapa on the Malay Peninsula had a temple of *maṇigrāmam* merchants in the late 9th c. (p. 168; p. 179 n. 19). Sumatra: a Five Hundred inscription of 1088 (p. 160 n. 47). Pagan: a 13th-c. gift by a Malabar man to a Viṣṇu temple bearing the Five Hundred's name (p. 175).
- **China.** A Cōḷa embassy reached China in 1015 (p. 169). A tablet of 1079 in a Daoist temple at Canton names the Cōḷa king as benefactor of 600,000 gold cash (p. 175).
- **Pagan.** Kyanzittha (1077–1112) claims he converted a "Chōli prince", who sent him a daughter as a gift (pp. 174–75). Hall reads the episode as trade diplomacy (p. 175).
- **Sri Lanka.** Cōḷa rule ended under Vijayabāhu I by 1069–70 (p. 174). The Polonnaruwa *vēḷaikkārar* inscription is in §4. South Indian merchant communities in Ceylon c. 950–1250 are Indrapala's subject, which Hall uses (pp. 157 n. 20, 161 n. 63).
- **Geniza traders.** Fusṭāṭ merchant families kept branches in Malabar and Coromandel ports; partnerships and gifts eased their access (pp. 173, 177 n. 4). Hall's source is Goitein. See §9 for the guardrail.
- **Kulōttuṅga I** is praised as the king who abolished tolls (p. 172) and renamed Visakhapattinam Kulōttuṅgacōḷapaṭṭiṇam c. 1076; a Malabar merchant built a Jain shrine there in 1090 (p. 171).

## 7. Status, the right to leave, and the king as referee

- Merchants were "politically and socially suspect" and confined to designated markets where they could be regulated (pp. 189–90).
- Hall suggests merchants were recruited from "the restless, the dissatisfied" who did not fit village life (p. 189). He gives no case.
- Artisans won royal grants of status signs: double conches at weddings and funerals, sandals, plastered houses; Kanchipuram's weavers had the conch and the litter (p. 110). Communities in a rank dispute often left and resettled (p. 110).
- The king could also side with the merchants against his own officer, or the merchants could leave (p. 198; p. 211 n. 46).

## 8. Women: findings and absences

- **No woman appears as a member of the Five Hundred, a *maṇigrāmam*, a *nagaram* committee, or a guild's troops.** Every named signatory and officer in the cited records is a man (*ceṭṭi*, *-vēḷār*, the chief merchant). The *samayam* membership list (p. 147) names no women. This is an absence in Hall's selection, not proof that no woman ever traded.
- **Women as donors through the market.**
  - The chief's daughter at Valikandapuram, 10th c., who gave a lamp "using the nagaram members as her intermediaries" (p. 194). This is the best single case for the Worn Garland: a woman who is not a merchant gives in her own name, and the assembly holds and runs the gift.
  - Merchant families' gifts by "daughters, and wives" (p. 158 n. 34, citing 17 of 1903 / *SII* 8 no. 287; 111 of 1926 / *SII* 13 no. 258; 149 of 1928 / *SII* 13 no. 101; and *SII* 1). Hall gives no names; the inscriptions would.
- **Queens.** A great market of Thanjavur bore the name of queen Tribhuvanamādēvī, and was the senior, "great" *nagaram* among four (pp. 53, 84). Rājendra I assigned the care of an image set up by queen Pṛthivīmahādēvi to an artisan quarter (p. 85).
- **Temple women, in Hall's words.**
  - Hall writes that Rājarāja I staffed Thanjavur by "pirating priests, musicians, dancing girls, temple prostitutes, and even local deities" from older temples (p. 7). This is the frame Orr corrects; do not use it.
  - The places that sent women to Thanjavur included Tiruviḍaimarudūr (a *nagaram*), "several temples" at Nagapattinam, the port of Kāvirippūmpaṭṭiṇam, the market quarter Taḷiccāttaṅgāḍi and two named *nagaram* (pp. 83–84). At Nagapattinam the Sēnāmukam and Naḍuvil-taḷiccēri quarters supplied them (p. 167, citing *SII* 2 no. 66). *Taḷiccēri* is the temple quarter of Orr's *taḷiccēri peṇṭukaḷ* (Orr 2000, p. 33); the link is mine.
  - Hall adds what Orr's account of the four hundred does not stress: many of them came from **market towns and ports**, not only temple towns.
  - Temple funds placed with a *nagaram* paid for "ceremonies, lamps, dancing girls, statuary" (p. 76). The temple women's support passed through the merchants' hands.
  - Pay: 30 *kalam* of paddy a year covered a temple woman's expenses at Narttamalai; at Thanjavur she had 100 *kalam* (pp. 111, 132 n. 29). 100 *kalam* is the one share of *SII* 2 no. 66, which the stage-2 share already uses.
- **A woman's share in an assembly.** At Maṉūr (9th c.) a man could represent a *sabhā* share he held as *strīdhana* "from his wife" (p. 37). The share was hers; the seat was his.
- **Diplomatic brides and rhetoric:** the "virgin daughter" given to Kyanzittha (p. 175); Kulōttuṅga's fame sung by "the young women of the Persians" (p. 173). Not usable.
- **Strīrājya.** The Ayyāvoḷe eulogy lists "Strirajya", the kingdom of women, among the countries they visit (p. 152). It is a stock land of legend in the list, not evidence.
- **Eunuchs, gender crossing, disguise:** none in the book.
- **Links to Orr.** Hall's market towns are often Orr's temple-woman centres: Kanchipuram, Takkolam, Tiruviḍaimarudūr, Tiruvoṟṟiyūr (a *nagaram* with three quarters, p. 53; sixteen *tēvaraṭiyār* sang the *Tiruvempāvai* there in the 11th c., Orr p. 108) and, if my identification holds, Tirupampuram. Orr's 21 deals and Hall's guild gifts fall in the same 1070–1300 window (Orr pp. 74–75; Hall pp. 154, 203). Orr also finds temple women's sons in the *kaikkōḷar* community in 13th-c. northern temples (Orr 2000, pp. 157, 163); Hall's Kaikkōḷa regiment is the merchants' armed guard (p. 154). A temple woman's son in the guard is a possible bridge, not attested in either book.

## 9. Cautions

- **"Dancing girls", "temple prostitutes"** (pp. 7, 76, 83, 111, 167): Hall's vocabulary. Never in loc. Say "women of the temple" or use Orr's terms.
- **Caste.** Hall discusses *saṅkara* and *anulōma*/*pratilōma* ranks of artisans and merchants (pp. 54, 110). Do not make caste an economic engine (`silk_and_silver.md`, "Care").
- **The Kottayam "slaves"** (p. 148): do not use.
- **Banditry labels.** Hall calls frontier peoples "tribal" and "turbulent" (pp. 10–11, 151). Bandits in the guild records are *merchants with swords* (p. 193), an occupation, not a people. Keep it that way; no culture is criminal.
- **Finance.** Hall's "investment", "interest", Spencer's temple loans and merchant war-finance (pp. 72, 76–77, 191, 207 n. 4). The Garland may endow; it does not lend.
- **Ha-Nehpakhot.** The Geniza merchants in Coromandel ports (pp. 173, 177 n. 4) are Jewish. If they ever appear beside Garland content, they appear as travellers, letter-writers and gift-givers (the halal/"kosher" cheese among "presents", p. 183 n. 70), never as lenders, financiers of war or as the reason a trade succeeds by money. Flag this before any cross-faith event at a Coromandel port.
- **Dates printed oddly.** p. 180 n. 34 gives both 1125 (Vikrama Cōḷa) and "roughly 1022" for related records at Nagapattinam; check *SII* 17 no. 601 before use. p. 159 n. 42 calls the Hemavati pillar "of Kulōttuṅga III (1165 A.D.)", but Kulōttuṅga III came to the throne in 1178 (general knowledge); check Nilakanta Sastri's article. Hall's Polonnaruwa inscription is dated only to Vijayabāhu I's time (r. 1055–1110, general knowledge).
- **A living community.** The Five Hundred have modern claimants among South Indian merchant castes (general knowledge). Never present them, or the *kaikkōḷar* (today's Sengunthar weavers, general knowledge), as the faith.
