# Leonora Neville, *Byzantine Gender* (Past Imperfect; Arc Humanities Press, 2019) — research dossier

**Source file:** `scratchpad/books/neville_byzantine_gender.txt` (full read, lines 0–3794; page numbers below are the book's printed pages, taken from the running headers preserved in the layout text).
**Purpose:** in-period anchoring for the "Transbian Maximalism" CK3 faith/culture (eunuch gendering, ritual castration, castrated men regarded as women, adoptive matrilines, women-/transfem-centred priesthood).

**Read-first caveats about this source.**
- It is a ~32k-word "lunchtime" synthesis, not a monograph; Neville says so herself: "I'm telling you what I think, not what I suppose a consensus opinion among scholars would be" (p. 2). Chronological focus is 9th–13th c. (p. 4).
- Eunuchs get one dedicated section (pp. 51–54) plus the physiology pages (pp. 23–27) and a passage in ch. 6 (pp. 90–91). For the deep eunuch material Neville defers to **Messis, *Les eunuques à Byzance* (2014)**, **Tougher, *The Eunuch in Byzantine History and Society* (2008)**, **Ringrose, *The Perfect Servant* (2003)**, **Kuefler, *The Manly Eunuch* (2001)** and **Mullett on Theophylact of Ochrid's *In Defence of Eunuchs***. Those are the next books to pull.
- The book does **not** discuss: the law of castration, who castrated whom (slaves/captives/children/family), eunuch monasteries, eunuchs in the women's quarters in any detail, grammatical gender of eunuchs, hermaphrodites, adoption, concubinage, same-sex law, cross-dressing law, icons/liturgy gender, or the empress Zoe. Those gaps are flagged in §3 and §4 rather than papered over.
- Neville's own thesis cuts *against* a "third gender" reading of eunuchs (p. 53) and *against* reading adelphopoiesis as marriage (p. 56 n. 22). Where the mod wants to go further than the source, the dossier says so.

## 2. Eunuch dossier (top priority)

### 2.1 How eunuchs were gendered

**Physiology (Galenic).**
- "The practice of creating eunuchs allowed humans to modify the physical development of the male body." (p. 23)
- "Within this system men were considered naturally warm and women cold. The flesh of men is naturally hard and dry, while that of women, children, and eunuchs is naturally soft and moist. The foods one consumed should match the natural tendencies of one's body." (p. 24) — eunuchs are placed in the *cold/moist* class with women and children, including dietetically.
- "Eunuchs began life as male children but were deprived of their testicles before puberty. They grew to be tall and long-limbed, with little musculature, no facial hair, and high, child-like voices. They aged quickly and died young, which modern doctors attribute to 'cardiac and skeletal problems connected with prolonged testosterone deprivation.' Eunuchs shared the moist, cool humours with women and children. Galen taught that eunuchs became like women because they lacked heat and they lost the masculinity and virile strength of men. Eunuchs were destined by nature to become men, just like pre-pubescent boys, but they remained perpetually not-men whereas the boys eventually hardened up and dried out. Eunuchs were thus fundamentally male, but lacked the virility, heat, and generative capabilities of men." (pp. 25–26; the embedded quotation is Ringrose, "The Byzantine Body", 2013)
- "Such a theory of human physiology makes it reasonable to understand masculinity as a matter of degree rather than an absolute attribute one either has or not. An adult male is a physiological ideal to which women, male and female children, and old men fell short." (p. 26)
- **The Aristotelian alternative — eunuchs as female:** "Aristotelian medical teaching on the other hand held that castration rendered eunuchs fundamentally female because they lacked masculinity. This line of thought was less prevalent by the era of late antiquity. Charis Messis, *Les eunuques à Byzance*… 54–55, 95–96; Shaun Tougher, *The Eunuch*… 34–35." (p. 31 n. 11) — **This is the single most useful line in the book for mechanic (d), "castrated men regarded as women": an attested ancient-medical doctrine, still cited in Byzantine scholarship, that castration makes a man female. Neville calls it the minority view by late antiquity; a fictional faith can simply adopt the minority doctrine.**

**Moral/ethical gendering.**
- "Through training their habits and their ethical choices men, women, and eunuchs could either increase or decrease their natural level of masculinity." (p. 27)
- "Men naturally had a leg up on women and eunuchs in their ability to exhibit sophrosene and phronesis, although women and eunuchs ought to do their best as well." (p. 34)
- The eunuch bishop case (14th c.): "Nikephoros Gregoras praised his uncle, the eunuch bishop John of Herakleia, on the grounds that since John was a eunuch, and hence womanly, he naturally had less capacity to control his impulses. John's virtuous behaviour was thus even more laudatory. This is the same reasoning that we have seen applied to virtuous women… Eunuchs, who were generally castrated before adolescence never gained full masculinity." (p. 52) — eunuch explicitly classed as "womanly" by a Byzantine author praising him.

**Third gender? "Neither"? Neville's verdict.**
- "Denigrating eunuchs for effeminacy was fairly routine, and they were probably most often considered mutilated men who lacked full masculinity. They were sometimes described as being neither male nor female. One text describes them as monstrous in that their male nature was changed to appear female, making them simultaneously non-male and non-female. Another calls them, with less condemnation, a 'strange sex.' Some scholars have seen eunuchs constituting a third gender in Byzantine society, and others have been unconvinced. Given how masculinity seems to function as an ideal that women and men were called to strive for, and femininity a failing that was a potential danger for all, it seems more likely that Medieval Roman people would have placed eunuchs on various points on the scale of virtue rather than conceiving of a separate gender for them." (pp. 52–53)
  - The "monstrous… changed to appear female" text is **Cyril of Alexandria**, "made prevalent in the Middle Ages by its inclusion in the history of George the Monk and the Suda encyclopedia" (p. 57 n. 53; Messis 90–91).
  - The "strange sex" text is a **Novel of Leo VI** (Noailles & Dain, *Les Novelles de Léon VI*, 320–27; Tougher 111) (p. 57 n. 54). — **An emperor's law-text calling eunuchs a distinct "sex" is the best in-period hook for mechanic (a), a distinct sex class.**
  - Third-gender scholarship: Ringrose, *The Perfect Servant* 67–86 and "Living in the Shadows" in *Third Sex, Third Gender* (1993); criticism in Tougher 109–11 and Messis 361–68 (p. 58 n. 55).
- Neville's own framing nonetheless keeps a three-term grammar throughout: "good or bad men, eunuchs, or women" (p. 93); "needed for men, women, and eunuchs to maintain their appropriate roles and decorum" (p. 94); "what Medieval Roman people believed to be natural about… men, women, and eunuchs" (p. 4).

**Inconsistency of the discourse (important for a mod that wants a *faith* to pick one line).**
- "The rarity of eunuchs may account for the divergent assessments of their morality and the apparent absence of a consistent normative set of gender expectations for eunuchs. Attitudes toward eunuchs described in Medieval Roman texts vary widely from disgust to admiration and depend far more on the rhetorical purposes of the authors than on variations of character among actual eunuchs. The eunuchs in histories look nothing like the eunuchs in hagiographies." (p. 52)
- "…when they are all brought together the images of eunuch are so contradictory and incompatible that no general social understanding of eunuchs has emerged so far." (p. 52)

### 2.2 Sexuality attributed to eunuchs
- "Some descriptions of eunuchs present them as enslaved to carnal passions, but more wicked than women in that they were indiscriminate in desiring sex with both men and women. Others present them as free from sexual desire and so having a unique freedom from one of the central moral problems of their culture. Their supposed lack of sexual desire rendered them pure and innocent. In theological literature eunuchs are discussed mostly as the evil foil for the heroic 'spiritual eunuchs' who were regular men with such strength of will that they completely conquered their sexual desires. In this discourse the eunuchs who were actually castrated get no credit for their ability to withstand sex since they supposedly came by it effortlessly. They are rather denigrated for being unnatural." (p. 53)

### 2.3 Numbers and rarity
- "While eunuchs loom large in research on Byzantine gender, nearly all Medieval Romans would go their whole lives without meeting or seeing one. Even in the eras in which eunuchs were most prevalent at the imperial court, they were likely not more than several dozen in imperial service and perhaps a hundred in Constantinople." (pp. 51–52) — **Design note:** a eunuch-centred faith is historically a *court/metropolitan* phenomenon; a mass-castration culture is not Byzantine. Keep the castrated class small and elite (court, clergy, monasteries) for the "veneer".

### 2.4 Court offices and the heavenly-court ideology
- "Eunuchs were employed at the imperial court because they were symbols of power and royal authority. It made the emperor appear to be more regal when his court was decorated by eunuch servants. Guides to court ceremonial from the ninth and tenth centuries indicate that certain ranks and positions within the administration were reserved for eunuchs. Roman emperors of the fourth century onward adopted the practice of employing eunuchs at court from eastern imperial traditions that were much older." (p. 53; Tougher 52) — The book does not name the offices (no *parakoimomenos*, *praipositos* etc. appear); pull Tougher/Ringrose for titles.
- "From the Medieval Roman point of view, it went without saying that the prophet Daniel was a eunuch because the story of how he came to enter the service of Nebuchadnezzar matched their expectations for court eunuchs. In the ninth to tenth centuries heaven was imagined to be a court with many of the same properties as the emperor's earthly court, and the more the earthly court imitated the divine one, the more God seemed like an emperor. God was imagined as attended by angels that looked like eunuchs, and eunuchs were thought to look like the angels of God. In stories about visions and dreams there is a recurring scene in which an imperial eunuch walks into the room and participates in the drama until he does something miraculous, and somebody recognizes, 'that's no eunuch, that's an angel!' The prevalence of the connection between angels and eunuchs led to some positive descriptions and associations with eunuchs. Eunuchs were described as beautiful, shinning [sic], and pure. They could be regarded positively as ideal imperial servants and mediators." (p. 54; Ringrose, "Reconfiguring the Prophet Daniel")
  - **Source caution (2026-10-26):** Neville's note sends this to Ringrose's chapter "Reconfiguring the Prophet Daniel" (2003), but the phrases "that's no eunuch, that's an angel!" and "beautiful, shining, and pure" are not in that chapter; the angel material is in Ringrose, *The Perfect Servant*, ch. 7. Daniel as a eunuch is implicit in the 9th c. and explicit from Symeon Metaphrastes (late 10th c.): see `byz_ringrose_prophet_daniel.md`.
- Ch. 6 restatement: "Eunuchs played a significant part in this court culture because they were thought to look like angels and angels were imagined to look like eunuchs. It was important for emperors of this era to be surrounded by ranks of eunuchs because that helped maintain the earthly court's imitation of the way they imagined the heavenly court." (p. 91)
- **Decline:** "The positive descriptions of eunuchs become far more rare from the twelfth century on and eunuchs seem to become less numerous." (p. 90) "Twelfth-century Byzantine political culture does not look much of anything like that of the ninth and tenth centuries. Emperors ruled on the basis of individual relationships with particular men, rather than matching ranks of courtiers… In this new political culture of personal connection, eunuchs had no ideological purpose that anyone cared about and became simply mutilated men." (p. 91) "Eunuch monks and churchmen seem to have remained a feature of Medieval Roman society and small numbers of court eunuchs are attested through the fifteenth century." (p. 91; Tougher 119–27) — **For CK3 timing: the angelic-eunuch ideology is at its peak exactly in the 867 start era and fades by the 1066 start → a good lore reason for the faith to be a 9th–10th-c. "preservation" movement.**

### 2.5 Eunuch clergy and bishops
- Eunuch bishop **John of Herakleia** (14th c., uncle of Nikephoros Gregoras), praised *because* "a eunuch, and hence womanly" (p. 52; Gaul, "Eunuchs in the Late Byzantine Empire").
- "Eunuch monks and churchmen seem to have remained a feature of Medieval Roman society" (p. 91).
- Messis: eunuchs "considered innocuous when they were involved in working with women or the church" (p. 54) — i.e., church and women's spaces were the two sanctioned eunuch spheres. **This is the direct precedent for a eunuch/transfem priesthood that is socially acceptable precisely because it is not "male political action".**
- Late-antique background: "In Late Antiquity Christian bishops and monks tried to refashion Roman masculinity to put themselves at the pinnacle because of their celibacy." (pp. 48–49), with Kuefler, *The Manly Eunuch* cited (p. 86 n. 73).
- Theophylact of Ochrid, late 11th c., "wrote a text that defended the virtue of eunuchs" (p. 57 n. 52; Mullett). — A bishop's apologia for eunuchs: ideal "scripture" for the faith.

### 2.6 Polemic against eunuchs
- "Yet the power of eunuchs at court also led to a tradition of denigrating eunuchs for usurping the authority that rightly belonged to whole men. In this discourse eunuchs were effeminate, greedy, treacherous, corrupt, cowardly, deceitful, and servile. This line of complaint was well established in late antiquity and continued through the medieval era." (p. 54)
- "When eunuchs were praised, it was generally for exhibiting the masculine virtues of courage, strength, or constancy. Much of the criticism falls into the category of feminine vices such as of lack of reason, capriciousness, cowardice, self-indulgence, or weakness. Messis argues that eunuchs were most consistently denigrated when they infringed on the male spheres of military and political action and considered innocuous when they were involved in working with women or the church. Those who were able to engage in governmental or political work and remain praiseworthy were exceptional eunuchs who transcended their deformed nature." (p. 54; Messis 318–19, 365)
- Western/modern polemic reuses the same triad: Lecky 1875 on Byzantine history as "a monotonous story of the intrigues of priests, eunuchs, and women" (p. 10); "the notion that women and eunuchs held power in Byzantium was enough to convince many people that the empire lacked real men" (p. 19); "power-hungry women and eunuchs whose flaccid men fought by means of treachery and bribes" (p. 93).
- Ringrose's positive-eunuch thesis is accepted only with caveat: "it is only by dismissing the contemporaneous negative descriptions of eunuchs as mere repetition of classical tropes that is she able to claim that the positive vision was the norm" (p. 57 n. 52).

### 2.7 What the book does NOT provide (gaps to fill from Tougher/Messis/Ringrose)
- Legal status of castration (Roman/Byzantine prohibitions, exemptions for foreign-born, Leo VI's novel on the subject beyond the "strange sex" phrase).
- Who was castrated by whom: the book only says "deprived of their testicles before puberty" (p. 25) and "generally castrated before adolescence" (p. 52). Nothing on slaves, captives, or aristocratic families castrating sons (a known Byzantine practice — verify in Tougher).
- Named court offices reserved for eunuchs.
- Eunuch saints (none named), eunuch monasteries (none named), eunuchs in the gynaikonitis (only the Messis "working with women" line, p. 54; and Basil I "left immoderate grieving… to the women's quarters", p. 44, which does not mention eunuchs).
- Grammatical gender of eunuchs in Greek (not discussed).
- Ritual or sacral framing of castration itself (none — the Byzantine material here is court/medical/moral, not liturgical; for sacred castration the galli remain the only model).

### A. Theory of sex, body, and virtue

**1. Galenic humoural sex theory** | Galen (129–210), dominant in Byzantine medicine throughout | Men hot/dry; women, children, eunuchs cold/moist; diet gendered accordingly | "We have more Medieval Roman copies of Galen's medical treatises than any other author from antiquity… The flesh of men is naturally hard and dry, while that of women, children, and eunuchs is naturally soft and moist… Since men and women had to eat different kinds of foods to be healthy, eating was a gendered activity." (p. 24) | Gives the faith a *medical* language for reclassifying bodies: cold/moist = the female class, into which castrates fall. Supports (a), (d). Could flavour a "humoural diet" tradition or event text.

**2. One-sex embryology / women as imperfect men** | Galen, *De usu partium* | All embryos start alike; heat makes genitals descend; lack of heat leaves them internal | "All human embryos initially had the same generative organs but when an embryo had sufficient heat, the penis and testicles would descend and project outside of the body, whereas when an embryo had insufficient heat they would remain inside the body becoming ovaries and a vagina… Galen consistently discusses female characteristics as imperfect versions of male physiognomy." (pp. 24–25) | Sex as a *continuum of one substance* rather than two kinds — the in-period doctrine closest to "sex is a spectrum". A faith can invert the valuation (coolness as perfection) without inventing new biology. Supports (a), (b), (c).

**3. Masculinity as a process one grows into and out of** | *Vita Basilii* (10th c.) | Boys become men by heating/drying; old men cool | "This medical theory posits masculinity as, physiologically speaking, something that a male child grows into and that an elderly man may grow out of… Young men could be described as 'becoming perfected in being a man,' reflecting the idea that masculinity was something that a boy grew into." (p. 25) | If manhood is *achieved* at puberty, then arresting puberty (castration) is a *refusal* of manhood, not a loss of it. Supports (b), (c), (d).

**4. Aristotelian doctrine: castration makes the eunuch female** | Aristotle; discussed in Messis 54–55, 95–96 and Tougher 34–35 | Minority view by late antiquity but on record | "Aristotelian medical teaching on the other hand held that castration rendered eunuchs fundamentally female because they lacked masculinity. This line of thought was less prevalent by the era of late antiquity." (p. 31 n. 11) | **Core precedent for (d).** The faith canonises the Aristotelian line over the Galenic one.

**5. Pathos as passivity; andreia as acquirable manliness** | general Byzantine moral psychology | Women passive, men active; a disciplined woman gains andreia | "The most fundamental meaning of the Greek word for emotion, pathos, is passivity. Passivity was seen to be a basic characteristic of women, just as activity was ascribed to men… A woman who cultivated the strength of her character, so that she controlled her passions and regulated her emotional responses, possessed the quality of andreia, which means both 'manliness' and 'courage.' Through training their habits and their ethical choices men, women, and eunuchs could either increase or decrease their natural level of masculinity." (p. 27) | Gender as *performance and training* is the book's thesis; in-period justification for a gender-as-practice trait system. Supports (b), (m).

**6. Sophrosyne and phronesis** | Byzantine ethics generally | The two gendered virtues of self-control | "Two virtues of self-control were profoundly important in Medieval roman culture: σωφροσύνη, sophrosene, and φρόνησις, phronesis. Sophrosene meant discretion and moderation, and hence chastity. Phronesis meant understanding and wisdom, but chiefly the wisdom not to do something, prudence… When men failed at exhibiting sophrosene and phronesis they therefore failed not only with regard to those virtues, but as men." (p. 34) | Virtue vocabulary for tenet names and trait flavour.

**7. Character formed by imitating models (mimesis)** | liturgy/hymnography; rhetorical *progymnasmata* (e.g., Nikephoros Basilakes, 12th c.) | Selves built by inhabiting biblical/classical personae | "Hymns and liturgical prayers had congregants ventriloquize the thoughts and feelings of biblical characters such as Adam or David… A standard part of education was for young men to practise making speeches in which they took on the persona of an ancient character… 'what the Mother of God would say when Christ transformed the water into wine for the wedding.'" (p. 29) "These people learned how to act by singing Psalms." (p. 30) | Basis for a doctrine where one *becomes* a model (e.g., becomes "a Pelagia") through liturgical imitation; supports (b), (j) as "performative sacrament".

**8. Nobility confers masculine self-control** | Psellos on Empress Theodora (1055–56); Anna Komnene | Highborn women credited with men's rationality | "People who were considered wellborn or innately noble were considered naturally more masculine than the common rabble… Theodora… substantiated her ability to address the court, although female, by explaining that she had 'blossomed from royal blood.'" (p. 42) | CK3 hook: a tenet/tradition where high rank or dynastic status unlocks gender-crossing roles.

### B. Women who "become men" through virtue; transvestite and penitent saints

**9. Female ascetics passing as male monks** | perennial hagiographic genre | "Stories of female ascetics who so far conquered their nature that they passed as male monks were perennially popular and witnessed to a measure of human control even over the manifestation of physiological aspects of gender." (p. 23) | Direct precedent for ritual/ascetic gender transition in a Christian frame. Supports (b), (j). [Marinos/Marina, Euphrosyne, Matrona are **not named** in the book; Eugenia is — see #11.]

**10. St Ioustina** | late-10th-c. *Menologion* of Symeon Metaphrastes | Praised for endurance beyond "her female and weak gender" | "'suffering this way, she endured so bravely that she showed nothing ignoble nor what one would expect of her female and weak gender.' The same author wrote about a different female hero that she, 'devise[d] a course of action that was wiser than one would expect of a woman.'" (pp. 36–37) | Formula for "exceptional woman" event text.

**11. St Eugenia (transvestite saint)** | Metaphrastes | Dresses as a man to enter a monastery; praised for manliness | "a woman who left her life of wealth and leisure to join a monastery by dressing as a man was praised as having 'a manly soul and [she] show[ed] great manliness in all respects.' (p. 37) | A bishop *blessing* a cross-gender life as victory over nature: liturgical model for a transition rite. Supports (b), (j).

**12. (pp. 37–38) | Note: the book treats Pelagia as a penitent, not as the cross-dressing "Pelagios" of other versions. Precedent for a conversion/rebirth narrative and for sex-worker saints.

**13. "Manly" as a compliment to women** | Anna Komnene (12th c.), Tornikes letters | "Anna Komnene was praised for having manly strength in dealing with her father's doctors." (p. 37) "It appears to have been fairly common to compliment women by saying they were manly" (p. 62) | Flavour for opinion modifiers.

### C. Female rule and agency

**14. Pope Leo III & Charlemagne, 800** | Rome/Aachen | Imperial office deemed vacant because a woman (Eirene) ruled | "Since the early ninth century, when Pope Leo and Charlemagne deemed the office of Roman Emperor to be vacant because the Eastern Emperor was a woman, Byzantium has been a foil for Western European cultural self-understanding" (p. 5) | Historical casus for "heresy/illegitimacy framing" of female rule by outsiders: (l), (f).

**15. ### D. Marriage, sex, clergy, kinship

**26. Clerical marriage and episcopal celibacy** | canon law (Balsamon 12th c.; Blastares 14th c.) | Married priests; bishops divorce wives | "Priests, who were normally married, were supposed to abstain from intercourse for three days before celebrating Eucharist. (pp. 34–35) | Byzantine clergy *can marry*; a faith can set clergy marriage to "allowed" with a Byzantine veneer. (e), (i).

**27. Remarriage penalties** | canon law | Second marriage = penance + temporary excommunication; third marriage worse | "Second marriages required penance and excommunication for a period of time. Third marriages were considered even worse, and even after the period of excommunication had ended, such a couple was only able to receive communion three times a year" (p. 35) | Mainstream Orthodox counter-norm; a divergent faith could abolish it (i).

**28. Frame it as adoptive siblinghood.

(p. 35) | Evidence of a tolerated counter-discourse; (i), (k) flavour.
