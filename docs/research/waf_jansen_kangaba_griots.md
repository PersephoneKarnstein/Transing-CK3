## 1. The argument in brief

**Secrets (2002).**
- **The sanctuary.** Every seven years the Kamabolon of Kangaba is restored and re-roofed in a five-day ceremony. It is "un sanctuaire de la forme d'une case traditionnelle en banco" (a sanctuary shaped like a traditional mud-brick hut) (p. 9).
- **The night recitation.** On the night before the last day, the Diabaté griots of Kela, a village 5 km south, recite inside it. Only the *kumatigi*, the "master of the word", is authorised to speak there (pp. 9–10).
- **What the secret is.** Jansen lived ten months with that *kumatigi*, Lansiné Diabaté. He argues that the "secrets" are not hidden content. The core text is the Sunjata epic, the *Mansa Jigin*, which has three published Kela versions (1924, 1979, 1992) with "un noyau remarquablement stable" (a remarkably stable core) (p. 12). The secret is the Diabaté's exclusive right to say these words in this place.
- **The Islamic stories.** The other recitations are Islamic and Arab stories: Adam and Eve, Abraham, Bilal as Sunjata's ancestor, Ali as the first Mande marabout. The *kumatigi* calls them optional embellishments (pp. 32, 37). They also include some modern ones picked up from print and radio, such as a Mali king's voyage to America (pp. 38–41).
- **Chronology.** The ceremony in its present form dates from 1880–1912. The sanctuary is older (p. 57).
- **Women.** The position of women in Kela tradition "a souffert d'une régression grave au XXème siècle" (suffered a serious decline in the 20th century), which Jansen puts down to Islam (p. 35).

**"Beyond the Mali Empire" (2018).**
- Jansen proposes a "new paradigm": the epic is not a record of 13th-c. persons. It is a "hybrid" that fuses selected parts of up to three millennia of savannah political economy (pp. 1–2, 22).
- **The smith.** Sunjata's enemy "always is a blacksmith" (p. 11). Smiths, with women potters, are "earth experts" who control fertility. The epic stages warriors taking power from them (pp. 7–12).
- **Masks and animals.** The characters are masks, cults and animals. Sogolon is a porcupine or aardvark woman whose traits match the Komo mask. Fakoli's praise describes a Komo mask. "Sunjata" is "Sun" (Sogolon) plus "Jata", lion. Tiramagan may be a lance of ordeal (pp. 12–15).
- **Origin.** Jansen suggests that the epic as now recited is "an altered version of a long-gone mask performance" (p. 15). Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's masked bards inspired this suggestion (p. 16 n. 60).
- **Rules for rulers.** Marriage and genealogy are lessons in smart rule:
  - avoid owing the bride-givers;
  - avoid a Muslim mother for a warrior heir;
  - restrain sexual desire;
  - give command of the army to the "youngest brother" (pp. 16–22).

**"The Younger Brother and the Stranger" (1996).**
- Mande status claims are made in pairs of brothers:
  - the older brother is immobile and keeps the paternal compound, and he commands;
  - the younger is mobile, makes war outside, and must give to his seniors.
- Every powerful dynasty claims to descend from the *youngest* brother, because that position equals the war leader (*keletigi*) when the whole region is attacked from outside (pp. 15–25).
- **Further pairs.** The same logic runs through village founder against stranger-chief, village against bush, mother against sister/wife (pp. 25–28).
- **Kangaba.** The French misread Kangaba's "younger brother" claim as usurpation and exiled its Keita rulers (pp. 8, 15, 32).

**Siramori Diabate tribute (1996).**
- A portrait of a Kela griotte (c. 1925–1989), "the paragon of old-style griottes (*jelimusow*)" (p. 180).
- In Kela "women and young men sing, while only old men speak" (p. 184). Men and women draw on the same knowledge but perform it differently.
- She lived *neolocally* in Kangaba rather than in her husband's village (p. 186). She "bridged" village tradition and the new Bamako audience.
- Her song "Sara" tells of a bride who escapes a betrothal by a feigned illness and marries her lover "honourably" (pp. 187–194).

## 2. What matters for the design

### 2.1 The Kamabolon ceremony: date, calendar, who speaks, who sings (2002, chs. IV–V)

**Date and calendar.**
- The 1997 ceremony ran from 28 April to 2 May (p. 46). It had been delayed a week because Tabaski (ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā) "devait être célébrée avant" (had to be celebrated first) (p. 46). In 1989 Ramadan delayed it (p. 47).
- The ritual calendar (p. 47):
  - the assembly of a new age-set at the full moon of mid-March, "quand les pluies ont définitivement cessé" (when the rains have stopped for good);
  - the ceremony ideally ends at the next full moon.
- Rehearsals are held on Mondays and Fridays, "un calendrier 'traditionnel' mandingue, dans lequel lundi et vendredi sont des jours sacrés" (a "traditional" Mande calendar in which Monday and Friday are sacred days) (p. 48).

**The age-set.**
- The ceremony is "une installation d'un groupe d'âge" (the installation of an age-set), the seven-yearly reorganisation of the army (pp. 44, 54).
- The new age-set's name, *Mogoyasi* in 1997, was announced first, before the date (p. 46).
- On the last day the youths ran out of the enclosure shouting it (p. 52).
- Before the ceremony each age-set cleans and restores the village. That work was once paid for by communal fields (pp. 45–46).

**Who does the work.**
- Young women plaster the walls and sweep. Men always redo the roofs: "la restauration des toitures est cependant toujours effectuée par les hommes" (p. 46).
- On the last day young men carry the new roof onto the walls. It is believed "ce sont les paroles du kumatigi qui font bouger le toit" (the *kumatigi*'s words move the roof) (p. 52).
- Then everyone rushes in to touch it for blessings (p. 52).

**Who speaks and who sings.**
- About fifty Diabaté men walk in age order round the sanctuary singing the "Dibi" incantation. The *kumatigi* recites praises as they walk (p. 50).
- Inside, from about 22.00 to dawn, he recites Adam and Eve and then a hurried epic (pp. 51–52).
- The Diabaté wives are present (p. 51). Kunba Diabaté, "la 'doyenne' des femmes de Kéla, assise à l'entrée du Kamabolon, cria plusieurs fois une bénédiction aux Diabaté" (the women's elder, seated at the entrance, several times cried a blessing to the Diabaté) (p. 51). This is the only woman's voice Jansen records at the ceremony.
- Offerings, mostly 500 F CFA, are taken at the door. Someone calls out the amount, the donor's name and "pour que vous lui fassiez des bénédictions" (so that you bless him) (p. 51).

**"Hot" and "cool".**
- While the roof is off "il fait chaud" (it is hot), and red clothes are forbidden (p. 53).
- Heat is the Mande idiom for social transformation. Smiths, as "maîtres du feu", are social mediators. A Sunjata praise line says "Mande bugu sumayalen", "le Mande s'est refroidi" (the Mande has cooled) (p. 53).
- Words are dangerous in the hot time: "toute parole incorrecte pourrait rétablir et reconstituer la société incorrectement" (any wrong word could re-make society wrongly). So only the *kumatigi* may speak (p. 54).
- Jansen concludes that the ceremony is "une véritable 're-création' de la société" (a true "re-creation" of society) (p. 54). The death of the oldest royal Keita is handled with the same "hot" ritual practice (p. 54 n. 105).

**Secrecy and violence.**
- Recording is forbidden, "sous peine d'être tué sur place" (on pain of being killed on the spot) (p. 13). Visitors' bags were searched, and even butterflies and chickens were chased away as possible shape-shifted humans (p. 48).
- On the last morning a young man who entered the enclosure was beaten. People said he died; that was never settled (p. 49).
- The roof was burned two weeks after the 1997 ceremony (p. 49).
- **Do not use any of this** (§4.7).

**Transmission of the word.**
- The *jelikuntigi* (chief griot) is simply the oldest Diabaté man.
- The *kumatigi* needs a long apprenticeship and is chosen by the elders, not by contest (pp. 15–17). Lansiné said "une génie (*jinnu*) [...] lui était apparu" (a jinn had appeared to him) (p. 17).
- **The troika.** The *kumatigi*'s words are checked by two older men. If he errs, "les vrais savants (les vieux) ne se sont pas trompés" (the true scholars, the elders, have not erred) (p. 17).
- **What the secret is.** Following Bellman, a secret is "information dont l'abus est frappé de sanctions" (information whose misuse is punished) (p. 18). It is the right to say what everyone knows (p. 19).
- **Who inherits it.** Secrets (*gundow*) pass by inheritance. When an old man or woman dies, "son fils ou sa fille" (his or her son or daughter) may inherit them (p. 19). They pass "de père en fils (ou de mère en fille)" (from father to son, or mother to daughter) (p. 20).

### 2.2 Dating the Kamabolon (2002, ch. V; 1996)

- The present ceremony dates to **1880–1912** (p. 57). The Kela procession to the Sacred Stones needs a Diabaté population at Kela, which grew only after 1883 (pp. 56–57).
- **What is older.** "le sanctuaire lui-même" (the sanctuary itself) is older, as is the epic (p. 57). Two pieces of evidence:
  - a Wa manuscript collected by Wilks describes an idol at "Qaba" served each year by the town and its villages (via Green 1991: 131; p. 57 n. 113). Its date is not given; *unverified*;
  - De Ganay names the founder as "Mansa Sema", nicknamed "le roi-éléphant" (p. 57 n. 113).
- **Rejected chronologies.** Jansen rejects Person's chronology that makes the Kamabolon a 17th-c. building (p. 57 n. 113). In "Younger Brother" he rejects the idea that the Keita took Kangaba in the 17th or 18th c. (pp. 8, 31–32).
- **Niane's claim.** Niane (1975) claims Mansa Sulaymān built it after a pilgrimage in the 14th c. (p. 36). Jansen reports this without endorsing it. Gomez rejects the pilgrimage (`waf_gomez_african_dominion.md`).
- **The local name.** Kangaba's local name is **Kaaba**, "d'où la comparaison facilement établie avec la Ka'aba" (hence the easy comparison with the Kaʿba). The Kamabolon is said to hold earth from the Kaʿba, or a Qurʾān left by al-Ḥājj ʿUmar c. 1840 (p. 36).

**Grade.** The ceremony in its present form is LATER (1880–1912). The sanctuary and a yearly town cult at "Qaba" are of unknown age. Nothing places the Kamabolon or a re-roofing in 867–1453.

### 2.3 Women in the Kela tradition (2002, ch. III; Siramori)

- **Two versions of Adam and Eve.** De Ganay recorded Adam and Eve in 1954. In that version Awa leaves Paradise by her own choice, "le Créateur lui donna des sandales" (the Creator gave her sandals), and the couple seek each other for a thousand years (p. 34).
  - In Lansiné's 1999 version she is punished with menstruation and expelled (p. 35).
  - Jansen reads the change as Islam's loss of women's "autonomie" (p. 35).
  - **Caution:** De Ganay belongs to the Griaule circle, and Jansen thinks her texts were shaped by assistants (p. 31).
- **The sister.** In Niane's version Sunjata's (half-)sister finds the means to defeat Sumaoro. In the Kela version "cette femme est capturée par Sumaoro et libérée par un griot masculin" (this woman is captured by Sumaoro and freed by a male griot), and marabouts prepare the weapon (p. 35).
  - This is the same regression. It confirms `waf_conrad_austen_sunjata.md`: the sister-guide belongs to Upper Guinea and Niane versions, not to Kela.
- **Sogolon's bracelets.** In Text II, Sogolon Kèjugu sets out "pour chercher du bois" (to fetch firewood). She takes off two bracelets and gives them to the Somono ferryman, Sansan Sakolon, as "son prix de traverser le fleuve" (the price of her crippled son's river crossing) (p. 127).
  - Since that day the bracelets are given "comme le prix de traversée de Magan Sunjara" (as the price of Magan Sunjara's crossing) (p. 127).
  - A mother pays her own jewellery so that her child can cross. The text also has the ferryman ask Sunjata's father for a wife (pp. 125–127).
- **Fisana's wife.** The Prophet blesses Fisana, ancestor of the *funé* bards. The text's commentary says his old wife's fertility returned (p. 123 n. 164). This is a minor motif.
- **Siramori.**
  - She "had to experience a long learning process", starting as dancer and chorus singer in her "older brother's" band (p. 183).
  - She was a poor storyteller in speech but overwhelming in song. Her hoarse voice was rumoured to come from a spell (pp. 183–184).
  - A listener said: "Now give me a knife! When I hear this, I want to see blood!" (p. 184).
  - She called herself "the pillar of society": "La femme est le pilier de la société" (p. 185).
  - Women play only rhythm instruments, such as the calabash and the iron *karinyan*. Her daughter Bintan nonetheless took the guitar and showed the guitarist the melody (p. 184).
  - In "Sara" "the griottes played their karinyan" for the bride (p. 192). Sara's name puns on "death", "divorce", "agreement" and "delightful" (p. 188). "Sara's first child was named Sara" (pp. 193–194).

### 2.4 Status discourse: mother, sister, younger brother, stranger (1996)

- **Badenya and fadenya.**
  - *Badenya* is "harmony" and "siblinghood (children of one mother)". *Fadenya* is "conflict" and "half-siblinghood" (p. 10).
  - Fadenya at one level becomes badenya at the next, against a common outsider (p. 11).
  - In Kela badenya was glossed "même mère, même père" (same mother, same father). Saskia Brand notes that it also holds for a mother's children by different men (p. 32 n. 5).
- **The mother at the centre.** Kangaba's foundation story has the youngest son bring his father a slave iron, not grain, and settle "auprès de ma mère" (beside my mother).
  - Jansen: the message to the other villages is "that their mother is in Kangaba" (p. 24).
  - Kangaba thus claims both the war leadership and the mother.
- **The sister's insult.** Sunjata's brother Mande Bori "insulted his sister and for that reason he will never be 'war leader' (*keletigi*)". Both lineages accept the story but read it differently (p. 18).
  - This independently confirms Conrad's sister's curse (`waf_conrad_austen_sunjata.md` §2).
- **The sister as stranger.**
  - As a man's mother a woman stays in the compound and stands for badenya. As a sister she is married out, crosses the bush and "becomes a stranger in the village of her husband". So "one cannot write about 'women in Mande'" without asking which relation is meant (p. 27).
  - The opposition table (p. 28) sets *mother* with older, village, order and badenya, and *sister/wife* with younger, bush, mobility, sorcery and fadenya.
- **The stranger is honoured, not feared.**
  - A stranger is welcomed "according to the strict rules of hospitality": "the stranger's wishes come first" (p. 26).
  - Sunjata's mother sang on the day he first walked: "Stranger, stranger, Oh! / Sunjata became a stranger today" (Bird and Kendall, cited p. 29).
  - Jansen reads "stranger" as "mobile", not "dangerous" (p. 29). A newly chosen ruler of Kangaba was also called "the stranger" (p. 29).
- **Gifts and freedom.** "The youngest is the most free of all", because the receiver of a gift is bound to the giver. "The oldest brother receives the most respect" (p. 30).
  - A Kela elder put it this way: "Les Kuyate sont les premiers griots du Mande. / C'est pourquoi ils sont inférieurs à tous les autres" (the Kuyate are the first griots of Mande, which is why they are below all the others) (p. 29).

### 2.5 Smiths, potters, masks and mothers' names (2018)

- **Female smiths.** "Female blacksmiths" are called potters in English, but African languages have one word for a "blacksmithing person" with male or female added (p. 9).
  - Smiths and potters together are "earth experts". That is "why blacksmiths are responsible for circumcision/excision" (p. 10). **Do not use** the cutting.
- **The potters' first clay.** In a myth recorded by Florusbosch, the first clay came from a sacrifice to a bush spirit (*jinnu*) "who transformed a termite mound into clay". Since then potters "must offer an annual sacrifice to the bush spirits" (pp. 9–10).
  - Termite mounds are creation sites and shrines (pp. 9–10).
  - The Kangaba creation myth of the blind hunter divides creatures into those "with ears" (live-bearers) and those without (egg-layers) (pp. 10–11; *Secrets* pp. 29–30).
- **The mother's name.** "It is a common practice in Mande to add the mother's name to a person's name" (p. 14 n. 50): Sun-Jata, "Sogolon's lion".
- **Sogolon.** She "features in the narrative in more episodes than Sunjata himself" (p. 12).
  - Her porcupine and aardvark traits relate to the Komo mask, which Brett-Smith reads as a vagina (p. 13). **Komo: do not use.**
- **Marriage lessons.**
  - The "wild princess" from the bush has no relatives, so her husband owes no bride-givers (p. 17).
  - The co-wife with the Muslim patronymic Berete bears the failed heir (p. 18).
  - The mother is "highly valued as the person who will always support you": "*Bèè b'i ba bolo*", "Everything is in the hands of your mother" (p. 17).
- **Women absent from genealogies.** "Women's names are absent in these genealogies" of Sunjata's descendants. The absence removes half-siblings and so represents badenya (p. 20).
- **Koulikoro.** The rock where Sumaoro turned to stone is "a cult site or place of amnesty for fleeing slaves, and a place to which the Keita (Sunjata's descendants) are denied access" (p. 15, after Bulman 2017). People of Koulikoro still sacrifice there (p. 15 n. 58).
  - In CK3 Koulikoro is `b_koulikoro` in `c_sibi_mali`, the planned seat.
- **Sumaoro as a Muslim's child.** In one version Sumaoro is "the child of two mothers", each with a Muslim patronymic (p. 18 n. 69).

## 3. Corrections to the candidate and to existing notes

1. **The Kamabolon is not 18th-c. by consensus.**
   - `waf_conrad_austen_sunjata.md` §4 says "18th c. or later (Austen pp. 73–75)". Gomez says the late 17th c.
   - Jansen, who observed it, dates the ceremony's present form to 1880–1912 (p. 57). He treats the sanctuary as older but undated, and rejects the 17th-c. building date (p. 57 n. 113).
   - Correct to: *present ceremony 1880–1912 (Jansen 2002); sanctuary older, date unknown; no evidence before 1453.*
2. **The Kamabolon is a living Keita sanctuary guarded by violence**, and its recitation is the Diabaté's hereditary right (pp. 13, 19, 48–49). Using its rites as the faith's would present a living institution as the faith. That breaks the hard rule.
   - Kangaba (`b_kangaba` in `c_tabon`) can be a holy-site barony only with text that does not describe the Kamabolon, its night, its incantation or its secrecy (§4.5).
3. **"Licence to admonish" and the masked bard.** Jansen 2018 suggests that the epic descends from a masked performance (p. 15). This supports the gate's bird-masked jeli as *atmosphere*. It is a hypothesis built on the same single source (Ibn Baṭṭūṭa), so it is not independent confirmation.
4. **The sister-guide is not Kela's.** In the Kela version the sister is captured and freed by a man (p. 35). Name her in the faith's voice from the Upper Guinea and Niane traditions, as `waf_conrad_austen_sunjata.md` already does. Do not say "the Mande bards" tell it that way.
5. **The candidate's step 3 ("a jeli sings her name").** At Kela "only old men speak" the epic; women sing praise (Siramori p. 184). A *jelimuso* singing a praise-name is well grounded (LATER). A woman *speaking* the founding word inverts Kela practice.
   - The faith may claim that inversion in its own voice ("at Kela the old men speak; in our House an old woman does"). The narrator says it is the faith's invention.
6. **The "younger brother" logic gives the line a mechanic the candidate lacked** (§4.3). It also corrects any reading of Mande succession as simple seniority: seniority governs the compound, while the youngest claims command against outsiders.
7. **Page fix in `south_more_sources.md` B1.3.** The "television" under Kolonkan's wrapper is Jansen's note citing Conrad (1999b: 207–208), on p. 33 n. 47. It is not a Kela text.

## 4. Design implications

### 4.1 Gate, step 1 (the smith's road): add the potter, and make the price a yearly debt

- **Who leads.** The bush step is led by a smith *and a woman potter*, "earth experts" together (2018 pp. 9–10). The potter shapes; the smith deals with the djinn (Brett-Smith).
  - This makes the first step women-centred without dropping Brett-Smith's contract.
  - The Bamana or Maninka word for a woman potter needs a native speaker's check. Use "the potter" in loc until then.
- **A yearly price.** Florusbosch's potters owe the bush spirits "an annual sacrifice" for the first clay (2018 p. 10). So the daughter owes a **yearly offering** to her djinn: a small recurring gold cost, or an event each dry season. This replaces a one-time loss and makes the price ongoing and owed, not a mutilation.
  - Faith voice: "The potter pays for her clay every year. I pay for mine."
- **Body language.** Termite earth: the potter's clay comes from a termite mound that the djinn turned to clay (2018 p. 9). Holy-site and event text can use the termite mound as the place of making.
  - **Never** use circumcision or excision (2018 p. 10), the Komo, or the Komo mask.

### 4.2 Gate, step 2 (the mother's house): the mother's name, the bracelets, the song

- **Naming rule.** The new name is the claiming mother's (or sister's) name plus the daughter's own name, as in Sun-Jata, "Sogolon's lion" (2018 p. 14 n. 50). Examples: *Kosiya Nyuma*, *Kolonkan Sara*.
  - This gives every daughter a visible line of claim and suits *Take as Daughter*.
- **The price of the crossing.** The claiming woman gives two bracelets (gold) as the daughter's "price of crossing", as Sogolon paid the ferryman for her son (*Secrets* p. 127).
  - Mechanic: a gold cost paid by the claimer, not the daughter. A good event title: "The Price of the Crossing".
- **The first walk.** On the day the daughter first walks out under her name, her mother sings "Stranger, stranger": the song Sunjata's mother sang (Jansen 1996 p. 29, after Bird and Kendall).
  - In Mande the stranger is the honoured guest "whose wishes come first" (p. 26) and the mobile one (p. 29).
  - Faith voice: "She became a stranger today: may her wishes come first."
  - Modifier idea: *Became a Stranger*, a short-term opinion bonus with the claimer's House.
- **Hot until cooled.** While the gate is open the daughter is "hot" (*Secrets* pp. 53–54):
  - red cloth is avoided;
  - gossip is dangerous, because "any wrong word could re-make her wrongly".
  - Mechanic: a *Hot* modifier during the gate. While it lasts a hostile courtier's slander scheme can force a repeat of step 2. The gate's last step removes it with *Cooled* ("the Mande has cooled", p. 53).
  - This joins Schulz's *suman*/*kalan* (cool/hot) idiom (`waf_schulz_jelimusow.md`).

### 4.3 Gate, step 3 (the word): who sings, who speaks, who confirms

- **Sung, then spoken.** Following Kela's split, a *jelimuso* **sings** the name, as women sing praise (Siramori p. 184). An **old woman speaks** it once as the faith's "mistress of the word". Do not borrow *kumatigi* as a title; it is the Diabaté's office.
- **Two elders confirm.** The *kumatigi* troika model (*Secrets* p. 17) gives a rule: the name holds only if two older women of the faith confirm it. This combines with the bowstring witnesses (`waf_ibn_battuta_mali.md` §4).
- **The doyenne at the door.** An old woman of the House sits at the threshold and cries the blessing, as Kunba Diabaté did (p. 51). Give the House's Elder Sister this role in event text.
- **Offerings called aloud.** Courtiers' gifts are called aloud with the giver's name, "so that you bless her" (p. 51). Each gift gives the giver opinion with the daughter and the House.
- **Where the word comes from.** The jeli may say a djinn gave her the word, as Lansiné said of himself (p. 17). This ties step 3 back to step 1's djinn.
- **What the secret is.** Everyone may know the songs; only the House may sing them in the House (pp. 18–19).
  - Faith voice: "Everyone knows our songs. Only we may sing them here."

### 4.4 The line

- **Mother House and daughter Houses.** Map the Sisters' Houses onto the brother pair (Jansen 1996 pp. 15–28):
  - the founding "mother House" is the immobile, seated one; it keeps the line and receives gifts;
  - daughter Houses are "younger sisters" who go out.
- **War leader.** When a faith ruler is attacked by an outsider, the **youngest House's Elder Sister** may be named war leader (*keletigi*): a council or commander bonus, with tension when the war ends.
  - This parallels Kangaba's claim. When the threat passes, the older Houses resent it (p. 25).
- **Shame a sister, lose the command.** Doctrine (faith voice, after Mande Bori, p. 18): "He who shames his sister will never lead the war."
  - Mechanical tie-in: a character with a "shamed a sister" event flag cannot be a faith ruler's marshal. This joins Conrad's sister's curse.
- **Mothers' genealogies.** The mansas' bards name only men (2018 p. 20). The faith's genealogies name only mothers.
  - Narrator note: "The faith inverts a known Mande convention; this is reconstruction."
- **Motto.** "*Bèè b'i ba bolo*: everything is in your mother's hands" (2018 p. 17). Use the Bamana phrase only as quoted by Jansen; check spelling before shipping.

### 4.6 Festival: "the New Roof" (working name)

- **Basis.** Build it on Mande *house* practice, not on the Kamabolon (*Secrets* pp. 45–47):
  - in the dry season (March–May) young women re-plaster the walls and sweep, and men renew roofs;
  - age-sets are installed after cleaning the village.
- **Annual.** Each dry season every Sisters' House is re-plastered by its sisters: a small House-stability event between the March and April full moons.
- **Seven-yearly.** Every seventh dry season a House gets a **new roof**.
  - All daughters who passed the gate since the last roof form a named **age-set**, shouted at the end, as *Mogoyasi* was (p. 52). Generate a set name; *karé* is the Mande word for age-set (p. 45).
  - Members get a shared modifier (*Same Roof*) and mutual opinion: a sorority cohort.
- **Timing.** Start at the full moon after the rains end. Never on the two ʿĪds: the Kangaba ceremony was moved for Tabaski and for Ramadan (pp. 46–47). Prefer a Monday or Friday (p. 48).
- **Narrator note.** "Re-roofing and age-sets are Mande house and village practice; the faith's seven-yearly roof is a reconstruction and is not the Kamabolon of Kangaba."

### 4.7 Names

- **The Sara set.**
  - *Sara*: the bride who keeps her promise (Siramori pp. 187–194). It is a common name and an Arabic loan; LATER as a song, but fine as generic stock. It suits a faith whose gate is a promise kept.
  - Faith line: "Sara's first child was named Sara."
- **From the Kela texts (BACK as epic or legend):**
  - *Sogolon Kèjugu*;
  - *Awa* or *Ba Awa* (Eve), and *Mère Hawa*;
  - *Fatumata Bintu* (Fāṭima) (*Secrets* pp. 34, 37 n. 58, 66–74, 124–127).
- **The mother-name prefix (§4.2)** turns any list name into a claimed name.
- **Do not use:**
  - *Siramori* as an in-game person (a real artist, d. 1989);
  - *Kunba Diabaté*, *Lansiné* and other Kela informants' names;
  - Diabaté, Kuyate or Keita as faith surnames attached to rites. The bards' patronymics are living hereditary statuses (1996 pp. 19–21; Siramori p. 196 n. 4).
