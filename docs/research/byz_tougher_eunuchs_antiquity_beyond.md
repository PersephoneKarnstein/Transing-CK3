## 1. The argument, by design question

### 1a. Third gender or perfect servant?

The volume leans firmly away from "third gender" and towards the servant reading. Third-sexing turns up mainly in **hostile** texts.

- **Lightfoot on the galli:** "Were they accorded the positive recognition of being a 'third gender'? I think not: the tendency is rather to regard them as neither one thing nor another" (p. 82). But Lucian writes "as if the removal of masculinity automatically equates with femininity": "they no longer wear male dress, but wear women's clothes and perform women's work" (§27; p. 82).
- **Galli speech.** The galli use feminine forms of themselves and each other while the narrative calls them masculine (p. 76). They call each other *puellae*, "little doves" (*palumbulae*), and **"sisters"** (Apuleius, *Met.* 9.10.3; pp. 76, 79). A devotee of the Mother in Philodemus is named **Trygonion**, "little dove" (p. 76).
- **Sideris on the hostile physiognomy:** "even if eunuchs are no longer men, they do not become women, so they do not have the advantage of womanly qualities; feminine aspects mean, in the case of eunuchs, vice and weakness" (p. 162).
- **Late Byzantine romance:** "this *genos* of double sex, or rather, of no sex at all, is insidious and envious" (*Kallimachos and Chrysorrhoe*; Gaul p. 206). This is the third-sex line, spoken in rage.
- **Mullett rejects Ringrose's thesis** that the text shows "a reassignment of gender roles to include what Ringrose has called 'the ultimate socially constructed gender category'" (p. 186).
- **Tougher's conclusion (via Patterson):** "the ultimate ruler requires the ultimate slave"; the answer to "Why eunuchs?" lies "with the condition of the eunuch as a castrated man" (pp. 151–2).
- **Gaul on the late period:** eunuchs were expected "to serve sufficiently and silently in the more private quarters" and were "well respected" for it (p. 209).

### 1b. The angel analogy (Sideris; Mullett)

- **The angel is a palace eunuch.** In Leontios's *Life of John the Almsgiver* (c. 640s), a eunuch "in gleaming apparel, a golden sceptre in his right hand" summons John to "the King of Kings". The text then calls the vision "the eunuch, or rather ... the angel" (pp. 166–7). The sceptre is the imperial **ostiarios's** golden staff (p. 167, n. 39).
- **The *Oneirocriticon* of Achmet** (9th–11th c.): to dream of a gracious, tall eunuch is to dream of an angel, because a eunuch "is pure, resembles an angel" (p. 167).
- **The *Narratio de S. Sophia*:** an angel appears as a "handsome eunuch" in white court dress (p. 167).
- **The *Vision of the Monk Kosmas* (933).** Kosmas was a former palace *koitonites*, by then a monk of the Theotokos tou Eusebiou **near the Sangarios, in the Optimatoi theme**. He found "luminous eunuchs" in a heavenly palace modelled on Constantinople: "we do not really know which one is the model for the other" (pp. 167–8). The source of the chapter title "eunuchs of light" is on p. 169, n. 1. **This is the nearest eunuch-angel text to Dorylaion geographically.**
- **Corippus on Narses:** "shining with glorious light: as the morning star" (p. 166). The emperor's light topos is applied to a eunuch in an official panegyric.
- **Sideris's verdict:** "it is power, and the way that it is portrayed, that appears central" (p. 169). Eunuchs "were themselves in a position to elaborate portrayals of power favourable to them".
- **Mullett on rival angels:** both ascetics and eunuchs live "angelic lives", since eunuchs are compared to angels "'neither male nor female'". The middle Byzantine tendency, however, was "to privilege the monks as livers of the angelic life" (pp. 187–8).

### 1c. Castration practice: ages, methods, trade and law

**Methods.** Paul of Aegina is not in this volume; see `hist_tougher_eunuch_byzantine.md`. Bullough lists excision with a cauterising blade, crushing, and ligature (pp. 1–2). Penectomy was rare because of bleeding and urinary harm. Liudprand gave Constantine VII "four penis-less" Carzimasians, a Verdun export to Muslim Spain (Tougher p. 149). St John of Herakleia was castrated "by means of 'an iron'" (Gaul p. 202).

**Ages.**
- Infant, for medical reasons: John of Herakleia, whose parents chose between "his genitals or his soul" (Gaul p. 202).
- Childhood, by family for a career: Constantine the Paphlagonian, castrated by his father "for the sake of a career in Constantinople", and "this was a custom amongst farmers in that region" (Tougher p. 149).
- Child, captured: Eutherius, captured as a child in Armenia, castrated, and sold to Roman traders (Sideris p. 162).
- Theophylact's eunuch speaker contrasts castration "good in childhood for chastity" with "bad in adolescence for contraception" (Mullett p. 181).
- Adult, self-castration: the galli (Lightfoot) and Origen (Stevenson).

**The trade** (Tougher ch. 8):
- Late Rome mostly imported its eunuchs from Armenia and Persia. Donatus adds that eunuchs in the west came from Gaul.
- Abasgia's kings ran a castration trade until Justinian stopped it, sending the Abasgian eunuch Euphratas with the order (p. 144; p. 154, n. 76).
- Middle Byzantium **produced its own**, especially from Paphlagonia: seven cases between 906 and 1042, including John the Orphanotrophos and his eunuch brothers (p. 149).
- Foreign eunuchs still arrived: Stephen the Persian, Damianos the Slav, **Samonas the Arab** (possibly a prisoner of war), Constantine the Saracen (pp. 148–9).
- Al-Jahiz (d. 868–9) "accuses the Byzantines of kidnapping Muslim children and castrating them" (p. 153, n. 60). Masudi says the Byzantines, like the Chinese, castrate their own children (p. 149).
- The Peloponnesian magnate Danelis sent Basil I **a hundred eunuchs** (Sideris p. 166).

**The law** (Tougher pp. 144, 150 and nn. 4, 77, 79; Stevenson p. 127; Witt p. 246):

| Law | Provision |
|---|---|
| Domitian | Broad ban on castration. Under Antoninus Pius, Justin's young Christian **petitioned the prefect Felix** for leave to have a doctor castrate him and was refused (Stevenson p. 127). |
| Hadrian | Further legislation (*Dig.* 47.8.4.2). |
| Constantine I | No eunuchs to be made inside the empire. The castrator faced a **capital penalty**; the castrated slave and the place were confiscated. |
| Leo I | Only castrated barbarians may be imported; Roman eunuchs may not be owned. |
| Justinian, Nov. 142 | Bans castrating Romans and castration by Romans. **Those castrated within the empire are to be freed.** |
| Leo VI, Nov. 60 | Still disapproves, but castrators are no longer to be castrated: confiscation and exile instead. **A castrated slave is freed. "If the castrated person was free they are responsible for themselves."** Castration is "often ... necessary on the grounds of health". |
| Edessa after Abgar's conversion (c. 200) | Penalty for self-emasculation: "the loss of a hand" (Witt p. 246). Rules against it were still being made in the fifth century (Lightfoot p. 73). |
| Islam | Ayalon, via Tougher: repeated anti-castration edicts are "practically unknown" (p. 150). The Prophet "expressly forbade mutilation" and included eunuchs in his curse on "men who imitate women" (Witt p. 245). |

Tougher's reading: the late Roman laws show "emperors were hoping to stop the practice ... rather than that they did stop it" (p. 150).

### 1d. Careers and offices (for the planned court offices)

**Middle Byzantine.**
- *praepositus sacri cubiculi* (grand chamberlain)
- *parakoimomenos* (keeper of the bedchamber)
- *sakellarios* (treasurer)
- *castrensis* (steward of the household)
- *ostiarios* (doorkeeper and usher, with a golden staff)
- *koitonites* (chamber servant)
- grand hetaireiarch (Constantine the Saracen)
- sword-bearer (Narses under Justin II)
- army command: the last was under Michael VIII (Gaul p. 204)
- patriarchs: Germanos I, Niketas I, Ignatios, Stephen II, Theophylact, Polyeuktos, and perhaps Methodios (Sideris p. 165)
- many bishops

Eunuchs also served as **mediators and envoys** (Gaul p. 204) and as imperial messengers who "introduce dignitaries to the sovereign" (Sideris p. 167).

**Late Byzantine** (Gaul appendix): *megas droungarios*; *tatas tes aules*; *prokathemenos tou koitonos*; *megas doux* of Trebizond (seventh in precedence); and *megas ekklesiarches*. George Pepagomenos, a eunuch, was "in charge of marital law at the church of Hagia Sophia" (p. 203). At her coronation the empress leans "on two relatives of the closest kind ... or, if she has none, on two eunuchs" (Pseudo-Kodinos; p. 204). A 1336 Trebizond horoscope ranks "abbots and eunuchs" just above "noble women" (p. 201).

**The decline.** Nikephoritzes (1071–8) was "the last truly notable" powerful eunuch (Tougher p. 151). Gaul explains the decline as eunuch offices passing "partly by relatives or *oiketoi* of the emperor, partly by the women of the imperial and aristocratic families who had strengthened their position in the household administration" (p. 209). Magdalino, via Mullett, adds that eunuchs were less prominent under the Komnenoi but "make a come-back" under the Angeloi (p. 185).

**Eunuch houses.**
- St Lazaros in Constantinople was "reserved for eunuchs by Leo VI" (Witt p. 246).
- Symeon the Sanctified, *droungarios* of the Watch, became the only eunuch permitted on Athos and **founded a monastery of eunuchs in Thessalonike** (Mullett pp. 182–4).
- The Valesians, a whole castrate order at Bakathi near Philadelphia in Transjordan, were within the Church for a time before being excommunicated (Stevenson p. 129).

**Music.** Castrato cantors were present by c. 400 (Brison). Balsamon (late 12th c.) says all cantors are now castrati. Eunuch choirs sang the Feast of Lights for the court (Witt pp. 245–7).

### 1e. Theophylact's *In Defence of Eunuchs* (Mullett; Sideris pp. 161, 168)

- **Commission.** Written for his eunuch brother **Demetrios**, who was "annoyed at criticism of eunuchs" and wanted judgement case by case (p. 183). Demetrios died probably in **1107** after carrying letters across the frozen Vardar (p. 178). The setting points to the emperor's stay in Thessalonike during the second Norman war, in the 1100s (p. 184). So the text dates to the **first decade of the 12th c.**, not the "late 11th c." given in `hist_neville_byzantine_gender.md`.
- **Form.** Two speeches overheard in Thessalonike. A non-eunuch rebukes a eunuch **for having castrated his own nephew**, who is present. The eunuch rebuts each charge point by point (pp. 179–80).
- **Its strongest arguments:**
  - It is not against the canons "if by loving families in aid of piety" (p. 179).
  - Justinian could not keep his own law, "and in view of Narses it was just as well" (p. 181).
  - Ascetics too act against nature but lack the courage to cut (p. 186).
  - Eunuchs are free of nocturnal emission, so "our purity proceeds from a choice which works along with this condition of our bodies" (p. 189).
- **Mullett's caveats.** It is "above all a personal document" (p. 183). It is "less about gender ... than about the relationship between eunuchism and asceticism" (p. 186). It cannot show "a current debate ... still less a reassignment of gender roles" (p. 186). Elsewhere Theophylact calls self-castration "accursed" (Matthew commentary), mocks a eunuch tax-collector, and writes an invective poem (poem 13) against a eunuch (pp. 181–2).
- **Local colour.** The metropolitan of Thessalonike (Theodoulos) and the bishops of Kitros, Petra and Edessa were all eunuchs (p. 184).

### 1f. Galli, Cybele and Pessinus (Lightfoot; Hales; Witt)

- **Almost no evidence from Pessinus itself.** Classical sources "repeatedly connect her eunuch galli with the Phrygian 'temple state' of Pessinus, though far and away the bulk of the evidence comes from her western, imperial, diaspora" (pp. 72–3). For galli in Anatolia there is "practically none before the Hellenistic period" (p. 79).
- **Roller's alternative.** A beardless silver statuette (7th/6th c.) may show a eunuch priest in "a dignified and honourable office, nothing like the noisy vagabond hysterics beloved of the classical sources" (p. 79).
- **Attis is a Graeco-Roman accretion,** "not attested in Phrygian material" (p. 80). The *dies sanguinis* is "western, mostly Roman, evidence, and centres upon the figure of Attis" (p. 84, n. 23).
- **Who joined.** Membership was likely mixed: born intersex people, the castrated-before-puberty, "those who had chosen to live as women as a matter of personal preference", uncastrated beggars, and people taken by coercion and kidnapping (pp. 80–1, with the hijra analogy).
- **Hostile sources.** The satirists (Juvenal, Martial, Apuleius) paint galli as thieves, extortionists and kidnappers (pp. 75–6). Hales: in Roman art galli are "Roman bodies in fancy dress"; "women, castrates and citizen men are all brought together by their costume" (pp. 95, 98). The inner-shrine Attis at Ostia has "a smooth pubis, its female characteristics complemented by the curves of the torso" (p. 97).
- **Music.** Cybele hymns were "chanted by large numbers of people in trance, led by a rambling eunuch core" (Witt p. 244). An early epigram centres on "Achrylis the girl from Phrygia", possibly a regendered singer (p. 244).

## 3. Contradictions with existing notes and design claims

1. **Leo VI's "loophole withdrawn" (era decay).** Nothing here supports a repeal. The decline is political and gradual (Gaul; Tougher p. 151), with a twelfth-century come-back under the Angeloi. This note adds to `hist_tougher_eunuch_byzantine.md`, which reads Nov. 60's loophole as **medical only**: Tougher 2002 (n. 79) adds that a free castrated person "is responsible for themselves" and that a castrated slave is freed.
2. **Date of Theophylact's text.** `hist_neville_byzantine_gender.md` (§2.5, #47) says "late 11th c.". Mullett dates it to the 1100s, before Demetrios's death c. 1107. Sideris says "early twelfth-century".

## 4. Primary-source cases usable as events or characters

- **The galli's "sisters"** (Apuleius *Met.* 9.10.3) and **Trygonion** (Philodemus): names and address forms.
- **Justin's petitioner to the prefect Felix** (c. 150): the consensual-knife precedent.
- **The Valesians of Bakathi** (Epiphanius): a heresy-label event.
- **The Edessa law** (loss of a hand for self-castration, after Abgar): Syriac-Christian hostile law, for any Edessa or Urhai holding.
- **Eutherius** (an Armenian captive, castrated as a child, became Julian's grand chamberlain, with a great memory; Sideris p. 162): a captive-to-courtier character template.
- **Samonas the Arab** (a captive domestic who rose by reporting a plot to Leo VI): a frontier character.
- **Constantine the Paphlagonian**, castrated by his father for a career: the "Paphlagonian Farmer" event.
- **The *Vision of Kosmas* (3 June 933)**, a monastery near the Sangarios: a local event.
- **Leo the sakellarios's Bible miniature** (c. 940), with a eunuch in proskynesis before the Virgin: artifact flavour ("a Bible given by a eunuch").
- **Symeon the Sanctified**: thrown off Athos, reinstated by Alexios, founded a eunuch monastery in Thessalonike.
- **Demetrios Hephaistos**, Theophylact's eunuch brother, died c. 1107 carrying letters across the frozen Vardar: a character in the Galenic wing's book.
- **St John of Herakleia** (1249–1328), cut as an infant for illness, "either his genitals or his soul": the medical-excuse event.
- **George Pepagomenos**, a eunuch *megas ekklesiarches* in charge of marriages: an irony event for the marriage-office court position.
- **Anna of Savoy's eunuchs** (1340s): a foreign empress relies on eunuchs. Template for a foreign-born queen consort who seeks Dorleyan attendants.

- "The Novel of Leo says a free woman answers for her own body. We hold the emperor to his word." (Nov. 60, via Tougher n. 79)
- "The Archbishop of Ochrid defended the uncle who cut the boy. No one asked the boy." (Aristotelian rite on Theophylact; Mullett pp. 179–84)
