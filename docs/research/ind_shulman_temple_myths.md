## 0. Framing and dating warnings

- **The texts are late.** The Tamil temple *purāṇas* that carry these myths are mostly 16th century or later. Puranic composition "began ... on a wide scale" only then (p. 32). The *Kāñcippurāṇam* is 18th century (Civañāṉayōki; appendix, p. 354). The *Kantapurāṇam* is "end of the fourteenth century" or 1625 (pp. 31–32).
- **Older strata exist.** Shulman argues "the myths themselves ... are often very ancient" (p. 32). For each myth below this note gives the oldest witness Shulman cites. The narrator uses only those witnesses as dated. Anything carried only by a later *purāṇa* or *māhātmya* is "temple tradition", undated.
- **His frame is sacrifice and castration.** Shulman reads sex-reversal as "castration", "death-in-love" and the devotee's self-sacrifice (pp. 297–99, 306). He calls the goddess "bisexual" and the androgyne "sexually ambivalent" (p. 294).
  - The faith refuses this frame. A daughter is not a castrated or sacrificed man, and not a "male-female hybrid".
  - Use his myths and his dates, never his categories. This is the same rule as for El-Azhari (`isl_el_azhari_queens_eunuchs.md` §0).

## 1. The argument in brief

- **The local marriage myth is the core of the temple myth.** The goddess is there first and draws the god to the site. He is "imported from the north" for a second, local wedding after the first on Kailāsa (pp. 138–39, 142).
- **The goddess holds the shrine down.** Her worship locates the god physically (*pratiṣṭhā*); Kāmākṣī "holds the sand-liṅga in place at Kancipuram" (p. 139).
- **The virgin goddess is dangerous.** Marriage controls her power, and the myths "split" her into dark and golden halves or into an androgyne (pp. 140–41).
- **Sex-reversal follows from this.** A male who becomes a woman, or a god who becomes a mother, expresses the goddess's double nature. Shulman reads such myths as castration and rebirth (pp. 294–316).

## 2. What matters for the design

### 2.1 The goddess's tapas and marriage (pp. 138–44, 158–62)

- **The pattern.** The goddess comes to earth to expiate a fault, such as hiding Śiva's eyes. She worships a *liṅga* at the site, and her worship makes Śiva appear and agree to a second wedding there (p. 138). Shulman calls this explanation "a late rationalization", but the sequence "is probably accurate" (pp. 138–39).
- **Human brides.** Several human women refuse mortal husbands, choose the god, and "merge" with him. Shulman retells Āṇṭāḷ's legend from Govindacharya and Das: she wears the garland, sees herself "in the water of a well", and merges at Srirangam "at the age of fourteen" (p. 161). Others include Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār (pp. 160–61) and Mahādēviyakka (p. 162).
  - Shulman's own conclusion: "Siva's virgin brides are, and remain, virgins" (p. 162).
  - This matches Orr's finding that such a woman "dies or disappears by merging into the image" (`ind_orr_temple_women.md` §2.7). It is no model for a living bride in the world.
  - The faith's daughter must stay alive and in the world. Her rite cannot end in a merger.

- **Kāmākṣī's penance under the mango tree** is attested in the *Kāñcīmāhātmya*, which Shulman does not date. The 12th-century *Periyapurāṇam* has the flood, the embrace and the wedding form, but Shulman cites no mango from it.
- **The name.** Shulman does not explain "Ekāmra" ("one mango"). The usual etymology is standard but not sourced in this book.
- **Why Kanchi suits the Garland.** It is the one site where a **Vaiṣṇava text has Viṣṇu act as the bride's brother and officiant at Śiva's wedding** (pp. 171–73). The faith crosses Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava devotion (`faith_design.md` §15), and Kanchi's myths cross the same line in their own telling.
- **It is the shrine of desire.** The goddess there helps Kāma against Śiva (pp. 169–71). Āṇṭāḷ's second poem opens with a vow to Kāma (NT 1; see `ind_dehejia_venkatesan_antal.md`).

- **Disguise is not sex-reversal.** Shulman separates "true sex-reversals" from "the common literary motif of the suitor who dresses as a woman" (n. 46, p. 419). The faith is about the first. Its narrator never treats a daughter's womanhood as a disguise.
- **A transformation dance.** Shulman cites "a seductive dance by a male who hides his manhood", Kāma's *pēṭi* dance in the *Cilappatikāram* 6 and *Maṇimēkalai* 3 (n. 85, p. 420). That source is c. 5th–6th century.
  - The *pēṭi* is a "neither" figure in Tamil, the kind of third thing the faith refuses.
  - It can serve as reaction data only. A hostile courtier might call a daughter "a *pēṭi* dancing for Kāma"; the faith answers that she is no *pēṭi*, but a girl.
- **In period, in Tamil.** Shulman cites Cuntarar, *Tēvāram* 36.5: the sages' wives refuse Śiva alms because he comes as the androgyne with Umā in half his body (p. 310). Use only as Śaiva scripture voice, never as a claim about people.

## 3. Corrections to the candidate

1. **Kanchipuram's in-period myth is the embrace, not the mango.** The *Periyapurāṇam* (12th c.) has the embrace and the wedding form. Cuntarar (8th–9th c.) names the Kāmakkōṭṭam. The mango penance is in the undated *Kāñcīmāhātmya*. Holy-site text should lead with the dated layer and may add the mango as "temple tradition".
2. **Tiruvāṉaikkā's sex-change myth is late**, and the cross-dressing priest is modern. Neither may be presented as medieval. Appar's 7th-century hymn to its water *liṅga* (Dehejia p. 19 n. 24) is the dated layer of that site.
3. **Bhaṅgāśvana's pleasure answer.** Shulman independently flags it as male hostility to sexuality (n. 39). This confirms the design rule to lead with her children.
4. **No living human bride of the god in the myths.** Every human bride merges or dies (pp. 158–62). The faith's daughters are a deliberate break with this pattern, and the narrator should say so: the faith keeps its brides alive.

## 4. Design implications

- **Holy site: Kanchipuram.** Proposed loc in the mod's existing format (`holy_site_tm_*_name` / `_effects`). ASCII spellings follow other holy-site loc in the mod.
  - `holy_site_tm_kanchi_name:0 "Kanchi"`
  - Option A (narrator, dated layer only): `holy_site_tm_kanchi_effects:0 "The earth among Shiva's five shrines. Here the goddess held a lingam of sand against the flood until it took the print of her arms, and here her own house, the Kamakkottam, stood so near his that a Pallava-age hymn asks why he goes begging."`
  - Option B (adds the marked temple tradition): `holy_site_tm_kanchi_effects:0 "Kanchi of the mango tree, where the goddess kept her penance in the burned tree's shade, embraced the sand lingam through the flood, and was given in marriage by her brother Vishnu, as the temple tells it."`
  - Option C (the faith's voice; use as a tooltip or event line, not the effects line): "At Kanchi the god of one faith gave away the bride of the other. We ask no more of either."
  - The seat stays Kanchipuram (`faith_design.md` §15). Srirangam stays a holy site, through the 1126 garden inscription (see the Dehejia note).
- **Gate refinement: the dawn bath and the sand image.** Step 2 (the song) or step 4 (the dream) can carry an in-period Kanchi image: the companions shape a sand image on the riverbank at dawn and keep it through rising water.
  - This draws on the goddess's sand *liṅga* (12th-c. layer) and the *Cilappatikāram*'s wife on the Kāvēri bank (c. 5th c.).
  - The *Bhāgavata*'s maidens also make a sand image during their winter vow (Dehejia pp. 17–18).
  - **Event:** "The river is rising." Hold the image (stress, small risk) or let it go (a setback to next Mārkaḻi). Holding it gives a "the print stayed in the stone" memory.
- **A Kāma thread, optional.** At Kanchi, Kāma is revived and Śiva loses. A daughter who has failed a Mārkaḻi can keep a Tai vow to Kāma (Āṇṭāḷ's NT 1, see the Dehejia note) at a Kanchi holy site, for a bonus to the next attempt. This gives Kanchi a mechanical role.
- **Faith-voice scripture beyond Bhaṅgāśvana**, every one marked as temple tradition:
  - Brahmā creating "as a woman" at Tiruvāṉaikkā;
  - Mohinī asking to stay loved after the part was played;
  - Śiva taking a mother's form at Tiruccirāppaḷḷi.
- **"The cage" lines.** Nārada, who reverts, and Aruṇa, who does penance for "having taken woman's form", are the orthodox counter-examples. A hostile Śaiva priest: "Even the sun's charioteer did penance for it." The faith's reply can use Shulman's own distinction (n. 46): she is no suitor in a borrowed sari.
- **Never.**
  - Shulman's castration, sacrifice or "bisexual" frame in loc.
  - The Tiruvāṉaikkā priest's rite and the Kūttāṇṭavar mourning (modern, living).
  - The Tirukkaḷar daughter-to-son myth as anything but reaction data.
  - A daughter who "merges" with the image as a success state.
