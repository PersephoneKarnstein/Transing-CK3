# The *mukhannathūn* of Baghdad, Rayy and Isfahan around 1066: a design dossier for a possible start

Written 2026-10-20 for the later-starts question (`docs/later_starts.md`). It follows the Islamic-world regional pass, which rated the troupes "plausible" and recommended an event pack rather than a start. The user asked for that verdict to be tested honestly. Nothing else in the repo was edited.

**Verdict in one paragraph.** The *mukhannathūn* cannot be a seventh faith or a current within Islam. No source gives them a religious idiom. The idiom the sources do give is a **craft** (*ṣanʿa*, *ṣināʿa*): masters and pupils, a shared argot, public work at processions and feasts, and a fixed place in the legal order as a kind of man. The mod's job is not to invent a Muslim heterodoxy, and a "faith" would also present a role with living descendants (the Omani *khanīth*; Rowson 1991b n. 60) as a religion. The honest answer is **an event pack with a recurring cast**. Its best playable form is an **optional, unlanded troupe start at Baghdad in 1066**: a landless adventurer camp, as vanilla does for Sulaymān b. Qutalmish in 1063. Its members keep their vanilla Muslim faith, and no rite changes their bodies. The start rests on the mod's shared Marked and naming mechanics plus new contract and event script. *Silk & Silver*'s merchant company does not fit, because the troupe sells performances, not goods. At 1178 there is nothing to start: Ibn al-Jawzī's Baghdad is the hostile voice, not the setting.

## 1. The argument and the evidence

### 1.1 What a *mukhannath* is, and what he is not

The term means a man who "imitates women" in gesture, speech and adornment. The lexica derive it from a root for bending and languor (Rowson 1991a, pp. 672–673). The hadith curse falls on "the effeminate among men" (*mukhannathī l-rijāl*) "who imitate women" (Ibn al-Jawzī, *Talbīs* p. 261). From the ninth century the figure was "always assumed to be sexually passive with other men" (Rowson 1991b, pp. 70–71). Every source below is written from outside and against him.

**The three categories the law keeps apart.** Eleventh-century Ḥanafī law, written in our window, separates them cleanly:

| | *Mukhannath* | *Khunthā* | Eunuch (*khaṣī*, *majbūb*, *khādim*) |
|---|---|---|---|
| What the law sees | A known man and his **conduct** | A body of **unknown** sex | A **made** man, cut |
| How it is handled | By conduct: one effeminate "in vile acts" is a sinner, kept from women and barred from testimony; one soft "by birth" who does not desire women may be admitted among them and may testify (al-Sarakhsī 10:158, 16:131) | By bodily signs: the urinary orifice, then puberty (30:103–105). The unresolved *mushkil* prays "behind the men's row and before the women's" (30:107) | "In testimony and inheritance like an intact man" (10:158). Castration is mutilation (*muthla*). He is still a sexual risk and kept from women "on the sounder view" (10:158) |
| Social register | Entertainer, broker, jester; "public persons" without respectability (Rowson 1991b, p. 72) | A legal problem without moral blame (Sanders 1991, pp. 77–81) | Slave servant of palace and harem (Ayalon; Marmon) |
| Where they meet | The Qurʾān's "men without desire" (24:31) is glossed as either the fully cut man or "the *mukhannath* who does not desire women" (al-Sarakhsī 10:158) | — | Same gloss |

Contemporaries kept the eunuch and the *mukhannath* apart too:
- **Al-Jāḥiẓ** had "never seen a eunuch who was a *mukhannath*" (Rowson 2003, p. 55).
- **ʿAbbāda**, offered a eunuch, refused: "I don't board a ship without a mast" (al-Ābī 5:186).
- **Al-Rāghib's absurdities.** "A *mukhannath* leading prayer" and "a eunuch with a wife" are two separate items in his list (1:540).
- **Jāḥiẓ, via al-Rāghib.** "The *mukhannath*'s piety is to become the women's broker; the eunuch's piety is raiding the Rūm" (2:431). It is a joke, but it fixes each in a different trade.
- **Ibn Buṭlān.** For him the eunuch's character "resembles women's" (p. 366). The *mukhannath* does not appear in his slave-buying manual at all, because he was not a slave category.

Rowson's own line is that "we do not hear of mukhannaths as slaves" (1991b, p. 71).

**Design consequence.** The troupe is not eunuchs and not *khunthā*. Its members are free men, in the law's eyes, who have stepped out of manhood in public. That is a different cage from the Adelphai's, and the content must not blur them. The *khunthā* is never a stand-in for a trans woman (`hist_sanders_khuntha.md`).

### 1.2 What is attested by c. 1066, and where

The base texts are themselves of the window and the region. Rowson's three vice-list sources are al-Ābī, vizier at **Rayy** (d. 1030); al-Rāghib of **Isfahan**; and al-Jurjānī (d. 1089) (1991b, pp. 68–69 and n. 50). Add the Baghdad chroniclers Ghars al-Niʿma (d. 1088) and Ibn al-Jawzī (who used eleventh-century diaries), and al-Mafarrūkhī's *Maḥāsin Iṣfahān* (c. 1030). The evidence is late-tenth to eleventh century. Most of the anecdotes it retells are set earlier, at Samarra, Baghdad and Medina.

**a. Feminine names and the *Umm* kunya.**
- **Names.** "ʿAbbāda is a feminine name, as are the names of virtually all the mukhannaths we hear of from the ninth century on" (Rowson 1991b, n. 57). Al-Ābī's chapter (5:184–195) supplies the stock:
  - ʿAbbāda;
  - **Zāʾida**, "a *mukhannath* master (*ustādh*) called Zāʾida" (5:190);
  - **Simsina**, "sesame" (5:189), which al-Rāghib gives as **Mishmisha**, "apricot" (1:134);
  - the rhyming pair **Shaʿra and Baʿra**, al-Mutawakkil's two jesters (5:186);
  - the diminutive **Nuwayfil** (5:186);
  - **Qarqar** (5:185);
  - **Salmān**, a drummer (5:184);
  - the Persian **Shāhik** (5:185).
- **Medinan names.** Rowson's Medinan list adds the plant and mood names: Bard al-Fuʾād, Nawmat al-Ḍuḥā, Nasīm al-Saḥar, Ẓill al-Shajar and Qand, "candy" (1991a, pp. 681, 691).
- **The *Umm* kunya.** Asked "Whose father are you?", Qarqar answers "Umm Aḥmad" (al-Ābī 5:185; al-Tawḥīdī 3:84; Rowson 1991b, p. 70). *Umm* plus the first son's name "was the polite form of address to women" (Rowson 1991b, n. 54). The exchange is told as a joke at the speaker's expense, and the same chapter has *mukhannathūn* calling each other "O father of so-and-so" (5:185). Feminine address was a register, not a rule.
- **Feminine address among them and to them.** *Mukhannathūn* "address each other in the feminine gender" (Rowson 1991b, p. 70). A Sufi mocks one in the street as "my sister" in feminine grammar (al-Tanūkhī, *Nishwār* 2:277–278).
- **Choosing the name.** Asked why he took his name, **Ḥaram** said: so that when she is mourned, they will cry "Alas, Ḥaram!" (al-Rāghib 2:369). Earlier, Ṭāwūs was renamed Ṭuways when he became a *mukhannath*, and his peers renamed his kunya (Rowson 1991a, p. 678). Naming was done by the person and by the group.

**b. Troupes and a corporate identity.**
- **Corporate as well as individual.** The *mukhannathūn* had "a distinct identity (certainly corporate as well as individual)" (Rowson 1991b, p. 72). In Medina they were "one might almost say a corporation" (Rowson 2003, p. 56).
- **The marks of a trade:**
  - a master and pupils (Zāʾida the *ustādh*, al-Ābī 5:190; Ṭuways and his pupil al-Dalāl, Rowson 1991a, p. 682);
  - a "provision-bag" (*zāmilat al-mukhannathīn*) carried by the novice, and an argot (*kalām*) (Rowson 2003, p. 61);
  - insult-matches between members (al-Tawḥīdī 6:83, an eyewitness; Rowson 2003, p. 62);
  - al-Rāghib's heading "the *mukhannathūn* boasting of their craft (*ṣināʿa*)" (ḥadd 16, c. 2:278).
- **Al-Ghazālī's word for it.** One *mukhannath* may feel superior to another by greater skill "in the craft of the *mukhannathūn*" (*Iḥyāʾ* 3:353). He sets their boasting beside that of cuppers and sweepers among their own kind (3:59): **a low trade with its own pride.**
- **Public appearances as a body, in our window** (*Muntaẓam*):
  - **Baghdad, 334/945:** Muʿizz al-Dawla's wrestling games, with *mukhānīth* "with drums and pipes" massed on the wall of the maydān (14:44).
  - **Baghdad, 441/1049:** the Sunni–Shiʿi gate processions, led by drums, pipes and *mukhānīth* "with their instruments of mimicry" (*ālāt al-ḥikāya*) (15:320).
  - **Hamadan, 455/1063:** on Ṭughril Beg's death, the *ʿayyārūn* (urban militias) killed the *ʿamīd* and seven hundred of the *shiḥna*'s men. Then they "brought the *mukhānīth* with drums and pipes" and feasted over the dead for the rest of the month (16:82).
  - **Baghdad, 488/1095:** Ibn ʿAqīl's letter to the vizier attacks festivities on the city wall: "drums … *mukhānīth*, shadow-play (*khayāl*)", with women present (17:16–17).
  - **Iraq, 517/1123:** Dubays brought "prostitutes and *mukhānīth* with instruments, pipes and tambourines" to battle against the caliph (17:216).
  - **Baghdad, 532/1137:** the illuminations (17:327).
- **Not a guild in the formal sense.** No source gives a headman, statutes or a register. Contrast Zhou Mi's guild head at Lin'an (`docs/later_starts.md`). "Troupe" and "craft" are as far as the evidence goes.

**c. Instruments.**
- **Drums above all.** The tie to drums is "society-wide", and a tenth-century source distinguishes "parade drums" from "*mukhannaths'* drums" (*ṭubūl al-makhānīth*) (Rowson 2003, p. 61 and n. 48, citing the Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ). Al-Ābī has "Salmān the *mukhannath*'s drumming" (5:184).
- **The *kūba*.** **Verified:** al-Ghazālī forbids it, "a long drum, slender in the middle and broad at the ends", because "beating it is the custom (*ʿāda*) of the *mukhannathūn*" (*Iḥyāʾ* 2:272; Macdonald 1901, p. 213). Otherwise it would be like the drum of pilgrims and war (*ibid.*). Later he lists pipes, strings and the *kūba* as "badges (*shiʿār*) of drinkers or *mukhannathūn*" (2:282; Macdonald p. 237). He allows the frame drum (*duff*), the ordinary drum and the *shāhīn* pipe (p. 237). So the *kūba* is a badge of the trade c. 1100 in the mouth of its sharpest critic. It may be the same as Rowson's *ṭubūl al-makhānīth* (*unverified*). Ibn al-Ukhuwwa (14th c.) calls the *kūba* "the drum of the Sūdān", without the *mukhannath* link. Ibn al-Jawzī's editor glosses it as backgammon or a drum (*Talbīs* p. 157 n.).
- **Other instruments and props.** The *duff* and tambourine (Medina and later), the *ṭunbūr* lute at court (Rowson 2003, p. 61), and the *kurraj* hobby-horse (Rowson 2003, p. 60; Moreh, secondhand).

**d. Patrons.**
- **The ninth-century court:**
  - al-Mutawakkil's jesters (al-Ābī 5:184–194);
  - al-Amīn's dances (Rowson 2003, p. 60).
- **The Buyid and early-eleventh-century patrons:**
  - princes who kept "buffoons" (*Eclipse* V, p. 249, Bakhtiyār);
  - an *amīr* paying a *mukhannath* a hundred dirhams for a song (al-Tawḥīdī 3:62);
  - Muʿizz al-Dawla's games (*Muntaẓam* 14:44).
- **In 1060s Baghdad, the patrons are Turkish military households and the crowd:**
  - a singer leaving "a Turk's house" at Nahr Ṭābaq starts the 464 riot (16:138);
  - the brothels formed part of the *shiḥna*'s land grant (*iqṭāʿ*) in 469 (16:184);
  - the *ʿayyārūn* hire *mukhannathūn* at Hamadan (16:82).
  
  No source shows the caliph al-Qāʾim keeping *mukhannathūn*. After the ninth century, court *mukhannath* musicians had too little celebrity "even to have their names preserved" (Rowson 1991a, p. 693).
- **Isfahan.** Al-Mafarrūkhī's local history (c. 1030) has a chapter of anecdotes on "women, *mukhannathān*, and fools". It includes a "celebrated *mukhannath* named Dukhandī" and "the *mukhannath* who wanted a fine shroud" (Browne 1901, pp. 672–674; *unverified* beyond Browne's summary).

**e. Weddings, brokering and mourning.**
- **Weddings.**
  - *Early:* attested for Medina (Rowson 1991a, p. 682).
  - *By c. 1100, indirectly:* Ibn ʿAbdūn orders that the *ḥiwā* be expelled from Seville and "not be let walk among the Muslims, nor at **weddings** (*al-aʿrās*)" (Arabic p. 51). The ban implies that they came. The Spanish rendering "fiestas" loses this. His word is *ḥiwā*, found in one manuscript only (n. 191), not *mukhannath*. This is a cousin category in al-Andalus, not the Iraqi trade.
  - *Not attested:* no eleventh-century Iraqi or Iranian anecdote found here shows a *mukhannath* playing at a wedding. The one wedding night in al-Ābī is a *mukhannath*'s mother's remarriage (5:193).
- **Brokering.**
  - *Attested:* a *mukhannath* "brought two together" and was taken before the ruler (al-Ābī 5:192; Rowson 1991b, n. 58).
  - *Lawful brokering:* Jāḥiẓ's "women's broker" (al-Rāghib 2:431).
  - *Procuring:* the eleventh-century Egyptian paraded for procuring "for five women out of his house" (Rowson 2003, p. 65).
- **Mourning.** The Meccan *mukhannath* singers began as professional lamenters, "an activity traditionally restricted to women" (Rowson 1991a, p. 683). Al-Qudūrī's Baghdad law code rejects in one breath the testimony of "a *mukhannath*, a wailing woman (*nāʾiḥa*), and a woman singer" (p. 220). The three trades shared a legal shelf.

**f. Dress and body.**
- Plucked beards and long hair (Rowson 1991b, p. 70).
- Henna (Rowson 1991a, p. 675).
- Face tattoos and mixing with women (al-Ghazālī 3:59).
- Coloured and silk clothes, "the business of women and *mukhannathūn*" (3:72).
- **The veil, a correction.** Rowson had "seen no references to mukhannaths wearing veils" (1991b, n. 52), and the mod's `tm_isl.0002` loc was changed on that basis. But Ghars al-Niʿma, writing in Baghdad in the window, has a Daylamite commander nearly kill an official over a pun read as calling him a "*mukhannath* who wears the *miqnaʿa*", a woman's head-veil (*Hafawāt* §291, p. 75). Al-Tawḥīdī has a *mukhannath* "in the guise of a woman" (*fī hayʾat imraʾa*) who tells a drunk Turk "my husband has sworn …" (4:221). The present loc ("a shaven man in women's finery") is still safe, but "no source has a veil" is no longer true.

**g. Legal and social status.** The status is low and stable in the law of our window:
- **Testimony.** Barred (al-Qudūrī p. 220; al-Sarakhsī 16:131), unless the softness is "by birth" (al-Sarakhsī 16:131).
- **Exile.** The Sunna's exile applies "to the adulterer and the *mukhannath*" (al-Shāfiʿī, *Umm* 6:146).
- **Earnings.** "The *mukhannath*'s earnings are foul; he earns them by singing" (Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal via al-Marrūdhī, in *Talbīs* p. 204).
- **Policing, in the anecdotes.** The police appear in them (al-Rāghib 2:769). Governors flog (al-Ābī 5:189–190).
- **A precedent for a round-up.** In 321/933 the caliph al-Qāhir had "all men known as singers, or hermaphrodites" (Margoliouth's rendering of *mukhannathūn*) arrested and banished to Basra and Kufa (*Eclipse* IV, p. 305). He still picked singing women for himself.

### 1.3 Seljuk Baghdad, 1063–1077: the clock

- **Who rules.**
  - The caliph al-Qāʾim (r. 1031–75) is married to Arslan Khatun Khadīja, Chaghri Beg's daughter (contract 448/1056; *Muntaẓam* 16:4).
  - Alp Arslan is sultan from 1063 to Nov.–Dec. 1072 (Bosworth, *EIr*). Niẓām al-Mulk becomes his vizier in Muḥarram 456/Dec. 1063 (16:86).
  - The caliph's vizier is Fakhr al-Dawla Ibn Jahīr, from 454/1062 (16:76).
  - The Seljuk military governor (*shiḥna*) of Baghdad is Aytakīn al-Sulaymānī, from 456 (Ibn al-Athīr 8:191), replaced by Saʿd al-Dawla Gawharāʾīn in 464 (8:228).
- **Recent history.** The Basāsīrī occupation ran from Dec. 1058 to Jan. 1060 (16:32, 42).
- **The Niẓāmiyya madrasa.** Begun Dhū l-Ḥijja 457/Nov. 1065 and opened Dhū l-Qaʿda 459/Sept. 1067 (16:91, 102).
- **The Hanbali campaigns** (all *Muntaẓam* vol. 16, and Cook 2000, pp. 118–121):
  - **461/1069.** The sharīf Ibn Sukkara raids two gatherings near the palace, "smashed musical instruments and poured out liquor" (Cook p. 118, from Ibn al-Bannāʾ's diary).
  - **464/1072.** Abū Saʿd al-Baqqāl meets a singing woman leaving a Turk's house, "seized her lute and cut its strings" (16:138). The Turk raids his house, and the Hanbalis gather with Abū Isḥāq al-Shīrāzī. They demand that the brothels be uprooted and "the corrupting women and wine-sellers" pursued. The caliph orders it: the women flee and houses are raided. He promises to write to the sultan (16:138–139).
  - **467/1075, after al-Qāʾim's death.** The vizier has the *muḥtasib* expel the "corrupting women": "several were paraded on donkeys" to the west bank (16:166).
  - **469/1077.** The brothels, part of the *shiḥna*'s *iqṭāʿ*, are pulled down after Niẓām al-Mulk compensates him (16:184).
  - **The leader.** The movement's figure is the sharīf **Abū Jaʿfar al-Hāshimī** (d. 470/1077), "a speaker of truth who favoured no one" (16:195–196), Makdisi's model Hanbali activist (Cook p. 119 n. 38).
  - **What the campaigns never name.** They target women, wine and instruments. **They never name the *mukhannathūn***, who stand on the edge of the targets rather than at their centre.
- **Hostile voices that sharpen after 1066:**
  - **Ibn ʿAqīl** (d. 1119). He sorts sounds into forbidden, disliked and permitted, and allows only the *duff* (*Devil's Deceptions* p. 356). His 488/1095 letter names the *mukhānīth* (17:16–17).
  - **Al-Ghazālī's *kūba*** (above).
  - **Ibn al-Jawzī himself:** clapping "is also an act of imitating women" (*Devil's Deceptions* p. 369), and "the *mukhannathūn* of Baghdad" are an example of artifice (*Talbīs* p. 330).
  - **Al-Shayzarī** (Syria, c. 1180). The *muḥtasib* banishes "effeminate men … well known for their perversions", forbids the *mukhannath* "from shaving off or plucking out his beard and from going amongst women" (Buckley p. 127), and forbids the barber to cut the *mukhannath*'s beard (p. 106). **Correction (2026-10-26):** in the Arabic (al-ʿArīnī ed. p. 110) that sentence names *al-khunthā*, not *al-mukhannath*; Buckley renders it "effeminate". The barber rule (p. 88) does say *mukhannath*. See `mukhannathun_fatimid_cairo.md` §2.6.

### 1.4 Is there a household or communal life to build on, and in what idiom?

- **What exists.**
  - Teacher and pupil (Zāʾida; Ṭuways and al-Dalāl).
  - Peers who rename one another (Ṭuways).
  - Houses:
    - Find's house in Medina (Rowson 1991a, p. 687);
    - the eleventh-century Egyptian's house, with "five women" or with singing slave girls (Rowson 2003, p. 65);
    - Mihrān the *mukhannath* owning a slave singer, Nabt (al-Tanūkhī 7:128).
  - Shared public work as a body (*Muntaẓam* 14:44, 15:320, 16:82).
  - Pride in the craft (al-Rāghib; al-Ghazālī 3:353).
  - A bag and an argot (Rowson 2003, p. 61).
- **What does not exist.**
  - No source shows *mukhannathūn* living together as a household of equals.
  - None shows a shared religious practice, a shrine, a saint or a confraternity.
  - Rowson notes cohabitation is not attested even for the Mamluks (2008, n. 3).
  - The religious idiom the sources attach to them is **irreligion**:
    - the Medinan persona mocks prayer (Rowson 1991a, pp. 680, 683);
    - Umayyad persecution was framed as their "lack of proper religious commitment" (Rowson 1991a, p. 687);
    - "a *mukhannath* leading prayer" is a stock absurdity (al-Rāghib 1:540).
  - The one devout note is defensive. Ṭuways answers the charge of unbelief with the creed and the pillars (Rowson 1991a, p. 680). And al-Sarakhsī's "soft by birth" exception (10:158) is a jurist's licence, not a community's theology.
- **Verdict on idiom.** The evidence supports a **troupe (a craft and a pupil line), not a congregation**. The houses on record are those of a performer or a procurer, some with slaves. They are not a refuge.

### 1.5 Why not a faith, a current, or a merchant company

1. **A new faith.** Every faith in the mod is a trans-women-centred *religious* current built on a real text or practice it can read against the grain: the Mother's rites, *seiðr*, inner alchemy, the Purim prayer, the *pāvai* vow, the smith's bargain (README; the six faith pages). Here there is no text and no practice to read: the only religious register is the accusation of irreligion.
2. **A current within Islam.** Ha-Nehpakhot is a "small, fictional current inside Judaism" (`docs/faiths/ha-nehpakhot.md`). It is defensible because it reads rabbinic texts that themselves discuss sex change (Rashi on Dinah; Yevamot), and it is set inside a minority community at Arles. No comparable Islamic text exists for a trans woman. Building one would make the narrator author a Muslim heresy out of hostile anecdotes.
   - CK3 1.20 makes this concrete. Vanilla Islam is organised into faiths (`sunni`, `shia`, …) with **rites** that are the real legal schools (`hanafi`, `shafii`, `hanbali`; `common/religion/rite_types/00_rite_types.txt`). A fictional current would sit beside real madhhabs and real sects.
   - The brief's rule against presenting Islam carelessly settles it.
3. **A living community.** Rowson ties the medieval figure to the Omani *khanīth* (1991b, n. 60; 2003, p. 65). Giving the *mukhannathūn* a faith would present a living community's ancestry as a religion. The project's rule is never to do that.
4. **Silk & Silver merchants.** The merchant company trades goods along routes, with patrons and charters (`docs/silk_and_silver.md`). The troupe's income is fees for performances, brokering and mourning, which no trade good models.
   - The two attested "household economies" are a *mukhannath* who owns a slave singer (al-Tanūkhī 7:128) and procuring (Rowson 2003, p. 65). Neither may become the troupe's business.
   - So no merchant company. *Silk & Silver* still matters as backdrop: Baghdad is an entrepôt, and Turkish military households are the troupe's patrons.
5. **An Adelphai current.** The Adelphai already claim Sulaymān's 717 castration of the Medinan singers as their founding trauma, in their own voice (`docs/faith_design.md` §9; `beliefs.md` 13). That is a real bridge. But converting a Baghdad troupe of Muslims into a Phrygian Christian heresy is incoherent, and it would read as Christian rescue. The bridge works best as one Rūm-born Adelphai daughter in the troupe, and as the Sisters' memory of Medina. The troupe stays Muslim.

### 2.2 Seat

**The troupe's domicile is the city of Baghdad**: a landless camp whose capital is `c_baghdad`. The camp does not hold the barony `b_baghdad`.
- **Its quarter.** The west bank, Karkh, is where the 467 expulsions sent the "corrupting women" (16:166), and where the 441 processions ran between Sunni and Shiʿi gates (15:320). The quarter is flavour, not a map title.
- **Its circuit.** Baghdad → Hamadan → Rayy → Isfahan: the sultan's road and the three eleventh-century anthologies' home cities.

This fallback **is a liberty**: no source puts *mukhannathūn* at al-Qāʾim's court. A fallback host of Alp Arslan's camp at Rayy is no better attested.

### 2.4 How one becomes a daughter there (the gate)

There is **no bodily rite**. That is honest to the sources: the *mukhannath* plucks, hennas, dresses and names, and is never cut by choice. Sulaymān's castration was punishment (Rowson 1991a, p. 690). So the troupe has no re-embodiment, and its women stay coded male. That is a real limit of this start, and the start should say so.

**The gate is a naming before the troupe**, built on *Declare Before Witnesses* (`tm_declare_decision`). It has three steps, each attested in some form:
1. **The bag.** The novice carries the *ustādha*'s provision-bag for a season (*zāmilat al-mukhannathīn*; Rowson 2003, p. 61). This is a short scheme or story cycle.
2. **The name.** The troupe gives her a name, and she may refuse it and choose her own, as Ḥaram did (al-Rāghib 2:369). Ṭuways's peers renamed him (Rowson 1991a, p. 678).
3. **The kunya.** She is first called *Umm* + a name before the troupe. The sources tell "Umm Aḥmad" as a joke (al-Ābī 5:185). The troupe means it. That turn is the mod's, in the troupe's voice, and the narrator does not claim the historical speaker meant it.

**A daughter of the troupe** is a Marked character who has taken the name. The mod's *Marked* trait marks trans women, fictional by construction. The narrator never says the historical *mukhannathūn* were trans women. The troupe's women may say what they are in their own voice.

**Onward roads.** A daughter may hear the call and go west to the Sisters' House at Dorylaion and the knife. That needs the call's region list to reach Iraq; at present it covers "Byzantium and Anatolia". Or she may stay. Both must be real choices, and staying must not be framed as failure.

### 2.6 Event ideas

Each event is grounded. **"Hire" contracts need new script** (see §2.7).

1. **The Lute Strings** (464/1072). A singer the troupe plays with comes home with her strings cut. Options:
   - hide her;
   - take her to the Turk's house, the patron, who will retaliate;
   - go to the sharīf.
   
   The text never cheers the riot.
2. **Paraded on Donkeys** (467/1075). The *muḥtasib*'s order. The troupe is not named in it, but the street does not distinguish. Options:
   - cross to the west bank;
   - take the road to Hamadan;
   - stay and keep quiet.
3. **Whose Mother Are You?** The naming gate, in the old joke's words (al-Ābī 5:185), taken seriously by the troupe.
4. **Soft by Birth** (al-Sarakhsī 10:158; 16:131). A daughter witnesses a theft, and the qadi asks whether her softness is "by birth" or "in vile acts". Options:
   - say "by birth", and be believed, at the price of disowning the troupe's craft before the court;
   - stand with the troupe, and have her testimony refused.
   
   This is the jurists' own distinction as a choice.
5. **The Wall Is Lit** (cf. 441/1049; 488/1095; 532/1137). The city celebrates. The troupe walks with drums and "instruments of mimicry". It brings gold and prestige, plus a chance that a Hanbali preacher names them.
6. **Bring the Drums** (455/1063, Hamadan). Armed men who have just killed want music over the dead. Options:
   - refuse, at a risk;
   - play, with stress.
   
   Grim, and rare. The event must show who pays.
7. **The Women's Broker** (Jāḥiẓ via al-Rāghib 2:431; al-Ābī 5:192). A family asks the *ustādha* to find a bride. A lawful brokering contract, with the attested risk of being "taken to the ruler" if it goes wrong.
8. **The Lament** (Rowson 1991a, p. 683; al-Qudūrī p. 220). A household hires the troupe to mourn. "Alas, Ḥaram!" (al-Rāghib 2:369) can be the line when a troupe member dies.
9. **Met with Tambourines and Spindles** (*tashhīr*; Rowson 2003, p. 64). A lord orders the troupe to shame his captives. Refusing costs coin and favour. Obeying costs stress and the troupe's own regard.
10. **The *Kūba* Forbidden** (from 1095). A preacher's ruling reaches the market. Options:
    - put away the *kūba* and play the permitted frame drum (al-Ghazālī allows the *duff*, Macdonald p. 237);
    - keep it, as a mark of who they are.
11. **The Veil Pun** (*Hafawāt* §291). A Daylami or Turkish officer takes a pun as an insult. The troupe's wit can save an official, or sink him.
12. *(Adelphai only)* **Medina, 717.** Theodote al-Rūmiyya tells the troupe the Sisters keep the Medinan singers' day. The troupe's answer is its own. The Adelphai claim is voiced by Theodote, never by the narrator.

**Reuse.** Rowson's existing event pack (`isl_rowson_gender_irregularity.md` row 4: the beard plucked overnight, the barber, "caliph or marriage-broker") can be widened to 1066 as the same troupe's remembered stories.

### 2.7 Mechanics: what carries over and what is new

**Carries over unchanged:**
- the Marked trait;
- *Declare Before Witnesses* (the gate's core);
- *Take as Daughter* (the *ustādha* adopting a pupil);
- the arrivals engine, limited to Marked characters of Islamic faiths in Iraq and Jibal;
- the existing Islamic reaction content (`tm_isl.0001`, `tm_isl.0002`; `tm_islamic_daughter_opinion`), which may need its trigger widened to troupe daughters who are not of a mod faith.

**Vanilla to use:**
- *Roads to Power* landless adventurer government, with a start-date camp title as `d_laamp_suleyman` does: `common/landed_titles` with `landless = yes` and `require_landless = yes`, and `history/titles` with `landless_adventurer_government`;
- the camp officers `master_bard_camp_officer` and `stooge_camp_officer`;
- the court positions `court_musician_court_position` and `court_jester_court_position`, for patrons.

**New script:**
- **Contracts** (`common/task_contracts`): play the wedding or feast, walk the procession, mourn, broker a match, shame the captives (refusable).
- **The gate's three-step chain.**
- **A troupe variable** marking the camp, so arrivals, the call and Islamic reaction can find it.
- **A DLC-gated bookmark entry** with a non-DLC fallback.
- **A loc pass.** Names include ʿayn and long vowels: run `tools/check_glyphs.py`, since the interface font lacks some dotted letters (ṭ ḥ ṣ ḍ). Use plain "Rayhana", "Nasima" etc. in UI strings.

**Not needed:** a new faith, doctrines, holy sites, a culture, re-embodiment, Sisters' Houses (they belong to the faiths), or *Silk & Silver*.

**Growth.** The troupe does not convert anyone. It gains members only through Marked arrivals and the gate, at a slow rate, with no visible cap.

### 2.8 1066 or 1178

**1066 only.** At 1178:
- Rayy and Hamadan are Ildegüzid;
- Baghdad's caliph is independent;
- the voice of the city is Ibn al-Jawzī preaching in the palace mosque;
- al-Ghazālī's ruling on the *kūba* is two generations old.

The troupe does not vanish: *mukhānīth* still appear in 517 and 532 AH (17:216, 327). But the start's premise, a public craft under military patrons, is the 1060s one. Its clock (1069–77) runs out well before 1178. At 1178 use the troupe only as remembered flavour in reaction text.

## 3. Attested / reconstructed / invented

**Attested in the sources read.**
- *Mukhannathūn* as a recognised, corporate category of not-men in Iraq and Iran through the eleventh century.
- Feminine names; the *Umm* kunya; feminine address; self-chosen and group-given names.
- Masters and pupils; the bag and the argot; pride in "the craft".
- Drums, including a *mukhannath* drum, and al-Ghazālī's *kūba* "custom of the *mukhannathūn*".
- Mass public appearances at Baghdad (945, 1049, 1095, 1137) and Hamadan (1063).
- Brokering, mourning and comic insult as trades.
- Testimony barred and exile prescribed; the Ḥanafī split between "soft by birth" and "in vile acts".
- The Hanbali campaigns of 1069–77 and their targets.
- The separation of *mukhannath*, *khunthā* and eunuch in law and in jokes.
- A veil (*miqnaʿa*) as a *mukhannath* marker in Buyid Baghdad.
- Ibn ʿAbdūn's expulsion of the *ḥiwā* from Seville, including from weddings.

**Reconstructed.**
- A single named troupe moving between Baghdad, Hamadan, Rayy and Isfahan in the 1060s. The pieces are attested in each city, but not the circuit.
- The *ustādha* as head and broker. *Ustādh* is attested for a teacher; leadership of a troupe is not.
- Weddings in Iraq in the window, inferred from Medina and Seville.
- The *kūba* as the troupe's own drum before al-Ghazālī wrote.
- The naming gate's three steps as one sequence.
- Turkish military households as the main patrons in 1066, inferred from the 464 and 469 entries.

**Invented.**
- Every starter character and every name in §2.3.
- The *Marked* reading: that some troupe members are trans women. This is the mod's fiction, and the narrator never extends it to historical people.
- The troupe's meaning of "Umm" as earnest.
- Theodote and any Adelphai link.
- The contract system as a model of hire.
- The choice not to give the troupe a faith is a design decision, not a finding. Nothing here claims that historical *mukhannathūn* lacked inner religious lives; the hostile sources simply do not show them.
