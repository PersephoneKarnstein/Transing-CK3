## 1. The argument in brief

1. **Ghana in 1067–68 is one of the best-described courts in medieval sub-Saharan Africa.** It is second-hand, from Córdoba, but it is dated to the year. It has the institutions Badenya rests on: inheritance by the sister's son with a dated case, and a guarded grove of ritual specialists beside the king's town. Muslims hold the court's offices of speech and money.
2. **Al-Bakrī names no royal woman.** The king's sister exists only as the heir's mother implied by the succession rule. The faith's claiming mother at Ghana is therefore invented, but her place in the court is structurally attested.
3. **No bard is attested at Ghana.** The court's speech runs through **Muslim interpreters**, and the audience opens with a drum. The Soninke word for a bard, *gesere*, is attested only in 20th-century sources. Castes among the Soninke are dated "no later than 1500" (Tamari 1991, abstract). The gate's third step must change.
4. **The Soninke have a ready-made "mother's children" word**, *maremmu*. A 1980 handbook gives it as the general term for "relative" (Kendall et al. 1980, p. 222). It is LATER and dialectal, and a speaker must check it before it goes into loc.
5. **Archaeology has not found al-Ghāba.**
   - Koumbi Saleh is a large stone town with a mosque, urban from the 11th century.
   - Whether it is al-Bakrī's Muslim town is disputed. The king's town and its groves have never been located.
   - A recent synthesis says Koumbi was not Ghana's capital (Fauvelle 2018, as summarised by Claessen 2020).
6. **1076–77 rests on one sentence of al-Zuhrī.** It says the people of Ghana became Muslim in 469/1076–77. It names no battle, and one edition reads 496 rather than 469. The "Almoravid conquest of Ghana" is a modern construction, completed by Delafosse in 1912 (Masonen and Fisher 1996). The start must not script it.

## 2. Al-Bakrī on the royal town (1067–68)

Al-Bakrī wrote in al-Andalus and never went south. He compiled from earlier writers and from merchants' reports. His informants for this passage are not named in what I read. Which parts come from older written sources is **unverified**. He dates the reigning king to 460/1067–68: "Le souverain qui les gouverne actuellement, en l'an 460" (de Slane p. 327).

### 2.1 Title, country and king
- "Ghana" is the **royal title**. The country is "Aoukar" (de Slane p. 327).
- The present king is Tenkamenîn (Tunka Manīn), on the throne since 455/1063 (p. 327).
- His predecessor Beci (Basī) took the throne at 85 and was praised for justice and "son amitié pour les musulmans" (p. 327).
  - Basī went blind before he died and hid it with his viziers' help, who coached him in coded speech (pp. 327–328). That is an attested court practice of whispered, coded speech to the king, and it could carry an event.
  - The modern Wikipedia article "Tunka Manin" gives the praise to Tunka Manīn. In de Slane it belongs to Basī.

### 2.2 Succession through the sister's son
- "Beci était l'oncle maternel de Tenkamenîn" (p. 328).
- Custom requires that the king's successor be his sister's son, because the king is certain his nephew is his sister's child but cannot be sure of his own son (p. 328). This is al-Bakrī reporting what "they say". It is the court's own stated reason, not his.
- The heir apparent appears again in the clothing rule: only the king and "his heir apparent (who is the son of his sister)" may wear sewn clothes (L&H p. 79–80 excerpt; de Slane p. 329).
- **A son outside the line.** "Canmer, fils de Beci", king of the town of Alouken, "said to be a Muslim who hides his religion" (de Slane p. 335; my translation, kept short). He is the late king's own son, so he is excluded by the sister's-son rule, and he is said to be a secret Muslim. He is PERIOD and historical. He can be a neighbour, but never one of the faith's own.

### 2.3 The two towns and the grove
- The city has **two towns** on a plain. The Muslim town has twelve mosques, salaried imams and muezzins, jurists and scholars (L&H excerpt; de Slane p. 328).
- The king's town lies six miles away. It is called "El-Ghaba « la forêt, le bocage »" (de Slane p. 328), and the land between the two is continuously settled.
- Houses are of stone and acacia wood. The king has a palace and domed dwellings inside an enclosure "like a city wall" (de Slane p. 328, "une clôture semblable à un mur").
- Near the king's court of justice is a mosque for Muslims who come on missions to the court (L&H excerpt; de Slane p. 328).
- **The grove.**
  - Around the king's town are "domed buildings and groves and thickets where the sorcerers of these people, men in charge of the religious cult, live" (L&H excerpt).
    - Hopkins's English says **men**. De Slane says only "les magiciens de la nation, chargés du culte religieux" (p. 329). Whether the Arabic specifies men is **unverified**.
  - The idols and the kings' tombs are there. Guards keep anyone from entering or learning what happens inside (L&H; de Slane p. 329).
  - **The king's prisons are there too:** "dès qu'un homme y est enfermé, on n'entend plus parler de lui" (de Slane p. 329).
- Al-Bakrī's term for the cult is *dakākīr*, idols (de Slane p. 330; the singular *dakkūr* at Takrur, p. 324). That is his word, a hostile outsider's.

### 2.4 Muslims at the pagan court
- "The king's interpreters, the official in charge of his treasury and the majority of his ministers are Muslims" (L&H excerpt; de Slane p. 329).
- Masonen and Fisher note that the *Corpus* places Tunka Manīn's "many Muslim councilors" at pp. 79–80 (n. 165, p. 231).
- Devisse speculated that relations soured after Basī, the friend of Muslims, died in 1063 (via Masonen and Fisher p. 218). That is a hypothesis, not a source.

### 2.5 Dress and gender marking
- Followers of the king's religion other than the king and heir wear unsewn wrappers of cotton, silk or brocade, according to their means (L&H; de Slane p. 329).
- "All of them shave their beards, and women shave their heads" (L&H). This is the only line in the Ghana passage about women in general.
- "The king adorns himself like a woman (wearing necklaces) round his neck and (bracelets) on his forearms" (L&H; the brackets are the translator's). De Slane has "comme les femmes, avec des colliers et des bracelets" (p. 329).
  - **What it shows:** the comparison implies that **women at Ghana wore necklaces and bracelets**. That is attested, by inference, of women.
  - **What it does not show:** it is an outsider's description of regalia. It is not evidence that the king crossed gender. Neither the narrator nor the faith may claim the king.
- The king wears a high gold-decorated cap wrapped in a turban of fine cotton (L&H).

### 2.6 The audience and the drum
- The king hears grievances in a **domed pavilion** ringed by ten horses in gold cloth.
  - Behind him stand **ten pages** with gold-mounted shields and swords.
  - On his right sit the sons of the vassal kings, their hair plaited with gold.
  - The governor of the city and the ministers sit on the ground before him.
  - Pedigree dogs in gold and silver collars guard the door (L&H; de Slane pp. 329–330).
- "The audience is announced by the beating of a drum which they call *duba*, made from a long hollow log" (L&H). De Slane spells it *deba* (p. 329).
- The king's co-religionists "fall on their knees and sprinkle dust on their head". Muslims greet him "only by clapping their hands" (L&H).

### 2.7 Royal burial (de Slane p. 330; not in the excerpt)
- A great dome of *sādj* wood is raised on the burial place. The king's body lies inside on a couch, with his ornaments, his weapons, and his dishes of food and drink.
- **Several of his cooks and drink-makers are shut in with him.**
- The crowd heaps earth over the dome into a great mound, ringed by a ditch with one way in. Sacrifices and intoxicating drinks are brought to the dead.
- This is PERIOD evidence of retainer burial. It matches vanilla Bidaism's `tenet_human_sacrifice` (see §6.1). Badenya must not adopt it (§9).

### 2.8 Law and the ordeal (de Slane pp. 335–336)
- In Ghana, someone who denies a debt, or is accused of murder or another crime, is brought before the provost and made to drink water steeped with a bitter wood. If he vomits it, he is cleared.
- The *Corpus* page for this is cited as pp. 83–84 in Wikipedia's "Ghana Empire" (**unverified**).

### 2.9 The ring of converted neighbours
- **Takrur.** Its king Wār Jābī (d. 432/1040–41) became Muslim and imposed the law (de Slane p. 324).
- **Silla.** Converted by Wār Jābī. Its king "fait toujours la guerre" on unbelievers and can hold his own against Ghana (pp. 324–325).
- **Awdaghust.** In 446/1054–55 the Almoravids under Ibn Yāsīn stormed it. They seized everything as lawful booty and treated its people with great severity "parce qu'elle reconnaissait l'autorité du souverain de Ghana" (pp. 317–318). The same passage records the rape of its women. That fact goes to the narrator only and must not become content.
- **Malal.** Its king turned Muslim after a guest's rain-prayer ended a drought. He then had "briser toutes les idoles de ses états et expulser les magiciens", while "la masse du peuple est encore plongée dans l'idolâtrie" (pp. 333–334). This is a conversion narrative, and its idiom is drought and rain.
- **Anbara.** Its king Tārem resists Ghana (p. 335).

| Ghana's polity c. AD 400–1100; Ghana is not one city | Gestrich and MacDonald 2018 MS p. 2; Gestrich 2019 abstract | read (MS) |

**What al-Ghāba is thought to be.**
- The name is Arabic, "the forest", al-Bakrī's informants' word, not a Soninke place-name.
- It means a royal enclosure with domed houses, a court of justice and a guest mosque, set among sacred groves holding the priests, idols, royal tombs and prison.
- It has not been located. If Koumbi is the Muslim town, al-Ghāba lies about 10 km off and is undiscovered. If Koumbi is not Ghana at all (Fauvelle via Claessen), the whole double city is unlocated.
- Claessen's own explanation, that "no traces are left" because the Almoravids destroyed it (p. 184), repeats the conquest story this note rejects (§5).

**For the game this is good news.** Vanilla's `c_awkar` already contains `b_al-ghaba` and `b_kumbi` as separate baronies (`00_landed_titles.txt` l. 59481 ff.). The mod can seat the House at `b_al-ghaba` without taking a side in the location debate.

## 4. Soninke terms and institutions

All of this is **LATER**: a 1980 Peace Corps handbook from Guidimaxa (Mauritania/Mali/Senegal border), written for learners, and a 20th-century lexicon. None of it is evidence for 1066. It gives the mod Soninke rather than Maninka words, and a speaker must check each one before loc.

### 4.1 An equivalent of *badenya*
- ***maremmu***: "The term for relative in general is 'maremmu' which means 'mother's children'" (Kendall et al. 1980, *Culture*, p. 222).
  - The handbook adds that Soninke trace descent through the father, and asks students to find out whether children of one mother share a special bond (p. 222). That is the same tension Koné describes for Maninka *badenya* (faith page).
  - Morphology, as I read it: *ma* "mother" + *remmu* "children" (the *Grammar* gives *remme* "child, son", plural *remmu*). **Unverified** by a speaker.
- **Kin terms** (*Grammar*, Lesson Six table):
  - *ma* "mother, mother's sister(s)";
  - *kaawe* (address *kaawu*) "mother's brother";
  - *baaye* "father's sister";
  - *faabe* "father, father's brother(s)";
  - *gide* "older sibling", with *gidan-yaxare* "sister".
- **"Namesake of my mother."** The polite way to address a mature Soninke woman you don't know (*Culture*, c. p. 26).
- **Names.** *Toxo* is the given name and *jammu* the father's family name. Women keep their father's *jammu* after marriage (*Culture*, p. 16).
- ***Gurabaguraba*, the Sweeping of the Uncles' Places** (*Culture*, calendar lesson, c. pp. 108–116).
  - On 6 Xasaane, sisters' children sweep their mothers' brothers' homes.
  - The uncles give money, cloth, or a daughter in betrothal.
  - Women announce the day by beating calabashes upturned on water jars.
  - It sits inside an Islamic lunar calendar and is 20th-century. As a 1066 event it is reconstruction only.

### 4.2 Smiths and the *nyaxamala*
- The handbook's casted people, *nyaxamala* (cf. Maninka *nyamakala*), are "intermediaries among all the other people" (*Culture*, p. 236). It lists:
  - *jaare*, "master of words";
  - *gesere*, who "play instruments… have guitars and they sing";
  - *tago*/*tage*, blacksmiths, who also work silver and gold, and whose women make pottery;
  - *garanke*, leatherworkers (pp. 236–238).
- It gives the endogamy rules and which group may demand gifts of which (pp. 237–238).
- It lists *jammu* for each group: for example, Cissé and Sylla among the *gesere*, Kanté among smiths. **The mod must not attach any living *jammu* to a fictional casted character.**
- **Dating.** Tamari 1991 (abstract) puts castes among the Malinke "no later than 1300", and among the Soninke "no later than 1500". In 1066 a smith caste at Ghana is not attested. The grove's specialists are never called smiths.
- **Archaeology.** At Tongo Maaré Diabal (5th–11th century, on Ghana's margins), iron was made on a "proto-industrial scale". In the contemporary Inland Niger Delta, metallurgists "appear to have been a small minority, and spatially clearly separated" (Gestrich and MacDonald 2018 MS, pp. 32–33).
  - So **specialist smiths living apart** is a plausible inference for 11th-century Ghana's hinterland. Their sacral role is not attested.

### 4.3 The *gesere* and the Wagadu legend: dates
- **Gesere.** The handbook (1980) is the firmest source I saw.
  - Frobenius's "Gassire's Lute" was collected in 1909 from a Soninke informant in Borgu, not the Sahel, and published 1921. It is often glossed as an origin tale of the *gesere* (Wikipedia, citing Belcher 1999).
  - Belcher judges the Frobenius texts suspect unless independently confirmed, and "Gassire" never was (via Wikipedia).
  - **I found no attestation of a Soninke bard before the 19th–20th centuries.** Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's bards of 1352 are at Mali, not Ghana.
- **The Wagadu legend** (Dinga, the serpent Bida, the yearly maiden, the killing of Bida and the dispersal):
  - first published 1879 (Bérenger-Féraud, *Les peuplades de la Sénégambie*, pp. 169–172);
  - first identified with Arabic "Ghana" by Tautain in 1895 (Masonen and Fisher p. 227, n. 96);
  - further versions in Frobenius (1909 collection), Monteil (1953, not read) and Belcher 1999 (pp. 79–80 via Wikipedia). One of these has Bida give four drums, and the gold drum makes the king.
  - Conrad and Fisher 1983 (p. 55, via Wikipedia) record that in some versions Dinga made a deal with Bida, a maiden a year for rain and gold.
  - **Grade: LATER**, recorded 800 years after 1066.
  - Farias rejected reading the killing of the snake as a coded memory of the Almoravid conquest (via Masonen and Fisher p. 218).
- **Vanilla Bidaism** rests on this legend. Its description has the serpent "who demands regular sacrifices". The mod should treat the legend as later Soninke memory, never as 1066 practice (§9).

## 5. 1076–77: what can and cannot be said

- **Al-Zuhrī** (c. 1137) says the people of Ghana became Muslim in 469/1076–77 (L&H p. 98, via Masonen and Fisher pp. 217, 221 n., 223 n. 24).
  - He specifies prior *kufr*, "unbelief" (L&H pp. 98, 310, 389 n. 16, via Masonen and Fisher p. 225 n. 69). Al-Ḥulal later turned it into conversion "from Christianity".
  - Coulibaly (1974) stressed that al-Zuhrī "made no reference to any military victory" (Masonen and Fisher p. 217).
  - Hadj-Sadok's edition (1968) reads **496**, not 469 (Masonen and Fisher p. 230 n. 154).
- **The conquest.**
  - The conquest story was assembled from al-Ḥulal (1381), Ibn Abī Zarʿ, Ibn Khaldūn and Mármol by Cooley (1841).
  - It was made "canonical" by Delafosse, *Haut-Sénégal-Niger* (1912), in a passage that "has no footnote" (Masonen and Fisher pp. 199–211).
  - Conrad and Fisher found no Arabic source to 1500 that mentions a conquest (via Masonen and Fisher p. 219). Burkhalter (1992) criticised their method but "nowhere affirms that the conquest actually took place" (p. 219).
  - Hunwick and Devisse proposed Almoravid "influence" behind a Muslim faction instead. Masonen and Fisher find that equally undocumented (p. 218).
- **The oral sources** stress drought and the dispersal of Wagadu, not war (Conrad and Fisher 1983, via the regional note).
- **Al-Idrīsī** (1154) describes Ghana as a Muslim kingdom, whose king claims descent from the Prophet's kin (L&H p. 109, via Masonen and Fisher p. 221 n. and Wikipedia).
- **What the game may say:** by the 1130s an Arabic geographer believed Ghana's people had become Muslim in or about 1076–77. How it happened is unknown.

### 6.2 Seat
- **Seat: the king's court at `c_awkar`, with `b_al-ghaba` as the House's barony in flavour text.** The House holds no county.
- **Do not reuse `tm_seed_foothold_effect` on `c_awkar`.**
  - Its first line is `set_county_faith`, which would convert Ghana's capital county to Badenya.
  - `c_awkar` is Tunka Menin's capital, so the effect's vassal branch would not fire. The capital would flip faith and the king would stay Bidaic. That is far too much for a small faith (`feedback-growth-tuning`).
  - Use a **courtier-only seed**: create the starter characters as courtiers of the `c_awkar` holder, set no county faith, and register the seat.
- **Kirina** stays an eminent holy site. If a 1066 cell is wanted there, it should be one hidden courtier at Lahilatoul Kalabi's court, again without converting the county. The existing Sibi branch would create a Badenya countess at Sibi in 1066, because Sibi is not his capital. That is plausible only if Kirina's dual liege resolves sensibly, so **test it**.
- `awkar` is already a Badenya holy site (`tm_faith_types.txt` l. 257). Its loc can stay as written until 1076 (§6.6, event 7).

### 6.3 Starter characters (all fictional)

**Names.**
- Al-Bakrī gives only royal personal names and titles (Tunka, Ghana, Basī, Kanmar). There is no attested 11th-century Soninke female name.
- The names below come from vanilla's Soninke name list (`00_sahelian.txt`), choosing the non-Arabic ones. That list's own sourcing is **unverified**, and a Soninke speaker must check the names.
- No character gets a living casted *jammu* (Kanté, Sissoko, Kouyaté and so on). The royal characters may use vanilla's Cisse dynasty.

1. **Tekhaye, sister of the king** (about 40; Bidaic; Cisse).
   - The claiming mother, and mother of the heir apparent.
   - She is **not** Badenya. She is the court's most powerful potential claimer.
   - She is invented: al-Bakrī implies her role but never names her.
   - In vanilla she cannot be Tunka Menin's full sibling without inventing a mother for both. See open question 3.
2. **Sandaki, her son, the heir apparent** (about 12; Bidaic).
   - The only person besides the king who may wear sewn clothes.
   - Set as Tunka Menin's designated heir by script (§6.7). He is not a faith character.
3. **Gundo, a Daughter of the House** (50s; Badenya; she/her; `tm_daughter_of_the_house`).
   - In her youth she served the men of the grove. She left it at her crossing, because the grove's specialists are men (L&H's wording) and the grove admits no one.
   - She lives in the king's enclosure, not the grove, and keeps the House's dead.
   - She is the elder, and an aunt in the faith's sense to the younger daughters.
4. **Kabayo, a page of the shield** (17; Bidaic; Marked; she/her; the game codes her as male).
   - One of the ten pages who stand behind the king with gold-mounted shields (L&H).
   - She is the playable seeker. Her claim would come from Tekhaye.
5. **A smith-diviner from Do** (40s; Bidaic or Badenya-sympathetic; dynasty none).
   - He comes from outside the grove, because no smiths are attested in it. Do, across the river from Iresni, is the nearest non-converted kingdom (de Slane p. 333).
   - His wife is a potter (*Culture* p. 237, LATER), and could be a second claimer.
6. **Isḥāq b. Ḥasan, interpreter and vizier** (50s; Maliki; a Berber or Soninke Muslim from the Muslim town).
   - The hostile voice, from a post al-Bakrī says Muslims held (L&H).
   - He renders the court's speech for visiting Muslims, and he calls the grove's rites *kufr*. That is al-Zuhrī's word, put in a courtier's mouth.
   - He is a person with a view, not a villain, and he serves a pagan king faithfully.

**Optional historical neighbour:** Kanmar son of Basī, at Alūken, "said to be a Muslim who hides his religion" (de Slane p. 335). He is not a faith character, and no event may claim him.

### 6.4 How one becomes a daughter at Ghana (the gate)

Keep *Go to the Smith* and its three-party shape. Change the third step and tune the second.

1. **The smith's bargain** (`tm_badenya.0010`). Unchanged in mechanics.
   - Loc variant at Ghana: the smith comes from Do or the south, because the grove's men will not see her.
   - "Djinn" is an Islamic-Mande loanword. Whether a 1066 Soninke court would use it is an open question; a neutral "bush spirit" desc variant may be better.
2. **The claim** (`tm_badenya.0011`).
   - Prefer the **king's sister**, the heir's mother, as claimer. If she claims, the daughter gets +opinion with the heir. The flavour line: "Here the line already runs through her."
   - Desc word: *maremmu*, "mother's children", in the faith's voice, with a speaker check.
   - **The bracelets** (25 gold) gain a local anchor. At Ghana, necklaces and bracelets are what women wear, as al-Bakrī's comparison implies (L&H). The desc may say so of women. It may not say so of the king.
3. **The word: "Before the Drum"**, replacing "The Word That Cools" at Ghana (`tm_badenya.0012` with a court or location check).
   - **The setting.** No jeli is attested at Ghana; say so in the event. The *duba* opens the audience. She kneels and throws dust on her head as the king's co-religionists do (L&H). Her claiming mother speaks her name before the king. No daughter names herself.
   - **The word passes through the interpreter.** Speech at this court passes through Muslim interpreters (L&H), so Isḥāq or another Muslim interpreter renders her name for the visiting merchants. This replaces the jeli's paid praise or satire:
     - **Pay the interpreter well** (50 gold): he renders it plainly. +100 prestige.
     - **Pay what she can:** a coin toss between a plain rendering and a sly gloss that the merchants laugh at (−prestige, +stress).
   - This keeps the satire mechanic and grounds it in an attested Ghana office. It also makes the hostile voice part of the rite.
   - **Optional dress line:** only the king and his heir wear sewn clothes, so she is given an unsewn wrapper, and nothing is cut or sewn on her.
4. **The djinn returns** (`tm_badenya.0015`). Unchanged.

### 6.5 Pressures and hostile voices (all attributed)

- **The interpreter and viziers.** Muslim office-holders at a pagan court (al-Bakrī). His line: the grove is *kufr* (al-Zuhrī's word, c. 1137).
- **Al-Bakrī himself.** "Their religion is paganism and the worship of idols" (L&H). Usable only as attributed narrator context: "a geographer in Córdoba wrote…".
- **The Malal precedent.** A neighbouring king broke his idols and expelled "les magiciens" after a rain-prayer (de Slane pp. 333–334). For the grove's people, that is what conversion means.
- **The Almoravids.** They sacked Awdaghust in 1054–55 for recognising Ghana (de Slane pp. 317–318). In vanilla, Abu Bakr ibn ʿUmar holds `d_adrar` on Ghana's northern border. This is a pressure, not an antagonist nation, and no scripted war.
- **Silla's king**, at constant war with unbelievers (de Slane p. 324).
- **The grove as prison.** Those shut inside are never heard of again (de Slane p. 329). It is a sanctuary and a danger at once.
- **The ordeal.** The bitter-wood water (de Slane pp. 335–336), as a threat to a claimer or daughter accused of a crime.
- **Drought.** The Malal story's idiom, and the oral tradition's cause of Wagadu's end (Conrad and Fisher 1983, via the regional note).

### 6.6 Event ideas

1. **"The Blind King's Viziers"** (flavour, at start). The court remembers how Basī hid his blindness through coded words (de Slane pp. 327–328). It teaches that at this court the right word in the right ear is power, which sets up "Before the Drum".
2. **"The Sweeping"** (yearly, Ghana courts with a living sister's son as heir). The heir sweeps his mother's brother's house, and the uncle gives cloth or gold.
   - Grounded in *Gurabaguraba* (Kendall 1980, LATER) and projected back.
   - A daughter of the House may sweep with the sister's children if her claimer is the king's sister: +opinion with the king, and the House's place is confirmed.
3. **"Kanmar's Secret"** (once). Word comes from Alūken that the late king's son prays in secret (de Slane p. 335). The interpreter asks the House to keep quiet or to speak. No faith claim on Kanmar.
4. **"The Guards of the Grove"** (once, for Gundo or the player). A grove guard turns a daughter away. Options:
   - leave it: the House keeps its own dead outside the grove (ties to `tm_badenya.0030`);
   - ask the king for the right to bury a daughter at the grove's edge: prestige cost, piety gain.
   - **Never** retainer burial.
5. **"The Bitter Water"** (rare). A claimer is accused of debt and sent to the ordeal (de Slane p. 335). The House may pay the debt or let her drink. Keep it non-lethal.
6. **"Rain at Malal"** (drought years, from 1070). A Muslim guest offers to pray for rain, echoing al-Bakrī's Malal tale.
   - The king's choice is AI-weighted, not forced: by Muslim courtier count, drought, and opinion of the interpreter.
   - Conversion is possible, not scripted. If the king converts, fire event 7.
7. **"The Year Four Hundred Sixty-Nine"** (conditional). Only if Ghana's ruler actually converts, whenever that happens.
   - The House leaves the king's enclosure for Do or Kirina, carried by the existing mechanics.
   - The `awkar` holy-site loc switches to a past-tense variant: "where the heir was the sister's son, until the king prayed toward Mecca".
   - The desc cites al-Zuhrī's date as one writer's report, and notes the 496 variant in the design doc.
   - **No Almoravid army appears.**
8. **"The Drums of Wagadu"** (flavour, only after 1200 or never). Later tellers say Wagadu's kings won their drum from the serpent. Narrator-framed as tradition recorded from 1879. This is optional, and must leave out the maiden sacrifice.

## 7. Attested / reconstructed / invented

**Attested (PERIOD, al-Bakrī 1067–68):**
- the royal title "Ghana" and the country "Awkar";
- Tunka Manīn since 455/1063, after his maternal uncle Basī;
- the sister's-son rule and its stated reason;
- the two towns; al-Ghāba "the forest";
- the guarded groves with the cult's specialists (men, in Hopkins), the idols, the royal tombs and the prison;
- Muslim interpreters, treasurer and most viziers; a guest mosque;
- sewn clothes reserved to king and heir; women with shaved heads, and by implication wearing necklaces and bracelets;
- the *duba*/*deba* audience drum; kneeling and dust for co-religionists, clapping for Muslims;
- the ten pages with shields; the sons of vassal kings;
- domed burial with retainers;
- the bitter-water ordeal;
- Kanmar son of Basī, said to be a secret Muslim;
- Malal's conversion and the expulsion of its "magiciens"; Takrur and Silla converted; Awdaghust sacked 446/1054–55.

**Attested later, or by inference:**
- al-Zuhrī's conversion date, 469/1076–77 (c. 1137; variant 496);
- al-Idrīsī's Muslim Ghana (1154);
- castes among the Soninke "no later than 1500" (Tamari);
- specialist ironworkers living apart from others in the 1st-millennium Middle Niger (archaeology).

**LATER (recorded 1879–1980):**
- the Wagadu legend: Dinga, Bida, the drums;
- *gesere*, *jaare*, *tago*, *nyaxamala*;
- *maremmu* "mother's children"; *kaawe*, *baaye*;
- "Namesake of my mother";
- *Gurabaguraba*.

**Reconstructed (the mod's reading of attested institutions):**
- the king's sister as the House's claiming mother;
- the House living in the king's enclosure beside, not inside, the grove;
- the word spoken at the drum audience and rendered by the interpreter;
- the bracelets read as women's ornaments at Ghana.

**Invented:**
- that anyone assigned male crossed to womanhood at Ghana;
- the House and its gate;
- every starter character and name;
- Gundo's past in the grove;
- the "Before the Drum" coin toss;
- the Sweeping as a 1066 rite;
- the conditional "Year 469" chain.

## 9. Content to keep out

- **Retainer burial** (de Slane p. 330) and vanilla's `tenet_human_sacrifice`. Badenya's burial is the House burying its own, never servants with a king.
- **The Wagadu maiden sacrifice**, and the Sia episode in Frobenius, which involves sexual deception and a death from shame. Neither is to become content.
- **The rape at Awdaghust** (de Slane pp. 317–318). Narrator context at most.
- **Al-Bakrī's Iresni goats and the Bekem women** (pp. 333–334). Outsider exoticism; do not use.
- **Any reading of the king's regalia as gender crossing.**
- **Any living *jammu*** on a fictional casted character, and any suggestion that today's Soninke *nyaxamala* descend from or are the faith.
