# New starts at 1066 and 1178: the Islamic world

Region: al-Andalus, the Maghrib, Fatimid then Ayyubid Egypt, Abbasid Iraq, Seljuq Iran, Khurasan and Transoxiana.
Written 2026-10-20 for the Silk & Silver question. Nothing in the repo was edited.

**Headline.** Neither 1066 nor 1178 justifies a new *faith* start in this region. The region's gender order does not move toward anything a community of trans women could live inside. It moves away. Between 1066 and 1178 every institution that gave gender-crossing people a recognised place closes or hardens:
- **The Fatimid palace.** It was dismantled in 1171, and its men and women were deliberately separated.
- **The taifa courts.** The Almoravids ended them, and Seville then legislated expulsion (c. 1100).
- **The Abbasid demimonde.** It was already in decline, and by the 1170s it faced Hanbali and Ayyubid-era moral policing.
- **The Sufi antinomians.** The only "new" gender-transgressive movement, they post-date 1178.

So:
- **1066:** one hub is worth an *optional* starter set, Fatimid Cairo under Rasad and al-Mustanṣir. A second, the Iraqi-Iranian *mukhannath* demimonde, is worth flavour. Neither is a new faith.
- **1178:** nothing in the region deserves a start. It is a "worse" date, and that should show in reaction text.

The test asks whether something alters a faith's premises. Nothing here does for Ha-N or Badenya. For Adelphai the Islamic neighbour changes character between the dates: courtly at 1066, military and Sunni-revivalist at 1178. That is reaction-text work, not a new start.

## 2. Candidate hubs

Ratings are honest. "Hub for trans women" is a stronger claim than "place where a gender-crossing role existed". None of these sources say anyone lived *as a woman*. The faith may claim them in its own voice; the narrator may not.

### A. Fatimid Cairo, 1066 (al-Mustanṣir; the queen mother Rasad). Rating: **plausible as a court niche, thin as a hub.**

- **Institutions.**
  - Eunuch *ustādhs* held the police and the *ḥisba* (El-Azhari 2019, pp. 269–270).
  - The *muḥannak* rank had "more than a thousand" members, and the caliph attended its promotion ceremony (pp. 273, 277).
  - There were eunuch battalions (pp. 266–267).
  - Eunuchs were "viewed not with contempt but with esteem during most of the Fatimid period" (p. 278).
- **Women in power through eunuchs.**
  - Rasad sat behind a curtain in council and "would send out a eunuch who would always support the opinion of" her vizier (El-Azhari p. 208).
  - Sitt al-Mulk had ruled through the eunuchs Nassim and Miʿdad in 1021 (pp. 204–205, 260).
  - El-Azhari calls this "the rise of the first queens of Islam" (ch. 4 title).
- **Gender category.** Tolino 2018 (pp. 246–267, read 2026-10-26; `isl_tolino_eunuchs.md`) argues that Fatimid eunuchs were "not understood as a 'third gender'" but "gendered according to the different contexts in which they acted or were represented" (p. 248). That is the best fit in the region for the mod's anti-third-sexing line: the sources gender each eunuch by context. (An earlier wording here read more into Tolino than she says.)
- **Festival.** The Fatimids celebrated Nawruz "with tremendous jubilation", and caliphs had at times to "rein in their subjects" (Virani 2020, pp. 456, 461, citing Shoshan 1993, pp. 40–45, and Sanders 1994, p. 83). Shoshan's chapter is titled "a world turned upside down". *Unverified:* whether it records cross-dressing at Fatimid Nawruz. I could not see the chapter. Check before use.
- **Why only "plausible".** These are eunuch and slave institutions. They are Adelphai Dorleyaiou's ground, and the project already rejects the eunuch estate as a cage (`book_bhatt_third_sex.md`). There is no evidence of anyone living *as a woman* in them.

### B. The *mukhannath* demimonde of Baghdad, Rayy and Isfahan, 1066. Rating: **plausible (the region's strongest attested gender-crossing *corporate* role), but contemptuous.**

- **The texts are eleventh-century Iranian.** Rowson's 1991 vice-list chapter rests on three of them: al-Ābī (d. 1030, a vizier at Rayy), al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī, and al-Jurjānī (d. 1089). Rowson treats them as representative "from the ninth century to the present" (Rowson 1991b, pp. 72–73).
- **What the *mukhannathūn* in them do:**
  - they "address each other in the feminine gender";
  - one answers "I'm the Mother of Ahmad!" when asked "You are the Father of whom?" (p. 70);
  - their names are "virtually all" feminine from the ninth century on (n. 57, p. 78);
  - they have "a distinct identity (certainly corporate as well as individual)" (p. 72);
  - they play their own drum (p. 71) and keep "a kind of camaraderie with women ... not shared by other men" (p. 71).
- **The price.** "Public persons" of a demimonde "where ... fame [was] accessible, but respectability was emphatically not" (p. 72). They made "no attempt ... to 'pass' as a woman" (p. 70).
- **The legal seam, in Transoxiana, in the window.** The Ḥanafī al-Sarakhsī (d. c. 1090) wrote *al-Mabsūṭ* while imprisoned at Uzjand in Farghana, according to the usual biographies (*unverified*). He distinguishes the *mukhannath* "in evil acts" from one whose languor is "by way of gentle natural constitution, and who has no desire for women". Of that second kind, "some of our shaykhs would grant such a person license (*rakhkhaṣa*) to be with women" (Rowson 1991a, pp. 675–676 n. 34). This is the region's only legal text in the window that lets a feminine person into women's space on the strength of her nature. It is one clause, attributed to "some", and it still files her as a man harmless to women.
- **Why not "strong".** Rowson's verdict: they "joined the ranks of the not-male", and their status "sank noticeably under the Abbasids" (2003, p. 63). From the ninth century they were "always assumed to be sexually passive", and their gender role was read as cover for that (1991b, pp. 70–71). The material is a demimonde to depict, not a precedent to celebrate. `isl_rowson_categorization.md` row 3 already puts it as "a cage, not a precedent".

### C. The taifa courts of al-Andalus, 1066 (Seville under al-Muʿtaḍid; Córdoba; Toledo; Zaragoza). Rating: **thin.**

- **The basis is secondary.** Bollo-Panadero (2023, pp. 111–112), following Tahiri (*Las clases populares en al-Ándalus*, 2003, p. 131), says the caliphal and taifa periods "se mostraron tolerantes en la cuestión de la homosexualidad". She adds that homosexual men came "a tener sus propios barrios como el conocido con el nombre de derb Ibn Zaydun de Córdoba". This is third-hand; check Tahiri and his primary source. It concerns sexuality, not gender-crossing.
- **Women's legal standing.** Tahiri also reports that in caliphal and taifa Seville women had a specialised legal administration, *aḥkām al-nisāʾ bi-Ishbīliya* (Bollo-Panadero n. 25). *Unverified* beyond this citation.
- **Gender play in poetry.** The *muwashshaḥ* ends in a *kharja*, very often in a girl's voice and written by male poets. Wallāda bint al-Mustakfī (d. c. 1091) kept a literary salon at Córdoba. Both are standard knowledge; page cites *unverified* (check Zwartjes, *Love Songs from al-Andalus*, 1997). This is a literary convention of ventriloquising women. No institution, role or person living as a woman is attested.
- **Eunuch-founded taifas** (the ʿĀmirid *fityān* at Almería, Valencia and Tortosa, 1010s–30s) had passed to other dynasties by 1066 (Meouak 2004, not read; *unverified*).
- **No *mukhannath* was found in any Andalusi source in this pass.** The c. 1100 Seville term is *hāwī* (see §4).

### D. The eunuchs of the Prophet's tomb, Medina, 1178. Rating: **strong as an institution; thin as a hub for trans women.**

- **The foundation.**
  - A eunuch order was "established" by Saladin with endowments for 20 or 24 eunuchs (Ibn Farḥūn, in Marmon 1995, p. 42), c. 1171–93.
  - Named tomb eunuchs are already attested: Bayān in 1159 and Badr "the Weak" in 1174 (Marmon p. 48).
  - Ibn Jubayr saw "Ethiopian and Slavic eunuchs ... of elegant appearance" in 1184 (Marmon p. 33).
  - It had its own rules, ranks, novitiate and costume (`hist_marmon_eunuchs_islamic.md` §1).
- **Why thin.**
  - It is a slave priesthood of purchased cut boys, guarding a male-sacred boundary.
  - It is the best Islamic analogue for the Adelphai's Houses, and the furthest thing from a refuge.
  - Marmon's al-Subkī (14th c.) says eunuchs imagine themselves "a man" among women and "a woman" among men. That is later, and the jurist's voice.
  - Use the order as Adelphai reaction and event material ("a novice bought for Medina"), never as a hub.

### E. Seljuq Hamadan, 1178. Rating: **thin** (as a hub).

- It was the Iraqi Seljuq capital.
- In 1160 the eunuch commander Kurdbādhū had Sultan Sulaymān Shāh arrested and killed after the sultan had a jester expose his body (Ayalon no. 46; El-Azhari p. 337).
- It is also Baba Ṭāhir's and ʿAyn al-Quḍāt's city.
- Its value is to Ha-N (Esther's tomb) and to Adelphai reaction text, not as a hub.

### F. Fez and the Maghrib. Rating: **thin for 1066/1178.**

- The hostelry men of Fez are attested c. 1500: they dress and adorn themselves as women, shave, spin, keep house, lament with drums at funerals, cook for the army, and are barred from the mosques (Leo/al-Wazzān via Davis 2006, ch. 4 n. 29, ch. 7 n. 26).
- That is three centuries after both dates.
- Fez is Almoravid from c. 1069–70 and Almohad from the 1140s. Nothing links the c. 1500 corporation to either date.

### G. Sufi antinomians (*qalandar*, *malāmatī*). Rating: **not in the window. Drop.**

- **The Qalandars "emerged as a new and distinct group of dervishes in Damascus and Damietta during the early decades of the seventh/thirteenth century".** Their founder Jamāl al-Dīn Sāvī died c. 1232–33 (Karamustafa 1994, chs. 1, 4). That is after 1178.
- **The literary *qalandar* is earlier.** *Qalandariyyāt* poems were written by Aḥmad Ghazālī (d. 1126), ʿAyn al-Quḍāt Hamadānī (d. 1131), Sanāʾī (d. 1150–51) and Khāqānī (d. 1198–99) (Karamustafa ch. 3). That is a poetic stance (wine, gambling, "rejection of religion"), not a community.
- **The fourfold shave** (hair, beard, moustache, eyebrows) is the 13th-century Qalandar mark. Karamustafa reads it as renouncing honour and the Prophet's *sunna* (ch. 2), not as feminisation.
- **The *malāmatiyya*** belong to tenth-century Khurasan and are quietist (ch. 3).
- **The other strand is *shāhid-bāzī*,** contemplating the beardless youth as a witness of divine beauty. It is a homoerotic piety, and Ibn al-Jawzī attacks it at length in the 1180s–90s (*Talbis Iblis*, "Avoiding Beardless Men"). It is out of scope.

### Other leads checked

- **The *ghulāmiyyāt*.** Ninth to early tenth century: al-Amīn's reign, copied in 933 (El-Azhari p. 156). They were women dressed as boys by their masters (Rowson 2003, p. 51). Out of window, and out of scope as precedent. Do not use.
- **The *khunthā*.** Sanders shows eleventh-century Ḥanafī law (al-Sarakhsī) unchanged in substance through al-Marghīnānī (d. 1196): "minor variations" (Sanders 1991, n. 14). Alipour (AAR 2025 abstract) says some Mālikīs of the 11th–16th centuries treated the *khunthā mushkil* as a third category; that is abstract only. Omar 2025 (*JAOS* 145.2, pp. 401–418) argues that jurists "gendered sex along the axis of phallic penetration". So there is **no legal change between 1066 and 1178 that matters**. The law assigns ambiguous bodies and never moves a person across (Sanders p. 76: "could not be crossed legitimately simply by an act of will").

## 3. Start sketches (optional; neither is a new faith)

My recommendation: if anything is built, build **A as an Adelphai Dorleyaiou starter set at 1066**, and fold B into the existing proposed "Mukhannathun of Samarra" event pack, carried forward to 1066 and renamed. I would not build a sixth or seventh *Islamic* faith. The sources are all hostile or ambivalent. The only institution with any esteem (the Fatimid eunuch corps) is a slave estate. And inventing an Islamic heterodoxy of trans women from this evidence would mean reading a contempt-role as a community, which the Rowson notes already warn against.

### Sketch A: "Through the Curtain", Cairo 1066

- **Seat and holder.** No landed seat. The county of Cairo/Fusṭāṭ is held by the Fatimid caliph al-Mustanṣir (vanilla). The starter group are courtiers at his court, like Ha-N's unlanded women at Arles. The mod has that pattern already.
- **Who they are** (fictional, she/her):
  - **Sawsan al-Rūmiyya**, about 40, Rum-born, of the Adelphai. She was sold through Antioch as a boy and is now a *muḥannak* in the women's palace, where she serves as Rasad's curtain voice in council. Her name is attested as a eunuch name (El-Azhari pp. 148–149), and the Sisters keep it as a woman's name (El-Azhari row 6).
  - **Lu'lu'**, about 19, her adopted daughter (Take as Daughter), not yet through any rite. The gate is her choice, not her owner's.
  - **Bishāra**, about 55, a palace musician who keeps the palace's Nawruz music. Her Nawruz role is *unverified*; see §2 A. **Note (2026-10-26):** Nasab "the drummer-woman", rewarded by al-Mustanṣir in 1058, is attested (`mukhannathun_fatimid_cairo.md` §3.2); a palace drummer fits the sources better than a Nawrūz music-keeper.
  - **A hostile voice:** a fictional Sunni qadi of Fusṭāṭ who speaks Sanders's procedural line ("a castrated man, whatever she calls herself").
- **Pressures (in the window):**
  - the civil war between the Turkish and Black regiments, and the great famine and treasury looting of the later 1060s (standard narrative, e.g. Brett, *The Fatimid Empire*, 2017; pages *unverified*);
  - Rasad's party is tied to the Black regiments and loses;
  - the "market" that made them;
  - Sunni neighbours (the Seljuqs after Manzikert in 1071) who read them as *khadam*, never as women;
  - Badr al-Jamālī's military takeover in 1073–74 ends palace government. That is the start's clock.
- **The gate.** Keep the Adelphai rite as it is. The local touch is naming before the women's palace (Declare Before Witnesses). The precedent is Iqbāl al-Khātūnī's pride in bearing a woman's name, which is later, Aleppo 1236–42 (El-Azhari p. 353). Cite it only as the Sisters' example.
- **Mechanics that carry over:**
  - Adelphai doctrines and the knife houses;
  - Take as Daughter, Sister-Making, Declare Before Witnesses;
  - the proposed El-Azhari events: "Through the Curtain" (row 8), "Boys for Tarsus" (row 2, retargeted to Antioch and the Syrian ports);
  - the Islamic opinion design (courtier respected, ruler resented; El-Azhari row 3, Rowson categorization row 2).
- **At 1178:** drop. The Fatimid palace is gone. Qarāqūsh "separated the men from the women, so that they would not procreate, which will hasten their extinction" (Ayalon no. 54). There is nothing to start from.

### Sketch B: "The Mothers of the Drum", Baghdad and Rayy, 1066 (event flavour, not a start)

- **Seat and holder.** None. These are courtiers and town characters at the caliph al-Qāʾim's court (Baghdad) and at Alp Arslan's (Rayy/Isfahan).
- **Who** (fictional, she/her): a jester-drummer called **Umm Aḥmad** (the attested joke kunya, Rowson 1991b p. 70); a lamenter; a marriage broker. These are the attested *mukhannath* trades: drums, wit, mourning and matchmaking (Rowson 1991a, pp. 676, 683; 1991b, p. 71).
- **Pressures:**
  - the Hanbali "commanding right" campaigns in Baghdad in the 1060s–70s, which pressed the caliph to close brothels and taverns (led by Abū Jaʿfar al-Hāshimī, d. 470/1077–78; reported via Ibn al-Jawzī, *al-Muntaẓam*; pages *unverified*; check Cook 2000 and Makdisi 1997);
  - Miskawayh's "treatment, then penalties" (Rowson 2003, p. 64);
  - parades of shame (*tashhīr*, p. 65).
- **The gate.** Taking a woman's name and the *Umm* kunya before the troupe. This is Declare Before Witnesses again.
- **Recommendation.** Fold this into the existing Rowson event pack (`isl_rowson_gender_irregularity.md` row 4) and widen its date range from "867–c. 950" to the eleventh century. That is justified because Rowson's base texts are eleventh-century. No new start.

## 4. Counter-evidence: what closed or worsened

1. **Seville, c. 1100 (Almoravid).** Ibn ʿAbdūn's *ḥisba* orders that the *hāwī* "be expelled from the city, and punished wherever one of them is found". They are not to be allowed among Muslims or at festivals, being "fornicators, cursed by God and by everyone". This is my paraphrase of the García Gómez and Lévi-Provençal Spanish translation (1948, pp. 157–158), as quoted by Bollo-Panadero 2023, p. 111. The 1948 Spanish uses a slur; never reuse it in loc. *Hāwī* may mean either "homosexual" or "male prostitute" (translator's note, p. 57).
   - The same manual forbids women to "use coquetry when they are among themselves" (pp. 156–157, via Bollo-Panadero p. 111).
   - Bollo-Panadero reads the code as a deliberate break with taifa tolerance (p. 112).
2. **The Almohads.** Ibn Tūmart (d. c. 1130) attacked the Almoravids' veiled men and unveiled women as a gender inversion. He reportedly said of the emir's court, "I only see veiled women here", and struck the horse of ʿAlī b. Yūsuf's sister, who was riding unveiled. This is reported in Almohad sources and summarised in reference works; the scholarly page is *unverified* (check Bennison, *The Almoravid and Almohad Empires*, 2016, and Fierro).
   - The movement's founding rhetoric was a *gender-order* purification. That is the opposite of a seam.
3. **Cairo, 1171.** The Fatimid palace was abolished, and Qarāqūsh separated its men from its women "to hasten their extinction" (Ayalon no. 54). Sunni restoration followed.
   - Al-Shayzarī (d. 1193), a contemporary of Saladin, wrote the first Eastern *ḥisba* manual, *Nihāyat al-rutba* (trans. Buckley 1999). *Unverified:* whether it legislates on *mukhannathūn* or entertainers. Check Buckley.
4. **Baghdad, 1170s–90s.** Ibn al-Jawzī preached in the caliph's palace mosque. His *Talbis Iblis* condemns Sufi *samāʿ* and gazing at beardless youths, and calls clapping "an act of imitating women. A wise man elevates himself from imitating non-believers and women" (English trans., p. 369 of the archive.org PDF; translator and edition *unverified*).
   - *Unverified, from memory, check MacDonald, JRAS 1901–02:* al-Ghazālī (d. 1111) forbade the *kūba* drum as an emblem of the *mukhannathūn*. If confirmed, it shows the *mukhannath* drum becoming a marker of what is forbidden.
5. **The Seljuq courts castrated inside Islam,** for lust and punishment: Ṭughril and Khumārtakīn, al-Kundurī, Zengī's *ghilmān* (El-Azhari pp. 332–334; Ayalon nos. 40–41, 48, 64). Eunuch office in 1178 is a soldier's office (the atabegs), not a palace refuge.
6. **The mukhannath's long decline.** By Ibn al-ʿArabī's time (b. 1165) a Sufi master warns that *khadam* "are men", to be barred from women like "stallion" males (Ayalon §(f)). That closes even the Qur'anic "men without desire" door that al-Sarakhsī's shaykhs had opened.
7. **No later legal opening.** The law of the *khunthā* stays where Sanders found it.

## 5. Open questions

1. **Shoshan 1993, ch. 3 ("The festival of Nawrūz: a world turned upside down").** Does it attest masquerade or cross-dressing in *Fatimid* (not Mamluk) Cairo? This decides whether Sketch A gets a festival gate.
2. **Tolino 2018.** What exactly does "gendered according to context" mean for the *muḥannak* costume and the women's palace? Is it library-only?
3. **Tahiri 2003, pp. 125–131.** What is his primary source for "derb Ibn Zaydun" and for *aḥkām al-nisāʾ bi-Ishbīliya*?
4. **Ibn ʿAbdūn's Arabic** (pp. 57 and 157–158): *hāwī* against *mukhannath*. Davis 2006 links *hāwī* to Leo's "el cheva".
5. **Al-Ghazālī on the *kūba*** (MacDonald, JRAS 1901–02). Is the *mukhannath* link real?
6. **Al-Shayzarī, *Nihāyat al-rutba*** (Buckley 1999). Any clause on *mukhannathūn*, singers or women's gatherings c. 1180?
7. **Gesink 2018** and **Alipour and Gesink 2026, ch. 8.** Did eleventh- or twelfth-century jurists ever admit the "inclination of its nature" test that Sanders calls fringe (p. 78)? If any did, that is the only possible legal seam.
8. **Kecia Ali, *Sexual Ethics and Islam*** (2006; expanded 2016) was not read. Check its *mukhannath* and *khunthā* pages before any Islamic reaction text is finalised.
9. **CK3 1066 and 1178 history.** Who holds Cairo and Hamadan, and are Rasad and al-Mustanṣir in vanilla 1066 history? Check before scripting Sketch A.
10. **Benjamin of Tudela's page in Adler 1907** (57 or 81).
