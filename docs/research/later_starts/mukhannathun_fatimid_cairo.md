# The *mukhannathūn* of Fatimid Egypt: sources for the Adelphai's Cairo storyline (1066)

Written 2026-10-26 for the v2 start (branch `v2-1066`): an Adelphai Dorleyaiou set at al-Mustanṣir's court under the queen mother Rasad ("Through the Curtain", `region_islamic-world.md` §3 sketch A). **Revised the same day** after reading the books the user supplied. Their per-source notes hold the details and page numbers: `isl_tolino_eunuchs.md`, `isl_cortese_calderini_fatimid_women.md`, `isl_shoshan_popular_culture_cairo.md`, `isl_sanders_ritual_fatimid_cairo.md`, `isl_lev_state_society_fatimid.md`, `isl_moreh_live_theatre.md` and `isl_mukhannath_recent_studies.md` (court dance: `isl_kapitaikin_palatina_dancers.md`, added 2026-10-27). This note keeps the synthesis. It is the Egyptian companion to `mukhannathun_baghdad_1066.md`, which covers Iraq and Iran and is not repeated here. Three research passes were made: Fatimid chronicles, law and *ḥisba* manuals, and open-access scholarship. Raw texts and OCR stayed in the session scratchpad and are not in the repo. Quotes are 25 words or fewer. Translations marked *(trans.)* are ours. **Verified** means the passage was read in context in the edition named; **secondhand** means it was seen only through another author.

**Verdict in brief.**
- **What is attested.** Fatimid Egypt has a thin but real *mukhannath* record. One is a rich Baghdad-born broker and pipe-player with a house of singing slave-girls in Fusṭāṭ (murdered 1024). Another is paraded by the police for procuring (1024). A physician in Cairo c. 1050 claimed that Tinnīs "bore" two hundred *mukhannaths* a year. Zaknāda the *mukhannath* endowed a mosque in the Qarāfa in 1140.
- **The festival.** Al-Qāḍī al-Fāḍil, looking back on the Fatimids from 1188, says the *muʾannathūn* and loose women gathered under the Pearl Palace at Nawrūz "where the caliph watches them", with instruments (§3.1). Dress is not stated, and there is a textual variant.
- **What is not.** Nothing ties a *mukhannath* to the harem, the eunuchs, or Rasad. Nothing gives them feminine names or attests a guild. The palace storyline therefore rests on the eunuch and royal-women evidence (`isl_el_azhari_queens_eunuchs.md`), with the *mukhannath* world in Fusṭāṭ below it.
- **The law's seam.** The Fatimid code curses the *mukhannathūn* and orders them "out of your houses", but it punishes only receptive sex, not dress or manner. For a chaste daughter in the women's quarters, the threat is expulsion from the household, not death.

## 1. *Mukhannathūn* in Fatimid Egypt: the attestations

### 1.1 The broker-musician of Fusṭāṭ (Rowson 2003 n. 63). Verified
Al-Musabbiḥī, ed. Sayyid–Bianquis, **p. 104** (MS fol. 282b), in the obituaries for Tuesday 11 Shawwāl **415 / c. 15 December 1024**.
- **Name.** *al-mukhannath al-Baghdādī al-dallāl*, "the Baghdadi *mukhannath*, the broker". He is known only by origin and trade.
- **Death.** He had been missing four days when he was found killed.
- **Wealth and music.**
  - "He played the reed-pipe beautifully and had slave-girls in his house who sang; he was wealthy, with much money" *(trans.)*.
  - He brokered "goods, fine jewels and costly objects" *(trans.)*.
- **Youths.** He spent many dinars on beardless youths (*murdān*).
- **The killing.** Rumour named a son of the *qāḍī* Ibn Minhāl, "whom he loved" (the subject of the verb is ambiguous). The place was the house of Ibn Marzubān "the gambler".
- **Confiscation.** Since nothing more came out, "the *sulṭān* had the money, goods and singing slave-girls in his house carried off" *(trans.)*.

**Reading.** Forty years before 1066, a *mukhannath* could be a householder: rich, an immigrant from Baghdad, a broker of luxury goods and a skilled piper, and the master of a house of singing women. *Dallāl* (broker) is the same trade word Rowson finds in Iraq. The epithet "the Baghdadi" shows that *mukhannathūn* moved between Iraq and Egypt. The story ends in two things a design can use:
- the state's claim on a household with no recognised heir;
- violence from a lover of good family, with no one charged.

### 1.2 The procurer paraded by the police (Rowson 2003 n. 62). Verified
Al-Musabbiḥī, ed. Sayyid–Bianquis, **p. 68** (MS fol. 252b), Sunday 9 Shawwāl **415 / c. 13 December 1024**, at Fusṭāṭ. Rowson cites the same entry from Millward's edition, p. 187 (Rowson 2003 n. 62, **verified** in the endnotes, p. 72 of the volume).
- Ibn Kāfī al-Kutāmī, head of the lower police (*shurṭa suflā*), "beat a *mukhannath* alleged to procure five women in his house, and paraded him (*shahharahu*)" *(trans.)*.
- *zaʿama annahu* can mean "he claimed" or "it was alleged". No name or other detail is given.
- **The editors' n. 1 to p. 104** refers back to fol. 252b. That suggests the procurer and the murdered broker are one man. The text does not say so, and the dates sit awkwardly together: the broker had been missing four days by the 11th. Treat it as the editors' suggestion.
- **The parade (*tashhīr*), from the same volume** (p. 62, Ramaḍān 415). A thief is "beaten with whips, set on a camel and paraded round the city, with two bells in his hand" *(trans.)*, crying out his offence. Enforcement here is by the *shurṭa*, not the *muḥtasib*.

**Dating.** Both entries are 415 AH, not 414. The weekday of 9 Shawwāl is about Monday in 415 and Wednesday in 414 (tabular calendar, ± 1 day for the moon sighting), so only 415 fits the chronicle's Sunday. The entry also follows the ʿĪd al-Fiṭr on p. 67.

### 1.3 Tinnīs: "two hundred born every year" (c. 1050). Verified as quoted by al-Maqrīzī
- **Where:** *Khiṭaṭ* 1:479.
- **Chain:** Ibn Buṭlān, the Baghdadi physician in Egypt 1049–53 and rival of Ibn Riḍwān, quotes the physician Abū l-Sarī.
- **The claim:** the people of Tinnīs, the linen town on Lake Manzala, have natures inclined to "moistness and femininity". "Two hundred *mukhannaths* used to be born there every year; they love cleanliness, gentleness, singing and pleasure" *(trans.)*.
- **Attribution: two physicians, two claims.** The "two hundred born" line is Ibn Buṭlān quoting Abū l-Sarī (*Khiṭaṭ* 1:479, checked in the Arabic). Separately, his rival **Ibn Riḍwān**, al-Mustanṣir's chief physician, writes in his own treatise that the sea's humidity causes *taʾnīth akhlāq ahlihā*, "the feminizing of its people's characters" (Dols and Gamal 1984, p. 115; Arabic p. 19): a claim about the town's air, with no *mukhannath*, number or births (`isl_dols_ibn_ridwan.md`). Cortese and Calderini (p. 224) are right to give Ibn Riḍwān a Tinnīs remark. Both are Cairo physicians of Rasad's decades, so a court physician's climate theory of effeminacy is doubly attested.
- **Status:** a hostile theory of climate and temperament, written by an outsider, in Rasad's own decades. The verb is "born" (*yūladu*), so it treats the trait as inborn. Ibn Buṭlān's original wording was not seen.

### 1.4 Zaknāda the *mukhannath* (Qarāfa, 535/1140–41). Verified as quoted by al-Maqrīzī
- **Where:** *Khiṭaṭ* 4:870, from al-Jawwānī's late-12th-century list of mosques.
- **Text:** "Mosque of Zaknāda: … built by Zaknāda the *mukhannath*, after he repented, in the year 535" *(trans.)*. The Būlāq edition reads *Zankāda*.
- **Significance:**
  - It is the only named *mukhannath* of the Fatimid period.
  - It shows a person with the means to endow a mosque, whose trade name stayed on the building.
  - "After he repented" frames *takhannuth* as a way of life one could leave.

### 1.5 Festival crowds and neighbours (context)
- **ʿĪd al-Shahīd at Shubrā** (*Khiṭaṭ* 1:183). This was the Coptic Martyr's feast, when a relic was thrown into the Nile. No singer, entertainer, prostitute "or *mukhannath*" stayed home *(trans., paraphrase)*. This describes Mamluk practice up to its abolition in 1303, so it **cannot be dated to the Fatimids**.
- **Bayt Jibrīl**, in Fatimid Palestine, c. 985: a land of plenty "but it has declined, and it has many *mukhannathūn*" (al-Muqaddasī p. 174, *trans.*).

### 1.6 What is not attested for Egypt (all searched)
- No *mukhannath* at the palace or in the harem, no tie to the eunuchs, and none in Rasad's regency (1036–94).
- No feminine names or *kunyas* (the Iraqi material has them; `mukhannathun_baghdad_1066.md` §1.1).
- No troupe, guild or headman. The masked performers of the processions are **market guilds** (§3.1).
- No *kūba* drum in an Egyptian *mukhannath* context.
- **No *khawal*.** No medieval text searched uses it; the sources date it from the late 18th century, and it is best known for the dancers of 19th-century Cairo (Lane; Fortier 2025, p. 2). **Do not use it for 1066.**
- **No *zār*.** The spirit-possession rite, led mostly by women and important to some 20th-century Egyptian transfeminine people, came to Egypt from Ethiopia through the 19th-century slave trade (el-Hadidi 2016, via Botros 2023, p. 140; `isl_botros_asyut_transfemininity.md`). **Do not use it in 1066 or 1178 content** (user, 2026-10-27).
- **Al-Ḥākim's edicts (996–1021) never name *mukhannathūn*** (§2.4). Compare the ʿAbbasid caliph al-Qāhir, who "exiled the *mukhannathūn*" in 933 (*Nujūm* 3:239).

## 2. Law: what the Fatimid state and Egyptian jurists said

### 2.1 The Fatimid code: *Daʿāʾim al-Islām*, Book of *ḥudūd* (Fyzee 2:455, §§1597–1600; *Pillars* 2:456–457). Verified in Arabic and English
- **§1597.** The Prophet "cursed the *mukhannathūn* among men and said: Put them out of your houses" *(trans.)*. He also cursed *al-mudhakkarāt* among women and *al-muʾannathīn* among men. The text curses and excludes them from households. **It sets no penalty.**
- **§1598, ʿAlī:** desire for men is "cast upon" whoever willingly yields himself. Desire is treated as the consequence of the act.
- **§1599, ʿAlī, the only penal rule about effeminacy.** "If his speech and gait are women's, and he lets himself be penetrated as a woman is, stone him" *(trans.)*. The Arabic is **conjunctive**. Death is for receptive intercourse, with speech and gait only as its signs. **Dress and manner alone carry no penalty.**
- **§§1600–1603.** Both partners in *liwāṭ* are stoned, harsher than Ḥanafī law. Sex between women (*saḥq*) carries 100 lashes.

**Not a settled precedent.** The reports credit ʿAlī with several incompatible punishments for the passive partner (burning, stoning, a fall from a height), and Sunni jurists of the 11th century already called the Abū Bakr burning report broken in its chain (Omar 2025, pp. 473–474; `isl_mukhannath_recent_studies.md`). §1599 is the Ismaʿili code's choice among them.

**Reading for the design.** A daughter who is chaste and lives veiled in the women's quarters falls outside §1599, but she is inside §1597. The real threat at Rasad's court is **"put them out of your houses"**: expulsion from the household and the harem, and the loss of the patron's protection. It is not the sword.

### 2.2 Other *Daʿāʾim* rules. Verified
- **Gender imitation.**
  - Women must wear some ornament at prayer and "not imitate men" (2:162 §580).
  - The curse on *muʾannathīn* is repeated in the Book of Marriage (2:215 §796).
  - "Whoever imitates a people is counted among them" (2:513 §1838, in the Book of Testimony, *trans.*). The Sisters could take this up in their own voice.
- **Prayer.** No castrated man may lead entire men in prayer, nor may a woman or a *khunthā* (1:151). Eunuchs and *khunthā* are separate categories here, and *mukhannath* is not named.
- **Testimony** (2:511–512 §1836). The *mukhannath* is not named. Testimony is lost through the clauses on those who sit with singers, on suspicion and on the *fāsiq*, and **only "while they persist"**: repentance restores it.
- **Music** (2:206–207 §§754–757). The wedding drum is *required*: it is "the distinction between marriage and debauchery". The *kūba*, pipes and dancing are forbidden, and so are singing-girls. The *kūba* ban has no *mukhannath* link here.

### 2.3 Ismaʿili esoteric reading: *Taʾwīl al-Daʿāʾim* 1:299 (OpenITI pagination). Verified
Henna and jewellery are adornment for women and disgrace for men. The curse on gender imitators is then read **as an allegory of the ranks of the *daʿwa***: the pupil must not imitate the teacher, and "each rank should adorn itself with its own adornment" *(trans.)*.
- **For the design:** a real Ismaʿili move, in which gender difference becomes a metaphor for spiritual rank. A *dāʿī* character could argue about which "people" a daughter belongs to.
- **Status:** this use is invented, though grounded in the text. It belongs in a character's mouth, never the narrator's.

By 1066 the al-Ḥākim regime was fifty years past. The pattern to model is **cycles of crackdown and licence**, not a steady regime.

### 2.5 Egyptian Mālikī law (Ibn Abī Zayd, *Nawādir*). Verified
- **Exile, 14:237.** Ibn al-Mawwāz, citing Aṣbagh: *mukhannathūn* "are exiled only to a nearby place, not imprisoned, and let out … to beg and earn a living" *(trans.)*. This is the mildest exile in the record. A Fusṭāṭ case would be sent across the river to Giza, not to prison.
- **Slander, 14:346–348.** Calling a man "*yā mukhannath*" is full slander (80 lashes) only if he shows no softness of speech, no "women's work" and no ornament. A man whose softness of speech is "his nature" (*khalqatuhu*) cannot claim the slander. The jurists distinguish *mukhannath* in body or manner from *mukhannath* in the sexual sense, and they recognise **effeminacy by nature**.
- **14:370.** "So-and-so the *mukhannath* insults me" is heard as an ordinary court case between litigants.

### 2.6 *Ḥisba* manuals (12th–14th c., Syria and Egypt: after the window; use as flavour). Verified
- **al-Shayzarī p. 110** (Buckley pp. 126–127). A prostitute or singing-woman is first called to repent. If she returns, the *muḥtasib* gives her discretionary punishment (*taʿzīr*) and banishes her from the town, and "he does the same with the *mukhannathūn*" *(trans.)*.
- **The *taʿzīr* ladder, p. 109** (Buckley pp. 124–126). It runs from rebuke, through the whip, to the *ṭurṭūr* (a felt cap with coloured patches, shells, bells and tails) and a ride on a camel or donkey: the shaming parade.
- **Barbers, p. 88.** The bath barber must not shave "the beard of a *mukhannath*" *(trans.)*. Ibn Bassām p. 322 adds that he must not dress the temple-locks. The ban falls on the trade that would carry out the change.

**Correction to `mukhannathun_baghdad_1066.md`.** In al-Shayzarī's Arabic (al-ʿArīnī ed. p. 110), the ban on shaving the beard and entering among women falls on ***al-khunthā***, not *al-mukhannath*. The apparatus shows no variant. Buckley translates it as "an effeminate man". Ibn Bassām (pp. 383–384) has *al-mukhannath* in the same sentence. Ibn al-Ukhuwwa (p. 51) has *al-khunthā*. Whether this is a slide in vocabulary or a scribe's change is open.

### 2.7 Distinctions the jurists draw (summary)
- **Nature vs choice:**
  - the Mālikī "softness by nature" (*khalqa*);
  - Ibn Buṭlān's "born" *mukhannaths*;
  - the *Daʿāʾim*'s desire acquired by the act.
- **Manner vs act:** the *Daʿāʾim* punishes only the act. The Mālikī slander rule separates manner from sex.
- **Access to women:** "put them out of your houses" (*Daʿāʾim*); the *muḥtasib* forbids entry (Ibn Bassām).
- **Punishments, mildest to harshest:**
  - repentance;
  - a nearby exile with work allowed;
  - beating and parade by the police;
  - the *ṭurṭūr*;
  - confiscation of the estate at death;
  - stoning, for receptive sex only.

## 3. Festivals, court and city in 1066

### 3.1 Masquerade without cross-dressing
- **Effeminates at the Fatimid Nawrūz (retrospective). Verified in the Arabic.** Al-Qāḍī al-Fāḍil, writing in 584/1188, describes Nawrūz "in the days past and the departed dynasty": an elected **Amīr al-Nawrūz** rode out and mock-taxed the great houses, and "the *muʾannathūn* and the *fāsiqāt* gather under the Pearl Palace, where the caliph watches them, instruments in their hands" *(trans.)* (*Khiṭaṭ*, ed. Sayyid, 1:728). A hostile Ayyubid official describing the dynasty he served; undated within it, probably the 12th-century court he knew. **Būlāq agrees:** it also reads *al-muʾannathūn* (*Khiṭaṭ*, Būlāq 1853, 1:493, checked 2026-10-27 in the Princeton copy's OCR). The *al-mughannūn* variant once recorded here was not confirmed and is withdrawn. **Dress is not stated**, so this is effeminate performers at the festival, not attested cross-dressing. Moreh (p. 50) wrongly gives it to Ibn Zūlāq and 975.
- **The Pearl Pavilion stood in 1066, and the caliph lived there at Nawrūz.** Verified in al-Maqrīzī, *Khiṭaṭ* ed. Sayyid 2:528–532: built by al-ʿAzīz (Ibn Muyassar); demolished and looted on al-Ḥākim's order on 26 Rabīʿ II 402 / c. Nov. 1011 (al-Musabbiḥī); **rebuilt by al-Ẓāhir** (r. 1021–36), "after his father al-Ḥākim had demolished it" (Ibn ʿAbd al-Ẓāhir, *trans.*). Ibn al-Maʾmūn says the court lived there in the Nile-flood season "according to the first custom, before the vizierate of Badr and al-Afḍal" *(trans.)*: the custom lapsed under Badr (from 1074) and was revived by al-Maʾmūn in the 1120s. Since the Coptic Nawrūz (1 Tūt, late August) falls in the flood season, in 1066 the caliph and his women would be at the Pavilion on the Khalīj at Nawrūz. Al-Qāḍī al-Fāḍil's scene fits the period.
- **Masked pageant, 415/1024.** The market people went through the streets for about two weeks with *khayāl*, *samājāt* (comic masks, as the editors gloss it) and figures, up to Cairo for the caliph to watch. They carried a decree (*sijill*) that no one should obstruct them. The twelve markets were given **8,000 dirhams** (Musabbiḥī pp. 42–43; *Ittiʿāẓ* 2:146). Al-Ẓāhir "laughed at them" (*Khiṭaṭ* 1:564). This is state-licensed street masquerade. It was the guilds' annual **summer outing in July, not Nawrūz** (Shoshan note §2).
- **Nawrūz, 364/975.** Three days of masks in Cairo. Al-Muʿizz banned it and paraded some offenders on camels (Ibn Zūlāq via *Khiṭaṭ*, Būlāq 1:493: the market people made elephants, played three days and showed masks, *samājāt*, and ornaments; some were jailed, some paraded on camels). The *Ittiʿāẓ* version has the ban and arrests but no masks or camels (Jiwa p. 205; `isl_jiwa_maqrizi_muizz.md`), but the ban "was disregarded the following year" (Sanders 1994, approx. p. 83, secondhand).
- **Shared festivals.** Epiphany, the Festival of the Cross and Nawrūz "were celebrated by the entire population" (Sanders 1994 p. 81, secondhand). For Byzantine-born sisters this means their own feasts are already Cairo's.
- **Cross-dressing at Nawrūz** (Shoshan 1993 ch. 3, read in full):
  - **913**, ʿAbbasid governor: *muʾannathūn* "dressed in 'their special costume'" paraded in disgrace round the old mosque of Fusṭāṭ (al-Kindī; Shoshan p. 49). The only Egyptian case where their dress is mentioned.
  - **Fatimid period:** the *muʾannathūn* of al-Qāḍī al-Fāḍil above, dress not stated.
  - So a Fatimid *cross-dressing* festival is still extrapolation; a Fatimid Nawrūz with effeminate performers before the caliph is attested, retrospectively.
- **The date.** Egyptian Nawrūz is the Coptic New Year, **1 Tūt**: in 1066, **29 August** (Julian), four days after the end of Ramaḍān (c. 25 August). Never March 21, which is the Persian date (Shoshan p. 42; Ibn Riḍwān via *Khiṭaṭ* 1:727). In 415/1024, a famine year, a crier banned water-play at Nawrūz: a direct model for the famine years after 1065.

### 3.2 Rasad's court and the city, 1066
- **Rasad's path to power.** She was a slave woman, "Sudani, Abyssinian or Nubian", whom al-Ẓāhir **bought** from the Jewish merchant Abū Saʿd al-Tustarī (Cortese and Calderini; Lev; Şen p. 85 says "presented", which the fuller studies do not support). As regent she made al-Tustarī her steward. He was murdered by Turkish troops in 1047.
- **Her office.** She ruled through her own *dīwān al-Sayyida*, which outweighed the state's offices (Lev p. 43), with civil stewards (al-Tustarī, al-Yāzūrī), a Jewish agent (Ibn ʿUṣfūra) and some 5,000 slaves (Cortese and Calderini).
- **The curtain is thinly sourced.** That she sat "behind a curtain" in the dīwān under al-Yāzūrī (1050–58), sending a eunuch with messages, rests on El-Azhari p. 208 and Şen p. 86 only. Cortese and Calderini and Lev do not mention it. Keep it as a scene, but do not lean the whole set's identity on it.
- **Her regiments.** After 1058 she funded a Black regiment (Şen p. 86).
- **Civil war and its clock.**
  - 1062: the quarrel opens at the caliph's summer festival at Jubb ʿUmayra; Rasad arms the Black regiments (Şen p. 86).
  - 1062–late 1066: **no clashes recorded** (Lev pp. 43–44). The bookmark (15 September 1066) falls just before open war, which begins in 459 AH (from c. 22 November 1066).
  - 1067: the Black troops are defeated. 1069: her treasury is sold. 1071: she is arrested and stripped of her assets (Cortese and Calderini pp. 112–113; Şen pp. 86–88).
  - **1078: she is still queen mother**, receiving the Coptic patriarch (Cortese and Calderini p. 180 n. 18). Her death date is not in these books.
  - 1073–74: Badr al-Jamālī arrives and ends the war (Lev p. 45). The *shidda* famine runs c. 1065–72.
  
  The pressure on the set is a city about to go to war and starve, and a patron who loses her money and her troops but not her rank.
- **The drummer-woman.** In 450/1058, Nasab "the drummer-woman" (*al-Ṭabbāla*) sang with the drum before al-Mustanṣir, who granted her wish of land by al-Maqs. It became the *Arḍ al-Ṭabbāla* (*Ittiʿāẓ* 2:254, verified). This is a woman performer rewarded at court in Rasad's own decade.
- **Nāṣir-i Khusraw, Cairo 1047–50.**
  - A thousand men drum and play cymbals round the palace through the night (Schefer p. 128).
  - Eunuchs bearing censers flank the young caliph at the opening of the Khalīj (p. 141).
  - He names no performers or *mukhannathūn*.

### 3.3 Eunuchs as a category: scholars disagree (report, do not adopt)
- **Tolino** (2018, p. 248; conclusion p. 262) says Fatimid eunuchs were gendered "according to the different contexts in which they acted or were represented", not as a separate category (`isl_tolino_eunuchs.md`). This is the line the project keeps (Bhatt).
- **El-Azhari** (2019) calls them a third gender.
- **Hirsch** (2023, p. 397) calls the *khunthā* "a third sex", against Sanders 1991.
- **Fortier** (2025) uses "third gender" as an umbrella term.

Use these scholars' data, never their category labels, in loc.

**The *muḥannak* rank is usable in 1066.** The turban-tail under the chin goes back to al-ʿAzīz (975–96; `isl_sanders_ritual_fatimid_cairo.md`) and Halm dates the eunuch rank to 1025 (`isl_halm_kalifen_assassinen.md`). What is 12th-century (via Ibn al-Ṭuwayr) is the detailed list of offices and the "more than a thousand" figure (`isl_tolino_eunuchs.md`), so use the rank, not that list.

## 4. Design reading for the Adelphai at Cairo

1. **Two worlds, linked by trade.**
   - **The palace (the *qaṣr*):** Rasad's curtain, the eunuch *ustādhs*, singing-girls kept as diplomatic gifts (Musabbiḥī p. 22), and Nasab the drummer-woman.
   - **The city of Fusṭāṭ:** the broker-musician with a house of singing women. In the 1020s and 1050s this is where Cairo's *mukhannathūn* are actually attested.
   
   The Adelphai set could sit in the palace's women's quarters, with a Fusṭāṭ household as its link to the city. The attested link is commercial: a *dallāl* who brokers jewels and singers to the palace.
2. **The stake is the household, not the sword.** "Put them out of your houses" (*Daʿāʾim* §1597) is the hostile instrument. Rasad's favour is the shield. The civil war makes that favour fragile: from 1067 her troops are beaten, from 1069 her money is gone, and in 1071 she is arrested. She keeps her rank, so the shield thins rather than vanishes, which gives the faith a clock.
3. **Hostile instruments, all sourced:**
   - a police parade on a camel with bells (Musabbiḥī);
   - a barber who refuses (al-Shayzarī; late);
   - a slander suit ("*yā mukhannath*"), where the outcome turns on whether she does "women's work" (*Nawādir*);
   - exile to a nearby place with work allowed, i.e. Giza (*Nawādir*);
   - confiscation of a household with no recognised heir (Musabbiḥī);
   - the physician's theory that Tinnīs "breeds" them (Ibn Buṭlān).
4. **Openings, all attributed to characters, never the narrator:**
   - "Whoever imitates a people is counted among them" (§1838), turned by a sister;
   - the *Taʾwīl*'s ranks of adornment, argued by a *dāʿī*;
   - the Mālikī recognition of softness "by nature";
   - Zaknāda's mosque, which shows that a *mukhannath* could become a founder with a name on a building.
5. **Modern guardrails (read before writing any Cairo loc).** `isl_noralla_egypt_trans_law.md` §2 (19 rules) and `isl_arab_queer_trans_feminisms.md` §2 (18 rules and a terminology table). The essentials:
   - "Put them out of your houses" and the "born so / by choice" split still close modern Al-Azhar fatwas used against Egyptian trans people today. In the mod they are hostile voices only; no storyline resolves by a Sister proving she is "the blameless kind", and "born so" or "sick" are tactics, never the narrator's truth.
   - No examination or "correction" scenes; no Byzantine saviours or flight-to-Constantinople endings; no respectability politics against the singers' houses; Qur'an and hadith arguments belong to Muslim characters, never the narrator.
   - No modern identity words or slurs in loc (*mithlī*, *khawal*, "ladyboy", "sister of men"); see the terminology table.
   - Al-Azhar in 1066 is the Fatimids' own Ismaʿili mosque: no Al-Azhar fatwa event.
6. **Rules carried over:**
   - No *khawal*, no *zār*, and no Fatimid cross-dressing festival unless request 3 confirms one.
   - No narrator claim that any historical person was a trans woman. The Baghdadi *dallāl* and Zaknāda are cited as *mukhannathūn*, the sources' word.
   - Rasad, al-Mustanṣir and every vanilla character are never converted or claimed.
   - No "third gender" in the narrator's voice.
   - No Omani *khanīth* parallel: they are a living community.
