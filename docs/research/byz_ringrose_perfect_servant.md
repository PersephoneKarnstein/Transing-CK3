## 1. The argument

**Third gender, but explicitly not a third sex, and not women.** Ringrose's thesis is that eunuchs were "a separate gender category, one that was neither male nor female" (p. 3). But she is precise about what that means:

- Eunuchs "were still considered to be men. After Late Antiquity there is little indication that eunuchs were believed to constitute a 'third sex.'" (p. 4)
- "Thus, eunuchs constituted a third gender within Byzantium. They were men, but a differently acculturated kind of men." (p. 4)
- "Third sex" language is Late Antique invective (Claudian: "whom the male sex has discarded and the female will not adopt"; Lucian: "composite, hybrid, and monstrous", n. 3 to ch. 1, p. 221). By the twelfth century "there is no question about what eunuchs are. They are men" (p. 37).
- Early authors stress how eunuchs are like women, "but none of them ever suggest that eunuchs actually are women" (p. 37). Greek grammar always treats them as masculine (pp. 24, 34).

**The insult runs through women.** "All of the negative attributes in the category 'eunuch' are derived from ones ascribed to women. Conversely, most positive attributes are shared with men." (p. 6). Eunuchs "are never defined in terms of positive female attributes" (p. 37) even when they nurse and protect. Hostile names: Leo the Deacon calls Joseph Bringas *gunaikarion*, "little woman", and "an artificial woman" (*gunaion technêton*) (pp. 36, 41). Read against Bhatt this is degendering in plain form: likened to women for every vice, never granted womanhood.

**Perfect servant is the core.** "The fundamental features around which a gender was constructed for eunuchs were the separation of eunuchs from reproduction and family obligations and the aptness of eunuchs for what I will call 'perfect service.'" (p. 5). The two positive words are "well-minded" (*eunoos*, the false twelfth-century etymology) and perfect servant (p. 39). "Service was a mark of unmanliness" (p. 39).

**Medicine: Aristotle vs Galen (the source of the rite split).** "Aristotle ... says that eunuchs are changed into women, or as he puts it, into a female state. He lacks the vocabulary to discuss eunuchs as a separate third category. Galen occasionally entertains the hypothesis that ... [they] might be changed into a third type of being" (p. 16). Ch. 2 adds that Galen still files eunuchs' flesh with women and children: soft, cold, moist, needing gentle medicines (pp. 54-55); Aristotle says castration brings "close approximation to the appearance of the female" (p. 53). In practice both schools put the eunuch on a ladder below whole men: "stuck" in arrested development (p. 19).

**Castration practice.**
- *Methods.* Paul of Aegina (7th c.) gives two techniques for children: crushing in a hot bath, or incision and removal; he dislikes doing it but "powerful men sometimes insist" (p. 60). Psellos's legal definitions include a boy whose testicles were "crushed" by mother or nurse (p. 60). Ringrose finds no evidence of full penectomy in Byzantium except eunuchs "purchased in the West" as a gift (p. 61); the tenth-century term *kurzinas* for the doubly castrated comes from Khwarizm (p. 15).
- *Ages.* Of 196 named eunuchs, only 25 have any detail of castration; "nearly all indicate that the subject had the surgery before puberty, some as early as seven years old" (p. 61). Medical writers set fourteen as the threshold for sexual feeling (p. 58). Nikephoros of Miletos was sent to the capital at eight; Niketas Patrikios at seventeen after castration at home (pp. 73-74).
- *Adult castration was the despised kind.* "Society distinguished between eunuchs castrated before puberty and men castrated as adults of their own volition. The first group was honored ... The second was looked down upon." (p. 15). After the tenth century adult castration appears almost only for dynastic reasons (Ignatios at 14, Basil Lekapenos, John the Orphanotrophos's adult kin in 1042: "a life that was half a death", p. 62). The Byzantine world "did not normally practice castration as punishment" (p. 68).
- *The trade.* Early eunuchs were slaves from the Black Sea and Caucasus, above all Abchasia, where (Prokopios) the ruler "selected beautiful boys to be castrated and sold to the Romans and then killed their fathers" (p. 10). By the ninth-tenth centuries freeborn Byzantines were "intentionally castrated by their own parents" (p. 11), with Paphlagonia the classic source (pp. 73, 189). The widow Danelis gave the court a hundred eunuchs (p. 78).
- *Law.* Civil law punished castrators "with death, confiscation, exile, or fines", but "it was permissible ... for barbarians to make each other into eunuchs who could then be sold in the empire" (p. 13). Nicaea deposes self-castrating clergy unless there are "good medical reasons", and "certain kinds of medical problems became a subterfuge that legalized castrations" (p. 13). Old laws stayed on the books but "not enforced" (p. 72). Adoption: the Justinianic code and the Ecloga barred *thlibiai* and *kastratoi* from adopting (only *spadones*, the born-impotent, could), and Justinian confiscated the eunuch Euphratas's estate when he tried to leave it to a nephew; Leo VI (Novel 26) then allowed eunuchs to adopt (pp. 187, 219, 252).

**The angel analogy (ch. 7-8).** Heaven's court and the emperor's mirror each other: angels "sing hymns and control access to God ... act as doorkeepers and ushers" (p. 143), and St Michael is pictured as *praepositus sacri cubiculi* of heaven (p. 143). Mistaken identity is a stock motif: the Hagia Sophia legend in which a boy takes Michael for a palace eunuch in a shining robe (pp. 148-149); four rose-faced eunuchs tending a tortured martyr (St Andrew the Fool, p. 151); the dream book calls an angel-like eunuch good luck; the *Timarion* jokes that the eunuch whispering to Theophilos in Hades is "his guardian angel" (p. 205). Apophatic language is shared: both are described by negation (pp. 40-41). Eunuchs, like angels, are "created beings" living "beyond nature" (p. 162). But eunuchs are the angels for *dirty* errands (money, sex, disease), and the mortal eunuch-guide cannot pass heaven's gate (pp. 154, 158-159).

**The Pessinus link (important for Adelphai Dorleyaiou).** Mango's argument, reported by Ringrose: cults of St Michael "took over those of the Anatolian mother goddess Cybele", connected with the galli (p. 147); the cult centre "was at Pessinous, near Germia ... Mango suggests that these sites were transferred to St. Michael" (n. 12, p. 245; Mango, "St Michael and Attis", *Deltion* 12, 1984). Michael's Phrygian shrine at Chonai is named (p. 147).

**The palace (ch. 8): offices and eunuch space.** From Philotheos's *Kletorologion* (899) and *De ceremoniis*:
- *Eunuch titles, ascending:* nipsistiarios (holds the hand-washing bowl), koubikoularios (bedchamber, carries the Cross on campaign), spatharokoubikoularios (sword from the emperor's hand), ostiarios (introduces people; jewelled staff), primikerios, protospatharios of the eunuchs (gold and pearl collar), praepositos (chief; ivory tablets like a patrician). Eunuch patricians outranked bearded ones (pp. 167-168).
- *Eunuch offices, ascending:* papias of the lesser palaces and deuteros (doors, keys, lamps, clocks, the gong; furniture, clothing, regalia store); pinkernes (cupbearer, emperor and empress each); papias and deuteros of the Great Palace; epi tes trapezes (master of the table, emperor and empress); protovestiarios (wardrobe, sleeps in the chamber, golden baton); parakoimomenos, "one who sleeps beside the bed", the highest (pp. 168-169).
- *Bearded-only:* the city eparch ("father of the city"), the domestikos, the quaestor (p. 166).
- *Eunuch space.* No bearded man may watch the crown put on or taken off: "the emperor cannot be crowned before the faces of bearded men" (p. 175). Bath doors at Blachernai: "Bearded men cannot enter here, only koubikoularioi" (p. 171). The empress had a parallel eunuch household, and ruling empresses (Irene, Zoe, Theodora) relied on it (p. 165).
- *The koubikoularios rite* takes place in the oratory of St Theodore. The candidate gets his robe from the holy doors and is told "you have received your dignity from the hand of the Lord" (p. 180). He is forbidden to strike a bearded man unless ordered, to drink, to brag, or to keep company with "innovative" men. "Only the eunuch courtiers received their offices from God rather than from the emperor", but the rewards promised are worldly only (p. 181).

**Reproduction without children (ch. 9).** Eunuchs were formally kinless and took their master's oikos, but from the sixth century they built families anyway: nephews (Solomon, John the Orphanotrophos), protégés (Samonas made the eunuch Constantine "his own man", p. 188), sisters married into the aristocracy. St Metrios, a Paphlagonian peasant, envied neighbours who castrated sons for the capital; an angel promised him a son he "could do with ... as he wished" (p. 189). "Castration ... had become yet another strategy for family promotion" (p. 191).

**Theophylact's *Defence of Eunuchs* (c. 1100-1107 per Mullett in the Tougher volume; Ringrose says "eleventh-century"; pp. 49-50, 121-123, 194-202).** Written for his eunuch brother, a cleric at Hagia Sophia. It is a dialogue between a hostile monk and a eunuch, overheard "over the head of the recently castrated nephew" (p. 195). The eunuch's arguments:
- The monk too refuses procreation, so why condemn pruning "a fig tree that does not fulfill the purpose" or cutting off a sixth finger? (p. 49)
- Eunuchs live "beyond nature", not against it (p. 122).
- The laws are obsolete: Justinian's should be thrown out "like the rotten soles of shoes" (p. 26). The emperors wink at castration because they need eunuchs: "either abolish castration ... or else legalize the practice" (p. 198). Today's eunuchs are not barbarian-made; the vices critics list belong to "Persians or Arabs" (pp. 48, 196).
- Isaiah 56: eunuchs have descendants in the heavenly Jerusalem (p. 197).
- Crucially, he **condemns adult self-castration**: men who come to manhood "and then arrange for their own castration are murderers of the self", and those who cut themselves for sterile sex are "enemies of the creation of God" (p. 197). He **praises parental castration of children** to keep them chaste (pp. 122, 197).

His Matthew 19 gloss also quietly drops castrated men from "eunuchs for the kingdom" (p. 121). A poem of his savages a lecherous eunuch, "a Priapus in your deeds" (p. 200).

**Decline.** Eunuchs were strongest c. 600-1100. "Their political influence was seriously curtailed by the Komnenos dynasty in the twelfth century" (p. 3); "the power, though not the number" declined "until the coming of the later Komnenoi in the middle of the twelfth century" (p. 10). Fewer eunuch generals after Alexios I (p. 140).

**Women passing as eunuchs.** St Matrona escapes an abusive husband by cutting her hair and putting on "the garments of a eunuch" (p. 80). St Anna the Younger lives in a monastery as a eunuch, and an accuser tries to strip her (pp. 78-80). Women with a flow of blood get into St Tarasios's tomb by disguising "their faces as eunuchs" (pp. 80, 150). A seven-year-old girl is admitted to a monastery as a eunuch (p. 62).

**Flags against existing notes and design.**
1. *Era decay date.* `faith_design.md` l. 26 and `beliefs.md` G2-7/14 make the eunuch-angel ideology "contested by 1066". Ringrose dates the political turn to the Komnenoi, especially the mid-twelfth century, and Theophylact (c. 1100) shows the positive view at its fullest. 1066 is a CK3 era boundary, not a Byzantine one.
2. *beliefs.md (d)* says "no Byzantine text calls eunuchs women". That holds as a classification but not as an insult: Leo the Deacon calls Joseph Bringas an "artificial woman" (p. 41) and Photios puts a eunuch in "the women's quarters" (p. 76).
3. *Penal path.* Ringrose: Byzantium "did not normally practice castration as punishment" after the tenth century (pp. 62, 68). Aunt Bea's House is a deliberate Dorley invention, not a Byzantine practice; its text should not claim Byzantine precedent (Andrew's 668 revenge on Sergios, pp. 44-45, is the rare exception and ends in hanging).
4. *Tougher vs Ringrose.* The existing Tougher note sides with Tougher against the "third gender" label. Ringrose's own text is narrower than her reputation: third *gender*, explicitly *men*. Both readings support Adelphai Dorleyaiou's refusal.

**Primary-source cases usable as events or characters.**
- *Ignatios*, castrated at 14 when his father Michael I fell, patriarch 847-858 and **again from Nov. 867** (pp. 62, 118). A eunuch patriarch in the start window; the faith can claim him in its own voice.
- *Photios to John Angourios* (9th c.): "Those who are wise among the Greeks liken you to Attis, calling you one of the galli" (p. 76). This is the 867-era Orthodox reflex the Sisters hear when Pessinus is named.
- *Andrew vs Sergios* (667-668): "You are neither a man nor a woman" (p. 43). Andrew later has Sergios's genitals cut off and hangs him. Precedent for the "Al-Kunduri" class of achievements, and a warning text for Aunt Bea's House.
- *Chrysaborios* under Irene: "If you have a eunuch, kill him, if not, buy one and kill him." (p. 134). Hostile courtier text.
- *St Metrios* and Paphlagonian villages castrating sons for the capital (p. 189). A Paphlagonia county event.
- *Danelis's hundred eunuchs* (p. 78). Redeem-the-boys event.
- *Symeon the Sanctified* on Athos (1078-1089), expelled for bringing beardless boys, reinstated by Alexios I (pp. 125-127).
- *Michael V castrating John the Orphanotrophos's adult kin* (1041-1042): "a life that was half a death" (Psellos, p. 62).
- *Peter Phokas*, a slave-born eunuch general who killed a Rus champion in single combat (p. 137); *Narses*, "more energetic than would be expected of a eunuch" (p. 132).
