## 1. The argument in brief

- **One word for "female" and "coward".** In *Gísla saga* Auðr bloodies the bounty hunter Eyjólfr with his own silver. He orders her killed "though (it) be *blauðr*", and his men stop him from a *níðingsverk* (p. 363). *Blauðr* (soft) and its antonym *hvatr* (bold, brisk) mean both "female/male" and "cowardly/brave". The dictionaries split this into "literal" and "metaphoric" senses. Clover argues the split is modern: "in Old Norse there is no 'both' in the modern sense, but a single notion" (p. 371).
- **Women could become social men.** Thirteen of Iceland's land-takers were women, among them Unnr in djúpúðga (p. 366). Women held land, financed voyages, may have led viking bands (the "red girl" of Munster), composed skaldic verse, and (by one source) brought suit until 992 (pp. 366-67). Women could be outlawed and executed, could own a *goðorð* (though not act as goði), "choose the avenger" in feud, and be buried with weapons (p. 368). A widow had the most control over property (p. 368). "'Woman' is a normative category, but not a binding one" (p. 371). A *hvatr* woman is "unusual, but not unnatural" (p. 371), and usually admired: Bergþóra and Hildigunnr are each called a good *drengr* (p. 372).
- **The law makes daughters into sons.** Grágás *Baugatal* has a single woman in its all-male wergild schedule, the *baugrýgr* (ring-lady). She is an only daughter with no brothers who pays or takes the main ring "like a son" until she marries, when the duty passes to her kinsmen (p. 369). Since the wergild list also tracks inheritance and feud duty, "in the genealogical breach, a woman becomes a functional son" in all three (p. 370). Similar clauses stand in Gulaþing and Frostaþing law (n. 28). "Better a son who is your daughter than no son at all" (p. 370). The maiden-warrior legends (Hervör "trades the needle for the sword", p. 384) are the same idea in story.
- **Níð law.** The Gulaþing law forbids *tunguníð* (verbal níð) and *tréníð* (carved níð). It also forbids *ýki* (exaggeration): saying of a man what "cannot be, nor come to be, nor have been": that he "is a woman every ninth night or has born a child". The penalty is outlawry, deniable by a six-man oath (pp. 373-74). Gulaþing's *fullréttisorð* (full-compensation words) are: saying he gave birth; calling him *sannsorðinn* ("demonstrably fucked"); comparing him to a mare, a bitch, a harlot or any female animal (p. 374). Grágás gives lesser outlawry (three years) for *ýki* and *tréníð*, and full outlawry for *ragr, stroðinn, sorðinn*, "for these three words one has the right to kill" (p. 374).
- **Only the penetrated man is shamed.** The active partner is "boastworthy regardless of the sex of the object" (pp. 374-75). Following Meulengracht Sørensen, *argr/ragr* for a man has three meanings: sexually penetrated, "versed in witchcraft", and cowardly or unmanly (p. 375). In níð, "(male) anus and vagina are for all imaginary purposes one and the same", and the *ragr* man's body "looks very much like that of a woman" (p. 375).
- **Níð is one insult among many.** Men call each other poor, slaves, captives and stay-at-homes as often as they call each other women (p. 376). The insult complex runs on *hvatr/blauðr*, "a gender continuum" rather than "a sexual binary", and on power more than on sex (p. 377).
- **A one-sex model.** Clover follows Laqueur: "there was one sex and it was male", and "finally just one 'gender'", something like masculine (p. 379). Bodily sex "was not that decisive"; what moved a person between statuses worked "at the level of social relations" (pp. 378-79). Femininity as such barely exists; there is only effeminacy, the default for anyone of either sex who falls short (p. 379). What is feared is "powerlessness, the lack or loss of volition", and what is admired is "sovereignty" (p. 379).
- **The real binary.** The line runs between able-bodied men (and the exceptional woman) and "a kind of rainbow coalition of everyone else (most women, children, slaves, and old, disabled, or otherwise disenfranchised men)" (p. 380). The laws call them *ómagi* (dependent) and *magi* (provider): "swordworthy and unswordworthy" (p. 380). Masculinity was "in a kind of double jeopardy" because it could be lost by men and won by women (p. 381).
- **Everyone falls.** Old age drops heroes among the women. Egill, mocked and managed by women, composes *Sonatorrek*, a lament of the women's type, in bed (pp. 382-84). Bjargey's maxim: "It is manly for those unfit for vigorous deeds to be unsparing in their use of the tongue" (p. 383). The tongue is the "sword" of the unswordworthy, and whetting is women's work. The proverb from *Hrafnkels saga*: "Svá ergisk hverr sem eldisk", "Everyone becomes *argr* who gets older" (pp. 384-85). *Argr* "finally knows no sex" (p. 385).
- **Christianity built a wall.** Conversion "entailed a radical remapping of gender": femaleness became "more sharply defined and contained", women-only religious orders appeared, and masculinity expanded into once-effeminate ground (weeping monks; Njáll recuperated) (pp. 385-86). The north moved toward two-sex thinking "of revolutionary proportions" (p. 386). Old ways lingered: priests' marriages, and the girl Ingunn who tutored Latin at the Hólar cathedral school in 1110 (n. 79).
- **Close.** Sexual difference "used to be less a wall than a permeable membrane": a physical woman could become a social man, a physical man "could (and sooner or later did) become a social woman", and "Óðinn himself, played both sides of the street" (p. 387).
