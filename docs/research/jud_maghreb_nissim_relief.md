# Nissim b. Jacob of Qayrawan, *An Elegant Composition Concerning Relief after Adversity*: design reading

Source: Nissim ben Jacob ibn Shahin, *An Elegant Composition Concerning Relief after Adversity* (Judeo-Arabic *Kitāb al-Faraj baʿd al-shidda*; Hebrew *Ḥibbur yafeh me-ha-yeshuʿah*), translated from the Arabic with introduction and notes by William M. Brinner. Yale Judaica Series 20 (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1977). Extraction (Internet Archive OCR): `sources_pending/maghreb/nissim_elegant_composition.txt`. Page numbers are the book's, from running heads and the page numbers at the foot of chapter-opening pages. The OCR garbles some numerals, so pages marked *unverified* below need checking against a scan.

**What was read.**
- In full: the Introduction sections "The Jews of Kairouan" and "Life of R. Nissim" (pp. xx–xxiii); Goitein's note on Geniza letters that mention the book (p. xxxii); Hirschberg's outline of the contents, quoted in the Introduction (c. p. xxiv, *unverified*); the Prologue (pp. 3–6: "R. Baroka and the Jailer", "R. Baroka and the Two Men Who Spread Cheer", "Prologue—Concluded").
- Tales read in full: ch. X (Zakkai's mother; Hanina b. Dosa's wife, pp. 44–46); ch. XI "The Shining Robe" (pp. 48–51); ch. XII "Whoso Findeth a Wife", with "The Perfidious Wife" and "The Story of Yohani bat Retibi" (pp. 53–64); ch. XIII "Despise Not Proselytes" (pp. 69–71); ch. XIX "The Young Woman of Jerusalem and the Oppressor" (pp. 92–95); ch. XXV "R. Meir and the Perfidious Woman" (pp. 118–120); ch. XXXIV "The Abandoned Girl" (pp. 175–176).
- Only searched: the whole text for disguise, garb, dressed, veil, eunuch, transform/turned into, witch, Purim/Esther, daughter, women and consolation. The other tales (chs. I–IX, XIV–XVIII, XX–XXIV, XXVI–XXXIII) were not read.

**Relation to existing notes.** This is the first North African Jewish *literary* text in the repo. Its rabbinic sources overlap with `jud_rabbinic_primary_texts.md` (the *aylonit* of bYevamot 80b, §4 below) and with `jud_strassfeld_classically_queer.md`. Nissim retells Talmudic tales in Arabic for Jews under Muslim rule, so the tales are what a Maghrebi Jewish household might actually have heard.

**Nissim's family.** He received money from Samuel ha-Nagid of Granada. "His only son died in infancy; and his daughter, unhappily married to a son of the aforementioned Samuel, was eventually abandoned by her husband" (p. xxii). The book was written for a relative by marriage, Dunash, to comfort him in a loss (pp. 3–4 and n. 2; cf. p. xvii).

**Community authority.** The only institution named is the *kallah* led by a *rosh*. Its holders were Jacob, Hananel and Nissim (p. xxi). The *dayyan* of al-Mahdiyya appears in 1061 (p. xxii). Nothing is said about bans, the Zirid ruler's licence, a *nagid* of Qayrawan, or the Babylonian yeshivot beyond the general remark that the Spanish and Qayrawan communities sent queries and money to Babylonia (p. xx).

## 2. How the book was used

- It was written in **Judeo-Arabic** "to speak directly to his fellow Jews living under Muslim rule", from mainly Jewish sources (p. xxxi). It is modelled on the Arabic *faraj* genre (pp. xxiii ff.). Later Hebrew versions spread it to Christian Europe and into Yiddish folklore (pp. xxxi–xxxii).
- **A brother sends it to his sister.** About 1200, a man named Barakat wrote to his sister **Rayyisah** after their mother's death. He told her not to mourn too much, and sent her the book, telling her to "occupy yourself with it" (Goitein's note, p. xxxii). This is a Jewish woman reading this book for comfort, in Egypt.
- **Nissim invites readers to mend his book:** "whosoever may see any gap in it, let him fill it, or any mistake, let him correct it" (p. 6).

## 3. Tales usable as in-world stories

Each tale is listed with what it offers the sect and what makes it risky.

1. **The jailer in black shoes** (Prologue, pp. 4–5; source bTaʿanit 22a). Elijah points out a man "wearing black boots and having no fringes" as one worthy of the world to come. He wears black shoes "so that it would not be known that I am a Jew". He sleeps between the men's and women's cells to protect the women. He also listens at the palace and warns the sages of decrees, so that they may fast and pray. *For the sect:* worth is not read off dress or outward signs. Someone who passes unrecognised is still among the righteous. *Risk:* none, provided the "listening at the palace" detail is not turned into spying on a ruler, which would come close to the betrayal trope.
2. **The two men who spread cheer** (pp. 5–6). Elijah says two jesters are worthy of the world to come because "we cheer the grieving soul". *For the sect:* a short blessing on the revel at Purim, and on companions.
3. **The widow who walked further** (ch. XII, p. 63; R. Johanan). A widow went at nightfall to a synagogue farther than her own, "to gain recompense and earn reward" for the extra steps. A maiden prays not to cause anyone to sin. *For the sect:* a woman chooses her synagogue and walks to it. It fits "going down to the women's synagogue" (`tm_hipukh.0010`) and the seat below (`tm_hn2.0050`).
4. **Despise not proselytes** (ch. XIII, pp. 69–71). Shemaiah and Abtalion, of Gentile descent, are followed by the crowd instead of the High Priest. They answer that those who do "the like of Aaron's works" are better than those of Aaron's stock who do not (p. 70). Onkelos converts the emperor's messengers (pp. 70–71). The chapter ends: "Your own deeds can cause you to be far [from them], and your own deeds can cause you to be near" (p. 71). *For the sect:* the closest text in this material to its tenet that a woman is made by what she keeps. Birth is outweighed by deeds.
5. **The Shining Robe** (ch. XI, pp. 48–51). The robe of Joseph the gardener, a poor man who gives half his income to the poor, lacks a collar. His wife has him sell her so that the price can go to alms. She keeps her oath against her buyer and his shepherd, and her husband finds her again in disguise. A voice declares her robe "even finer than thine" (p. 51). *For the sect:* a wife's robe outshines her husband's. *Risk:* she is sold, and the plot turns on chastity under threat. **Use only the closing line.**
6. **R. Meir disguised** (ch. XII, pp. 53–54). Beruriah asks Meir to free her sister from a brothel. He goes "having disguised himself and changed his appearance" to test her (p. 53). *Risk:* forced prostitution. Do not use.
7. **The Abandoned Girl** (ch. XXXIV, pp. 175–176). An uncle leaves his orphaned niece, an heiress, in the Street of the Perfumers in a city overseas, to "test" Ps. 37:25. She waits until sunset and weeps with her sleeve over her face. The Gentile elders offer her a night with their womenfolk, and she answers "I cannot do so, inasmuch as I am a Jewess". The qāḍī and the market inspectors then fetch a Jew of the market. **The manuscript breaks off there** (p. 176 n. 6). *For the sect:* the best find. A girl stranded abroad is protected by Muslim officials, who send her to her own people. The story has no surviving ending, and Nissim himself told readers to "fill the gap" (p. 6).
8. **The Young Woman of Jerusalem** (ch. XIX, pp. 92–95). A Judith tale: a "daughter of the prophets" goes out to the besieging king and beheads him in his sleep. *Risk:* violence, and an "unbelievers" enemy who could be read as Muslim or Christian. Do not use as event text.

**Not to use.** "The Perfidious Wife" (pp. 54–57), the bad-wife sayings (pp. 58–61) and **Yohani bat Retibi**, the widow-witch whose prayers for women in labour were a trick (pp. 61–62), are misogynist. Nissim uses Yohani to justify R. Eliezer's ban on teaching daughters Torah (p. 62). The sect's elder teaches the prayer, so this tale could only appear as an argument *against* her. Even then it is best left out.

## 4. Gender and the body: the *aylonit* passage

Expounding Ben Sira, "A daughter is a deceptive treasure to her father" (Sir. 42:9), Nissim lists a father's fears. One is that she "might turn out to be an *aylonit*", which he defines as "one who does not have protruding breasts, does not menstruate, has a deep voice indistinguishable from a man's" (p. 61; explanation from bYevamot 80b, n. 19).

This is the only body-category passage in the book. It shows the rabbinic *aylonit* reaching an 11th-century Qayrawan reader as an everyday fear. For the sect it cuts both ways:
- the law holds that a woman of this body is still a woman, the *aylonit*;
- but here it is framed as a father's dread.

The faith page already refuses third-category status for its daughters (`jud_strassfeld_classically_queer.md` on *aylonit* as "the female version of a *seris chammah*"). **Recommendation:** do not quote Nissim's description in event text, since it is a list of bodily deficits. The sect may say, in its own voice, that the *aylonit* "is a woman in law". That claim rests on the rabbinic sources, not on Nissim.

Absent from the tales read: eunuchs, transformation of sex, cross-dressing, Purim customs. Esther is named once, as a book of deliverance already well known and so not retold (p. 6).

## 5. Women's names

Only one Jewish woman's name in this book belongs to the period: **Rayyisah** ("leader"), Egypt, c. 1200 (p. xxxii). The women in the tales are Talmudic or nameless.
