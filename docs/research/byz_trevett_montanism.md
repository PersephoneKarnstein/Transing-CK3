**Relevance, candidly.** High for structure, geography and the record of persecution. It is not a precedent for the knife. Montanism was a movement of cis women and men. Its gender radicalism concerns women's public authority and celibacy, not changing sex. Trevett treats the "Montanus was a castrated priest of Cybele" story as late polemic. The useful lesson is that the Byzantine church already had a ready-made script for a Phrygian heresy with women at the altar, and it ran that script for four centuries.

## 1. The argument in brief

**The Cybele accusation is late and polemical.**
- Ancient critics called Montanus "a former priest of Apollo or the idol ... or of Cybele ('an emasculated man', Jerome *Ep.* xli.4)" (p. 78). In the *Dialexis* the Montanist does not deny it. He answers that Paul too was once an enemy of the Church (p. 78).
- The castration story comes from Jerome, following Didymus. It is "a nugget of information seemingly, and oddly, unknown either to the earliest writers or to later Asian commentators" (p. 154). The earliest source says only that the devil deceived Montanus, with "no explicit reference to paganism" (p. 79). Aune sees "a deliberate attempt by heresiologists ... to paganise Montanus" (p. 79).
- From Neander (1827) to Ramsay, 19th-century scholars looked for Attis-Cybele roots (p. 8). Schepelern (1929) found the closest parallels in the lamenting virgins and the pricking of infants with needles, but the sources are late, and the roots were Johannine and Christian (p. 9). The modern consensus is that the movement was Christian: "we should distinguish between its Phrygian Christian character and a real connection with paganism" (p. 10).
- "Had the Prophets really appeared ... after the manner of the Galli of the Great Mother, our sources ... would not have been slow to make this clear" (p. 89). Benko's claim that Montanist chastity was "a bloodless castration" like the Galli's is rejected as fanatic-hunting (p. 123).
- A side note on eunuch Christians: Justin approved of an Alexandrian Christian who sought a surgeon to castrate him, and Melito of Sardis was called a eunuch (p. 110).

**Geography.**
- Montanus first prophesied at Ardabau. Pepuza and Tymion, "little towns" of Phrygia, were renamed "Jerusalem" (pp. 15, 21). None of the three sites is "locatable with certainty" (pp. 19–20). Trevett leans to the Bekilli area east of Philadelphia, towards Eumeneia (p. 22). The Anonymous's "Phrygian Mysia" for Ardabau "could indicate a site anywhere between Philadelphia and Dorylaeum" (p. 21).
- "'Phrygian' could be a near synonym for 'slave' and for boorish hill-billy" (p. 18). The Phrygian language survived into the 3rd century (p. 18).
- Later Pepuza: it was in Phrygia Pacatiana under Diocletian and had no bishop at Nicaea in 325, "perhaps because of its known Christian heterodoxy". Epiphanius (d. c. 402) calls it razed, "ruined and a desert" (p. 20). Philostorgius has a man exiled there c. 356. **The abbot of a monastery at Pepuza, Theophylactus, attended Nicaea II in 787** (p. 20). By then that monastery was orthodox.
- **Dorylaion has a Montanist grave.** From the region of Dorylaeum comes Loupikinos's epitaph for his wife **Mountane**, a "spiritual Christian", whose name echoes Montanus's. Trevett takes her as very likely Montanist (pp. 172, 204). Nanas of Cotiaeon (Kütahya), who spoke in tongues and was visited by angels, is "probably" Montanist. Her husband recorded her gifts on her grave, and she "was not vilified" (p. 171).

**Women's authority.**
- The heresiological sources are written by men who use "the figure of the heretical female" to define themselves (Burrus, p. 151). Salmon's 19th-century fear sums it up: doctrine shaped by "wild and excitable women" (p. 151).
- The women were not charged with heresy. The fight was about "a mode of prophesying and a Christian lifestyle" (p. 155).
- **Only the women were exorcised.** Bishops were sent to exorcise Maximilla and Priscilla. "Here was war: male against female; prophet against prophet; cleric ... against laywoman" (p. 156). "Exorcism here should be seen not least as an attempt at social control" (p. 157). "I know of no cases of the exorcism of Montanist men" (p. 158).
- Maximilla's answer: "I am driven away like a wolf from the sheep. I am not a wolf, I am word and spirit and power" (p. 156). And: "Do not hear me but hear Christ" (p. 164).
- Jensen argued that the women founded the movement and that "Montanism" is a misnomer. Trevett's verdict is "unproven": the women were equal to Montanus, not dependent on him (pp. 159–62).
- The prophetic succession runs from Philip's daughters through **Ammia of Philadelphia** to Priscilla and Maximilla, with no Montanus in it (p. 161).
- **Quintilla**, a later prophetess, saw Christ come to her at Pepuza "in the form of a woman and in a bright robe", giving her wisdom and revealing that Jerusalem would descend there (pp. 167–69). Pepuza had two churches, one to Priscilla and one to Quintilla (p. 167).
- Origen attacked "disciples of women". Their sayings were wrong because they "come from the mouth of a woman" (pp. 174–75). The Montanists answered with **Galatians 3:28** and **Joel 2:28** (pp. 175, 185, 195).
- **Adoption by the sisterhood.** In the *Passio Perpetuae*, Felicitas's newborn daughter is taken by an unnamed "sister" to raise: "a family which was bound not by blood but by the Spirit" (p. 182).
- **Women clergy are certain.** The Quintillians ordained women presbyters and bishops, citing Galatians 3:28 and honouring Eve as the first to taste knowledge (pp. 185, 221). Seven white-clad, lamp-bearing prophesying virgins led worship and moved hearers to tears of repentance (pp. 185, 203). An epitaph at Uşak names **Ammion the presbytera** beside the bishop Diogas (p. 186). Canons 11 and 44 of Laodicea bar *presbytides* and bar women from the altar (pp. 186–87).
- Women became clergy because they were "clericalised by default" once driven out of the Church. The cause was belief that the Spirit empowers both sexes, not confessor status, as Klawiter argued (pp. 190, 195).
- **Limits.** "Montanist clerical egalitarianism probably never extended to the patriarchate" (p. 211). Nothing shows women held more authority than men (p. 196). Trevett warns against calling them "feminist" (p. 197).

**Organisation.**
- Montanus organised the movement: assemblies at Pepuza, collectors of offerings, and "(perhaps for the first time in Christian history)" salaries for teachers (p. 77). Theodotus was *epitropos*, steward (p. 159).
- Jerome's hierarchy: **patriarchs of Pepuza first, *koinonoi* second, bishops "only third"** (pp. 186, 210). There were village bishops as well as town bishops (p. 210).
- Patriarchs: there was probably always one at Pepuza, and possibly others, for example at Ankyra, where the *Life of Theodotus* names a "church of the patriarchs" (pp. 210–11).
- *Koinonoi* ("companions"): Praylios *koinonos* "according to region", north of Philadelphia, 514/15 (a regional bishop, Tabbernee). Paulinos, *koinonos* and *mystes*, aged 85, near Sebaste (pp. 211–12). Trevett reads the title as "companions of the Spirit", who guarded the founders' tradition, taught and oversaw. She compares the Companions of the Prophet (pp. 213–14). A garbled 6th-century Gallic letter may echo women *koinonoi* (pp. 188, 212).
- Pepuza was the "Montanist Mecca". At Easter it sent a share of the paschal offering to congregations elsewhere, the sick came to the shrine of the Three for healing, and initiates were received there (pp. 168, 201). Montanists kept their own Easter reckoning, a solar calendar fixed on the 14th of the month (p. 202).
- Diversity: Priscillianists, Quintillians, Pepuzites, Artotyrites ("bread and cheesers"), Tascodrougites ("nose-peggers"). Opponents report tattooing "mysteries of the Name of Christ" and a finger-to-nose gesture of sorrow in prayer (pp. 199–200).

**Suppression and survival.**
- Montanists survived because "local feeling against them was not strong" (p. 209). Officials were apathetic and some could be bribed (pp. 199, 228).
- Constantine closed their churches and forbade meetings (pp. 201, 223). From 379, "All heresies are forbidden ... and they shall cease forever" (p. 228). The 4th–5th-century codes burned their books and threatened death to anyone hiding them. They executed organisers of rural assemblies, voided Montanist wills, **gave heretics' property to the Christian next of kin**, cleared a fleeing Montanist slave of guilt, and stopped poor relief and the purchase of slaves (pp. 199, 228–29).
- Converts were rebaptised: "Montanists were treated like pagans" (Tabbernee, p. 218). They were not condemned at Nicaea, but were condemned by Laodicea canon 8 and by Constantinople in 381 (p. 218).
- In the West: Honorius's edict of 407; Innocent I (402–17) exiled Cataphrygians to a monastery (pp. 225–26). In the East they were "numerous in Phrygia" in the 5th century (p. 224). Exiled orthodox bishops were sent to Phrygia, "the den of Montanus and Maximilla" (p. 226).
- Justinian, c. 527–31, was the "death-knell" (p. 227). Under Justin a local bishop had colluded for money, and the relics were saved by being **switched for other bones** (pp. 229–30). About 550, John of Ephesus forced open the shrine at Pepuza, burned the Three's bones and the books, and turned the building into a catholic church. The Montanists cried "Now the world is overturned and will perish". The founders "had lain long ... with plates of gold upon their mouths" (pp. 228, 232; from Michael the Syrian, which Tabbernee doubts).
- **Last traces:** Leo III ordered the forced baptism of Jews and Montanists in 721–22 (Theophanes; Trevett is cautious, and Tabbernee dismisses it). Nicephorus (d. c. 829) "routed heretics" (to be treated "with caution") (pp. 230–31). John of Damascus still mentions Pepuza (p. 227). **Answer to the brief: survival is secure to the mid-6th century in Phrygia, thin and doubtful in the 8th, and unattested by the 9th.**
- **Excluded by the mod's rules:** Procopius and Theophanes both say Montanists burned themselves in their churches rather than submit (pp. 230, 231). The rumour that Montanus and Maximilla hanged themselves is also excluded (p. 155). This material must not be used.

**Contradictions and flags.**
- `byzantium_reading_list.md` item 66 is marked "Not downloaded"; it has now been read. (Not edited, per the brief.)
- `byz_sideris_eunuques_societe.md` row 8 lets the faith claim Heortasius of Appia and Aurelios Appa as "perhaps Montanist" eunuch clergy. Trevett does not discuss them. She warns that epitaph markers ("Christians for Christians", even *pneumatikos*) do not prove Montanism (pp. 204–08). The claim is fine in the faith's voice, but not in the narrator's.
- Varberg (in the Gardeła note) quotes Epiphanius, "women are bishops and women are presbyters". Trevett confirms this (p. 185).
- Faith_design §1 says the Sisters are a "secret survival" bridging c. 250–867. Montanism is a documented Phrygian heresy that lasted to c. 550 and possibly to 722. That shortens the bridge to about 150 years and gives it real, sourced ground.
- Outside the book, to verify before use: Pepuza and Tymion were reportedly identified in 2000–01 near Karahallı (Uşak), in the Ulubey canyon, by Tabbernee and Lampe. That is close to Sebaste (vanilla `b_sebaste`, `c_lydia`, `d_thracesia`).
