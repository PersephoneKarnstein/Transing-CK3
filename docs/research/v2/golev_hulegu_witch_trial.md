# Witches for v2: Golev, "The Witchcraft Trial at the Court of Hülegü" (2019)

Scope: a comparative reading for the "Good Ladies" persecution layer (`witch_design.md` §5) and for any Mongol-adjacent content (Yutai Dao, steppe events). It is not a source on the Ladies themselves; it is about how a 13th-century Mongol court used a witchcraft charge as a political weapon.

Pronouns: Konstantin Golev is *they* here (not stated). Historical people whose pronouns the paper doesn't give, or which are uncertain (Kamšā, Yakšā, the *ayġāq*), are *they*. The sources call the Jöchid princes "he"; this note mostly uses their names.

Quote rule: every verbatim quote is 25 words or fewer and carries the printed page of the journal (327–368). Golev's English translations of Persian, Arabic, Armenian and Georgian texts are quoted as Golev's. Everything else is paraphrase.

Text: `pdftotext -layout` copy in the session scratchpad (`…/scratchpad/witch/golev.txt`), not in the repo.

## 1. Bibliographic line and verdict

**Bibliographic line.** Konstantin Golev, "Intra-Mongol Diplomacy and Witch-Hunt during the Dissolution of the Empire: the Witchcraft Trial at the Court of Hülegü", *Eurasian Studies* 17 (2019): 327–368. doi:10.1163/24685623-12340079. Golev was at the Institute for Historical Studies, Bulgarian Academy of Sciences, Sofia, and works on the Cuman-Qipchaqs and the Mongols (p. 368).

**Verdict: high value as a model of the witchcraft charge as political pretext; low value for gender or "shamaness" material; it does not cover the earlier cases in detail.**

- The thesis, in one line: "Hülegü used an accusation in witchcraft as an official pretext to remove fellow Chinggisid prince (or princes)" (p. 327).
- The method is source criticism. Golev lines up three Persian court versions against Mamluk (Arabic), Armenian and Georgian accounts. They argue that the Persian court historians split one coordinated purge into a string of natural-looking deaths, and invented or repeated a "referral to Berke" that made the execution look lawful.
- **What the paper does not contain** (the brief asked for these; they must come from elsewhere):
  - **Töregene and Fāṭima (1246).** Not narrated. Golev only cites their own 2017 article on Fāṭima Khatun (n. 91; bibliography p. 367). The repo's existing note on Fāṭima is `../east_mongol_sex_genghis.md` E25 (Terbish), which is where the orifice-sewing and drowning detail comes from, not from Golev.
  - **Oghul Qaimish.** Covered only in notes 92 and 109 and on pp. 361–362: executed for witchcraft in Möngke's purges, with "the mother of another prince" (n. 92). Golev gives no year; "1251–52" is not in this paper.
  - **Shiremün's mother.** Not named as such. Golev names a "Qadaqač, the mother of one of the Ögedeid rivals of Möngke" (n. 109, p. 362), from the *Yuanshi*. Identifying that son as Shiremün is standard scholarship but **is not stated here**; check before using.
  - **The yasa text.** Golev does not reconstruct it. They accept al-Maqrīzī's report that witchcraft was a capital offence and set aside "the controversy regarding the real existence of the Yasa" (n. 91, p. 355).

## 4. Golev's argument

1. **There was one purge, not three deaths.** Only the Persian court texts spread the deaths out ("banquet", "poison", "trial"). Every other tradition has a simultaneous strike, and the armed attack on the Jöchid camps looks prepared in advance: "there was a purge of Jöchid commanders in Hülegü's camp, which was obscured in the pages of Jāmiʿ al-tawārīḫ and Aḫbār-i Muġūlān" (p. 352).
2. **The referral to Berke is fiction.** Three reasons: (a) only the Persian texts have it; (b) the Arab account gives fifteen days between arrest and death, too short for a round trip to the Volga; (c) Rashīd himself twice has Nogai marching to avenge Tutar's blood, which makes no sense if Berke had confirmed the verdict (pp. 350–352). The episode copies a real precedent: Ögedei sending Güyük to Batu for judgment as "a battlefield matter" (*Secret History* §§275–277; p. 339).
3. **Why the referral was invented.** It moves the responsibility to Berke as the senior kinsman (*aqa*), cites the Yasa, and removes any suspicion of a rigged trial. Golev calls it "a convenient means to avoid Hülegü's central role in the execution of a Chinggisid under such a dishonorable charge" (p. 362). It may have been the Ilkhanid house's own official version (Su'unchaq's role suggests court information), not just the historians' (pp. 359–362).
4. **Why witchcraft was the charge.** "What better excuse than an accusation in illegal practice of witchcraft, which was considered exceptionally blameworthy by the Mongols" (p. 355). It was a capital crime (al-Maqrīzī) and grave enough to kill even a member of the Golden Lineage (Oghul Qaimish). So ordinary Mongols in Hülegü's camp would accept it as normal custom (pp. 355–356).
5. **It was staged in public, and an insider was sacrificed.** That one of Hülegü's own people (Yakšā or Ṣadr al-Dīn) died too "hints to a carefully planned strike … intended to be publically delivered and to mobilize the public opinion" (p. 356). Golev doesn't rule out that a prince really hired a wizard.
6. **Timing.** The informer "appeared at the most convenient moment" (p. 355), when tension was already high.
7. **Witchcraft was one weapon among several.** Berke answered with Islam (avenging the Caliph) and with the steppe blood feud. "No doubt, a hundred years' war is not waged only for a witchcraft trial" (p. 358). Following Lane, religion was not the main cause (n. 97).
8. **Shame differs by audience.** "While for the Mongols the accusations of illegal practice of witchcraft were absolutely natural and had a wide social impact" (p. 361), the Persian administrators found them dishonourable. Juvaynī hides the accuser in the ʿAlī Ḫwāja trial ("some other person"); Rashīd describes Oghul Qaimish's death but not her crime (pp. 361–362, n. 108).

## 5. What "witchcraft" meant in Mongol law and custom (as far as this paper goes)

- **Word.** Persian *siḥr* (sorcery); Arabic *saḥara* (sorcerers), *sāḥira* (sorceress); Latin *sortilega* (Rubruck). A "charm" (written) is enough to make a scribe complicit (p. 338).
- **Status.** Rashīd: "the Mongols were extremely disgusted by witchcraft" (n. 90, p. 355). Capital under the yasa as al-Maqrīzī reports it (n. 91). Golev keeps the yasa's existence an open question; this matches Terbish's scepticism in `../east_mongol_sex_genghis.md`.
- **Procedure** (as the court texts present it): an informer (*ayġāq*) denounces; an investigation; confession (only in Rashīd's later versions); referral to senior kin; execution. An investigator (*yarghuchi*) such as Su'unchaq handles it (n. 104, p. 360). In theory a Chinggisid could be sentenced to death only by a *qurultai* of all branches (n. 93, p. 356), which is exactly what the fake referral covers up.
- **Bloodless death.** "The shedding of royal blood was sacrilege according to the perceptions of the Mediaeval Turks and Mongols" (n. 56, p. 345). Princes were strangled, with a bowstring in Grigor (p. 345); Ricoldo of Monte Croce gives the reason (n. 56). Oghul Qaimish was "interrogated naked, wrapped in felt and thrown into the water – the usual way by which the Mongols put to death practitioners of illegal magic" (pp. 361–362). Drowning in felt is the witch's death; it is also bloodless.
- **A culture of suspicion.** Sudden illness or death was readily blamed on poison or magic. A commander in Quli's service blamed an Armenian abbot's dinner for his illness, and the abbot was cruelly killed (Grigor; n. 94, p. 356). Hülegü's men were "as open for such 'conspiracy' explanations of sudden accidents as those in the political core of the empire" (n. 94, p. 356).
- **Other cases mentioned:** a chain of three trials before and after Möngke's accession, ending with ʿAlī Ḫwāja; Juvaynī's moral is that every slanderer ends up slandered (n. 108). A Chinese source has Hülegü executing two soothsayers, Ḥussām al-Dīn and Majd al-Dīn, for "evil intentions" (n. 95, pp. 356–357, via Endicott-West 2000). Early in Aḥmad Tegüder's reign (1282–84) a political investigation also raised witchcraft, but Su'unchaq "apparently remained skeptical" (n. 104, p. 360).

### Who was accused (gender)

| Case | Accused | Gender as the sources give it |
|---|---|---|
| Hülegü's camp, 1260–61 | Balagha and/or Tutar (Jöchid princes) | men |
| same | Ṣadr al-Dīn Sāvjī, killed for writing a charm | man (Muslim, given "martyrized") |
| same | Yakšā/Taktā, Hülegü's sorcerer | not stated in Golev's paraphrase; *they* |
| same | Berke's unnamed sorcerers | not stated |
| Möngke's purge (c. 1251–52, date not in Golev) | Oghul Qaimish, ex-regent; Qadaqač, a prince's mother | women |
| Möngke to Rubruck | Oghul Qaimish "fuit pessima sortilega" | woman |

So in the 1260s case the people killed for witchcraft were mostly **men of rank**, and the one woman named, Kamšā, was the **informer**, not the accused. In the 1250s purge the accused were **royal women with political power**. The charge followed political danger, not gender as such. (The 1246 Fāṭima case, not covered here, follows the same pattern: a powerful favourite of a female regent.)

**A woman among the dead?** Grigor and Kirakos list "Гataγan/Gatahan" among the executed. Hanlaryan suggested Qadaqan, one of Quli's chief wives; Golev notes that Rashīd records only another wife as having come to Iran (n. 55, p. 345). This is an uncertain identification; don't build on it.

## 6. Women ritual specialists and the "qam" problem (handle with care)

- **Kamšā.** Al-Baġdādī calls them a "sorceress from Khatay" (North China). Golev thinks the Arab informant "perhaps by mistake" took the Turkic **qam**, "used by the Mongols for designation of their shamans", to be part of a personal name (n. 49, p. 343). Golev also suggests Kamšā may be the *ayġāq* of Rashīd's version, "given that both in Persian and in Turkic gender markers are absent" (p. 345).
- **What it does and doesn't show.** It shows that a Mongol court of the 1260s employed ritual specialists from several places, including a woman from China, and that such people served as spies as well as diviners: "The circulation of specialists in magic arts is also noteworthy" (n. 54, p. 344). It does **not** show a gender-variant shaman, a "shamaness" office, or anything about Kamšā's own religion. The "qam" label is a garbled, third-hand guess by a modern scholar about a medieval Arab informant.
- **Misreadings by medieval outsiders.** Rubruck thought *khan* and *qam* were one word; Carpini thought "Kam" was the Cumans' name for the Mongol god (n. 49, p. 344). This is a useful reminder that 13th-century travellers' "shaman" data is itself unreliable.
- **Ritual specialists were dangerous to keep.** Yakšā, the khan's own sorcerer, died with the accused. Being a court specialist meant being disposable when a show of impartiality was needed.

No other gender material is in this paper.

## 7. Design implications

### (a) The Good Ladies' persecution layer: a comparative model, not a Mongol import

The Ladies are Latin, and none of this paper's Mongol detail should appear in their events. What carries over is the **logic** of the accusation as a political weapon. That logic is already implicit in §5 of `witch_design.md` ("a second accusation of the same woman is graver").

1. **The accusation follows the patron's enemies, not the practice.** Proposal: suspicion against a Lady rises mainly when **her protector** (lord, spouse, abbess) has a rival with a claim, a feud or a succession dispute, and only a little from her own rites. In CK3 terms: weight the "denounced" event by the protector's rivalry or hostile-scheme state, not only by piety level. This matches Golev's timing point (p. 355) and Kirakos's motive (p. 350).
2. **The planted informer.** Kamšā's role becomes a generic one: a servant placed in her household reports on her. Option set: find and dismiss the servant (stress, intrigue check); feed them a harmless story; or say nothing.
3. **The accuser sacrifices one of their own.** Hülegü let his own sorcerer die to make the strike look fair (p. 356). Generic version: the accuser's own chaplain or physician is "found" guilty alongside her. Flavour line from a neutral narrator, e.g. "Even the count's own physician was taken. People said that proved the count was fair."
4. **Passing the decision upward.** The fake referral to Berke shares out the blame (pp. 359–362). In the Latin setting the accusing lord sends the case to the bishop, and the bishop sends it back ("You know what to do with her"). A mechanic: if the case is referred, her protector's opinion of the *lord* falls less, but the outcome is the same.
5. **The record is rewritten afterwards.** Rashīd adds the confession that his earlier source lacked (p. 360). Fits the mod's "hostile overlay" voice: a later chronicle event can "find" a confession that the player saw never happened. This is good material for the narrator's distance from the accusers.
6. **Shame differs by audience.** For the Mongols the charge was ordinary, for Persian clerks dishonourable (p. 361). The Latin analogue: in the penance phase a witchcraft charge against a noblewoman embarrasses her lord's house, and later it does not. That could be a modifier that flips with §5's phases.
7. **Feuds outlast the trial.** Toqdai was still raiding 28 years later (n. 76). After an execution, a kin-opinion malus or a feud hook for the dead woman's family.
8. **Execution methods: don't borrow.** Bloodless royal death is a steppe norm. Latin executions stay off-screen per the existing guardrails. Neither drowning in felt nor strangling goes into Ladies content.

### (b) Mongol-adjacent content: a court witchcraft-accusation event, 1240s–1260s

**Who it's for.** A character of the mod's faiths (most plausibly Yutai Dao: a daughter from the Hexi or North China ritual world) who serves as a diviner, physician or scribe at a Tengrist Mongol court. That is exactly the role Kamšā and Ṣadr al-Dīn held. **Window and trigger:** a ruler of Mongol culture (keys to verify: `mongol` culture, `tengri_pagan` faith, the Mongol-invasion story cycle's titles) with a **succession dispute or a rival royal kinsman in the court**, from about 1240 to about 1300. The trigger must be the political conflict, not the character's faith.

**Event sketch: "The Ayġāq Speaks"** (working title; avoid diacritics the font lacks, see `tools/check_glyphs.py`. "Aygaq" or "the informer" is safer).

- Setup: a royal kinsman at court has fallen out with the khan. An informer claims that the kinsman has hired sorcery, and that the charm was written by someone in his household, possibly the player's character.
- Options:
  1. **Stand by the accused** (honour; risk sharing their fate). The real Ṣadr al-Dīn was killed "with the pretext" of a charm (p. 338).
  2. **Testify** (the khan's favour; the accused's kin become enemies and seek "his blood" years later; a feud hook).
  3. **Flee with the accused's troops** (lose holdings; arrive at a rival court with a debt of gratitude). Historically the refugees went to Berke, to the Mamluks or to the Indian frontier (pp. 353–354).
  4. **Accuse the khan's own diviner** (high-stakes intrigue: the khan needs a scapegoat from his own side, p. 356).
- Outcomes: if convicted, death **off-screen**, with a line that "no blood was shed" (paraphrasing p. 345), never shown. Afterwards the court chronicle tells it differently (§7a.5).
- **Narration rule:** the event does not name Hülegü, Berke, Balagha, Tutar, Quli, Kamšā, Yakšā or Ṣadr al-Dīn, and does not make any real Chinggisid an accuser in the mod's story. Generic "the khan", "the khan's cousin". A loading tip or encyclopedia line may cite the real case as history, in the narrator's distant voice, without claiming anyone in it.
- **Optional second hook, "Too Short a Road":** a defender who argues the trial was rigged by pointing to the timing (the "fifteen days" logic of p. 352). An intrigue/learning check to save someone.

**The khatun-court variant** (Oghul Qaimish, c. 1250s; Fāṭima 1246) is already sketched as H4 "Fatima's Shadow" in `../east_mongol_sex_genghis.md`. This paper adds two things to H4: the charge comes when a **new ruler purges the old regime**; and the regent herself can be the accused, not only her favourite. One reservation about H4: it proposes the orifice-sewing as an outcome. That is Terbish's account, not Golev's; it is a genital-adjacent punishment and should stay a reported rumour, off-screen at most, never a mechanic applied to a daughter.

### (c) Women ritual specialists and gender

- Kamšā supports **only** a generic "foreign woman diviner at a khan's court" figure. That figure can be a character option (a Yutai daughter as court diviner or informer) with no claims about Mongol shamanism.
- Don't use Kamšā, the "qam" reading or Gatahan/Qadaqan to suggest gender-variant shamans. Nothing in this paper supports it, and the mod's guardrail (`../../later_starts.md`: Cuman shamans ruled out because the evidence is modern ethnography and leads to the "third gender" trap) stands.

## 8. Guardrails specific to this paper

1. **No "third gender" or "shamaness" romanticism.** Kamšā is a sorceress-spy in a hostile Arab report with a doubtful "qam" gloss. No office, no gender variance, no continuity to anything modern.
2. **No living community.** Nothing here about modern Mongolian, Buryat, Tuvan or Kazakh shamans; no claim that any of them continues or is a faith of the mod.
3. **The narrator never claims real people.** None of Hülegü, Berke, the Jöchid princes, Oghul Qaimish, Qadaqač, Töregene, Fāṭima, Kamšā, Yakšā or Ṣadr al-Dīn is a daughter or an adherent of any of the mod's faiths, in any text.
4. **Grigor's "evil Jewish doctor".** Grigor of Akner says a Jewish physician killed over thirty children trying to cure Quli's gout (n. 57, p. 346). This is a hostile trope and Golev rejects it. **It must never appear**, not even as a rumour or a hostile chronicle, and no witchcraft or poison accusation in any Mongol or Ladies event targets a Jewish character (standing project rule).
5. **Jūzjānī and the Mamluk writers** have anti-Mongol and pro-Berke religious slants (n. 53, n. 98). Berke's Islam was a **pose of vengeance**, not the cause (Lane, n. 97). Steppe events must not frame Muslim conversion as the source of the witch-hunt.
6. **Violence stays off-screen.** No strangling, drowning or flogging is shown. Numbers are fine in a distant voice ("seven hundred, one chronicle claims", n. 87) but are flagged as one source's claim.
7. **No new pseudo-Mongol law.** Don't invent yasa clauses. Use only "the khan's law" in the abstract; Golev and Terbish both treat the yasa's text as uncertain.

## 9. Open questions

1. **The earlier cases.** Read Golev, "Witch-hunt in the Mongol Empire: Prolegomena", *CAS Working Papers* 8 (2015–16): 3–36, and "Witchcraft and Politics in the Court of the Great Khan … The Case of Fāṭima Khatun", *CEU Medieval Annual* 23 (2017). They should cover Töregene and Fāṭima (1246), the 1251–52 purge, Shiremün's mother, and the drowning of witches in full.
2. **Endicott-West 2000**, "Notes on Shamans, Fortune-tellers and Yin-yang Practitioners and Civil Administration in Yüan China" (in Amitai-Preiss and Morgan, eds., *The Mongol Empire and its Legacy*, pp. 224–239). This is the best lead for **who** the court ritual specialists were, including women and Chinese practitioners, and is directly useful for a Yutai daughter at a Mongol court.
