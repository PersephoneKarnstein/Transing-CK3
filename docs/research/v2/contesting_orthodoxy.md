# Witches for v2: Kallestrup and Toivo, eds., *Contesting Orthodoxy* (2017)

Scope: a reading for the v2 "Good Ladies" faith (`witch_design.md`, esp. §5, not edited here), for Bædling's Christian-court reactions, and for anything the volume offers or forbids for the Adelphai and Ha-Nehpakhot. It was read against `faith_design.md` §16 (rule 25 as amended 2026-10-25) and `witch_even_ezra.md` (skimmed, not edited).

Pronouns: every modern scholar below is *they* (none is stated in the book). Medieval people are named only as historical record; the narrator claims none of them.

Quote rule: every verbatim quote has 25 words or fewer. Everything else is paraphrase.

Citations: the EPUB carries **no printed-page markers**. Citations give the chapter number (as in the table in §2), the section heading where the chapter has one, and the item number where an author numbers a list. Each chapter's printed start page, from the table of contents, is in the §2 table, so a reader with the print book can find the place. The EPUB was unzipped and stripped to text in the scratchpad (`…/scratchpad/witch/co_*.txt`), never in the repo.

## 1. Bibliographic line and verdict

**Bibliographic line.** Louise Nyholm Kallestrup and Raisa Maria Toivo, eds., *Contesting Orthodoxy in Medieval and Early Modern Europe: Heresy, Magic and Witchcraft*. Palgrave Historical Studies in Witchcraft and Magic. Cham: Palgrave Macmillan (Springer Nature), 2017. ISBN 978-3-319-32384-8; e-ISBN 978-3-319-32385-5; DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-32385-5. 14 chapters, bibliography pp. 301–44. Proceedings of the 2010 Odense symposium "Witchcraft, Heresy and Natural Philosophy".

**Verdict: useful, but mostly for procedure, vocabulary and the Nordic courts. It has nothing on the night company itself.**

- **No Ladies evidence.** No chapter discusses Diana, Herodias, Holda, Abundia or Oriente before 1453. The Canon *Episcopi* appears only as a later authority, cited by Geiler in 1509 (ch. 10) and by Ponzinibio and Alciato in the 1510s (ch. 11). Never cite this book for the Ladies' beliefs.
- **The strongest chapters for the mod** are Mitchell (ch. 3: dated Nordic heresy and sorcery cases, 1264–1517, with the church's own words) and Kieckhefer (ch. 5: how a tolerated devotion becomes condemned "magic" by context). Caciola (ch. 2: a belief the Church tolerated without endorsing) and de Blécourt (ch. 13: Low Countries witchcraft c. 1400–1426 as rumour, slander and fines) follow.
- **Pre-1453 share:** about half the volume. Chapters 4, 8, 10, 11, 12 and 14 are 1490–1692. They give only retrospective context, or guardrails.
- **No Murray.** Murray is not cited. The volume's framing of the first witch trials is Cohn and Kieckhefer (demonized magic plus the anti-heretic machinery), which matches `witch_design.md` §1.
- **Beguines and visionaries:** thin. The only beguine item is Vadstena 1506 (ch. 3). Visionary women appear only in Kieckhefer's survey of tolerated angel devotion (ch. 5).

## 3. Usable pre-1453 material

### 3a. A tolerated belief about the night (ch. 2, Caciola)

- **The category the Ladies sit in before c. 1230.** Medieval ghost and revenant stories "occupied a capacious middle ground of toleration without endorsement, an unusually ambiguous emplacement for such a significant area of thought" (ch. 2, opening section). They were "not quite orthodox" but "never were declared heretical" (ch. 2, "Thinking with the Dead"). They survived because they served the Church: they gave first-hand proof of life after death.
- **Two readings of one story by one bishop.** Thietmar of Merseburg (Chronicon, c. 1013–18) first told the night masses of the dead (Walsleben, Magdeburg, Deventer) as proof of the Resurrection (Book I). Four or five years later (Book VII) he called such sights demons: "The cunning enemy of mortals appears in the image of dead people and tries to delude us" (ch. 2, "A Gathering of the Dead, or of Demons?"). He never went back to revise Book I, so both readings stand in the same book. Caciola calls the shift Gregorian to Augustinian.
- **Night-time companies of the dead** keep a social order: they hold mass, carry candles, give offerings and in one tale marry and raise children (Henry of Erfurt's Reyneke). Caciola reads this as a pagan, material afterlife absorbed into Christian tales (ch. 2, "Marvelous, Ancient Sacrifices"). For the mod this belongs to the army of the dead, which `witch_design.md` §8.10 keeps out of doctrine. It is useful only as **the neighbouring belief that churchmen also tolerated**.
- **Montaillou (Fournier register, 1320s):** the dead move between churches as penance (ch. 2, same section). The Pyrenees in 1319–25 are already a design county (`b_pamiers`).

### 3b. Nordic heresy, sorcery and lay cult (ch. 3, Mitchell; ch. 6, Søndergaard)

**Terms (ch. 3, "Terminology").**
- In 1277 (the Norwegian crown–church settlement), Latin *heresis* is rendered in Old Norse as *villa eða vantru*, "error or false belief". *Villa* also means going astray.
- Other words: *aftrúa*, *falstrú*, *rangtrúaðr* ("wrong-faithed"). In Danish and Swedish the later loanword is *kätteri/kættere*, which came from the Cathars' name through Middle Low German. It also covered sexual offences and magic, especially incest.
- Two early-14th-century *Hauksbók* sermons (in Ælfric's and Martin of Braga's line) condemn magic (*taufr*, *galdra*), sorcerers and *völur*, land-spirit veneration, love potions, divination and "sitting out" to wake trolls. They label these *diofuls villa* ("the devil's delusion"), *fianda villa oc diofuls þionasta* and *hindr vitni*. The voice is paternal: "I instruct you as a father should instruct his children" (ch. 3, "Terminology").

**Definitions.**
- Grosseteste (via Matthew Paris): heresy is "an opinion chosen by human perception contrary to holy Scripture, publicly avowed and obstinately defended" (ch. 3, opening section).
- Lesser grades below first-degree heresy: *sententia haeresi proxima*, *propositio theologice erronea*, *sententia de haeresi suspecta* (ch. 3, opening section).

**Dated cases (ch. 3, "Heresy in Medieval Scandinavia"; list numbers as Mitchell gives them).**

| Date | Place | What | Use |

Mitchell's conclusion: heresy and apostasy were distinct in canon law but carried the same fire. For the crowd, "death by fire was purely and clearly the ultimate exercise of social control" (ch. 3, "Concluding Discussion"). Lea's view that Martin V's 1421 inquisitor for the three kingdoms left no trace is noted. There was no standing Nordic Inquisition. Bishops did the work.

**Spells (ch. 6, Søndergaard; no section headings).**
- **Ribe rune-stick (c. 1300):** "Earth I ask to help / and the high heaven / sun and holy Mary / and the God-king himself…". It binds nine fever-demons on a black stone in the sea.
- **Romdrup (Jutland) charm:** conjures "male elves and female elves" away from one Niels. Søndergaard reads it as evidence of clerical use.
- **Odense lead amulet (c. 1300):** for a woman, Åse. More than 75 lead amulets are known in Scandinavia, against very few elsewhere.
- **The Phol-and-Wodan horse charm** is later Christianised as "Our Lord was riding the hill". Søndergaard: "the magic form seems to be constant whereas the protagonists in the spell itself are interchangeable, that is, 'Christianized'" (ch. 6).
- **Ragnhildr (1325)** in Søndergaard's version: sentenced to fast for life and to a seven-year pilgrimage outside Norway, after abjuring. Her sentence was eased because she was penitent and called "moon sick".

### 3c. How a devotion becomes "magic" (ch. 5, Kieckhefer)

- **Tolerated devotion and condemned magic shared every feature:** apocryphal angel names, neutral spirits, angels as guides to rapture. Holy women's lives are full of them: Elizabeth of Schönau, Hadewijch, Angela of Foligno, Adelheid Langmann, Francesca Romana, Lidwina, Birgitta (ch. 5, "Devotion to Angels…: The Scope of Toleration"). No clear criterion divided the two.
- **What tipped the balance was setting.** "The three things that matter most in religious culture are context, context and context" (ch. 5, "The Hazards of Conjuration"). The setting is not the believer's to control: "an observer can always recontextualize someone's act or statement, framing it in a way that was not at first intended" (same section). And "eccentric veneration of angels might be tolerated more in a saint's vita than in a work that brought necromancy to the reader's mind" (same section).
- **Recoding milestones inside the window.** In 1398 the Paris theology faculty condemned 27 propositions on the magic arts. In 1402 Gerson argued that any effect not explicable by miracle or by nature must be held "superstitious and suspect" of demonic involvement (ch. 5, "Four Texts…"). The burden of proof flips: an unexplained good is now presumed suspect.
- **Why spirits that move in companies worried churchmen.** Angels (and demons) differ from saints in three ways. They are usually collectivities, they move about, and their presence is unknowable (ch. 5, "The Hazards of Conjuration"). This is Kieckhefer on angels, not on the Ladies. It is a fair analogy for why a travelling company of women unsettled clergy, but label it as inference.
- **Older regulation.** The Council of Laodicea (4th c.) forbade leaving church to "name angels" and form congregations. The *Admonitio generalis* (789) banned unknown angel names. Aldebert (745) was condemned partly for invoking Uriel, Raguel and others (ch. 5, "Efforts to Regulate…").

### 3d. Low Countries and Paris, c. 1390–1426 (ch. 13, de Blécourt)

- **Ascribed, not practised.** Neighbours ascribed bewitchment, chiefly of milk and butter. The counter-charms were practised; the bewitchments "were only ascribed" (ch. 13, "Contemporary Concepts"). And: "In a number of circumstances a witchcraft accusation was just that, something that was only talked about" (ch. 13, "Ascribed Witchcraft").
- **Dated cases.**

| Date | Place | Case | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1391 | Paris | A cunning woman and an innkeeper's wife: wax image, love charms, a toad; they raised "the Enemy" | Burned |
| 1398 | Paris | A royal physician confessed to demon-invocation and ritual magic around Charles VI | Burned |
| c. 1399, 1423 | Berlin | Women for poisons and *toverighe* | Burned |
| 1408 | Leuven | A sex worker buried a thief's hand under her threshold to draw clients | Pilgrimage to Santiago |
| 1414, 1417 | Turnhout | Slanders of milk-witching | — |
| 1417 | Antwerp | A woman claimed to unwitch milk and butter | "Composition" (a payment to avoid prosecution) |
| 1417 | Utrecht | A midwife practised *toverie* and "unbecoming things" | Banished a mile from the city for 50 years |
| 1426 | Antwerp | A couple, the same claim | "Composition" |

- **The language boundary.** Witch trials of the new, diabolical kind ran on the French side of the Burgundian lands (Arras, Metz, Gorze). Dutch-speaking Flanders stayed "relatively free of witch trials". A rare execution there turned on harm done, not on a pact (ch. 13, "The Waning of the Middle Ages").
- **Popular belief versus learned theory** (Kieckhefer, as quoted there): ordinary people held that the witch's power was her own, while the learned insisted it was the demons' (ch. 13, "Ascribed Witchcraft").
- **Kieckhefer's caution, via de Blécourt:** most Swiss trials were in the urban Swiss midlands, not the Alps (ch. 13, "The Waning of the Middle Ages").

### 3e. The woman of Endor (ch. 9, Zika)

- Before the 16th c. she is a *phitonissa*, a diviner or necromancer, "seldom" a devil's ally. In most of 45 images she stands behind Saul (ch. 9).
- The earliest images fall in or near the start window: Bamberg c. 1170/1180, and the **Gumbertus Bible, Regensburg c. 1180–85**, where she lifts Samuel from the tomb. Regensburg is a planned holy site.
- Her own agency grows after c. 1400: NYPL c. 1445; Regensburg, Furtmeyer workshop c. 1465. A Boucicaut-workshop image (Paris, c. 1400–24) adds a bat-demon. Zika ties it to the climate after the 1398 Paris *Conclusions*.
- **The World Chronicles** (Rudolf von Ems, mid-13th c.; Heinrich von München, early 14th c.) end the story with Josephus's "effusive eulogy of this necromancer as an exemplar of the virtues of generosity and compassion" (ch. 9, closing pages). She feeds the broken king. This reading circulated in German until the 1470s.

### 3f. Smaller items

- **The divining rod is late (ch. 7, Dillinger).** No finding-rod exists in any source before Tepl's *Ackermann* (c. 1400). Medieval anti-superstition polemic never mentions one. Hazel rods conjured to find treasure appear in Swabian spells of the later 15th c. The Lombard staff that revives slaughtered beasts (`witch_design.md` §2) is a different object. Keep it that way.
- **Lausanne paradigm (ch. 1, "Witchcraft").** The first full witch concept was written down in 1428 and 1442 around Lausanne. The trials of 1448 there called the accused "modern Waldensian heretics". This is Kieckhefer's label, cited in the introduction.
- **Bernardino of Siena (ch. 10).** His preaching (1420s) urged audiences to denounce witches, sorcerers and soothsayers. Siena, Perugia and Todi sharpened their laws, and executions followed.
- **Ambrogio Vignati (ch. 11),** a jurist writing by the mid-15th c. (published 1581): witches' confessions cannot be evidence against accomplices.
- **Confessors' manuals (ch. 10).** From the 14th c. onward, "Mirrors of Confession" and Decalogue commentaries multiplied. Their lists of superstitions go back to Gratian. Voltmer: from the early 15th c. theologians began tying these "pagan arts" to devilish witchcraft.
- **Old secular law (ch. 10, statement 11).** The *Decretum*/Roman-law text *De maleficis* threatened death only for magic that sickened or killed, while "beneficia bringing health was praised". Geiler (1509) attacked exactly this. Before the sect phase, healing by the Ladies' people sat on the safe side of secular law.

## 4. Implications for the Good Ladies' four-phase Church response

All of these are design proposals. Churchmen in events stay generic (rule: no historical person claimed). Rule 25 holds in every voice: before c. 1430 clergy say "illusion" and "the devil's delusion", never "pact". No sabbat, synagogue, familiar, broom, or bodily flight to a gathering, at any date.

### Phase 1, Illusion (to c. 1230)

- **Frame: toleration without endorsement** (ch. 2). The confessor's question is pastoral, not judicial. The belief is "not quite orthodox" but useful, or at least not worth a fight. Add a line to the confessor event: *a priest who half-believes the dead walk is in no hurry to forbid your Lady.*
- **Event idea, "Two Books, One Bishop"** (after Thietmar): a cleric writes the Lady's night as a marvel one year and as a demon's trick a few years later, and never corrects the first page. The player's household may be recorded twice. Mechanic: a chronicle modifier that flips from neutral to "illusion" with no trial attached. Generic cleric; do not name Thietmar.
- **Procedure:** private confession and a penance (fast, alms). No written articles, no summons.

### Phase 2, Tools forged (c. 1230–1320)

- **The trigger is obstinacy, not belief.** Grosseteste's "publicly avowed and obstinately defended" (ch. 3) gives the mechanic. A woman who confesses the night and takes penance stays an erring Christian. One who *teaches* it publicly, or refuses correction, moves up the grades (ch. 3: *suspecta*, then *haeresi proxima*). Suggested suspicion ladder: *erronea* (spoken privately), *de haeresi suspecta* (taught to others), *haeresi proxima* (defended after correction).
- **Episcopal edict before trial.** Bergen 1319 is the model: a bishop forbids the "offerings, pilgrimages … fasting or prayer" of a lay cult without trying anyone. Event idea, "The Bishop's Letter": a diocesan letter forbids laying the Lady's table on named nights. Options: obey (lose the tenet's household bonus for some years); keep the table in secret (secrecy check); argue (raises the suspicion grade). This date, 1319, matches the Pamiers register, so a Latin bishop issuing such a letter c. 1300–20 is in period.
- **Learned recoding starts in the schools** (`witch_even_ezra.md` §5) but stays words, not trials. Ch. 5 adds the mechanism that matters for later: a devotion is judged by what it sits beside. A household that keeps the Lady's table *and* consults a known conjurer is read through the conjurer.

### Phase 3, Recoding (c. 1320–1430)

- **Penance with a condition; the relapse is fatal.** Ragnhildr (Bergen 1324–25) got penance plus a clause: fail it and stand condemned of heresy before the secular arm (ch. 3; ch. 6). This is the design's "Second Accusation" (§6 event 11) in Nordic dress, and it is attested. Mechanic: the first conviction sets a hidden "under condition" flag. A second accusation with the flag set goes straight to the secular arm.
- **The burden of proof flips** (Paris 1398; Gerson 1402; ch. 5). After c. 1400, an unexplained blessing on a house (abundance, a healing) can itself be the evidence. Event idea, "Too Well-Kept a House": the Lady's blessing, the faith's reward, becomes the accuser's proof. It fires only after 1398 and only where a university or friars are near (Paris, Lombardy).
- **Who accuses: neighbours, over the household economy** (ch. 13). The Ladies bless clean, open-handed houses with plenty. The attested accusation of c. 1400 is the mirror image: a neighbour's churn fails and her cow gives water, so the prosperous house must have drawn the butter away. Event idea, "The Churn": a neighbour slanders the player's household. Regional outcome:
  - Flanders and Brabant: slander suit or a "composition" payment (gold), no trial;
  - Paris and the French-speaking lands after 1391: a real risk of the stake if any object (wax, a written charm) is found;
  - Utrecht-style banishment as a middle outcome.
- **Healers are safest early.** Secular law punished harmful magic and praised healing (ch. 10, on the *De maleficis* text). The Ladies' healer and finder (`witch_design.md` §4, non-holy standing) is protected by secular courts until preaching turns that around: Bernardino in Italy, 1420s.
- **Preacher-driven waves in Italy** (ch. 10): a travelling friar's Lenten preaching raises the accusation chance in a county for some years and tightens the town statute. This fits the design's Lombardy-first rule (§8.9) and gives it a carrier.

### Phase 4, The sect (from c. 1430)

- **The engine is the accomplice-naming chain.** The objection that witches' confessions cannot convict those they name (Vignati, by the mid-15th c.; ch. 11) only makes sense because inquisitors were convicting on them. Mechanic: a convicted Lady's woman can "name" other believers. Each named believer faces a trial check. A lay judge (podestà) can block it (mixed jurisdiction, ch. 11), at the cost of a quarrel with the inquisitor.
- **"The body never left the bed" becomes a legal defence.** The Episcopi line, as later jurists used it, separates what was seen awake from what was seen "while sleeping and dreaming" (Alciato, c. 1516, quoted in ch. 11; post-window, so use only the logic). This fits tenet 2 (the Body Keeps the House) exactly. **The household member who watched the body is the defence witness.** Inquisitors may reject her; a lay judge may accept her. This turns the faith's own rite into its best protection, without breaking rule 25. The accusers say "gatherings"; the defence says "she lay here all night".
- **The old register survives beside the new.** Even in 1509, Geiler preached the Diana ride and "a nocturnal banquet of the good dames, meeting secretly in human houses" as demonic delusion (ch. 10, statements 3–4), while also preaching the pact. So after 1430 the "illusion" verdict should stay available in Rhineland pulpits. The sect charge spreads unevenly (Valais, Lombardy, French-speaking Burgundian lands first; Flanders hardly at all, ch. 13).
- **Women-only household gatherings become suspect** (Geiler on lying-in feasts, ch. 10, statement 10; 1509, so treat as a late tendency). After c. 1430, the Lady's table with only women present raises suspicion more than a mixed household's table. Keep it late and regional.
- **Vocabulary.** Accusers may say "sect", "night gatherings", "the devil's delusion", "error". Do not use "Vaudois"/"Waldensian" for the Ladies (see §6).

### What the book does not support

- There is no evidence here of any trial of a Ladies' believer as such before 1453. The Milan trials (1384/1390) are not in this volume. Keep citing Hutton and Lecouteux for them.
- Inquisitorial procedure before 1430 (citation, articles, abjuration) is attested here only in Nordic **episcopal** cases (Hemming 1442, Ragnhildr 1324–25). Do not import Roman Inquisition practice (ch. 1, 4, 12: 1542 onward) into the window.

## 5. Bædling: Christian Nordic courts

- **A heathen cannot be a heretic** (ch. 1, "Heresy": "infidels, pagans and other outsiders could not be heretics"). This splits Bædling's opponents in two:
  - *Unbaptised Bædling* face the conversion arm: outlawry, missionaries, the king.
  - *Baptised people who keep a völva's or seiðr practice* are in *villa eða vantru*, "error or false belief". If they cast off baptism, they face apostasy, which carried the same fire as heresy (ch. 3, "Concluding Discussion").
  - Mechanic: the reaction depends on whether the character was baptised.
- **The priest's voice** is the *Hauksbók* sermon's: paternal, a list-maker. It names "sitting out" to wake trolls, land-spirit offerings, *völur*, love potions and divination, and calls them *diofuls villa*, the devil's delusion (ch. 3, "Terminology"). This suits a 13th–14th-c. Norwegian or Icelandic court chaplain. Before c. 1430 the clergy call a seer's seat delusion and sin, never a pact (rule 25).
- **The escalation is by bishop and by century** (ch. 3, case list). Use these as the reactions of Christian Nordic courts to Bædling acts:
  - *to c. 1300:* sermon and penance; outlawry under the church sections of the law (`norse_mitchell_witchcraft_magic.md` already covers the laws);
  - *1319:* a bishop's edict against a lay cult. **Bædling use:** an edict against offerings at a Bædling site, without a trial;
  - *1324–25:* penance with a clause that a relapse means the secular arm, possibly with a pilgrimage abroad (seven years, ch. 6). **Bædling use:** exile-pilgrimage as a punishment that takes a Bædling character off the map for years, not death;
  - *1343, 1407:* burnings in Iceland and Greenland. Use only "burned on the bishop's sentence for sorcery". The book's details are banned (§6);
  - *after 1450:* the "devil Odin" framing of Stockholm 1484 and 1492. It is post-window, but it shows where a 15th-c. Swedish court's language was heading. Use sparingly, after c. 1430 only.
- **Polyphony, not purity** (ch. 6). Ordinary Christian households kept spells that mixed Mary with earth, sun and sky, or Christ with the old horse charm. Priests used them too (Romdrup). So a Christian court's reaction need not be a clean line, and a Christian neighbour may carry a lead amulet. Flavour line for a Bædling event: *the words change; the charm does not.* That paraphrases Søndergaard (ch. 6); it is not a quote.
- **Ribe c. 1300 as a seeded artifact.** A rune-stick charm against fever (earth, heaven, sun, Mary) fits a Danish Bædling-adjacent household at the right date. Present it as Christian-pagan polyphony, not as Bædling scripture.
- **The continental gaze** (ch. 14). Adam of Bremen (1070s) makes the North a land of storm-raising sorcerers and of "wild, ravenous women" (Hagen's paraphrase; *terra feminarum*). Walsperger's 1448 map says northern Scandinavia is where "demons frequently appear in the guise of humans – whom they stalk". A visiting German or Italian cleric can voice this. A Nordic bishop should not; his voice is the *Hauksbók* one.

## 6. Other faiths and guardrails

### Adelphai Dorleyaiou (Byzantine)

- **Laodicea (4th c.):** a Greek canon against leaving church to name angels and form congregations (ch. 5). It is a period-correct hostile authority for an Orthodox bishop against a sisterhood that meets apart. Use it as a paraphrase, never in the narrator's voice.
- **Isaac of Antioch (5th c.)** cursing priests who use variant angel names (ch. 5) is a usable Eastern hostile register.
- The Adelphai's own angels should keep to Michael, Gabriel and Raphael, or stay unnamed, so the faith does not read as angel magic.

### Ha-Nehpakhot (Provençal Jewish current): do-not-use list

- Kieckhefer says angel magic was "more often indebted to Jewish tradition" (*Liber iuratus*, *Liber Raziel*; ch. 5, "Four Texts…"). **Never** let any voice make Jews or Jewish texts the source of illicit magic.
- Aslak Bolt's 1435 statute equates Saturday rest with "Jews and heathens" (ch. 3, case 6). Do not use.
- The Moralised Bibles identify Saul with the Jews as Christ's persecutors (ch. 9). Do not use.
- Saccardini is described as "a converted Jew" (ch. 4). Do not use.
- The one neutral item: German Jews knocked on the synagogue door in the morning to warn the dead who worshipped there at night (ch. 2, "A Gathering of the Dead…"). It is Ashkenazi, not Provençal. Use it only if the user wants an Ashkenazi contact flavour, and only as a gentle custom.

### General guardrails from this reading

1. **Rule 25 against the sources.** The book's own material includes the 1343 Kirkjubær annal (written pact with the devil, host thrown in the privy), Ragnhildr's "surrendered to the devil" and wedding-night impotence charm, the 1490 impotence case, Geiler's incubi and stolen semen, and the Paris 1391 devil-raising. **None** of these goes into loc in any voice:
   - no pact;
   - no stolen-member or impotence line (rule 25's castration clause);
   - no host desecration.
   A Nordic hostile voice may say "burned for sorcery" and stop there.
2. **No Cathar or Waldensian ties.** *Kätteri* comes from "Cathar". A Nordic voice may use the word, but no voice may imply the Ladies, the Bædling or anyone else *are* Cathars. Likewise, keep "Vaudois"/"modern Waldensians" (Lausanne 1448) out of the Ladies' accusers' text, by analogy with rule 25's Cathar clause. Flag it for the user (§7).
3. **Rule 25's "illusion" term is attested.** *Diofuls villa* (Norse), "the cunning enemy … tries to delude us" (Thietmar), the "good dames'" banquet as delusion (Geiler). Use these, not "pact", before c. 1430.
4. **No Murray.** Nothing in the book supports a surviving pagan cult. Caciola's "afterlife for paganism" means beliefs about the dead that persisted inside Christianity, not an organised religion. Søndergaard's pagan elements are formulas inside Christian spells. Do not cite either for continuity.
5. **Living peoples.** The Sami (ch. 14) are a living people. Do not make them, or *noaidi*, the Bædling or any faith's sorcerers. Do not reproduce Hagen's quoted slurs or wind-knot selling as Bædling practice; wind knots are Olaus Magnus, 1555, after the window.
6. **Historical people.** The False Margaret, Ragnhildr, Hemming, the Kirkjubær nun, Kolgrímr, Machtelt, Luutgaert and Juliane (characters in *Die Hexe*) are record or fiction. The narrator does not claim any of them. Events use generic or fictional people built on the pattern.
7. **Fiction as fiction.** *Die Hexe* (c. 1410) is a farce, and its three women were played by men (ch. 13). Do not use it as evidence of practice. Do not make a daughter into the farce's man-in-a-dress joke (rule 5). Its value is the comic, sceptical *tone* of Flemish towns.
8. **Sex work.** The Leuven case (1408, a thief's hand under a sex worker's threshold) can appear only as a neighbour's practice handled with care, under the sex-work memory. Never as the Ladies' rite, and without the body-part detail on screen.
9. **Late sources stay late.** Geiler (1509), Ponzinibio (1511), Alciato (c. 1516), Palladius and Hemmingsen (Denmark, 1540s–), Olaus Magnus (1555) give logic and tendency only. No dated event cites them before their year.

## 7. Open questions

1. **Waldensian vocabulary.** Should rule 25's "no Cathar ties" clause be widened to "no Cathar or Waldensian ties", so that "Vaudois" never labels the Ladies? Recommended: yes.
2. **The Bishop's Letter (phase 2).** Is a diocesan ban on the table, without a trial, a good fit for the c. 1250–1320 window in the Latin West? This book attests it only for Bergen 1319 (a different cult). Check a Latin synodal statute against *mensae* left for the *bonae dominae* before building. Candidates: the Paris-region and Rhineland statutes in Hutton and Lecouteux, or Burchard's *Corrector* as reused.
3. **Ragnhildr in Bædling.** `norse_mitchell_witchcraft_magic.md` already uses her case. Should her exile-pilgrimage sentence be the standard Nordic non-lethal punishment for relapsed Bædling? Recommended: as one option among outlawry, fine and pilgrimage.
4. **The dream defence (phase 4).** Is the household witness to the sleeping body a defence in-game before c. 1430 too? Recommended: no, because no trial happens then. From 1430 it should weigh more before lay judges than before inquisitors.
5. **The False Margaret's cult** (Bergen, 1301–19) is an attested lay cult around a woman, banned by edict. Could it seed a Nordic flavour event independent of any faith? It is not a daughter's story; the record gives no gender question. Recommended: optional, generic, with no claim.
6. **Endor's generosity.** Should the faith's own voice cite "the woman of Endor who fed the king" as a precedent for the table? The World Chronicles (13th–15th c., German) carry the reading, so it is in period for German culture. It is a hostile figure in clerical Latin, however. Recommended: German culture only, faith voice only, after c. 1250.
7. **Sources to read next** (from this book's notes): Kieckhefer, *European Witch Trials* (1976), for the 1300–1500 trial calendar; Utz Tremp, *Von der Häresie zur Hexerei* (2008), for Lausanne and Fribourg; Bailey, *Battling Demons* (2003), on Nider; Schmitt, *Ghosts in the Middle Ages* (1998), on Thietmar; Hansen, *Quellen* (1901), used with care because it includes Lamothe-Langon's forgeries (ch. 13).
