# Orientalism guardrails for the Japanese faith (Treat 1999, Hinton 2023)

Scratchpad note, 2026-10-24. Nothing in the repo was edited. Audits `japan_design.md` ("JD"; events "e1"–"e16"). Writing rules are `docs/faith_design.md` §16 ("rule n"). Builds on `sw_books_b.md` ("SWB"; its Principles 9–11 and §5a: foxes, the gloss for *asobi*, no geisha, no docile or temptress singer, no exotic garnish, the emperor's summons as a commission). Those points are cited, not repeated. Quotes are verbatim and 25 words or fewer. Page numbers are the printed ones.

**Standing.** Both authors are Western scholars writing about the Western gaze. Neither is an insider to Japanese trans women's history. They outrank nobody on what the houses were. They are authorities on the *frames* a Western-made game will bring, and on how those frames came about. Mitsuhashi stays the authority on trans women's recognition in Japan (`mitsuhashi_theory.md`). Tsuji, Wakita, Okimoto and Goodwin stay the authorities on the houses.

## 1. Treat, *Great Mirrors Shattered: Homosexuality, Orientalism, and Japan* (Oxford UP, 1999; series Ideologies of Desire)

**Form.** A memoir of a gay American professor of Japanese literature in Tokyo in 1986–87, during the Japanese AIDS panic. It is cut together with short pieces on Hearn, Loti, Barthes, Benedict, Burton, Saikaku and Said. It has no chapter called "Orientalism". The argument runs through the montage. Treat says he "freely invented names" (p. x), so the book is a witness to the gaze, not a source on Japan.

**Argument.**
- Orientalism is desire as method. It is "the Western study of everywhere else" (p. ix), and the scholar's "desire for knowledge is easily confused with his desire to possess" (p. x). The gay Orientalist is Treat's case, and he includes himself.
- The mirror cuts both ways. Japan looks back: "a nation of voyeurs", with *kaimami*, "a view stolen through a screen" (p. 7). It also defines itself as remote and immune: "Of course, we don't have homosexuals here" (a Japanese scientist, p. 8). The West's "paradise" and Japan's "none here" are two mirrors of one gaze.
- Tolerant Japan is a fantasy with a pedigree: Saikaku's "In those days ... having fun was easy" (p. 14); "An American man's paradise" (p. 11); Capote "declared himself to be in heaven" (p. 209); Benedict's homosexuals who "have been especially acclaimed" in other cultures (p. 115) and her "abnormals" who "function at ease and with honor" (pp. 203–4); "They are not Puritans" (Benedict, p. 165).
- The older Western move was the reverse: "men from the West condemned the East, above all else, for its lewd perversions" (p. 211). The Jesuit Vilela blamed Kūkai for inventing the "accursed sin" (pp. 116–17). Praise and blame are the same projection.
- The Oriental "transvestite" as sign. Barthes: "The Oriental transvestite does not copy Woman but signifies her ... translation, not transgression" (p. 104). Transvestism aims "to transform oneself into a describable object—and not into an introspectible subject" (p. 203). Treat shows Barthes using Japan to write himself.
- The Orient as distance and archaism. Barthes: "Desire always proceeds toward an extreme archaism, where the greatest historical distance assures the greatest unreality" (pp. 206–7). Said, quoted: the Orient "is old and distant", so the teacher's display is "a restoration" of its "most representative parts" (p. 86).
- Emptiness and ciphers. Barthes's "Empty Center" of Tokyo: "An empty Japan is proof of our own fullness" (p. 211). In Western eyes the Japanese "are not individuals to us, only ciphers of a still strange and exotic race" (p. 99).
- Self-Orientalising. At the flea markets the antique is "Japan re-imported, quaintly Oriental now that foreigners want them, too" (p. 22).
- Against over-correction. Treat warns that the zeal for "rectifying" Western othering can "lose sight of" the point that "they are different" (p. 204). He asks why critics want so much to think the reputation is false: "Why do we want so much to think it is?" (p. 209).

**Tropes he names (for this mod):** paradise or tolerant Japan; perverse Japan; the "Oriental transvestite" as sign without interiority; Japan as archaic distance; the empty, mysterious centre; Japanese people as ciphers; "Old Japan" as a "delicate little wonder-world of sylphs and fairies" (Chamberlain, p. 23); weird, mysterious script (Hearn, p. 60); boys "with their faces painted, and feminine gestures" in a Dutch traveller's account (p. 98), the Western hostile gaze on Edo youths; self-Orientalising re-import.

## 2. Hinton, *The Japanese in the Western Mind: A Psychological History of the Cultural Other* (Routledge, 2023)

**Argument.** Stereotypes are not mental errors. They are cultural constructions that track "the state of politico-economic relations between groups" (Prothro and Melikian, p. 10). Western views of Japan therefore "tell us just as much – if not more" about Westerners (p. 1). Over 500 years the Japanese were cast as white and civilised, then yellow; as artisans, as children, as fanatics, as imitators, as corporate samurai, as weird otaku, as cool (pp. 134–41). Each image explained something unexpected in Western terms. A fixed "national character" is "a fruitless and academically bankrupt approach" (p. 141). A stereotypical claim is "a denial of history" (Pickering, p. 17). Hinton finds Said's frame useful but "inadequate" for Japan, which was never colonised and was not always cast as inferior (p. 144).

**Tropes, with where they formed.**
- **The Orientalist binary** (ch. 1). The East is "ancient, mysterious, inscrutable, exotic and erotic – essentially feminine" (p. 6). Ryang: "the problem inherent in Japan anthropology has been over-exoticization and romanticization" (p. 7).
- **The introverted samurai** (ch. 1). Benedict's Janus-faced "gentle ... courteous civilian and the brutal, aggressive soldier" (p. 4). Her Japanese are "alien" (p. 5), with a fixed "cultural essence" (p. 6), and her book fed *nihonjinron* (p. 6). Hinton's correction: a cultural norm is misread as a character trait, the "fundamental attribution error" (pp. 14–16).
- **The land of opposites** (ch. 3). Frois's 1585 *Tratado* lists over 600 European–Japanese contrasts (p. 40). It includes Japanese women who "attach no importance to their virginity" (p. 40).
- **Perverse clergy** (ch. 3). Xavier and Frois charge the bonzes with sodomy and devil worship. Buddhist nuns' convents "are little better than a street full of whores" (Frois, p. 42).
- **The closed, frozen country** (chs. 4, 8). Kaempfer's "shut up" Japan. A Japanese translator renders it *sakoku* in 1801, and it passes into Japanese self-description (p. 72). Hence "the stationary character of eastern civilization" (1853, p. 75). Japan is "frozen in time" (p. 136).
- **Medieval Japan, Old Japan, fairyland** (ch. 5). "if the visitor wishes to see the Middle Ages, he must visit the Japanese Court" (1862, p. 76). Chamberlain's "Old Japan" is "between 1750 and 1850", which is Edo (p. 81). Knapp's Japan is "the land of dream and enchantment ... its people not of this world" (p. 82). In *The Mikado* "there are no Japanese people ... only objects come to life" (p. 77).
- **Doll, child, toy** (ch. 5). Bird's "doll-like women" (p. 83). The Japanese are "for all intents and purposes ... children" (Wood, p. 84). Loti: "Is it a woman or a doll?" and "this little doll, with whom I play" (p. 85). *The Mikado* gives its girls nursery names (p. 85).
- **Geisha and *musume*** (ch. 5). "a distorted combination of the geisha and tea-house girl dominated the image of the Japanese woman" (p. 85). Fraser's Western analogy is the Greek *hetaira* (p. 92 n. 9). The Western man leaves "a little girl back yonder" (p. 86). The submissive Japanese woman is praised against the suffrage movement (pp. 86–87). Michener's "perpetual woman" (p. 106).
- **Innocent nudity and Eden** (chs. 5, 8). Public bathing is read as prelapsarian innocence, with women as "Eves" and "Dianas" (pp. 83, 136).
- **Acceptance of death** (ch. 8). From Frois to Titsingh, seppuku and the Japanese "disregard of death" mark strangeness (pp. 135–36). Cherry blossoms scatter "with a Samurai's indifference to death" (Y. Okakura, p. 88).
- **Invented tradition from inside** (ch. 5). Nitobe's *Bushido* (1899) answers the child image by inventing a "timeless 'way of the warrior'" (Benesch, p. 90). Chamberlain notes the word is in no dictionary before 1900 (p. 90). "Scratch a Japanese ... and he will show a samurai" (Nitobe, p. 88). Japanese self-description can carry an Orientalist frame.
- **Mystic Zen** (chs. 6–7). Postwar Japan returns to "a feminized, ancient – mysterious Eastern" image (p. 106). "Zen" becomes a Western catch-all (pp. 120–22).
- **Weird, cute and *kawaii*** (ch. 7). Western media read manga's large-eyed adults as children and Japan as perverse (pp. 128–29). The "weird Japanese man" explains Western anxieties (p. 141).
- **Japan as playground** (ch. 5). A Japanese writer in 1889: "these foreigners regard Japan as the world's playground" (p. 84).

## 3. What these two books add beyond SWB

SWB already rules out the docile or temptress singer, sensory garnish, geisha and "floating world", the fox line near a daughter, and the emperor as romance. These books add six things:
1. The **tolerant/perverse pair** as one projection (Treat pp. 8, 115, 211). The mod must refuse both "Japan accepted her" and "Japan had none of her".
2. The **sign without a subject** (Barthes via Treat pp. 104, 203). This is the risk in the design's *mitate* framing.
3. **Archaism and timelessness** (Treat p. 207; Hinton pp. 72–81). "Old Japan" is Edo, so Edo imagery leaks in under the word "old".
4. **Infantilisation as the core image** (Hinton pp. 83–86). Doll, girl and child words are banned for these women, including "doll", the mod's own community word.
5. **Self-Orientalising Japanese sources** (Treat p. 22; Hinton pp. 72, 88–90). This covers Nitobe, *nihonjinron*, *sakoku*, and also Mitsuhashi's "uniquely Japanese" claims.
6. **Norm misread as trait** (Hinton pp. 14–16). Characters act from situations, never from "Japanese reserve" or "Japanese honour".

## 4. Guardrails (22)

Each item gives the rule, the trope it prevents, and the source.

1. **No Edo or modern imagery on Heian singers.** In loc, art and docs, never use: geisha, maiko, oiran, tea-house girl, *musume* or *mousmé*, kimono as a word, *obi*, the floating world, pleasure quarter, *ukiyo-e*, kabuki, *onnagata*, the shamisen, *ikebana*, the tea ceremony, Zen (Eisai's Rinzai dates from 1191), *bushidō*, ninja, *seppuku* as honour, cherry-blossom death, Mount Fuji, red lanterns or torii tableaux. *Trope:* "Old Japan" (Chamberlain's 1750–1850; Hinton p. 81; Treat p. 23). Extends GK §6 and SWB §5a.2.
2. **No timeless Japan.** Never use "ancient custom", "as it had always been", "for a thousand years", "unchanging" or "the old ways" in the narrator's voice. Date things. The houses rise, peak in 1178 and decline after 1186. *Trope:* the "stationary character of eastern civilization" (Hinton p. 75); archaism as unreality (Treat p. 207); Pickering's "denial of history" (Hinton p. 17).
3. **The narrator never says Japan was tolerant, and never says Japan had none of this.** The honest middle is the design's own: no source records such a woman in a house (JD §1). Mitsuhashi's "tolerant" line (M 357–58) is declined, as JD and `mitsuhashi_theory.md` already decide. *Trope:* paradise and acclamation (Benedict via Treat pp. 115, 204; Capote p. 209) and its twin denial (p. 8).
4. **No perverse East either.** Hostile sexual lines come only from named Japanese period speakers (rule 3). No narrator irony suggests the court was decadent. *Trope:* "lewd perversions" (Treat p. 211); the "perversion" of the bonzes (Hinton pp. 41–42).
5. **No eroticised exotic woman.** SWB Principles 9 and 11 hold. Beauty is praised only by a named man and framed as his taste (SWB §5a.4). *Trope:* "exotic and erotic – essentially feminine" (Hinton p. 6); the geisha composite (p. 85).
6. **No infantilisation, and the absolute bar on minors.** Never call these women "girl", "little", "dainty", "doll", "pretty thing" or "child of the house". Apply this even though "doll" is the mod's own word: the Japanese loc never uses it for Japanese women, because Loti's "Is it a woman or a doll?" (Hinton p. 85) and Bird's "doll-like women" (p. 83) are the trope itself. Nobody under 18 appears. That covers Otomae's age (K 277), the Dragon King's daughter (age eight, quoted only), *Torikaebaya*'s childhood, *chigo* and *jisha*. No *kawaii* styling in art (Hinton pp. 128–29).
7. **No inscrutable or mystic framing of shrines, shamans or diviners.** Religion is practice with logistics: vows, gifts, travel, a drum to mend. Use Goodwin's "interpenetration of sacred and secular" (G 90–102). Words to avoid: mysterious, eerie, otherworldly, ancient wisdom, spirits whisper, unknowable. *Trope:* Hearn's "weird, mysterious" script (Treat p. 60); "mysterious Eastern" (Hinton p. 106); Barthes's empty centre (Treat p. 211).
8. **No samurai-centric frame.** Warriors in this faith are patrons, creditors, proprietors and poets. Yorimasa is known for his verse. The Genpei war appears as lost patrons, floods and moved roads, never as glory. Never use "samurai code", *bushidō* or "warrior spirit". *Trope:* the introverted samurai (Hinton pp. 4–7); the invented, "timeless 'way of the warrior'" (p. 90).
9. **Never explain a character by "Japanese character".** Explain reticence, loyalty, shame or a choice to die by the situation, by persons and by stakes, never by "as was the Japanese way". *Trope:* a norm misread as a trait (Hinton pp. 14–16); Benedict's shame culture (pp. 3–5).
10. **No Japanese acceptance of death.** No suicide, ever (rule 17 and the brief guardrails). Yorimasa's end at the Byōdōin in 1180, Antoku at Dan-no-ura and the Nachi drownings are never narrated. No blossom or falling-petal death imagery. *Trope:* Frois to Benedict on seppuku (Hinton pp. 135–36); Okakura's blossoms (p. 88).
11. **Japanese terms are used for accuracy, not seasoning.**
    - At most one or two Japanese words per event body, each glossed in plain English the first time.
    - Use English wherever English is enough: head, house, song, drum, landing.
    - No honorific seasoning (*-san*, *-sama*, *-chan*). It is Loti's Kiku-san and wrong for the period besides.
    - Never translate personal names into English ("Little Chrysanthemum"; Hinton p. 85).
    - No Western analogies: courtesan, *hetaira*, troubadour, vestal, temple prostitute or harem (Hinton p. 92 n. 9).
12. **Consistent romanisation.** Use modified Hepburn with macrons in our own text: ō, ū and ā are glyph-safe. Kanji appear in docs only.
    - Pick one form per word and keep it: *Kanzaki* (not *Kamusaki*) in loc; *chōja*; *Hyakudayū*; *Tōsandō*; *Hōjūji*; *Ryōjin hishō*.
    - Titles and faiths that vanilla names (Kyoto, Shinto) keep vanilla's spelling in the UI. Explain the split once in the README.
    - Names keep Japanese order with *no* (Taira no Kiyomori, never "Kiyomori Taira").
13. **Interiority and agency for every Japanese woman on the page.** Each event gives at least one woman a want, a judgement or a craft decision in her own words. The house argues, sues, bargains, refuses and teaches. Kokō's "in this body / there's a heart" (Kim 12–13) is the model. *Trope:* "ciphers" (Treat p. 99); the "describable object" rather than the "introspectible subject" (p. 203); "objects come to life" (Hinton p. 77).
14. **Hostile voices are Japanese and of the period.** No Jesuit, Dutch, Victorian or modern Western speaker appears, and none could in 1178 anyway. Hostility uses period idiom: karmic obstacles, the five obstructions, "sunk in sin" (K 297), "base" (late law), Masafusa's "lose all sense of shame". Never use Christian or Victorian words ("fallen woman", "harlot", "heathen", "idolatrous"). *Trope:* Frois's "street full of whores" for nuns (Hinton p. 42); Vilela on Kūkai (Treat pp. 116–17).
15. **Hostile lines name a person, not a culture.** Write "Ōe no Masafusa wrote", never "the Japanese despised singers". Pair this with SWB Principle 10 (no culture-blame).
16. **Use self-Orientalising Japanese sources critically.** A Japanese author is not automatically free of the frame. *Sakoku* is Kaempfer re-imported (Hinton p. 72), *bushidō* was written for Western readers (pp. 87–90), and *nihonjinron* grew from Benedict (p. 6). Treat calls this "Japan re-imported" (p. 22). It applies to:
    - Mitsuhashi's four-part thesis that Japanese people have a "strong taste" for sex-crossing performance and that this is "globally rather unusual" (M 18);
    - her *mitate* as a "Japanese way of thinking" (M 329);
    - the museum narrative of a continuous "History of Transgenderism in Japan";
    - García Bustos's title "los cantos de las prostitutas", a Western scholar's frame on *imayō*.

    Report all of these as claims. Never put them in the narrator's voice.
17. **How to use Japanese scholars.**
    - Mitsuhashi: an insider. She is the authority on how a trans woman is recognised in place, by doing gender among women (M 304–322; memory note "weight community voices"). Her historical continuity claims (Yamato Takeru, the Hirota burial) and her "tolerant Japan" claims are checked against Tsuji, not adopted (JD decision 13).
    - Tsuji, Wakita, Okimoto and Goodwin's Japanese sources: the authorities on what the houses and the guises were. Where they disagree with Mitsuhashi on history (Tsuji: "dual-sex power" "may rather harm understanding"), the narrator takes the historians' line. Mitsuhashi's view may be voiced by a mother.
    - Cite Japanese scholars by name in the README, in Japanese order where they publish in Japanese. Do not let the Western translators (Kwon/Kim, Goodwin) speak for them.
18. **Do not sand off real difference.** *Honji suijaku*, a kami inside a Buddhist faith, plural marriage, the houses' trade and gifts in place of prices are period facts. Write them plainly as ordinary, neither exotic nor modernised into Western sameness. *Trope:* the over-correction Treat warns of (pp. 204, 209).
19. **The player is not a tourist.** No event exists to show off "Japan". Every event has a stake for the house. No sightseeing tableau of festival, shrine or bath. *Trope:* "the world's playground" (Hinton p. 84); Bird's "fairy scene" (p. 82).
20. **No Western man's romance, and no Western frame on Japanese men.** Patrons are Japanese and judged by their acts. Do not write a patron as tender or wise in ways that make him the player's surrogate lover. *Trope:* Loti, Pinkerton and Sayonara's "perpetual woman" (Hinton pp. 85–86, 106).
21. **Daughters are subjects, not signs.** Her womanhood is lived and known by her house. It is not an aesthetic in which she "signifies" woman. *Trope:* Barthes's "Oriental transvestite" (Treat p. 104); "translation, not transgression".
22. **Art and portraits follow the period, not the poster.** No geisha-white face chosen for effect, no Edo hairstyles and no anime styling. See §6g.

## 6. Specific checks

### 6a. The name, *Imayō no Ie*
- **Good.** It names the song, not a body, a trade or a mystery. *Imayō* means "present-day style": the faith is named for the modern song of 1178. That is the opposite of "timeless Japan", and the README can say so in one line. Macron safe. No Western analogy.
- **Check.** It is an invented compound. Whether the period called a singers' establishment an *ie* (家) is *unverified*. The later *ie* system (patriline household) is a different idea, so the README should gloss it as "the houses (households) of the singers", not a family-corporation term. The English loc name "the Houses of the Present-Day Song" is plain and good. Do not render it as "the House of Modern Songs" or "Houses of Pleasure".
- **Alternatives.** *Kuden no Ie* is also safe. *Tsuzumi no Ie* (the drum) risks a "drums of the East" exotic register.

### 6b. The daughters' term, *uketsugi*
- 受け継ぎ is an ordinary noun for inheriting or succession, not a word for a person. A Japanese reader sees "the succession". That is acceptable as the house's coined word, and it fits the line-transmission idea (K 282–84).
- **Risks.**
  1. A Western player may take it for a real historical term for trans women: a fabricated ethnographic word, the most "exotic seasoning" kind (guardrail 11).
  2. Overuse.
- **Fix.**
  - The tooltip says "the house's own word, 'those who took up the line'; invented for this mod" in the README.
  - In event text use "daughter of the house" and keep *uketsugi* for the trait name and tooltips.
  - Never gloss it as "the Japanese word for trans women".
  - Do **not** use *musume* as a substitute. It is Loti's *mousmé* (Hinton pp. 84–85).

### 6c. The trade tenet's wording
- "The House Keeps Its Trade" is plain English, with no exoticism and no Western analogy. Good.
- **Risk 1.** The reasoning must not rest on a sex-positive Japan: Benedict's "They are not Puritans" (Treat p. 165), Arnold's Japanese "not in the least ashamed of the body" (Hinton p. 83). The tenet's ground is the house's living and its god, not "Japanese culture had no shame". Fix: keep the world's hostility visible (Masafusa, the preacher), so that the faith's stance is the house's, held against its own society (M 352: "kept against prejudice").
- **Risk 2.** "Honest work" is a modern phrase (SWB §5d.16). Use the period-voiced line SWB drafts ("What the river brings, the house earns ...").
- **Risk 3.** The god "asked for lovers" (G 19) must stay a petition, never erotic-sacred colour (no kami-marriage, already decided).

### 6d. The *Torikaebaya* event (e15)
- **Risks.**
  1. A Western player meets it as proof that "Japan had trans novels", which is the tolerant-Japan proof-text (guardrail 3; rule 19).
  2. Frois's land of opposites: Japan as the country of exchanged sexes (Hinton p. 40).
  3. Barthes's sign: the exchanged siblings as an aesthetic of signifying.
- **Fix.**
  - Call it "a court tale" (title glossed "If Only I Could Exchange Them"), one fiction read by the house among others.
  - The narrator states that the tale ends by "returning" both. The house's reading is its own, not the author's intent.
  - Keep JD's cuts (childhood, coercion, Uji, death-wish lines). Use no English marketing gloss such as "gender-bending classic".
  - Interiority: the daughter's response is about her own years in service, not about the tale's cleverness.

### 6e. Michichika's foxes
- *Kitsune* who take women's forms is a genuine Japanese period image, and Michichika really wrote it of Muro's singers (G 129).
- **How it reads to a Western player.** The anime fox-girl (Hinton ch. 7, *kawaii* and "weird Japan") plus the exotic seductress (guardrail 5). Its logic, a thing that only takes women's forms, is the trans "deceiver" (rule 3; SWB §5a.1).
- **Recommendation (agrees with SWB).** Keep it in notes. If it is used, use it once:
  - spoken by Michichika, at Muro, about all the port's women, never near a daughter;
  - with no fox art, no fox trait, no fox modifier and no "fox" nickname;
  - answered materially by a mother.
  Never make it the "uncanny" outsider of decision 2 (see 6f).

### 6f. The dual-sex power (双性) branch (e11, decision 2)
- This is Mitsuhashi's own claim (M 26, 36–38, 350), contested by Tsuji ("may rather harm understanding").
- **Western reading.** Benedict's sacred, honoured third (Treat pp. 115, 204), and the "mysterious, ancient" East (Hinton p. 106). It is also an "every shaman is a little woman" universal (rule 18).
- **Option (b)**, giving it to an "uncanny" outsider such as Michichika, is the worst choice. It turns an insider's claim into a hostile exoticism and adds a mystic Orient voice.
- **Recommend (a)**: one mother's story, explicitly hers ("my teacher said ..."), answered by another mother ("we need no god to have stood near us to be owed our place"). No mechanic, no art, no "spirit" register. Or choose **(c)**, none. This follows the memory rule to weight insiders: the claim is Mitsuhashi's and gets an insider's voice, contested. It does not get the narrator, and it does not go to a hostile outsider.

### 6g. Art and portraits (vanilla 1.20 with All Under Heaven, read for this note)

**What vanilla does:**
- `06_makeup.txt` `japanese_makeup`: white base, shaved brows, *ohaguro* teeth, for ages over 12, Japanese culture. It applies only to men and to married women or concubines at ritsuryō-administrative courts of high tier. This is Heian court practice, so it is period-correct. A Western player may still read white face as "geisha".
- `03_clothes_religious.txt`: devoted or high clergy of `buddhism_religion` with Japanese clothing get `tgp_japanese_buddhist_clothes`, the monk outfit. Shintō clergy get `tgp_japanese_shinto_clothes`. Lay clothes come in tiers: commoner, nobility, high nobility, royalty, imperial, war nobility. `tgp_japanese_hairstyles` and royal hairstyles exist.

**Guardrails.**
1. Do not make seeds concubines to obtain the makeup, and do not add custom white-face makeup for singers. Leave vanilla's period logic alone. If a court lady has it, a footnote in the README can say it is Heian court practice, not geisha.
2. With `doctrine_clerical_gender_female_only`, the faith's devoted women get the Buddhist monastic robe. That is acceptable (Mei died a nun), but it can make every house head look like a nun. In the observe run, check whether the *chōja* reads as a nun. If so, accept it (no custom art) or keep the office off the clergy triggers. Do not invent a "singer costume".
3. No custom event art with Edo-style hair, kimono-and-*obi*, *shamisen*, paper parasols, cherry trees or Fuji. If background art is needed, use vanilla riverside, court or temple scenes from All Under Heaven.
4. Re-embodiment: vanilla's no-beard and hair genes work by sex and culture, so no change is needed. No "before" portrait (rule 5).
5. Minors: vanilla applies makeup from age 13 to Japanese characters in general. The mod adds no Japanese child characters, scenes or portraits connected to the houses.
