## 1. Chapter 6: how Japanese society recognises sex/gender (pp. 304–334)

This chapter is sociology of the present day (Shinjuku, the 1990s–2000s). Her model has six parts.

1. **Gender is done and then read by others.** "Doing female gender" means building a woman's gender pattern, then a gender image, through dress, movement and speech (pp. 305–306).
   - Self-identification is not enough. She needs 「『女』としての他者からの認識」 ("recognition from others as a 'woman'", p. 306).
   - p. 310: 「性自認と性他認のフィードバックの絶えざる反復」, "the unceasing repeated feedback of self-recognition and recognition-by-others". Through it, an MtF person "crosses socially from man to woman" (「男性から女性へと社会的な性別を越境していく」, citing 三橋h 2006).
2. **Being "read" does not end being treated as a woman.** 「男とバレても『女扱い』はつづく」 ("even when she is read as male, 'treatment as a woman' goes on", p. 310).
   - She rejects 「パス至上主義」, "pass-supremacy" (p. 311).
   - Daily recognition is 「性器の形や戸籍に記された続柄」 ("not genital shape or the registry entry", p. 312) but 「doing gender を指標にした個人の性別認識の積み重ね」 ("an accumulation of individual recognitions using doing gender as the index", p. 312).
3. **「女」錯覚, the "woman" illusion (pp. 313–315).** People who treat her as a woman come to forget her body: 「いつの間にか飛んでしまい」 ("somehow it just flies away", p. 313).
4. **Place (場) and the group (pp. 319–324).**
   - Recognition changes with 「いつ、どこで、どんな状況で」, "when, where, in what circumstances" (p. 319).
   - 「妖怪の法則」, the "law of ghosts" (p. 321): people see a trans woman only where they expect one, at night and in the entertainment quarter.
   - 「多数決の法則」, the "law of the majority" (pp. 321–322): in a group of born women, 「多少異質なものがわずかに混じっていても」 she is read by the group's character; the odd one is 「打ち消され」 ("cancelled out").
   - **This is the nearest this range comes to "a woman among women"** (that exact phrase is from QV 2007, p. 310, and is not in this range). For the faith, being received among the women of the house is how she is read as a woman.
5. **Bathing (pp. 323–326):** the one place where the body is "un-shelved". Personal material, not used.
6. **見立て, *mitate* (p. 329; citing 松岡 2000).** Japanese tradition has "a way of thinking that projects the image of one thing onto another".
   - Reading a woman-dressing person as 「女」 is one such *mitate*: 「女装した男性のイメージを女性のイメージに転位」, "transposing the image of a cross-dressed male onto the image of a woman" (p. 329).
   - Her example is the audience who praise a kabuki *onnagata*'s beauty while knowing he is male.
   - The rest (pp. 329–334) is about sexuality and is not needed.
   - p. 334 makes a general claim: Japanese sexual culture keeps 「曖昧・微妙な領域」 ("an ambiguous, subtle zone") between heterosexual and homosexual, and Western sexology "made it marginal".

**Use for the faith.** This is the part least touched by Tsuji, and it fits the existing gate (§4.1 of `japan_sources.md`).
- She is a woman because the house does woman with her and reads her as one, in a place and among women.
- The narrator can hold this view without any metaphysics.

## 2. 終章 and おわりに: 双性, 双性原理, 性別越境, "third gender"

### 2a. Universality and a "third gender": she uses the term, plainly
- p. 344: 「女装という文化は、地域や時代を越えて…古くから広く普遍的に存在した」, "the culture of woman-dressing existed widely and universally since ancient times, beyond region and era".
- p. 344: 「神・祭への奉仕者、神と人との仲介者」 and four functions in all (paraphrased below).
  - English (paraphrase): third-gender-like people, she writes, had important social functions as "servants of gods and festivals, mediators between gods and humans", counsellors to women, and go-betweens of men and women (a gender "buffer device").
  - Over the 40-character limit, quoted whole because it is the key definition. Shorten it before any wider use.
- p. 344: societies with a third, 「所によっては第四ジェンダー」 ("in places a fourth gender"), were widespread. The Western modern two-sex split is 「相対的な文化所産」, "a relative cultural product".
- p. 344: colonial Westernisation took away "third gender" roles. The Tahitian *māhū* were re-read as homosexual and 「次第に本来の姿を失っていった」 ("gradually lost their original form").
- p. 357: premodern Japan, unlike the Christian world, 「性別越境や同性愛を一種の『文化』として許容」 ("tolerated gender-crossing and same-sex love as a kind of 'culture'"). Gender-crossers were tolerated 「サード・ジェンダー的存在として」, "as third-gender-like beings".
- p. 358: 「日本が本質的にサード・ジェンダー的な存在に寛容な社会」, "Japan is essentially a society tolerant of third-gender-like beings".
- p. 366, on the man-woman figure at the Urakami bow festival: 「祭礼における第三の性の存在がほのかに見える」, "the existence of a third sex in the festival faintly shows".
- p. 383, against the GID model: 「なぜ、『あいまいな性』『第三の性』のままで生きてはいけない」, "why may one not live as an 'ambiguous sex', a 'third sex'?"
- **But** p. 372: 「MtFトランスジェンダーは『女』であっても女性ではない」, "MtF transgender people are 'women' (*onna*, social) but not *josei* (female)". She keeps her own social womanhood (「女」) apart from birth sex.

**Answer: does she claim a "third gender"?** Yes, as a cross-cultural category (*sādo jendā* / 第三の性). She calls premodern Japan tolerant of "third-gender-like" people, and she defends living as a "third sex" today. She also calls herself 「女」 and works by "doing female gender". The two sit side by side; she does not reconcile them in these pages.

### 2b. 双性 and 双性原理: the sacred-power claim
- p. 350, section 「聖性から『性』へ」 ("From sacredness to sex"). The origin of the functions is 「双性（Double-Gender）的特性」: 「ジェンダー的に男でも女でもあること」 ("being, in gender, both man and woman"), and 「ある種の神性」 ("a kind of divinity") derives from that trait.
- p. 350: 「原始的な社会では、双性的特性は…異能として理解され」 ("in primitive societies the dual-sex trait was understood as an unusual power"). Such people were 「神により近い人として聖視されました」, "held sacred as people closer to the gods".
- p. 350, the definition of **双性原理**: 「人は女装・男装して双性的な存在になる」, "people, by dressing as women *or* as men, become dual-sexed beings". Thereby they 「通常の人とは異なる力、聖性を身に帯びる」, "take on a power and sacredness different from ordinary people". She labels this 「（双性原理）」.
  - It explicitly covers **both** 女装 and 男装. This is exactly what Tsuji objects to.
- p. 350: from this, gender-crossers were 「畏敬/畏怖され」 ("revered and feared") and took special social roles.
- p. 350, how the five functions grow from it:
  - religious service, then performance (music and dance offered to gods, later watched by people);
  - shared feasting with gods, then food and hospitality service;
  - sacred marriage (聖婚), then sexual service;
  - mediating gods and humans, then mediating men and women and counselling women.
- p. 351, the historical arc: 「聖性から『性』性へと展開（発展?）」, "unfolded (developed?) from sacredness to sexuality".
  - From the early modern period, 「畏敬/畏怖の念は次第に薄れ、蔑視/差別視」 ("reverence and awe gradually faded, replaced by contempt").
  - The Western 「変態概念」 ("perversion" concept) made this decisive.
- p. 380: 「古代以来の双性原理は、現代にも生きている」, "the dual-sex principle from antiquity lives on today".
  - Here it is about the charm of a modern new-half singer's show. She herself asks: 「私の妄想でしょうか」, "is this my fantasy?"
- p. 376, the commercial side: complete feminisation 「双性性の喪失につながりかねない」 ("risks losing dual-sexedness"), and with it the performer's charm. She quotes a club *mama*: 「ニューハーフが女になっちゃっては意味がない」 ("no point if a new-half becomes a woman"; p. 377).

### 2c. Her evidence and dates for the sacred and shamanic claim
- **Comparative ethnography,** all modern:
  - Myanmar *acault*, spirit-medium 依りまし (p. 340);
  - Korean cross-dressed shamans (photo 1930, from 秋葉 1950; p. 340);
  - North American "berdache", from 石井 2003; the 20th-c. Korean MtF shamans; Indian hijra at births and funerals, from 石川 1995 (all p. 346);
  - Mexican muxe and Polynesian māhū as counsellors (p. 342);
  - Thai kathoey and rebirth thinking (p. 338);
  - Chinese 相公 to the early 20th c. (p. 340).
- **Japan, no dated record given.**
  - p. 346: 「日本の古代〜中世社会においても、女装の巫人、男装の巫女は見られました」, "in ancient and medieval Japan too there were woman-dressed shamans (巫人) and man-dressed shamanesses (巫女)".
  - No primary source or date is cited at that point. The bibliography suggests her basis: 国分 1970/1975/1976 「双性の巫人」 (south-western islands), 金関 1975 「双性の神人」, 野沢a 1976 「双性と古墳の被葬者」 (archaeology), and 安田 1974 on a modern male *miko* in Tokara.
- **Festivals** (pp. 365–369): Kumano Hayatama 御船祭; Asuka-ni-imasu 御田植祭; Myōkō 春駒; Urakami 弓祭; Katsushika 雷の大般若; Kanamara 祭. All are modern observation. She calls them 「きわめて古い習俗」 ("very old customs", p. 365) without dates.
  - She rejects the "substitute for impure women" explanation as insufficient (p. 366).
  - Her conclusion is a self-marked conjecture (p. 369): 「根源的に深い関係にあったのではないかと推測」 ("I conjecture a deep root relation"). There she also writes 「女装のシャーマンが主宰した儀式の残像」 ("an after-image of rites led by woman-dressed shamans"), and admits 「まだまだ調査も考察も足りない」 ("research and reflection are still far from enough").
- **Myth:** Yamato Takeru's woman-dress (pp. 365, 386; *Kojiki* 712 and *Nihon shoki* 720, cited via 畠山 1994).
- **Gods and Buddhas who change sex** (pp. 358–366):
  - Kannon, 「仏教の中でも最も双性的」 ("the most dual-sexed in Buddhism", p. 362), from the *Lotus Sutra* 普門品 thirty-three forms, which include female forms;
  - the Atsuta deity as Yang Guifei (Muromachi; see §3).

**Summary.** The sacred power is claimed as a cross-cultural "principle". It is argued from 20th-century ethnography, myth and present-day festivals. In these pages she cites **no dated Japanese record from 1100–1250 of a gender-crossing shaman**, and she twice marks the Japanese shaman link as conjecture (p. 369) or possible fantasy (p. 380).

## 4. Her insider framing: how trans women should be read in history

- **Lineage kept against prejudice** (p. 352): 「必死にその系譜と生とをつないできた」 ("desperately kept their lineage and their lives joined"), 「偏見と差別に抗しながら」 ("resisting prejudice and discrimination"). This is "the fight kept" (rule 2), and the best fit for the faith's own voice.
- **Society once made use of them** (p. 384): 「人間社会はそうした人々の特性を生かす社会システムをもっていた」 ("human society had social systems that made use of such people's traits"). In Japan this produced varied performance and trades, 「日本文化を構成する一つの文化要素」 ("one cultural element composing Japanese culture"). So she cannot accept the 「病」 (illness) model (p. 384).
- **Five functions, not one holy office** (pp. 346–351): religious, performance, food and hospitality, sexual service, and go-between/counsellor. She stresses that before the modern era one person usually held several at once (p. 351). This matches rule 7 (roads to standing that are not holy office) better than her 双性 origin story does.
- **Her task** (p. 385): 「性別越境者の存在を記録し、文化・社会史として叙述すること」 ("to record the existence of gender-crossers and narrate it as cultural and social history").
- **Self-determination** (p. 383): 「自分の『性』のあり様…自分で決める」 ("the shape of one's own sex… one decides oneself").
- **Pride** (p. 386): 「ヤマトタケルの末裔である日本のトランスジェンダーとしての誇り」 ("the pride of Japanese transgender people, descendants of Yamato Takeru").
- **Recognition is social** (ch. 6): a woman is someone doing woman and read as one in a place, among women. Neither the registry nor the body decides it.
- **For the mod, the house's own voice could say** (invented paraphrase, not a quote): *we kept the line of our songs and our lives against those who scorned us; the house reads us as women, and we do woman together.*
  - Leave out "we were once revered" (that is rule 2's "honour lost").
  - Leave out "Japan was tolerant, unlike…" (pp. 357–358). It is the Japanese twin of rule 13's "the pagan North was free".

## 6. Comparison with Tsuji (2024)

- **Tsuji's summary is fair.** His account of her 2022 essay (`japan_tsuji.md` §5) matches the 2008 book: (1) cross-dressing pervades premodern performance; (2) holding both sexes (双性) gives power and sacredness, sought by cross-dressing. The book adds the five-function scheme and the arc from the sacred to the sexual.
- **Where his critique lands.**
  1. She does treat 女装 and 男装 alike under one 双性原理 (p. 350: 「女装・男装して」; p. 346: 女装の巫人 / 男装の巫女). He rejects exactly this lumping, because medieval men's and women's social positions differed (his pp. 10, 27).
  2. She gives no dated Japanese record for the sacred-power claim in this range. Its basis is modern ethnography elsewhere, myth and present-day festivals, and she calls the Japanese link conjecture herself (p. 369; p. 380 「妄想」). He works from dated 12th–13th-c. records; on what a record was and when, his reading stands.
- **Where it does not land.**
  1. He has nothing to say on ch. 6: how a trans woman is recognised day to day, by "doing gender", by place, by the majority and by *mitate*. Her insider authority is strongest there.
  2. He has nothing to say on lineage kept against prejudice (p. 352), or on self-determination (p. 383).
  3. His men's guise against women's gaze contrast has no place for a woman whose dress is her life. Her chapter 6 is about exactly that woman.
- **Within her own theory, 双性 cuts against the project's frame.**
  - It defines the source of power as being 「男でも女でもある」, "both man and woman" (p. 350).
  - It values keeping some maleness for charm (pp. 376–377).
  - It sits beside a "third sex" category (pp. 344, 357–358, 383).
  - Her other line is 「女」 by doing gender (ch. 6). The project's daughters are women, so the faith follows that line.

## 7. Recommendation: may 双性 appear? Rules 2 and 18

Rule texts (`docs/faith_design.md` §16):
- **Rule 2:** "No golden past. The faiths say 'the fight kept', never 'the honour lost'. No creation story of an undivided first sex in any faith's main voice."
- **Rule 18:** "No line says every woman is a little Marked, or that no one is truly a man or a woman."

**(a) As doctrine (tenet or narrator voice): no.** Three reasons:
1. Her 双性原理 comes with a decline story: revered, then sacred power lost to contempt and the West's "perversion" (pp. 350–351). That is the "honour lost" rule 2 forbids.
2. A tenet that daughters are 「男でも女でもある」 contradicts rule 8 and the faith's premise that daughters are women. It also comes close to rule 18's "no one is truly a man or a woman" when put in universal form, and to rule 2's "undivided first sex" when put as origin.
3. The brief bars "third gender" romanticisation, and her 双性 sits in a third-gender frame (p. 344).
Her own hedges (推測, 妄想) also mean the claim is not hers at full strength.

**(b) As one character's contested belief: yes, allowed, with limits.** This keeps the decision already in `japan_sources.md` §4.3, now grounded in her own words rather than Tsuji's summary.
- One mother may hold that a woman-dressed shaman "carried both" and was near the gods, as a story she tells about the past.
- Another mother answers in the faith's main frame: *we are women of this house, and we do not need the gods to have been near us to be owed our place* (rule 19).
- Never in a tenet, the narrator, a decision title or a modifier.
- No mechanical bonus for "both".
- No claim that Japan was once tolerant.
- No male-dressing women folded in (out of scope, and Tsuji's point).
- Better still, give that belief to an outside voice: a priest or a court diarist who thinks the house's women are "uncanny". Then 双性 is the world's gaze (rule 1), not the faith's self-account.

**(c) Not at all: the fallback, if the user prefers.** Nothing in the 1178 setting needs it.

**What to adopt instead, in the faith's own voice, from Mitsuhashi:**
- the lineage kept against scorn (p. 352);
- the five roads (song, hospitality, counsel, rite, and sex work, the last only as the setting already handles it), not one holy office (rule 7);
- recognition by the house, among women, in place (ch. 6);
- self-determination (p. 383).

The Kyōen and Eison stories (1243–51) may at most be a later monks' tale, told in a monk's voice, never as proof (rule 19).
