# The Witch Studies Reader (2025): research note for a v2 witch faith

Scope: does this book help Crusader Dolls build a culture or faith on *actual historical witches* inside CK3's timeline (starts 867, 1066, 1178; game ends 1453)? Companion to `witch_papers_and_timeline.md` (two papers plus a pre-1453 evidence timeline) and `../book_federici_caliban_witch.md`.

Quote rule: every verbatim quote is 25 words or fewer and carries a printed page number. Everything else is paraphrase. Page numbers are the book's printed numbers (PDF page = printed + 13).

Pronouns: authors and interviewees are called by the pronouns the book uses for them. Where the book gives none, *they*.

---

## 1. Bibliographic line and verdict

**Soma Chaudhuri and Jane Ward, eds., *The Witch Studies Reader*. Durham, NC: Duke University Press, 2025. ISBN 978-1-4780-2813-0 (hardcover), 978-1-4780-3135-2 (paper).** 32 chapters in six parts, plus an editors' introduction (pp. 1–20).

**Verdict: low value for building a pre-1453 faith. Medium value as a guardrail document.** This is a reader in contemporary feminist and decolonial witch studies. Its weight is on the present: witch-hunts in India, Tanzania and Ghana today, Black and queer witches in the US, Wicca, tarot, pop culture and protest. The editors say so. Part III aims to lay to rest "the singular and sometimes even romanticized feminist association of witch hunts with early modern Europe" (p. 16). No chapter is about medieval Europe. The book never mentions the Canon Episcopi, Diana or Herodias as night-riding leaders, the *bonae dominae*, Holda or Perchta, Valais (1428), Arras (1459), Alice Kyteler (1324), Nider's *Formicarius*, or Margaret Murray. The **only true pre-1453 primary material** is Saira Chhibber's two-page summary of witches in Somadeva's 11th-century *Kathāsaritsāgara* and the 12th-century *Śukasaptati* (pp. 291–93). The rest is secondhand pointers: Old Norse valkyries and *idisi*, a 13th-century source on night-flight ointments, and the claim that the witch/cunning-folk split is medieval. The book is more useful for what it warns against: Gardner's invented "Old Religion," flying ointment as a witch-hunters' fiction, the midwife thesis, and the colonial gaze on living African and Indian traditions. It also gives a strong, citable model of why witch accusations happen (patriarchy + belief in harmful magic + economic transition). That model can drive mechanics without inventing a fake pagan church.

## 3. Usable historical material for a faith c. 867–1453

The book has very little here. Everything I could find is listed below, with how solid it is.

### 3a. South Asia (on-map; directly pre-1453): the best material in the book

- **Somadeva, *Kathāsaritsāgara* (Kashmir, 11th c.).** Chhibber retells a tale of a bored princess who sees a group of young women flying through the sky and asks to join them (pp. 291–92). Witchcraft here means breaking taboos, including murder and cannibalism. The tale dwells on the fun of a female band of flying friends. It ends in disaster when she tries to bring her husband into the women's pact: they accidentally eat their own son and then kill themselves. Chhibber reads the moral as aimed at the husband's intrusion and at the attempted killing of a Brahman, more than at witchcraft itself (p. 292). Source: Penzer trans., pp. 104, 115 (Chhibber nn. 6–7).
  - *Design use:* a **women-only band of night-flyers** with a strict boundary against men is attested South Asian imagery that predates the European sabbath. **But** in the source it is a *cautionary tale told by outsiders*, ending in cannibalism and suicide. It is not evidence of a real cult.
- ***Śukasaptati* ("Seventy Tales of the Parrot," 12th c.).** A witch-maiden whose father arranges her marriage like any other daughter's. She is bored by her husband, takes a lover, and is abandoned rather than punished with violence. Chhibber reads this as witchcraft folded into ordinary social life, a comic tale with no horror (pp. 292–93). Source: Wortham trans. 1911, p. 26.
- **Folk *churail*.** The ghost of a woman who died in childbirth or by gendered violence. She has backward-turned feet (*mudiapairi / muriapairi churail*), haunts cremation grounds and cemeteries, and lures men at night (p. 293). The book gives no date. Treat it as folklore of uncertain age, not as attested medieval belief.
- **Regional witch terms** (contemporary, but some are likely old): *dayan, dakan, tonhi, bishahi, banamati*, all feminized (p. 221). *Ḍākinī* ancestry is not discussed. **Caution:** these terms belong to people being hunted and killed today (see §4).

### 3b. Northern Europe (on-map): pointers only, all secondhand through Steinhart

- **Idisi and valkyries.** Steinhart links female warriors to divine women called *idisi*. They often appear as a triad ("the three women") with possible roots in the Romano-Germanic *Matres/Matronae*, as Norns, and as the three valkyries of the Volund poem. In the First Merseburg Charm (Old High German, 10th-c. manuscript; date not given in the book) they bind and unbind (p. 132). He cites the *Njáls saga* weaving loom (p. 136) and Kathleen Self's argument that the valkyrie "breaks the man-woman binary to create a third gender" (p. 136).
  - *Design use:* a **binding/unbinding triad** is an attested Germanic motif. The "three women" charm pattern carries on into early modern German grimoires (pp. 132–33). That gives a continuity of *motif* without claiming a continuous *cult*.
  - *Flags:* Steinhart is a practitioner building a modern synthesis, his "Way of Sophia" (pp. 138–39). His claim of "a high degree of gender equality among heathens" from archaeology (p. 132) is overstated. Birka Bj.581 (Price et al. 2019) is contested.
- **Seiðr.** Mentioned once, in passing: Deitsch "sleeping preachers" "perhaps recalling heathen seidr trances" (p. 138). There is no discussion of *ergi* or gender transgression in seiðr. Get that from Norse specialists, not from this book.
- **Medieval witch/cunning-folk split.** Steinhart, citing Mitchell (2001), says pre-Christian North Sea cultures had many words for magic-workers. Most fell out of English with Christianization, and "a medieval distinction emerged between malevolent witches and benevolent cunning folk" (p. 135, citing Davies and Hutton). The *helping* vs *harming* practitioner split is a solid design axis. Kelkar & Nathan show the same split elsewhere: witch vs. witch-finder among the Santal (p. 177), and in Tanzania, healers who diagnose "witchcraft problems" (p. 190).

### 3c. Latin Christendom (on-map): what the book says, and where it is thin

- **Night flight and ointments.** Clay & Quilty describe early modern accusations: flight on oven forks or straw, shapeshifting, the spirit leaving the body (p. 119, citing Tuczay 2018). They say the consensus is that ointments were a fiction of witch-hunters, inquisitors and physicians (p. 124). They also flag **Ayelet Even-Ezra, "Cursus: An Early Thirteenth-Century Source for Nocturnal Flights and Ointments in the Work of Roland of Cremona" (2017)** as "compelling counterclaims" (n. 44, p. 128). This is the **single most useful pre-1453 lead in the book**: a scholastic source from about the 1230s that discusses a *cursus* (night ride) and ointments, long before the 15th-century sabbath. Read it next.
- **Sabbath as myth, not ritual.** Kelkar & Nathan quote Ginzburg: sabbath descriptions "document myths and not rituals" (p. 180). Peasants accepted supernatural explanations. The clergy turned them into diabolism (p. 180). This supports building the faith from **folk belief and practice** and not from the sabbath of the demonologists.
- **Visual codification from the 15th century.** Zika's iconography: witchcraft shown as a female group activity, the cauldron, oven forks before brooms (pp. 332–34). Woodcuts cited: Vintler, *Buch der Tugend* (Augsburg 1486); Molitor, *De laniis et phitonicis mulieribus* (c. 1496–1500); Baldung Grien (1510). All are post-1453, so useful only as the end-point the faith is heading toward.
- **The Reformation as the break.** Neitz & Goldman: medieval Catholicism was full of magical objects and devotions (relics, holy water). Reformers attacked these, and household magic carried on among women (p. 453). For 867–1453 this means **witch-adjacent practice was inside Christian culture**, not a separate church.
- **The plague and midwives (contested).** Kelkar & Nathan, following Heinsohn & Steiger (1999), argue that after the mid-14th-century plague, hunts targeted midwives' birth-control knowledge to repopulate Europe (p. 178). They admit midwives were a "small portion" of the accused (p. 178). **Do not use as fact** (see §4).
- **Gossip.** Charnock & Schaller, via Federici, say "gossip" once meant women friends and in the Middle Ages came to mean idle talk (p. 410). This is a usable flavour detail. Check it against the OED (*godsibb*, "godparent").

### 3d. Sub-Saharan Africa and elsewhere: no pre-1453 material

- Kelkar & Nathan's comparative cases (Santal, Lua, Rungus *Bobolizan*, Asante royal women, Dai in Yunnan, Khasi *Thlen*) are 19th–21st-century ethnography (pp. 176–81). The Asante case is precolonial but well after 1453 (McCaskie). None of it is usable as medieval evidence.
- The Bakiga/Batooro material (ch. 1) has no dates and is a living tradition (see §4).

### 3e. A usable *model* for mechanics

Kelkar & Nathan's three conditions (pp. 175, 184): (1) **patriarchy** being made or remade; (2) **cultural belief** that people can cause harm by supernatural means; (3) **structural transformation**, especially a shift from a sharing economy to accumulation. Supporting points:
- Accusations happen **between neighbours and kin**, not strangers (p. 181, citing Briggs and Geschiere).
- Targets include widows with land, quarrelsome or cursing women, and the "neighbour who asks" after charity is refused (pp. 178–79, 183).
- Some **men** were targets too: smiths, shepherds, and wealthy men "who did not give" (pp. 176, 183).
- A Sierra Leone village study found accusations peak where **old and new norms are in conflict** (p. 184).

Ernstberger adds that gender ratios **varied by region and era**. In Iceland, 10 of 170 accused were women (p. 238). In Muscovy most of the accused were men (p. 238). Finland flipped three times (p. 239). One student finding: the more sexualized and diabolical the concept of witchcraft, the more women were targeted (p. 239).
*Design use:* persecution pressure on the faith could scale with economic change, such as county development, new buildings or a change of holder. Being a single woman, a widow with land, or a "cursing" woman would raise suspicion. Regions where witchcraft was not yet diabolized would target more men. All of this is consistent with the book and avoids an invented cult.

## 4. Guardrails

What the book warns against, and which claims are modern reconstruction rather than evidence.

**Explicit warnings in the book**
1. **Do not romanticize the witch-hunt or locate it only in early modern Europe.** The editors' core thesis (p. 16; also p. 7): more than 25,000 witch-killings in India, 1987–2003 alone (p. 8). The *dain/daini/tonhī/dakan* figure is a live, lethal accusation (pp. 2–3, 205, 221). **Do not make any of these terms the name or identity of a mod faith.** Using a word that gets real adivasi women killed as a playable "empowered" identity would repeat the problem the book is about.
2. **Gardner's "Old Religion" is pseudo-history.** "Gerald Gardner (along with others) invented a pseudo-history of the Anglo witch" (p. 135). Claims that the Old Religion "had existed underground for hundreds of years were untrue" (p. 319, citing Hutton). **The book never names Margaret Murray**, but this is the Murray/Gardner witch-cult thesis, and the book treats it as refuted. Arthur Evans's goddess-and-horned-god "old religion" with a gay horned god (p. 252, quoting Evans 1978) belongs to the same line. Treat it as 1970s gay-liberation myth-making, valuable as a *modern* document only.
3. **Flying ointments are probably a witch-hunters' fiction** (p. 124). The exception is the Even-Ezra lead.
4. **Sabbath accounts record myths, not rituals** (Ginzburg, p. 180). Do not build a "real sabbath."
5. **Colonial gaze and the "savage slot."** Nichols-Belo (ch. 13) and the Uganda authors (ch. 1) show how outsiders turn African healing into "witchcraft." The Ugandan authors say Christianity and colonial law reclassified *omufumu* healing as evil (pp. 23–25). **Do not present Bakiga Nyabingyi practice, Ifá, Hoodoo, Vodou or any African traditional religion as the mod's witch faith.** These are living communities, and several are closed or initiatory. Joshi & Brooks (ch. 4) back this with the open/closed-practice debate.
6. **Homophobic, elite "Wicca" is no model either.** The editors criticize an Anglicized Indian Wiccan scene for homophobia and class exclusivity (p. 3).
7. **Trans exclusion in witch feminism.** Chernoff notes the 1960s W.I.T.C.H. is now criticised for gender essentialism and trans-exclusion (p. 357). The 2016– W.I.T.C.H. names trans women explicitly (p. 358). An Irish practitioner rejects the "air of witchcraft that says you need to have a vagina to be a witch" (p. 53). For the mod: vulva/womb-centred "sacred feminine" theology (e.g. Rogel ch. 23, Dianic Wicca ch. 19) is a modern feminist idiom. It is not historical, and it fits a trans-women-centred faith badly.

**Modern reconstruction vs. evidenced history (my flags)**

## 5. Trans women and gender transgression

Nothing in the book is about a trans woman in a pre-1453 witchcraft context. What exists:

- **Uganda, precolonial memory (undated).** It was "common knowledge" that intersex people, people attracted to the same or both sexes, and "those who cross-dressed occupied a special place in community" with extra knowledge (p. 26). The intersex *ebihindi* were said to gather at night at the *omusisa* tree, which was later burned. An elder's account (translated): "They burned down the tree and intersex people burned with it" (p. 27). The authors set this against colonial and Christian anti-LGBT law. **Strong as testimony, but undated, off-map (Kigezi/Tooro are outside CK3's Africa), and a living community's memory.** Use as comparative framing only.
- **Nyabingyi** is believed to have feminine energy "but was neither woman/female nor man/male" (p. 26). This is a living Bakiga devotion, so do not borrow it.
- **Valkyrie as third gender.** Kathleen Self (2014), via Steinhart (p. 136). On-map and pre-1453 in its sources (eddic and saga material). But the subject is women taking a masculine warrior role, not people assigned male at birth taking a feminine role. It is a weak fit for "daughters."
- **Early modern witch-body as queer.** Clay & Quilty: the witch was imagined as sexually inverted, with misplaced teats, interchangeable orifices, and the Malleus's penis-stealing (p. 119). They say witches "have always been queer creatures" (p. 118). This is a modern queer reading of early modern demonology. It does not show that gender-nonconforming people were targeted. They link queer and trans bodies today to the witch-body as "ruined" or freakish (p. 126).
- **Mey Rude** (p. 120) is quoted on sex as ecstatic "flying." The chapter does not say she is trans. She is publicly known as a trans woman writer. Verify before citing her as a trans voice.
- **Gender of the accused was not fixed.** Male-majority hunts in Iceland and Muscovy, and Finland's three shifts (pp. 238–39). Kivelson: "gender, when configured differently than in the West, could weaken the association between women and witchcraft" (p. 239). Useful for a faith where the witch is defined by role and knowledge, not by birth sex.
- **Modern law.** Karnataka's 2017 anti-black-magic act defines the accused person as "male, female, or a transgender" (p. 242). Three of seven Indian state acts define a witch as a woman (p. 242).
- **Hijra in *Churails*.** A hijra woman is part of the feminist "churail" crew in the 2020 Pakistani series (p. 312). This is contemporary media, not history. The mod already has better hijra sources (Revathi, Bhatt).
- **W.I.T.C.H. (2016–)** names trans women explicitly as witches (p. 358).
- **Drag witch** Mista Boo (ch. 17): a contemporary drag performer whom the chapter calls *he* offstage and *she* in persona; no trans identity is stated (pp. 249–61). Not relevant to trans women.

**Net:** the book offers **no evidence that historical (pre-1453) witchcraft accusations targeted trans women or people assigned male who lived as women.** The closest on-map leads (Norse *ergi*/seiðr, men in women's dress at the Kalends, Rykener) are not in this book. See `witch_papers_and_timeline.md` for those.

## 6. Open questions and further sources

**Open questions this book raises but does not answer**
1. What exactly does Roland of Cremona's *cursus* passage (c. 1230s) say about night rides and ointments, and are women and men both involved? This is the one hard pre-1453 lead.
2. Can the Somadeva flying-women band be tied to *ḍākinī* or *yoginī* cults (Kashmir, 9th–12th c.)? That would be on-map, pre-1453, and religious rather than only literary. Chhibber does not go there. Needs Indological sources, not this book.
3. Does Mitchell's North Sea terminology (2001) show a gendered split, with men as *wicca/warlock* and women as *wicce/hægtesse*, before 1100?
4. Is there any pre-1453 trial where gender nonconformity was part of a sorcery charge? Not in this book. Check Joan of Arc (1431) and the Valais/Lucerne trials (1428).
5. How should the mod show persecution without adopting the *dain/tonhī* label of living victims? Possible answer: model accusation as a pressure on the faith, using the Kelkar & Nathan conditions, with in-world terms drawn from medieval Latin/vernacular sources (*striga, lamia, maleficus/-a, wicce, hægtesse, seiðkona*).
