## 0. Framing warning

- **It is a teaching anthology, and its sources are hostile.** The editors warn that the devil here is "very much the product of churchmen and churchwomen" (p. xv). Where popular views appear, "they were generally presented in a hostile light" (p. xv). Every selection that mentions the night company is a clerical description of what other people believed. None of it is the believers' own voice.
- **The editors warn of graphic material** that is "racist, misogynistic, and otherwise bigoted" (p. xvi). Much of chapters 8 and 11 is accusers' fantasy (orgy, child-eating, devil-sex, demon cats). It is recorded below only so that it can be kept out. It appears nowhere in the mod, not even as an allegation (`witch_design.md` §10).
- **Headnotes are brief and sometimes loose.**
  - Doc. 32's headnote calls Regino "abbot of Trier" (p. 99). The same page's source line cites "Regino of Prüm". Keep the notes' "Regino of Prüm".
  - Doc. 86 dates the Latin Theophilus to "the end of the eighth century" (p. 304). This is unverified here; see §5, lead 11.
  - Doc. 92 reports that the authenticity of *Super illius specula* has been questioned (p. 352).
- **Translations embed glosses.** Burchard's c. 70 is McNeill's English: "the witch Hulda" (p. 99). "Witch" there renders a hostile clerical word (Hutton 2022 p. 115 has *striga* in a minority recension). It is not a word for the faith's own voice.
- **User rule (2026-10-27), applied throughout.** The daughters are women. They are never treated as gay men, cross-dressers or transvestites. Selections on demons in women's form, men in women's clothes, or sodomy are recorded **only** as how hostile voices classify people. No proposal here frames a daughter's womanhood as disguise or costume.

## 2. Findings (printed pages and dates)

### 2a. The illusion register, 572–1438

1. **Martin of Braga, c. 572 (doc. 28).**
   - Demons took the names of gods and preside over waters and woods. "In the forests, the Dianas, which are all worthless demons" (p. 86).
   - Among the "worship of the devil" practices he lists: "To observe Vulcan's day and the kalends, to set out tables" (p. 87).
   - **This is a laid table in a clerical list of superstitions about four centuries before Burchard's table for the three Fates** (`athlone_vol3_middle_ages.md`). It is not said to be for female spirits.
2. **Regino's Canon *Episcopi*, c. 910 (doc. 32, pp. 99–100; Lea's translation, revised against Migne).**
   - Women "believe and profess themselves, in the hours of night, to ride upon certain beasts with Diana, the goddess of pagans" (p. 99).
   - They obey "her commands as of their mistress" and are "summoned to her service on certain nights" (p. 99).
   - Satan, "who transforms himself into an angel of light", shows "joyful and mournful things and people known and unknown" (p. 100).
   - The unbelieving mind "thinks this happens not in spirit but in body" (p. 100).
   - The canon ends with a transformation clause: whoever believes that a creature "can be changed for better or worse … into another appearance and likeness, except by the creator himself", is "beyond doubt an infidel" (p. 100).
   - The headnote says the canon was carried by Burchard and Gratian. Later it became "a significant stumbling block" for 15th-century witchcraft theorists (p. 99).
3. **Burchard's *Corrector*, c. 1008–12, Worms (doc. 31; McNeill's translation).**
   - c. 64: women's incantations at the start of weaving, 30 days on bread and water (p. 98).
   - c. 68: belief in storm-raisers, one year (p. 98).
   - **c. 70 (pp. 98–99): one year on the appointed fast days for believing what "some, deceived by the devil" say they must do.** That is: "with a throng of demons transformed into the likeness of women", to "ride on certain beasts on special nights and be numbered with their company".
   - The leader is "she whom common folly calls the witch Hulda" (p. 99).
   - So in this text the company itself is **demons in women's likeness**. Usable as a confessor's charge against the **whole company**, never against a daughter alone, with the faith given an answer (§4 row 1) (revised 2026-10-27, user).
4. **Augustine, early 5th c. (doc. 36).** The theory behind the register: demons mingle with men's thoughts "through certain imaginary visions whether they are awake or asleep" (p. 115).
5. **Aquinas, 1265–66, Rome (doc. 79).** Demons work "through a real bodily transformation" or "through a certain delusion of the senses resulting from some manipulation of the imagination" (p. 278).
   - **Learned opinion in the 1260s therefore did not hold that everything was illusion.** That leaves rule 25 unchanged: it governs what clergy say about the Ladies, and the illusion reading stayed available.
   - Aquinas's account of demons assuming air bodies (pp. 279–81) and moving human seed (pp. 280–81) is excluded (§2f).
6. **Gerson, 1415, Council of Constance (doc. 94).**
   - No general rule "infallibly distinguishes between a dream vision" and a waking one (p. 360).
   - The fervour of "adolescents and women" is "too great, greedy, changeable, unrestrained, and for that reason, suspect" (p. 361).
   - A woman visionary's conduct with her confessors is to be investigated (p. 363).
7. **Nider, *Formicarius*, 1438 (doc. 97, p. 374). The illusion register survives into the sect phase.**
   - A Dominican meets a woman who believes she is carried by night "with [the goddess] Diana and other women".
   - She agrees to be watched. She anoints herself, sleeps, and dreams of "Lady Venus" through a demon. Shouting in her dream, she falls from the bench.
   - Witnesses confirm she "never left the bowl". Nider cites the Canon *Episcopi* as the reason (p. 374).
   - On the same pages Nider gives the Bern sect material (pp. 375–78). That shows both registers side by side in one 1438 text.

**Reading.** Pact *stories* about learned men are old (Cyprian, Theophilus, Gerbert). In this book, the first pact *charge* against a lay woman is 1431. The 1326 bull is the first pact language aimed at the ordinary baptised. It is aimed at their objects and books, not at a belief about night journeys. The book has **no "tacit pact" text.** The Aquinas selection is *De potentia* q. 6, which does not discuss pacts. The c. 1250 date for a tacit pact rests on Athlone vol. 3 alone. See §3 row 2.

### 2c. Rhineland and Lotharingian items

- **Aachen, 9th c. (doc. 39, pp. 123–24).** Notker tells how King Pippin met "the old enemy" at the hot springs of Aix before the baths were built. He struck a shape in human form, and the waters ran with gore and slime until they flowed clear. A local tale for `c_aachen` (the county of `b_prum`) at a 1066 start. It is about the devil, not the Ladies.

- **Sodomy:**
  - Peter Damian, 1049–56 (pp. 243–47). Sodomy is a vice of demonic possession. Following the Council of Ancyra, those who commit it "are to pray among those who are struck with an unclean spirit" (p. 245).
  - The *Visio Pauli* has a pit for those who sinned "male with male" (p. 174).
  - Vox in Rama (p. 260) and the *Errores* (p. 370) put same-sex acts inside their orgies (excl.).

  Under the user rule this is **how hostile voices classify people**, not a frame for the daughters.
- **Eunuchs.** Only Origen's self-castration, in a headnote (p. 50). Nothing for the Adelphai.
- **Possession of women:**
  - Gregory's nun who ate an unblessed lettuce (p. 206, late 6th c.);
  - Caesarius's possessed girl (pp. 294–95);
  - Birgitta, whose confessors tested her in case her experience "might be an illusion" (p. 233, 1340s).

### 2e. Hostile repertoire at a 1066 start

What a Latin cleric near Prüm could plausibly draw on in 1066, in this book:
- Regino (c. 910) and Burchard (c. 1008–12) for the ride;
- Martin of Braga and the penitentials (Columban, the Burgundian penitential) for tables, springs, trees and "the table of demons" (p. 97);
- Augustine for the theory;
- Damian (1049–56) for the newest polemic on sodomy.

This fits rule 24: Latin hostility before 1123 leans on the penitentials.

Tertullian's c. 200 line, "Whatever is born is the work of God. Whatever has been modified is the devil's business" (p. 77), is aimed at cosmetics. In a hostile mouth it would be an attack on any change to the body. **It is withheld, not proposed.** It needs the user's decision under rules 3 and 8.

## 5. Hostile-voice lines

In-world lines, each 25 words or fewer, each from a named speaker type of a vanilla faith (rule 3). They are paraphrases, not quotes. The narrator confirms none of them. None is spoken of Jews, Ha-Nehpakhot or any other faith. None says or implies Sabbat, familiar, broom, bodily flight, castration or the accusers' fantasies, and none frames a daughter as a disguise.

1. **Catholic confessor, Rhineland. Any date (penitential register; rule 25 "illusion").** After Burchard c. 70 (p. 99).
   > "Have you believed you go out with the *holda* on certain nights, among a throng of women? The enemy painted it. A year of fasts."
2. **Catholic parish priest, preaching. From c. 906 (rule 25 "illusion").** After Regino (p. 100).
   > "Satan shows sleepers joys and griefs, faces known and unknown. Her spirit wandered; her body never left the bed."
3. **Catholic priest at the Kalends. Any date (superstition, not pact).** After Martin of Braga (p. 87).
   > "Tables set out at the Kalends, bread thrown in the spring: you renounced these at the font. That is the old error of the pagans."
4. **Cistercian novice-master, Rhineland. From c. 1220 (rule 25 "illusion").** After Caesarius (p. 296). Hostile; of the Lady, never of a daughter.
   > "The enemy can wear a lady's light brighter than noon. Sign yourself. A true vision consoles; his leaves only a stench."
5. **Master of theology. From c. 1265 (rule 25 "illusion").** After Aquinas (p. 278).
   > "Demons work on bodies or on the imagination. Your Lady's nights are the second kind: a game played on the humours."
6. **Dominican or papal canonist. Good Ladies only, from c. 1326, within rule 25's tacit-pact exception (from c. 1250).** After *Super illius specula* (p. 352) and Isaiah 28:15. The narrator never confirms it.
   > "Whoever keeps such nights has made a league with death, as the prophet says, though she never spoke the words."
7. **Conciliar theologian. From c. 1415 (rule 25 "illusion"; discernment).** After Gerson (p. 361).
   > "The fervour of women and the young runs too hot and too changeable. Test the spirit before anyone praises her nights."
8. **Episcopal prosecutor. Good Ladies only, sect phase from c. 1430 (rule 25: outright pact charge).** After the 1431 articles (p. 365), without the assemblies.
   > "Article: she took counsel with evil spirits, held talk with them, and concluded pacts with them."
9. **Dominican preacher. From c. 1438 (the illusion verdict survives into the sect phase).** After Nider (p. 374), without ointment or bowl.
   > "We watched till dawn with witnesses. She never left the bench. Whatever she saw, a demon showed her sleeping."

Not offered: any line from Damian, the Templar's false women, or any Jewish figure (§2f). Burchard's c. 70 and the transformation material are usable under §4 row 1, and Tertullian p. 77 as a hostile voice under the same rule (user, 2026-10-27).
