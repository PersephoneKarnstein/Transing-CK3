# Witches for v2: Even-Ezra, *Ecstasy in the Classroom* (2019)

Scope: the third reading for a possible v2 "Good Ladies" faith (the night company, mostly of women, around a Lady: Diana, Herodias, Holda, Abundia, Oriente). The prior notes are `witch_papers_and_timeline.md`, `witch_hutton_lecouteux.md` and `witch_studies_reader.md`. The last of these flagged Even-Ezra's 2017 article on Roland of Cremona, night flight and ointments (via Clay & Quilty, n. 44, p. 128). This note checks whether her 2019 book carries that material and what else it offers.

Pronouns: Ayelet Even-Ezra is *they* here (not stated in the book). Silence, the romance character, is *they* here; the book uses "she" and "her/his."

Quote rule: every verbatim quote is 25 words or fewer and carries a page. Everything else is paraphrase. Page numbers are the printed book pages.

---

## 1. Bibliographic line and verdict

**Bibliographic line.** Ayelet Even-Ezra, *Ecstasy in the Classroom: Trance, Self, and the Academic Profession in Medieval Paris*. Fordham Series in Medieval Studies. New York: Fordham University Press, 2019. xii + 313 pp. ISBN 978-0-8232-8191-6 (pbk).

**Verdict: no direct Ladies material. Medium value as theory and context. The Roland "cursus" lead is not in this book.**

- The book never mentions night flight, ointments, the Canon Episcopi, Diana, Herodias, Abundia or the *bonae dominae*. The only "Diana" hits in the text are "Indiana" in citations. The 2017 *Magic, Ritual, and Witchcraft* article is not in its bibliography. The "cursus" lead from the prior note is therefore **still unread**.
- The book is about how twelve Paris theology masters (1215–45) classified **Paul's rapture** (II Cor. 12:1–5, "whether in the body or out of the body I do not know"), prophecy, faith and the vision of God. It reads those debates as the new university profession marking its turf against visionaries, simple believers, heretics and philosophers.
- That still makes it useful in three ways:
  1. It gives the period's **best learned theory of how a soul can "go" while the body stays alive in bed**. The theory was built for Paul, not for the Ladies, but it is the theory any 13th-century master would reach for.
  2. It shows the **institutional and doctrinal tools** that a century later let churchmen recode the Ladies from delusion into heresy: theologians recruited against heresy, the culpability of the misled "old woman," and the 1210 Amalrician template of a private ecstatic sect.
  3. It shows how learned men **gendered ecstatic knowledge**: women and the simple may see, but men with training interpret and preach.

## 2. Argument summary

- **Introduction (pp. 1–22).** Three "non-exemplary" meetings of masters with ecstasy:
  - Odo of Soissons asks Hildegard to settle a Trinitarian dispute (pp. 3–5). In the 1228 canonization inquiry, the Paris masters, via William of Auxerre, judged her writings "purely divine" (p. 5).
  - The Amalricians (Paris, c. 1200–1210): theology students formed a sect with ecstatic meetings and were burned (pp. 5–7).
  - Thomas Aquinas's hagiographic raptures (pp. 7–11).

  The corpus is the *quaestiones* of twelve masters, 1215–45: Godfrey of Poitiers, William of Auxerre, Philip the Chancellor, Alexander of Hales, William of Auvergne, William of Durham, Guiard of Laon, Roland of Cremona, Guerric of St. Quentin, Hugh of St. Cher, John of La Rochelle and Odo Rigaldi (p. 13). The method reads these texts as the "boundary-work" of a new profession (p. 26).
- **Ch. 1 (pp. 23–58): where was Paul's soul?** The masters start from Augustine's three kinds of vision (corporeal, spiritual or imaginary, intellectual) and the earth/heaven grid (pp. 27–30). Older masters say that if the soul is in the body, vision must be dimmed (an "intermediary vision"). The Dominicans (Roland, Guerric, Hugh of St. Cher) and the Franciscan John of La Rochelle use medical theory to argue that **full abstraction is possible while the soul stays in the living body** (pp. 38–42). Even-Ezra links this to the mendicant ideal of perfection lived inside the world (pp. 42–44).
- **Ch. 2 (pp. 59–80): memory after rapture.** How can Paul remember an imageless vision? The answers involve a lingering habitus, images God inserts afterwards, or remembering only *that* one saw (pp. 61–67). Roland makes *experientia* the ground of knowledge, using a honey simile (pp. 77–78).
- **Ch. 3 (pp. 81–110): seeing without intermediaries.** Light, habitus and *medium*. Courtly romance is used as a cultural parallel for seeing essences through images, including *Silence*'s gender disguise (pp. 105–09).
- **Ch. 4 (pp. 111–34): does faith need anything external?** Interior illumination versus authority. This chapter holds the heresy material: the old woman, the woodsman, theologians as anti-heresy troops (pp. 129–34).
- **Ch. 5 (pp. 135–57): what happens to old cognitions in transitions?** Two habitus can sit in one soul, with one active. This models the learned friar who can "play simple" without hypocrisy (pp. 153–55). Also: the theologian and the old woman in heaven (pp. 146–47, 157).
- **Ch. 6 (pp. 158–88): knowledge as virtue.** Charismatic gifts, preaching, and the exclusion of women and unlicensed laymen from public preaching (pp. 175–76).
- **Epilogue (pp. 189–98).** The self as a "platform of potencies" with habitus loosely attached (p. 194). William of Auvergne is an outsider to the faculty's discourse (pp. 194–95).

## 3. Usable pre-1453 material (with dates and places)

### 3a. The body stays home: soul-in-body rapture theory (Paris, c. 1220–1245)

**Why it matters for the Ladies.** The Canon Episcopi tradition (Regino c. 906, Burchard c. 1010) says the riders go "in spirit," while demons fill sleeping minds with images. By the 1230s Paris masters had a precise physiology for a soul that is "away" in its powers while the body lies alive and unmoving. Even-Ezra never applies this to the Ladies; the link is this note's inference. It could cut two ways:
- **It confirms the "delusion" reading.** The body never leaves the bed, so the journey is internal.
- **It makes the experience real cognition rather than mere dream.** That is acceptable for Paul (grace) but alarming if the source is demonic.

### 3b. What demons can and cannot do (Roland, c. 1230–44)

Roland discusses magical techniques (the *Ars notoria* type, n. 10) that promise instant learning from demons. He answers that demons "cannot inspire thoughts, just corporeal images" (p. 114). They must teach aloud, and learning takes time.

This is the period's working assumption, and it fits the Canon Episcopi model exactly: a demon can show a sleeping woman a ride, a Lady and a feast, because these are corporeal images, but it cannot inject understanding. In-game this is a ready-made **churchman's verdict line** for the delusion phase.

### 3c. Memory: you remember that you went

- Even-Ezra borrows the anthropological contrast that after trance "the shaman remembers his or her celestial voyage," while the possessed "often experience amnesia" (p. 61).
- William of Auxerre: on return, Paul has only "nominal" knowledge of what he saw, like a blind man's knowledge of colour (pp. 61–62).
- Hugh of St. Cher: Paul kept only a residue, like remembering *that* one dreamed and perhaps its gist (p. 67).
- Douai q. 454: Paul remembered "for the benefit of the church" (p. 66).

### 3e. Gendered ecstasy

- Ecstatic authority "allows more flexibility in social and gender roles, as many have shown regarding Hildegard and later female mystics" (p. 112). Its limits show in the Hildegard case: the masters legitimated her words; she did not legitimate theirs (pp. 4–5).
- The 12th–13th-century "new mysticism" features women (Hildegard, Elisabeth of Schönau, Marie d'Oignies) with repeated long ecstasies, alongside masters who claim a rival, rational mode (p. 26).
- Dinzelbacher's shift: early medieval visions are one-off transports; from the 12th century, visions become a **repeatable gift** (p. 22). The Ladies' riders, going on set nights, fit that repeatable pattern rather than the one-off otherworld tour.
- Jacques de Vitry on the urban holy women around Marie d'Oignies: living an "angelic" life in the world (p. 43). Even-Ezra links this to the mendicant claim that one can be momentarily angelic while still in the body (pp. 43–44).
- **The vetula as the theologian's foil.** In the 1220s–40s the stock contrast changes from "layman and literate" to "a theologian and an old woman" or "a simple peasant" (pp. 146–47, 157). Will both have equal knowledge of God in heaven? William of Auxerre at last agrees they stand "on square one." Roland says flatly that the theologian's knowledge "will cease" (p. 157). The epilogue lists "old devout women who seemed to possess superior knowledge of God" among the masters' rivals (p. 193).

### 3f. Gender ambiguity and the self as habitus

- ***Silence*** (Heldris of Cornwall, 13th c.; sole manuscript c. 1286). A girl raised as a boy so the family can inherit. Their father plans to "make a male of a female" (p. 107). The Latin ending *-us* is a pun on French *us*, "habit." Silence weighs *us d'ome* against *us de feme* and chooses to keep the male habits. No one can see their "nature" until Merlin exposes it (pp. 106–09). Even-Ezra reads this as the same habitus-over-nature model as the theologians'. William of Auvergne asked why seeing the king in his garments is not seeing the king's essence (p. 108).
- **Role-switching without hypocrisy.** In Celano's Francis story (1240s), a learned friar performs simplicity and a simple friar performs learning. Neither is a liar, because both habitus are really in them (pp. 153–55).
- **The epilogue's model.** "To discuss trance is to discuss the possibility of transformation" (p. 194). The self is a natural platform of potencies with acquirable habitus attached, tied to "the roles of gender and social status" in courtly literature (p. 194).

### 3g. A side lead

Gerald of Wales reports "shaman-like practice" in Wales (*Descriptio Kambriae* 1.16, c. 1194; book n. 4 to p. 2). These are presumably the *awenyddion*, Welsh soothsayers who speak in trance. They are not Ladies material, but they are an on-map, pre-1453 trance role.

## 4. How this changes the prior notes' timeline (delusion → heresy)

**The flip date does not move.** Nothing here moves the recoding away from c. 1320–1430 (*Super illius specula* 1326, Kyteler 1324, Milan 1384/1390, Valais 1428). Even-Ezra's masters never discuss the Ladies.

**The "delusion and penance" phase was not static, though.** Three refinements:

1. **Add a "tools forged" sub-phase, c. 1210–1250 (Paris, Toulouse, northern France).** In these decades:
   - the Amalricians make "private meetings + ecstatic claims" a burnable heresy (1210);
   - the papacy enlists Paris theologians against heresy (1217, 1219);
   - William of Auxerre makes the misled simple believer **culpable** (1220s);
   - masters such as Roland and Philip the Chancellor take part in preaching and inquisition (1230s).

   None of this targets the Ladies. It is the machinery later inquisitors inherit. In-game: from about 1230 the *capacity* for persecution rises, mendicant preachers appear, and secrecy and gatherings matter. The Ladies stay filed as superstition.
2. **In the 13th century, learned theory made the belief *less* dangerous intellectually.** The masters had a clear account: the soul can be abstracted while the body lies alive, and demons work only with images (pp. 38–41, 114). That supports Regino's and Burchard's line that it happens only in spirit and is delusion, so penance, not fire. Danger grows when the question shifts from "is the experience real?" to "**who** is giving it and **whom** do they worship?" That is the 14th-century move, outside this book.
3. **The prior note's William of Auvergne entry (Abundia, c. 1230s) needs a caveat.** Even-Ezra shows that William was an outsider who did not write in the faculty's genres and was rarely cited (pp. 194–95). His demonizing reading of Abundia is one bishop's view, not "university opinion" in the 1230s.

**The prior notes' Even-Ezra lead.** `witch_studies_reader.md` flagged the 2017 *Cursus* article as the "single most useful pre-1453 lead." That stays open. The book confirms Roland's interest in medicine (Galen, vital spirits; a possible teacher of medicine in Bologna, pp. 38–39, n. 45) and in *experimenta*, a "gray zone between medicine and magic" (p. 75). That background makes a medical discussion of ointments by Roland plausible. It does not show what the article says.

## 5. Design implications for a CK3 "Good Ladies" faith

All of these are design suggestions. Any line voiced by a churchman should be a generic, unnamed master or friar. The narrator does not claim Roland, Guerric, Hildegard, Marie d'Oignies or anyone else for the faith.

1. **Event: "A Master's Quaestio on the Night Riders"** (Latin Christendom, 1215+; likelier with a university or mendicant presence). A visiting master disputes whether the Lady's riders really travel. Options mirror the period's positions:
   - *"The demon shows images to sleepers; she never left her bed."* This is the Canon Episcopi plus Roland's demon theory (p. 114). Hostility stays low and the outcome is penance.
   - *"The soul may be away in its powers while it keeps the body as form."* This is the Guerric/Hugh mechanism (pp. 40–41). The experience is conceded as real. That gives a small piety gain for the faithful but raises the master's suspicion: *who*, then, does she see?
   - *"Such joy can stop the heart."* This is Roland's Galenic warning (p. 39). It gives a health-risk flavour line and a reason for the household to keep watch.

   Before about 1320 no option should lead to a trial. After about 1320 the second option should feed the heresy track.
2. **Rite: "The Body Keeps the House."** On a Lady's night the rider lies down. A household member keeps the hearth and watches the body. In mechanics: a short state in which the character cannot travel or act, then stress relief or a minor boon, and a memory of *having gone* but not of all she saw. This is grounded in Burchard's women lying beside their husbands (prior note), plus the scholastic "form, not mover" and "remember that you dreamed" models (pp. 40, 67). Label the scholastic parallel as design analogy, not attested Ladies practice.
3. **Gendered framing as church doctrine, not faith truth.** Church-side text should voice the period's division: women and the simple may *see*, but only trained men *interpret* and only good men preach (pp. 27, 176). The faith's own doctrine can quietly invert this with the masters' own admission: in the end the theologian's knowledge "will cease" and the old woman stands level with him (p. 157). That gives a tenet or flavour line along the lines of "the old woman knows as much as the master."
4. **Persecution modifiers (c. 1210–1320).** Raise the church's *capacity* (mendicant preachers, an inquisitor available) from about 1230 in Languedoc, the Île-de-France and Lombardy. Do not yet apply heresy status to the Ladies. Add a trigger: if believers hold **regular gatherings with claims of visions**, use the Amalrician template (secret meetings plus ecstasy equals sect), and hostility rises earlier.
5. **Event: "The Feigned Rapture."** A cleric joins the night company pretending to go out with the Lady, in order to inform. This models Master Ralph among the Amalricians, 1210 (p. 6). It works as a spy event in the late phase.
6. **Daughters and habitus.** The habitus language ("second nature," *us*, role worn without hypocrisy; pp. 107, 154–55, 194) gives the narrator period vocabulary for a woman's self as something lived and acquired, not only given by birth. Use it in narrator voice, or in a churchman's mouth as a hostile argument ("she has put on the *us* of a woman"). Do not present *Silence* as a trans-women's precedent.

## 6. Guardrails

- **The book has no Ladies evidence.** Never cite Even-Ezra for night flight, ointments or the Lady. Every link between the Ladies and the scholastic rapture theory is this note's inference.
- **Even-Ezra limits their own claim.** The study of this elite group "tells us more about them than about anyone else" (p. 194). It cannot stand for peasant or women's beliefs.
- ***Silence* is not a trans woman.** The character is assigned female, raised male for inheritance, and restored to womanhood by "Nature" at the end. The romance is useful for 13th-century ideas of gender as *us*/habit. It is not evidence for daughters, and no pre-1453 source shows a male-assigned person in the Ladies' company *as a woman* (prior note).
- **Paul's rapture is graced, orthodox ecstasy.** Do not imply that the masters approved trance in general. They classified it to control who could claim it.
- **No Murray.** Nothing here supports covens, a horned god or a surviving pagan church.
- **Historical people stay historical.** Roland, Guerric, Hildegard and Marie d'Oignies appear only as period voices or context. Event masters should be generic.
- **Living communities.** The book's references to modern shamans, anthropology and trance are comparative frames. They should not be put into the faith or presented as it.

## 7. Open questions and further sources

**Open questions**
1. What exactly does Roland's *cursus* passage say? Who rides (women, men, both)? Does it name a Lady? Is the ointment discussed medically, as demonic, or both? Is it in the edited *Liber tercius* or in an unedited book of the *Summa*?
2. Did any 13th-century master apply the "soul away, body alive" mechanism to the Canon Episcopi riders, or did they keep the two topics apart?
3. When did the riders' experience stop being classed as *phantasia* and start being classed as a pact? Was it Aquinas on demonic illusion (Sent. IV, d. 34), or only 14th-century inquisitors?
4. Did the "culpable old woman" argument (William of Auxerre) reach confessors' manuals that also carried the Canon Episcopi?
