# Ancient background: Athlone *Witchcraft and Magic in Europe*, vols 1 and 2 (triage)

Scope: a triage read for the v2 Good Ladies (`witch_design.md` §1–3, §10) and for the Adelphai, Bædling and Ha-Nehpakhot, checked against `faith_design.md` §16 (rules 2, 9, 24, 25 as amended, 26) and against `stratton_naming_the_witch.md`, which this note does not repeat. Contents, introductions and conclusions were read in full; the passages cited below were read closely; the rest was skimmed by keyword.

Pronouns: every modern scholar named here is *they* (none is stated in the books).

Quote rule: every verbatim quote has 25 words or fewer and a page number. Everything else is paraphrase. Pages are the printed pages. Vol. 1 has a text layer. Vol. 2 is an OCR scan: its pages were checked against the running heads (PDF page minus 25 in Parts 3–4).

## 1. Bibliographic lines and verdicts

**Vol. 1.** Marie-Louise Thomsen and Frederick H. Cryer, *Witchcraft and Magic in Europe: Biblical and Pagan Societies*. Ed. Bengt Ankarloo and Stuart Clark. Athlone History of Witchcraft and Magic in Europe 1. London: Athlone Press, 2001. xiv + 170 pp. ISBN 0-485-89001-1 (hb), 0-485-89101-8 (pbk).

**Verdict: almost nothing usable. Background only.**
- **The book has only two parts**: Mesopotamia (Thomsen) and Syria-Palestine and the Old Testament (Cryer). **There is no Celtic, Nordic or Germanic chapter.** The series blurb names those traditions, but they are not in this volume (Nordic material is in Athlone vol. 3, *The Middle Ages*).
- **No assinnu, kurgarrû or kalû.** Neither part names Ishtar's gender-crossing cult personnel. Ishtar (Istar) appears only as the goddess of love charms, potency rites and the "Hand of Istar" illness (pp. 41, 63–70).
- **No Deut. 22:5.** Cryer discusses Deut. 18:10–14 (forbidden divination) and *kešep* (sorcery), not cross-dressing.

**Vol. 2.** Valerie Flint, Richard Gordon, Georg Luck and Daniel Ogden, *Witchcraft and Magic in Europe: Ancient Greece and Rome*. Ed. Bengt Ankarloo and Stuart Clark. Athlone History 2. London: Athlone Press; Philadelphia: University of Pennsylvania Press, 1999. xiv + c. 420 pp. ISBN 0-485-89002-X (hb); the file carries 978-0-485-89101-0.

**Verdict: low to medium value, almost all of it for the Good Ladies' hostile learned voice, plus one Adelphai item.**
- Gordon (Part 3) is the useful essay. Their "night-witch" is a learned fantasy figure that no real person was ever charged as. That fits the Ladies' design: the clerics' lore is a book-image, not a description of the women.
- There is **no Diana of the night ride, Herodias or Canon *Episcopi***. Diana appears once, as a bronze temple statue (p. 147). The series stops at Augustine and Theodosius.
- **There are no gallae.** Cybele appears three times in passing (pp. 46, 105, 132). The one usable item is a hostile apologist's line on the Mother's castrations (Flint, p. 304).

## 3. Usable items, by faith

### The Good Ladies (clerical learned voice only; never the faith's own)
1. **The night-witch is never a person.** Gordon: "No one is ever accused of being a night-witch; but she does have a kind of history" (vol. 2 p. 204). They separate the everyday healer or wise woman one might meet from the *strix*, "the night-owl that is really a flesh-devouring woman" (p. 204). *Use:* design support for `witch_design.md` §1 and §5: the clerics lay a book-image over real household women. A narrator or tooltip may say the lore "came from books". It is not loc.
2. **Learned, not inherited.** The editors set out the view that ancient–medieval likenesses are "the result of later scholarly interpretations and adaptations from literary sources" (p. xiii). They note it is contested and do not settle it. *Use:* supports the no-continuity guardrail; a 12th–14th-century cleric naming the Lady "Diana" is reading Latin books, not meeting a survival.
3. **The Ovid rite against *striges*** (Fasti 6.155–64): Cranaë touches the door-posts and thresholds with arbutus, sprinkles water, and leaves an offering outside; no one may look back (Gordon pp. 192–93). Gordon reads it as a civic rite that Ovid's circle found quaint: "for who else would believe in striges either?" (p. 193). *Use:* a learned cleric's sneer at a household's thresholds. **Cautious**: the *strix* leads to child-eating (banned by §10). The rite may be cited; the bird may not be described.
4. **Roman law on magic**, for a jurist or inquisitor voice after c. 1300 (Stratton already has the Twelve Tables and the *lex Cornelia*; these are additions):
   - Paulus, *Sententiae* 5.23.15–18: professional magicians burned; participants to the beasts or crucified; magic books burned, owners exiled or, if humble, beheaded (Ogden pp. 82–83; Gordon p. 265 dates the book clauses to the late third century).
   - Knowledge or practice? An Ulpian text (Coll. 15.2): the older jurists held "it was the performance, not the knowledge, that was prohibited" (Gordon p. 265). Hadrian's declamation argues the reverse: knowing the art already makes one suspect (p. 264).
   - *Use:* a Bolognese-trained jurist in the Milan trials (1384–90) can argue that *knowing* the Lady's ways is itself the crime. Paraphrase only, and only after the trials begin. It must not be used to imply execution before 1390 (`witch_design.md` §5).
5. **Lilith as *lamia*.** Flint notes the Vulgate renders Lilith (Isa. 34:14) as "lamia", the "night monster" (p. 293). *Use:* a cleric's Latin word for a night-woman. **Caution:** keep it Christian and Vulgate. Never let it touch Ha-Nehpakhot or a Jewish character (Lilith via Jewish sources is not to be used).
6. **Hekate**, "goddess of night-witches", among the tombs (Gordon p. 207); Medea's and Erictho's catalogues (pp. 205–08). These repeat Stratton's classical stock and add nothing new. The Ladies' Lady is *not* Hekate in any voice.

### Adelphai Dorleyaiou (ancient memory, hostile voice)
7. **Athenagoras, *Plea* 26–27** (2nd c.), on demons who love blood: "some castrate, as Rhea, others wound and slaughter, as Artemis" (Flint p. 304). *Use:* the oldest Christian slander against the Mother's servants, which a Byzantine cleric would have in his Fathers. It suits a named Orthodox speaker (rule 3: exposing or unpersoning shape). The Sisters may answer it. It is a hostile line about the Mother's rite, not about Attis, so it keeps rule 2 (no Attis imagery, no golden past).
8. ***Metragyrtai*.** Luck (p. 132) cites Philostratus's Apollonius sneering at "certain needy vagabonds", the Mother's begging priests (Lucian; Apuleius, *Met.* 8.24), who live off the god they carry. *Use:* a possible Greek insult ("beggar of the Mother") for a learned Byzantine voice. **Rule 9:** it needs a Byzantine source first. The *Suda* is the likely one, but that is unverified here (open question 2). Luck says the image is "Demeter or Dionysus", which looks like an error for the Mother; do not rely on Luck's wording.

### Ha-Nehpakhot
9. **Cryer's thesis** (vol. 1 pp. 113–20, 143–47): magic and divination are embedded in Israelite religion, not a foreign intrusion, and the courts rarely heard witchcraft cases. Cryer also finds that Israel's witchcraft ban did not target women: "the witchcraft accusation was probably not one of the mechanisms of social control designed to constrain women's behaviour" (p. 140). The Endor medium "is hardly a 'witch' in a social-anthropological sense at all" (p. 141). *Use:* background only. The faith's own voice cites the prophets (rule 26), not ancient Near Eastern magic. Nothing here should give the sect a "magic" register.

### Bædling, Yutai Dao, the Worn Garland, Badenya
Nothing. Vol. 1 has no Nordic or Germanic chapter, so there is nothing to set against the `norse_*.md` notes.

## 4. What stays out
- **Luck's Mother-Earth survival** (vol. 2 p. 105): "perhaps what we call magic is the survival of a very ancient religion, the cult of the mother Earth". This is a Murray-shaped continuity claim. Not in any voice, and not cited as support for the Adelphai's Mother either.
- **Cryer on Jews and "magical prowess"** (vol. 1 p. 117). Cryer says outsiders credit "purposely delimited ethnic groups" with powerful magic. As anthropology this is about outsiders' beliefs, but it is exactly the "Jewish magic" stereotype. Not in any voice, not even as a hostile rumour.
- **Foreign-woman witches** (Maqlû's Elamite and Gutian women, vol. 1 p. 29) and Livy's 170 poisoning matrons (vol. 2 p. 255). These are panic patterns; no faith's members are cast this way.
- **"Voodoo dolls"** (Ogden's chapter title and term). It is the wrong word, and doll imagery is banned for the Japanese faith. Use *figurines* if the topic ever arises.
- **Mesopotamian cult personnel.** These books give nothing; do not cite them for assinnu or kurgarrû. If the Adelphai ever need them, the framing must not be "third gender" in the narrator's voice. They are outside the Adelphai's Phrygian memory anyway.
- **Hekate, Erictho, Canidia, Medea, Thessalian moon-pulling** (Gordon pp. 205–08, 223): Stratton's stock. They stay in the hostile classical sneer only, as Stratton advises.
- **Matronymics in curses** ("whom a womb bore", Ogden pp. 60–61): rule 20 (no tying of motherhood to the womb). Not used.
