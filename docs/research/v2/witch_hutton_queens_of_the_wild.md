## 0. Framing warning

- **The title is the publisher's.** Hutton says it "does not quite deal with the problem of whether the subjects of the book were either pagan or goddesses" (p. ix). Do not cite the title as his verdict.
- **His frame is a historiography of "pagan survival"** (ch. 1). He separates *surviving paganism* (a persisting pagan religion, which professional historians agree did not outlast conversion for long) from *pagan survivals* (customs, motifs and images carried into Christian culture) (pp. 23, 30–31). His target in this book is a third path: figures that are neither (pp. 39–40, 196).
- **He concedes the limits of absence.** Before c. 1400, he says, the first record of a popular custom usually gives no sign of when it began (p. 24). His case against a pagan origin therefore rests on positive mismatches (no ancient goddess fits) and on the silence of 5th–9th-century decrees, not on late first attestation alone.
- **Much of his medieval material is second-hand.** He reaches the Percht texts through Grimm, Rumpf, Russell and Lecouteux (nn. 26–28, 32). He reaches the Italian trials through Ginzburg and Behringer (nn. 34–37). He has checked Regino, Burchard, Rather, William of Auvergne and some others in editions (nn. 1–6, 12, 16).
- **Living communities.** Holda's and Percht's modern folklore, the Perchten, the Befana, the Romanian *căluș* and Leland's *Aradia* (pp. 138–41) are modern. Per `witch_design.md` §10 none of them is the faith or its continuation. Wicca's debt to *Aradia* (p. 141) is reported, not used.

## 1. The verdict

**Hutton's 2022 answer is no: the Lady is not shown to be a pagan goddess, and he now leans towards a medieval creation.**
- "There is thus no known pagan goddess who can be identified as an obvious ancestress of the medieval lady or ladies" (p. 138).
- Elements of Diana, Hera, Hecate, Epona, the Matres, the Valkyries or the Disir might have gone into her. That cannot be proved, and "such a construction would still be a medieval one" (p. 138).
- It is "equally possible" that the idea arose new between conversion and the Canon *Episcopi* (p. 138).
- On the two German names: "neither of the German names of the medieval lady – Holda and Percht – seem clearly to have an ancient religious origin" (p. 136). No name given to her can be derived from ancient Germanic languages (p. 136).
- In the conclusion he generalises: "It seems wrong to refer to such figures as 'pagan survivals'". They "appear to have been products of the Christian period" (p. 196). Their believers were "otherwise orthodox Christians for their place and time" (p. 196). Yet they worked wholly outside the Christian picture of the world, so "Christian" does not fit them either (pp. 196–97).

**This moves past his 2014 position.** In 2014 he left a pre-Christian origin "open but plausible" (`witch_hutton_lecouteux.md` §1). In 2022 he tests every candidate and finds none that fits. Of Motz (1984) and Horsley (1979), Hutton names neither. He rejects their method (Grimm's Norse-and-folklore reconstruction, n. 67, n. 77) and their conclusion.

## 2. Findings

### 2a. What the tradition was (pp. 110–11, 117, 141–42)

- **Three constant features** (pp. 110–11):
  1. Commoners held it, "especially … poor women", and sometimes men of the same class (pp. 110–11). Those who claimed to travel with the Lady gained standing in their communities. Free entry to richer neighbours' houses was the wish of an underclass (p. 111).
  2. The women were often the service magicians (healers, finders, diviners) of their communities, taught by the Lady (p. 111).
  3. It "nowhere seems to have involved any actual group activity" (p. 111). Travel was in the mind while the body stayed still (p. 111). **This supports rule 25's spirit-travel clause directly.**
- **Not a heresy, not paganism, to its recorders.** It was found in societies with a full parish clergy, not "societies recently reclaimed from paganism" (p. 117). Churchmen called it a demonic illusion of foolish women and gave mild penances (p. 117).
- **Her character.** Above all good (p. 141). Her names mean abundance, satiety, wisdom, exotic wealth and kindness (p. 142). She is also wholly un-Christian: never made a saint, never paying homage to God, a female leader with no superior (p. 142). Hutton's closing judgement: "one of the great counter-cultural figures of the human record" (p. 142).

### 2b. The Frankish heartland, c. 900–1066 (pp. 111–17)

- **c. 900+, Prüm/Trier, Regino.** The Canon's original is lost. Its earliest appearance is in the canon-law collection made "soon after the year 900" for the archbishop of Trier by Regino, abbot of Prüm (p. 111; n. 1, Wasserschleben 1840, p. 355). It probably originated in the previous century in Frankish lands, author unknown (p. 111). Hutton does not mention Lecouteux's 872 Charles the Bald claim.
- **Regino's last directive** (preach against it, expel fortune-telling and magic) implies the believers were service magicians (p. 112). The belief must have been well established by then for the text to claim "an innumerable multitude" (pp. 111–12).
- **Early 10th c., Verona, Rather** (*Praeloquia* I.10, PL 136 col. 157). Many people, **women and men**, call Herodias a queen and say a third of the world is her realm. **He does not tie her to night rides** (p. 114). He came from Liège, so his information may not be Italian (pp. 114–15).
- **Early 11th c., Worms, Burchard** (*Decretum* X.29; XIX.5.70, 90, 170–71; PL 140) (n. 4).
  - One passage repeats the Canon and adds Herodias to Diana. This version was repeated through the Middle Ages (p. 114).
  - Another has a host of demons in women's shape "which the deluded populace calls Holda" riding beasts on certain nights (p. 114).
  - Two more traditions of women leaving their beds in spirit to gather. Hutton notes "it was always women" (p. 114).
  - **The Holda problem** (pp. 115–16). Burchard's recensions differ. In the earliest manuscripts, Hutton reports, *holda* names the ride itself: "The latest thought seems to be that the earliest known manuscripts of the work are those in which the name refers to the rides" (p. 116; n. 11, via Wasserschleben, Schmitz, Fournier, McNeill and Gamer, Austin 2004). A minority recension reads "the *striga* Holda", making her a leader and a *striga*, the man-eating night-woman of the Germanic law codes (pp. 115–16). One copyist changed the word to "Unholda" (p. 115; n. 9). The leader reading later became dominant (p. 116).
- **The 9th-century picture** (p. 117). By the 9th century the belief was well established in the Frankish zone, "possibly an epicentre … in the Rhineland" (p. 117). The churchmen who recorded it had no interest in its details, so the Lady is "faceless and formless" in the early sources (p. 117).

### 2d. Late medieval: Percht, the seasons, Italy (pp. 121–32)

- **Percht arrives** (p. 122). She "first seems to appear in the thirteenth century, in a bare reference to 'Lady Perchten'" (p. 122; n. 26, Rumpf, *Perchten*, 1991, p. 61). She comes into focus in two **mid-14th-century** texts (n. 27, via Grimm, *Teutonic Mythology* 1:276–81, and Lecouteux pp. 15–16):
  - *The Mirror of Souls*: those who leave food and drink on the table on Epiphany night for luck sin, as do those who offer food to Percht and red shoes to the "shrieker" or the nightmare (p. 122). This is the first tie of such offerings to Epiphany (p. 122).
  - Another pious work: "Berchten with the long nose" (p. 122).
- **Opening of the 15th c., Tyrol** (Vintler, via Russell 1972, p. 175). Women say they rode out at night in companies of twenty or more on calves, goats, cows, pigs, stools and cabinets, after Herodias, Diana or "Percht with the iron nose" (p. 122; n. 28).
- **15th c., Vienna, Ebendorfer.** Percht, active around Epiphany, equals Habundia and Satia (p. 122; n. 30).
- **1423, Padua, Bernardino of Siena.** Old women who worked as service magicians said they went "in the ride of Herodias on the night of Epiphany". **The midwinter timing has reached northern Italy before 1453** (p. 123; n. 33).
- **1354, Tuscany.** An unnamed Dominican preacher: the night company is called the *tregenda*; women name men and women of it, and its mistresses are Herodias and "the ancient Diana" (p. 128; n. 43, via Cohn). The belief now has popular roots in Italy (pp. 128–29).
- **1384 and 1390, Milan.** Hutton now dates the trials **1384 and 1390** (p. 123). The details match the existing notes; he adds that one woman said the Lady's presence did not allow God's name to be spoken, and that the first trial gave penance and the second, the stake (p. 124).
- **Post-1453 (late flavour only):** Herolt's sermons, **1474** ("Diana, commonly called … Unholde, that is the beatific woman", with her army, in the Twelve Days) and **1478** (adds "Lady Berthe") (pp. 122–23; n. 31). The *Thesaurus pauperum* (undated by Hutton; via Lecouteux pp. 16–17) (p. 123; n. 32). Richella, Val di Fassa, 1457 (p. 125). Mantua 1489, Fiemme and Ferrara, early 16th c. (pp. 125–26). Lady Selga, Tyrol, 1525 (p. 127). Geiler at **Strasbourg, 1508** (p. 128). Sicily, 1579–1651, with a late-15th-c. Sicilian penitential (pp. 129–30). Late medieval Catalonia (p. 130).
- **England.** *Fasciculus morum* (early 14th c.) merges Diana's rides with native elves (p. 132; n. 52). *Dives and Pauper* (early 15th c.) condemns "setting of meat and drink by night on the bench to feed All-holde" at the new moon or new year (p. 132; n. 54). Hutton reads both as Continental borrowings: the Lady never really reached Britain, northern France, the Low Countries, northern Germany or Scandinavia (p. 132).
- **Geography over time** (pp. 128, 130–32). The early record centres on the Rhine valley. By the 13th c. it runs from deep in Germany across southern France to the Pyrenees. By the 14th c. it has northern Italy. In the later Middle Ages it fades from France and, by the 16th c., splits three ways (pp. 131–32):
  - in Germany, a leader whose spirits visit homes but no longer take living people;
  - in the German Alps, leaderless companies that take living people;
  - in Italy, both a leader and human members.
- **Why Holda and Percht are absent from the German witch hunts:** by then neither was thought to take living people with her (p. 131). The one exception Hutton knows is Hesse, **1632**: a magician of Calbach went on the "night journey" to the Venusberg, where "Lady Holt" showed him the dead (n. 49, p. 218).

### 2e. Modern folklore, flagged as such (pp. 138–41)

- **Holda** (Hesse, Thuringia, southern Saxony, Franconia; via Grimm): she brings fertility and **blesses spinners**, sometimes appearing as a spinning wife; she is tied to winter and snow; she travels in a wagon between Christmas and Twelfth Night; and in her dark aspect she leads a host of malevolent spirits, witches and **unbaptised babies** (p. 139; n. 74).
- **Percht** (Alsace, Swabia, Switzerland, Bavaria, Austria, 19th c.): abroad in the Twelve Days, patroness of **neat homes and spinners**, punishes by **slitting bellies**, sometimes with a company of children (p. 139; n. 75).
- Grimm's 19th-century folklore fused the Lady's host with the processions of the dead, which led him to think both were one primeval religion (n. 77, p. 218).
