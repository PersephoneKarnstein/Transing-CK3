# Prüm and Regino: MacLean's translation of the *Chronicle* (2009), read for the 1066 Prüm seat

**Source.** Simon MacLean (trans. and annotated), *History and Politics in Late Carolingian and Ottonian Europe: The Chronicle of Regino of Prüm and Adalbert of Magdeburg*, Manchester Medieval Sources (Manchester and New York: Manchester University Press, 2009), ISBN 978 0 7190 7135 5. Read from a text extraction (an OCR of the Internet Archive scan) kept outside the repo. Page numbers are the printed ones, recovered from the running heads; where a head was lost in the OCR the page was fixed from the neighbouring heads.

Quote rule: every verbatim quote is 25 words or fewer and carries a page. MacLean's own words are quoted only where the wording matters. Regino's words (in MacLean's English) appear in §4 for loc. Everything else is paraphrase.

## 0. Framing warning

- **This is a political chronicle, not a witness to popular belief.** It is a history of Frankish kings written in 908 for the court of Louis the Child (pp. 17–18). It has no penitential material and no word on the Ladies. Do not cite it for the Canon *Episcopi*.
- **Regino is a partisan, and says so.** He wrote as a deposed abbot in exile, "favoured insider turned embittered outsider" (p. 6). He censored his own account of his deposition (pp. 47–51). His treatment of queens and wives is a moral and dynastic argument shaped by the Lothar II divorce (pp. 27, 43–44; p. 131 n. 34). His women are exempla, not portraits.
- **MacLean's text is provisional by its own account.** It follows Kurze's 1890 edition, which Schleidgen showed was built on 7 of 30 manuscripts (p. 58). The 892 entry is the worst affected (p. 59).
- **The OCR is imperfect.** Spellings such as "Priim" and "Prtim" are scan errors for Prüm. Where a quotation is load-bearing, check the printed book.
- **Violence.** The entries are full of raids, blindings, murders and a beheading. The notes below refer to them as events; nothing here is to be shown on screen.
- **Hostile categories stay hostile.** The sodomy, incest and "unnatural" charges in the divorce case (§1f), and the feminising insult to Narses (§1h), are recorded only as the way a hostile voice or the law of the day classified people. None of them describes the daughters. The daughters are women: no proposal below frames a daughter as a gay man, a cross-dresser or a man in disguise, and none treats her womanhood as costume.

## 1. Findings

### 1a. Regino himself (pp. 1–8, 47–51)

- **Abbot of Prüm, 892–899; at Trier, 899–915.** The brothers elected him in 892 under the free election guaranteed by royal charters of 884 and 891 (p. 4). He was removed in 899, during the fight over Lotharingia that put the child Louis IV in place of Zwentibald, and replaced by Richar, brother of the counts Gerard and Matfrid (p. 6). He fled to Trier, where Archbishop Ratbod (d. 915) received him and gave him the "very much lesser" house of St-Martin; he died there in 915 (p. 6).
- **Family and early years are obscure.** A 16th-century tradition makes him an aristocrat from Altrip near Speyer (p. 3). He may be one of two monks called Reginhardus in the Prüm lists under Abbot Ansbald (860–886) (p. 4).
- **His abbacy.** He kept the blinded rebel Hugh, son of Lothar II, in custody in the later 890s and tonsured him in person (pp. 5, 32). The estate survey of Prüm (the *Urbar*), commissioned after the Viking attacks of 882 and 892, was "probably completed under Regino's supervision in 893" (p. 5; Wisplinghoff dissents, p. 5 n. 13).
- **All three of his books were written at Trier, in exile** (p. 6): the treatise on music with its tonary (for Ratbod), the *Chronicle* (dedicated in 908 to Bishop Adalbero of Augsburg), and the canon-law handbook (§1b).
- **He deleted his own account of the deposition.** A long passage in the 892 entry is missing in every manuscript; MacLean thinks Regino cut it himself, for fear of offending the powerful (pp. 9, 47–50; p. 213 n. 412). Under 899 he says he will not record how the abbacy "was done against me" (p. 224). Who his enemies were is unresolved: the Matfridings, Charles the Straightforward and Zwentibald have all been proposed (pp. 50–51).

### 1b. *De synodalibus causis*: where and when the Canon was written (p. 7)

- **Compiled at Trier, at Ratbod's request, and dedicated to Archbishop Hatto of Mainz (d. 913), "after 906 (the date of the latest material included)"** (p. 7). So the canon-law book dates **906–913**, from Trier, not from Prüm.
- **Form.** About 900 excerpts from councils and other authorities, in two parts, "one aimed at priests and the other at the behaviour of the laity" (p. 7). It is "framed with lists of questions for the user to ask when travelling round his diocese": a handbook for the bishop's visitation and for training priests (p. 7).
- **Local practice.** Some procedures, "those concerning penance, for example", seem to reflect contemporary practice in the archdiocese of Trier (p. 7).
- **Reach.** Eleven manuscripts survive; the book passed into Western canon law through Burchard of Worms (p. 7). This is the documentary chain behind `witch_design.md` §2 (Regino, then Burchard).
- **Other canons MacLean cites from it** (useful for the Penitential's Question event): II.76 on Theutberga (p. 145 n.); II.109, that one spouse may not take a monastic vow unless the other does too (p. 146 n. 100); II.243–45, non-consummation as grounds for annulment, with proclamation of virginity, ordeal and divorce as the procedure (p. 196 nn. 331, 333). Korsvoll cites II.356 and II.444 on amulets under the alternative title *De ecclesiasticis disciplinis* (`witch_korsvoll_amulets.md`).

### 1c. Prüm the house (pp. 4–5, 13, 19–20, 25, 30–40)

- **Place.** "Located in a rural part of the Ardennes", close to the royal centres of Metz, Trier and Aachen (p. 4). **MacLean never uses the word "Eifel".**
- **Founded by a woman.** The noblewoman Bertrada founded it in 721. Her granddaughter, also Bertrada, refounded it in 752 with her husband Pippin, the first Carolingian king, who soon gave it a large grant of land (p. 4). Regino did not mention the founder even where his source invited it (p. 13 n. 50).
- **A royal house, but not a family one.** No abbot was a Carolingian or a lay supporter. The monks came from the region's leading families, and several became bishops (p. 5). It had royal protection and immunity (p. 4), confirmed again by Charles the Straightforward in 919 (p. 31).
- **Size, as of 893.** About 1,700 estates with an estimated 16,000–20,000 people, mainly in Lotharingia, not counting large holdings around Brittany. Perhaps 100 monks and dependants at the centre (p. 5).
- **Royal guests and prisoners.** Pippin the Hunchback was imprisoned there in the 790s; the young Charles the Bald in the 830s, who later called himself an "alumnus"; Emperor Lothar I became a monk there and died on 29 September 855; Hugh, son of Lothar II, was tonsured and buried there after 895 (pp. 4–5, 32; p. 135). Stories of kings who became monks were part of the house's sense of itself (p. 32).
- **Cults.** The abbey was dedicated to the Saviour, and Epiphany was "a significant day in the religious identity of Prüm" (p. 185 n. 281). Relics came from Rome in 844 under Abbot Marcward (p. 25), notably Chrysanthus and Daria (p. 76 n. 140). The cult of the cross was "intensively venerated" there (p. 78 n.). Also culted: Paulinus of Trier (p. 79 n. 156), Martin "since its origins" (p. 81 n. 171), Lupus, Albinus and Venantius (pp. 84, 91–92 nn.); relics from the Pays de Retz in the later 9th century; a vernacular poem on St George composed at Prüm in the 880s (p. 36).
- **Dependencies and estates named in the text.** St-Goar on the Rhine south-east of Koblenz, "a dependency of Prüm" (p. 224 n. 465); Villance, one of the most important estates (p. 39); Beltheim on the Rhine between Bacharach and Koblenz (p. 34; p. 219 n.); Gewelesdorf near the Dyle (p. 34); four manses at Duisburg (p. 37).
- **"The stories of the elders."** After 818 Regino's main source was the oral memory of the Prüm *seniores* (pp. 30–32). MacLean stresses that such stories made "the same claim to truth" as written ones (p. 32).
- **Abbots in the text.** Tancrad (d. 829), Marcward (829–), Eigil (resigned 860), Ansbald (860–886), Farabert (886–892), Regino (892–899), Richar (899–; later bishop of Liège 922–45) (pp. 130, 133, 136, 194, 213, 224; p. 248 n. 89).
- **The lord of Prüm in 1066: unknown from this source.** MacLean ends with Adalbert's continuation in 967 and names no later abbot or lord. Read the 1066 holder from vanilla history before building.

### 1d. The Eifel–Moselle country in Regino's memory (place names for loc)

- **Trier.** "The most noble city of the Gauls" (p. 186). Its houses: St-Martin, Regino's last charge (p. 6); St-Maximin, with lay abbots such as Megingoz (murdered 892) and Zwentibald, reformed in 934, Adalbert's own house (pp. 48, 55; p. 214 n. 420; p. 215 n. 423; p. 248 n. 89); Oeren, a monastery of St Mary (p. 221 n.). Archbishops: Theutgaud (deposed in the divorce case), Bertulf (870–83), Ratbod (883–915), then Roger, Robert, Henry and Theoderic in Adalbert (pp. 139, 160, 188; index).
- **Moselle and Rhine.** Remich on the Moselle (battle of 10 April 882, p. 186 n. 286); Echternach, St Willibrord's monastery, with lay abbots (p. 164; p. 186 n. 285); Koblenz, Andernach and Sinzig (pp. 176, 192); Bonn, Zülpich, Jülich and Neuss (p. 185); Malmedy and Stavelot (p. 185). The Bidgau, the county "around Trier", and the Moselgau (p. 188 n. 295; p. 186 n. 285).
- **The hill refuges.** In 892 the Vikings, after Prüm, took a stronghold "recently built on a certain prominent mountain" in the Ardennes, where commoners had fled (p. 212). MacLean notes the period's new refuge-fortresses (p. 212 n. 408).

### 1e. The Viking sacks of Prüm (882, 892) (pp. 185–86, 212)

Refer to these; do not show them.
- **6 January 882, Epiphany.** The Northmen came through the Ardennes into Prüm on the feast itself and stayed three days. A crowd of untrained commoners who came out against them was cut down. The fire left in the buildings then burned the monastery, "since there was nobody to put it out" (p. 185). MacLean: the date "shows the forward planning and local knowledge" of the raiders, given the abbey's Epiphany identity (p. 185 n. 281). He also calls the commoners' rising "rare evidence for the self-mobilisation of the non-noble classes", the sort of horizontal association Carolingian capitularies condemned (p. 185 n. 282).
- **5 April 882, Maundy Thursday.** They took Trier, rested there over Easter and burned it (p. 186).
- **892.** They returned to Prüm. The abbot and monks barely escaped; some monks were killed, most of the servants killed or carried off (p. 212). Regino is the only source (p. 212 n. 409).
- **Why it matters.** The *Urbar* of 893 was commissioned after these attacks (p. 5). At 1066 these are the abbey's oldest disasters, nearly two centuries back: a memory, not a present threat.

### 1f. Women, marriage, accusation and ordeal (pp. 27, 43–44, 131, 135–60, 189, 196, 225)

- **Regino's queens are moral exempla.** He blames Louis the Pious's deposition (833–34) "principally" on "the various fornications of his wife Judith" (p. 131). MacLean reads this as a 9th-century critique of queens' morality that culminated in Lothar II's divorce (p. 131 n. 34). He plans to discuss Regino's view of queenship elsewhere (p. 27 n. 102).
- **Lothar II, Theutberga and Waldrada (855–869).** MacLean's summary (p. 43): Lothar put aside Waldrada, with whom he had a son (Hugh) and two daughters, married Theutberga in 855, and in 860 divorced her at a synod at Aachen "using against her as justification a litany of sexual accusations including incest, sodomy, conception through witchcraft, and abortion" (p. 43).
  - **Regino's own text names only incest.** He has her "confessed" to incest with her brother at a council (he says Metz), separated and given penance (p. 140). MacLean corrects him: the "confession" to sodomy, incest and abortion was extracted at two synods at Aachen in January and February 860 (p. 140 n. 77). **The witchcraft charge is in MacLean's summary, not in Regino's chronicle.**
  - Waldrada was "concubine" to Regino (p. 139). MacLean adds that the common-law *Friedelehe* often claimed for her "is largely an invention of modern historians" (p. 139 n. 74).
  - **Ordeal.** Pope Nicholas I's letter, which Regino copies, reports that Lothar planned to charge Theutberga with adultery and settle it by a duel between champions, with her death if her man fell (p. 146). The pope rejects it: a matter "well judged once and settled by the taking of oaths should not be brought up again" (p. 146). A passage Regino omits "contests the validity of the ordeal by battle" (p. 147 n. 103).
  - Theutberga wrote that she would gladly give up her rank and marriage for a life of solitude; the pope refused unless Lothar did the same (p. 146; canon II.109).
  - Waldrada was excommunicated on 2 February 866, the Purification of the Virgin (p. 147 and n. 105).
  - Lothar's last communion with Pope Hadrian in 869 is told as "a kind of ordeal", which he fails (p. 160 n. 162; p. 43).
  - For Regino the sin brought a curse on all of Lotharingia, fulfilled in Hugh's rising of 883 (pp. 43–44; p. 189). He also saw "the sterility of their wives" as the Carolingians' doom (p. 44).
- **Engiltrude**, daughter of Count Matfrid, who had left her husband Count Boso, swears her submission before the papal legate (pp. 143–45). The Matfridings who later ousted Regino were her kin (p. 213 n. 411).
- **Richgard, 887.** Charles the Fat dismissed his adviser Liutward on a charge of adultery with the queen. Then before an assembly he said he had never lain with Richgard in more than ten years of marriage. She declared herself a virgin, untouched "by all male union", and offered to prove it by trial by combat or hot ploughshares. After the divorce she retired to her own foundation, Andlau in Alsace (p. 196 and n. 333). MacLean: proclamation of virginity, ordeal, divorce follows Regino's own canon II.244 (p. 196 n. 333).
- **Friderada and her daughter, 883.** Hugh had Bernarius murdered to marry his wife Friderada; her daughter by an earlier marriage was later beheaded by her husband Count Richwin "for committing a disgrace" (p. 189). MacLean ties the cast to Prüm and Trier (pp. 37–38). Refer only; never show.
- **"With women and the lesser-born."** Zwentibald was hated because he ran the realm "with women and the lesser-born", stripping the nobles of office (p. 225). A hostile magnate trope.
- **Women warriors as alien.** In the Hungarian digression Regino copies Justin's Scythian Amazons and edits Justin so that the Hungarians "attribute the same fierceness to women as to men" (pp. 203, 206 and n. 382).

### 1g. Magic, poison and spells (p. 178)

- **The only magic in the chronicle is a rumour against a Jewish physician.** Under 877 Regino reports that Charles the Bald was said to have been given poison by "a certain Jew called Zedechias", his trusted doctor, a "trickster" who deceived men "with magic tricks and spells" (p. 178). MacLean notes that the Annals of Fulda say Charles died of dysentery, and that royal deaths "frequently gave rise to accusations of witchcraft and poison, often against the kings' wives and others close to them" (p. 178 n. 243, after Reuter 2006a). **This passage is out of the mod entirely** (Jewish characters are never the wrongdoers).
- **No sorcery, *maleficium*, Diana, Herodias, night riders or penitential questions** appear in the chronicle or its notes. The Lothar divorce dossier's witchcraft charge appears only in MacLean's introduction (p. 43).

### 1h. Gender

- **No cross-dressing, effeminacy or gender crossing** is reported in the chronicle.
- **One eunuch.** Under 538–545, from Paul the Deacon: Empress Sophia sent word that because Narses was a eunuch she would set him to dividing wool for the girls in the women's quarters; he answered with a threat and called the Lombards into Italy (p. 89). Mockery of a eunuch by feminising him. Out (see §3 row 12).
- **Monks and kings.** Several entries turn on rulers who became monks and were "tested" by the Rule (746, 855, 868, 870, 885; pp. 32–33, 45). Carloman is received among the novices "fulfilling the apostolic saying: 'Test the spirits to see if they are from God'" (p. 123).

### 1i. Adalbert (pp. 53–58)

Adalbert of St-Maximin, later first archbishop of Magdeburg (968–81), wrote the continuation at Wissembourg in 967/68 (p. 55). He added stories of the Trier saints to Regino's Book I (pp. 13, 55–56). His Trier hits are church dedications, archbishops and St-Maximin's reform (index; pp. 241, 246). Nothing on women's beliefs.

## 4. Quotable lines for loc (in-world; never attributed to the scholar)

Regino's words, in MacLean's English, are marked as quotes. The rest are adapted.
