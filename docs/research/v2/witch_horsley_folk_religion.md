# Witches for v2: Horsley, "Further Reflections on Witchcraft and European Folk Religion" (1979)

**Source.** Richard A. Horsley, "Further Reflections on Witchcraft and European Folk Religion," *History of Religions* 19, no. 1 (1979): 71–95. Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo. Page numbers are the journal's printed ones.

Pronouns: Horsley is *he* here. The people named from trial records are given *she* or *he* as the records do.

Quote rule: every verbatim quote is 25 words or fewer and carries a page. Everything else is paraphrase.

**What was read.** The whole article, pp. 71–95, with all its notes (1–89).

**Relation to existing notes.** This is the seventh reading for the Good Ladies (`witch_design.md`, now planned for the 1066 start at Prüm: `../../TODO.md`, "Revised (user, 2026-10-26)"). It overlaps with `witch_papers_and_timeline.md` §3–§4 (Canon *Episcopi*, John of Salisbury, Milan, the benandanti) and with `witch_hutton_lecouteux.md` §1–§3 (Holda, Percht, the Ladies' company, the dead). Hutton 2014 is not redone here; it is used as the check on Horsley. The article adds one thing those notes lack: what the village *did* with healers, finders and suspected witches before the courts came in.

**Usefulness: moderate, mostly for event shapes.** Almost all of Horsley's evidence is from 1450 to 1710. Only one case falls inside the game (Lucerne, 1450). Nothing is from the Rhineland before 1300.

## 0. Framing warning

**Update (2026-10-27).** Horsley's surviving-goddess reading of Holda and Perchta is rejected by the leading recent study: Hutton finds no ancient goddess behind the Lady and treats her as a creation of the Christian centuries (*Queens of the Wild*, 2022, pp. 136–38, 196; `witch_hutton_queens_of_the_wild.md`).

- **A 1979 argument against two targets.** Horsley argues against Murray (there was no witch cult, p. 71) and against Keith Thomas (popular "magic" was a coherent religion, not debris, pp. 74–76). Both points stand. His positive picture is a **functionalist** one: witch belief kept the village together, and the hunts broke that (pp. 91–95). It is an argument, not a finding.
- **Holda and Perhta come from Grimm.** His account of Perhta/Holda as a surviving "fertility goddess" (pp. 87–88) rests on Grimm (1835/1875), Waschnitius (1913) and the *Handwörterbuch des deutschen Aberglaubens* (n. 66). That is the 19th-century method Hutton rejects (Hutton pp. 162–63, 175, in `witch_hutton_lecouteux.md` §1). **Do not build doctrine on pp. 87–88.**
- **His categories come from anthropology.** The witchcraft/sorcery split is Evans-Pritchard's, from the Azande (p. 77, n. 26). The Andalusian *sabias* are 20th-century fieldwork (Pitt-Rivers, p. 78). Use them as lenses, not as medieval evidence.
- **"Many of the victims … were the wise women"** (p. 77) is Horsley's own claim, argued in a companion article (n. 28). Later scholarship disputes it (not checked here). Treat it as his view.
- **Secondhand throughout.** The trial material comes from printed collections: Hoffmann-Krayer (Lucerne), Byloff (Austria), Heberling (Schleswig-Holstein), Delcambre (Lorraine), Macfarlane and Thomas (England). Milan and Sicily come through Bonomo, Abundia through Cohn, and the Canon and John of Salisbury through Kors and Peters 1972 (pp. 88–89, nn. 67–72).

## 1. Findings

### 1a. Learned witchcraft versus what villagers believed (pp. 71–77)
- **No cult.** "There was no such thing as a witch cult—except in the anxious minds of the witch-hunters and demonologists" (p. 71). There is also no solid popular evidence for sabbat or coven meetings (p. 73).
- **The learned theory is late.** Night flight, the sabbat, the orgy and the devil's pact form a composite that emerged on the continent only toward the end of the 15th century (pp. 72–73, after Cohn). Horsley dates it later than the existing notes (which put the shift at c. 1430). His 15th-century "end" is the full composite, not its first appearance.
- **Superior and inferior documents.** Statements made before inquisitors or demonologists intervened show "little or no evidence" of Satan, the sabbat or a God–Satan dualism. Confessions under torture are the "inferior" documents (p. 73, after Kieckhefer).
- **Witchcraft is not sorcery.** Witchcraft works by an inherent power in the witch; sorcery by a learned technique (pp. 76–77). Villagers accused witches of harm by inherent power: being "bewitched" or "forespoken". The sorcerers in the record are mostly the *accusers*, working counter-magic (pp. 77, 82).

### 1b. Wise women and cunning folk (pp. 78–82)
- **What they did.** They divined, found lost objects, named thieves, healed with folk medicine and charms, worked love and protective magic, and were often midwives (pp. 78–79).
- **How many.** In 16th-century England, they were reckoned at least as numerous as parish clergy (p. 78, after Thomas). Secondhand, and post-game.
- **Their own confidence.** A Holstein wise woman (1617) held that she worked by the true word of God, "only bringing evil back around again to a good end" (paraphrased by Horsley, p. 80).
- **They named witches.** Peasants who felt bewitched went to a diviner, who narrowed the suspects, often years before any trial (pp. 80–81). Lucerne has a "witch-finder" in 1480. In 1500 one Hans Tscholi claimed hereditary contact with the dead (p. 81).
- **Healer and witch were different people.** Wise women accused of witchcraft were almost never accused of sorcery (p. 82). The only harms Horsley finds by wise women are two Austrian self-defence cases against a lord's men (p. 82, n. 46).

### 1c. Counter-magic and Christian things put to village use (pp. 82–84)
- **Remedies.** For cows whose milk had turned to blood: consecrated salt or the ashes of consecrated palms to lick. Such remedies came from a wise woman, e.g. Margaret Jegerin, Lucerne, 1450 (p. 83). **This is the one in-game-date case.**
- **The Bophart case (Lucerne, undated in the text; Hoffmann-Krayer no. 24).** A man caught stealing a woman's cherries believed she had bewitched his milk. He hired a sorcerer, and his counter-magic made her ill. Then both men suffered misfortunes. Their trouble ended only when they got back into her good graces and took her advice to drop counter-magic (p. 83).
- **Sacramentals, re-used.** Consecrated candles, palms, salt and holy water were used for land, beasts and bodies (pp. 83–84). The people were translating Christian elements "into a symbolism more appropriate to their own life experience" (p. 84, quoting E. P. Thompson). One Austrian example is host-theft. **It is not usable** (see §3 row 8).

### 1d. Spirits, weather and the Lady (pp. 84–89)
- **Familiars are mostly a clerk's filter** (p. 85). The Lucerne cases that mention one are atypical, and in one the spirit appears only in a second "confession" (p. 85). Margret Jeger (1450) names spirits; one is a storm-spirit to be conjured *and* answered by ringing the village weather bell, "especially the latter" (p. 86). **Rule 25 bars familiars; nothing here is used except the weather bell.**
- **Weather without malice.** Lucerne neighbours saw a mother and daughter splash water and rain followed, but alleged no harm. Horsley reads it as a fertility rite (p. 87). An Austrian man (1658) learned a rain-making rite from the peasants (pp. 87–88). Post-1453.
- **The Lady.** Horsley's Perhta/Holda (alias Diana or Herodias) is a motherly figure of fruitfulness. He makes her active after the winter solstice, a guardian of spinning, weaving and childbirth, followed by the dead and unbaptized infants, and sometimes leading the "furious army" (pp. 87–88). **This is the Grimm composite** (see §0 and §2).
- **The company in spirit.** Cohn's French evidence: women went out "in the spirit" with the good ladies through chinks into houses, and partook of food left out without diminishing it (p. 88). The Canon and John of Salisbury show such beliefs "still thriving in the twelfth century" (p. 88).
- **Milan, 1384 and 1390** (via Bonomo, n. 72). Two women were tried for following "Signora Oriente". The society included living and dead people. It ate animals the Lady revived, and visited homes. The Lady taught herbal cures and "the divining of sorcery and theft" (p. 89).
- **Horsley's conclusion on the company.** Its women were joining, "at least in spirit, the souls of the dead in beneficent endeavors to promote fertility, health, and social welfare" (p. 89). The bond with the dead is his (and Grimm's); the *beneficent* is well supported.
- **The benandanti (Friuli, 1575 on; post-1453).** They fought witches at night while their bodies lay in bed, and "never suggested that they were bodily" (p. 90). Inquisitors took three decades to convince them they were themselves the witches (p. 90).

### 1e. How the hunts turned village belief around (pp. 90–95)
- **Suppression of healers.** In the 16th century, officials prosecuted cunning folk for divining and healing. Lorraine targeted healing, England divining (p. 90). The Inquisition punished beneficent as well as harmful magic "since the early fifteenth century", while many secular courts still told them apart (pp. 90–91). Midwifery was brought under official control at the same time (p. 91).
- **Before the hunters came.** A villager who suffered after slighting a poor old woman might consult a diviner, but would not accuse. Instead he took care not to offend her. "Witch suspicions actually protected the indigent and eccentric and promoted solidarity" (p. 92). Lucerne deponents recall incidents from six to ten years earlier: they had lived alongside the suspect (p. 92).
- **Procedure made the change.** Under the old accusatory procedure, an accuser who failed to convince the judge might be prosecuted himself (p. 92, after Cohn pp. 160–62). Witchcraft was by nature "unprovable". The inquisitorial procedure put an official in charge of finding evidence, by torture if needed, and that stimulated accusations (p. 92).
- **Officials chose the charges.** Where officials wanted storm-raising accusations, they got them; where they did not care, few came (p. 93, after Monter's own data).
- **"Who can cure can cause" was imposed from above.** Lorraine healers admitted being witch-doctors "but not witches—concepts which to them were mutually exclusive", until torture changed their answers (p. 93; Delcambre via Monter, **thirdhand**).
- **Charity.** Because people feared being forespoken, they gave bread or milk to a begging widow (p. 94). The trials removed that protection (pp. 94–95).
