# Witches for v2: Stratton and Kalleres (eds.), *Daughters of Hecate* (2014)

Scope: triage reading for the Good Ladies (`witch_design.md` §1–3, §10), the Adelphai Dorleyaiou and Ha-Nehpakhot. It is meant to sit beside `stratton_naming_the_witch.md` (Stratton 2007) and does not repeat it. Pronouns: every modern scholar named here is *they*. Quotes have 25 words or fewer and printed page numbers. Text came from the PDF's text layer; no book text is kept in the repo.

## 1. Bibliographic line and verdict

Kimberly B. Stratton, ed., with Dayna S. Kalleres, *Daughters of Hecate: Women and Magic in the Ancient World*. New York: Oxford University Press, 2014. c. 540 pp. ISBN 978-0-19-020214-9 (the ISBN in the file name). Fifteen chapters, with a bibliography and indexes.

**Verdict: worth a triage, not a close read.** It is still antiquity, ending c. 600 CE. Its value for the mod is in four places:
1. **Kalleres (ch. 7)** gives the **Greek Orthodox hostile register**: Chrysostom's sermons, which Byzantium read throughout the game's period, tie gender-crossing stage performers to *porneia* and sorcery.
2. **Tuzlak (ch. 8)** gives the full Firmilian letter. Stratton 2007 covered it in one paragraph.
3. **Byzantine leads in the notes and asides**: the Suda's definitions of magic, the canons of Laodicea and Basil, Syncellus's Enoch, the *Life of St Andrew the Fool* and the Greek *Apocalypse of Mary*.
4. **Copeland (ch. 10)** gives a date marker for how Latin hostility to the Ladies became gendered: late antique visions of hell punish "men and women" alike, while 13th–14th-century ones single out women.

For **Ha-Nehpakhot the book adds nothing usable**. It adds three more do-not-use items (§4c). There is **no material on the gallae**; the one gallus, at Pessinus, is Attis-cult and stays out. There is also nothing on medieval night-wandering beyond one secondary summary.

## 3. New beyond Stratton 2007

- **Medieval gender ratio.** Stratton reports, after Klaits, that "In the Middle Ages, also, accusations of magic did not target women in any special way" (p. 17). Women were only a slight majority before c. 1400. This strengthens the Ladies' "men attested" row.
- **The accuser profits.** Via Tuzlak, Frankfurter ties hostile magic to "the charisma of the one who identifies the problem, articulates its scope and nature, and provides effective remedies" (p. 264). This is a clean frame for the Church overlay: the cleric who names the danger also sells the cure (Caesarius's anointing, Chrysostom's sign of the cross).
- **Real accusations were rare and stayed at the level of rumour.** "'Real' witches, that is, were very rare in the world of Imperial Greece and Rome" (Graf, p. 406). Graf's suspects are outsiders and people of ambiguous status: freed slaves, immigrants (pp. 404–05).
- **The domestic battleground.** Kalleres (pp. 220–22, 233–35) reads the patristic "drunken old woman" as the Church's bid to take over the household's rituals of healing. This is the late antique ancestor of the Ladies' clean house and the priest's suspicion of it.
- **The full Firmilian file** (§4b) and **a Byzantine vocabulary of magic** (Suda, George the Monk; §4b).
- Stratton 2007 does not have Copeland's **"men and women" formula** or its medieval loss (§4a).

**Stays out:**
- *strix*, *gello*, *lilith*: "eats children and drains men's potency" (p. 323); also *lamia*, the child-devourer (p. 41).
- Chrysostom's magic-working *pornai*, and the *lena*/drunken-hag package (Ovid's Dipsas, Propertius's Acanthis, pp. 227–28).
- Cohn's later "night journey → sabbath" chain (p. 18), as anything but background.

### (b) Adelphai Dorleyaiou

**Hostile Orthodox voice** (a named speaker; rule 3; the demon register, not the Latin illusion register):
1. **Chrysostom, *Hom. in Matt.* 37 (the theatre).** A young man "changing his nature into that of a wom[a]n, is striving both in aspect, and in gesture, and in garments" (p. 242). Then the *goētes* behind the stage "proceed so far in sorcery (sagganeias) as not even to shrink from disturbing the bones of the dead" (p. 243). It is the strongest patristic text tying gender-crossing to *porneia* and sorcery, and every Byzantine preacher had it. **Conflict:** it attacks gesture and dress, while rule 3 aims hostile lines at the imagined body. If used, a monk paraphrases the "changing his nature" half and the narrator answers it. Do not use the dress half. Open question 1.
2. **"Even the demons uttered the name of God."** Chrysostom's answer to the defence that the healer is a Christian: "she makes use of the name of God, with a view to ribaldry. For even the demons uttered the name of God" (p. 232). This is the template for a bishop dismissing the Sisters' orthodox prayers. Shape: *exposing*.
3. **Canons.** Laodicea (Phrygia, late 4th c.): "They who are of the priesthood, or of the clergy, shall not be magicians, enchanters, mathematicians, or astrologers" (p. 428), with amulet-wearers cast out. Basil, *Ep.* 188.8: a death caused by love-philters counts as intentional homicide (p. 250 n. 71). Both were in the Byzantine canon collections. Laodicea is in the Sisters' own region. Proposal: a metropolitan cites Laodicea against a Sister's blessing-cords. Verify the canon number (36) before loc.
4. **Suda (10th c.), from George the Monk (9th c.)**: *goēteia*, *mageia* and *pharmakeia* are distinguished. "'Wizardry' is used for the calling up of a corpse through invocations" (p. 392; source note p. 410 n. 31). This partly answers Stratton-2007 open question 5. To a learned Byzantine, *goēteia* means **necromancy**, not mere fraud. So *goēs* fits a "grave-rites" slander, not the *exposing* (deceiver) shape. Recommend: no *goēs* for the deceiver line until a heresiological use is found.
5. **Syncellus (c. 810)**: Byzantium's own copy of the Watchers story blames women, saying "they have taught them to make hate-charms" (p. 123), alongside Asael's cosmetics (p. 121). A monk could turn "Asael's paints" on the daughters' adornment. This is an adornment attack, like item 1. Open question 1.

**The Sisters' own voice:**
1. **Firmilian's prophetess, in full** (Tuzlak, pp. 252–67).
   - **The setting:** Cappadocia c. 235, after earthquakes and a persecution.
   - **What she did:** she baptized "many" using "the customary and legitimate wording". She consecrated the Eucharist so that "she appeared to deviate in no particular from ecclesiastical discipline" (p. 254). She walked "in the very depths of winter through the bitter snow in bare feet" (p. 253). She said she must go to Judaea and Jerusalem.
   - **The Sisters' line:** she was condemned for the hand, not the rite. Firmilian and Cyprian took her exclusion as axiomatic (p. 255). Tuzlak: dissenters "often did so in good faith and for good reasons" (p. 263).
   - **Use:** as an example only. She is unnamed; never a foremother (rule 19).
   - **Out:** the seduction of the deacon and the rustic presbyter (p. 253). It is a sexual slander.
   - **Montanist:** probably, but unproven (p. 260). Do not assert it.
2. **Cyprian's Korah, Dathan and Abiram** (p. 261): the orthodox proof-text that correct rites by the wrong hand anger God. It is a hostile line the Sisters can quote and answer.
3. **Clergy made the amulets** (Luijendijk, pp. 426–29): the Oxyrhynchus amulet has *nomina sacra*, liturgical phrasing and local saints. Laodicea and Shenoute's "great monk" (p. 428) show clergy did this. In the Sisters' voice, the bishops' "old women" may be their own priests (p. 429). This supports `doctrine_clerical_function_alms_and_pacification` without inventing practice.
4. **Byzantine flavour, outside the daughters' story:** in the *Life of St Andrew the Fool* (10th c.), a wife offered death, possession or impotence for her husband says "I want nothing else . . . but that he loves and cherishes me" (p. 338 n. 49). Possible event colour in Constantinople. Verify in Rydén's edition first.

**Stays out:**
- The gallus Asclepius of Pessinus (Graf, p. 393; p. 410 n. 38). Attis cult; the rule bars it.
- Sosipatra (pagan; "no golden past").
- *Apoc. Pet.*'s punishments for same-sex acts (p. 305).
- Chrysostom equating women's gospel amulets with Jewish phylacteries (p. 418). It is anti-Jewish, so no voice uses it.

### (d) Other faiths
- **Bædling.** Iceland's early modern trials were mostly male (120 to 10 accused), because *galdur* was men's learned word-craft (Hastrup, p. 19). It is early modern and comparison only, but it is a reminder that Norse sorcery was not female-coded by default. Do not import it into the 12th-c. voice without a medieval source.
- **Worn Garland, Badenya, Yutai Dao.** Nothing new.

## 5. Open questions

1. **Hostile lines that aim at adornment** (Chrysostom's "aspect, gesture, garments"; Syncellus's Asael cosmetics). Rule 3 says aim at the imagined body. Allow one paraphrased Chrysostom line, body-half only, in a named monk's mouth? Default: no.
2. **Dated gendering of Latin hostility**: "men and women" before c. 1200, women-only after (Copeland). Adopt as a loc rule for the Ladies?
3. **Dante's distaff line** for a Lombard voice after c. 1320: approve? (It is about soothsayers, not the Ladies; the cleric would be conflating them.)
4. **Laodicea canon against Sisters' blessing-cords**: verify the canon number and the Byzantine nomocanon text, then approve?
5. ***Goēs***: given the Suda's necromantic sense, drop *goēs* as the "deceiver" word and look for the heresiologists' term instead?
6. **Firmilian's prophetess**: Stratton-2007 open question 8 stands. Add the barefoot-in-snow and Jerusalem details?
7. **Andrew the Fool vignette**: worth an event? Verify the text first.
8. **New Ha-N guardrail** (no table/childbirth-custom link to the Ladies): adopt into `witch_design.md` §10 and §13?
