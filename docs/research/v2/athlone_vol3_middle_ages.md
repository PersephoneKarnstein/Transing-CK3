## Verdict

A good survey, and useful mostly as a **cross-check**. It adds little that is new about the Ladies themselves: Peters never names Abundia, the *bonae dominae*, the Milan trials or *Super illius specula*, and treats the night ride in one paragraph (p. 189) and the Canon *Episcopi* in two pages (pp. 203–04). What it adds:

1. **For the Good Ladies:** a firmer legal timeline for `witch_design.md` §5 (Carolingian capitularies, Gratian, the 1258/60 rule keeping inquisitors out of sorcery, Lateran IV, Kyteler, the 1398 Paris condemnation, the 1430–40 turn and its exact geography), and a few pre-1453 texts the v2 notes do not have. The best is **Burchard's table set for the three Fates** (c. 1010), the earliest laid-table-for-female-spirits item in any of our notes (Jolly p. 18).
2. **For Bædling:** Raudvere is the most useful part. They push back on three things the faith page leans on: the völva's **trance**, the weight put on Snorri's ***ergi*** sentence, and a **women's** seiðr. These are good correctives for the narrator, not reasons to change the faith's own voice.
3. **For the English content:** Jolly's chapter on charms is a sound guide to the *Lacnunga*, the *Leechbook*, the *Æcerbot* and the journey charm. It reads them as Christian practice, not pagan survival, which is what the mod needs to avoid a Murray-style "Old Religion" in England.

Grade of the book as evidence: secondary synthesis (2002) by three specialists. Primary claims below should be checked in the editions the authors cite before they go into loc.

## 1. Part 1: Jolly, "Medieval Magic" (pp. 1–71)

**Thesis.** "Magic is more a concept than a reality" (p. 3). The label tells us about the people using it more than about practice (p. 4). Jolly rejects the magic→religion→science model and the "pagan survival under a Christian veneer" reading (pp. 8–12, 16).

**Three shifts** (p. 13):
- **Conversion, c. 300–1100.** The cosmology changes; daily healing and protective practice continues, now Christianized (pp. 14–20). Christians shared with local people a belief in the power of words, in invisible beings, and in hidden virtues in things (p. 16).
- **The twelfth-century renaissance.** Learned men split "high" natural magic from "low" common practice. The Church's link shifts from magic-as-paganism to **magic-as-heresy** (pp. 20–23).
- **After c. 1350.** Magic becomes "a coherent and organized demonic cult" in the minds of prosecutors (p. 13). A priests' manual of 1385 still calls charms worthless; Bernardino of Siena in 1427 calls for "to the fire" (p. 24). "A corner has been turned here, somewhere around 1400: the illusory nature of demonic magic is no longer a possible defence" (p. 25).

**Burchard of Worms** (c. 1008–12; Jolly pp. 17–18, 33, 48, 56, 59, 60). Items not in our notes, all paraphrase of Jolly's summary of McNeill and Gamer:
- belief in "the three Fates, sisters for whom women set extra places at the table" (p. 18);
- child-sized bows, arrows and shoes left in the barn for satyrs and goblins, so they will increase the household's store by stealing from the neighbours (p. 18);
- women weaving with incantations and spinning "magic skeins" on 1 January (p. 59);
- a rain ritual: a stripped girl digs henbane with her little finger, drags it in a procession of girls with twigs and water, and is led back walking backwards (p. 48);
- belief that women "dedicated to Satan" leave the body, kill, cook and eat people and leave "straw or wood" for their hearts. Burchard condemns the **belief**, not the women (p. 18);
- belief that women ride by night "with the Roman Diana or the Germanic witch Hulda" (p. 18). "Witch" is Jolly's gloss, not Burchard's word.

**Healing, protection, divination** (ch. 3):
- **Charms are mostly Christian.** The few charms with no Christian reference (the *Lay of the Nine Herbs*, the *Nine Twigs of Woden*, the Merseburg charms) sit in Christian manuscripts. "'Pagan' cannot just mean the absence of overt Christian references, but is relative to the manuscript context" (p. 36). OE *galdor* means "song" (p. 36).
- **Narrative charms** are "hagiographic reliquaries": Longinus's spear, Peter's toothache, the Jordan formula, the Three Good Brothers (pp. 37–38). Reduction charms count down from nine (p. 39).
- **Women's practice is mostly invisible** in the male clerical texts. Childbirth charms are one of the three commonest kinds, and raise questions about female practitioners (pp. 31, 47).
- **Sibyls.** Pre-Christian Germanic and Scandinavian divination "was dominated by female seers or sibyls"; men came later, mostly in sorcery and rune-magic (p. 58, after Jochens 1993). Raudvere disagrees (§2 below).
- **Spirit travel with the body at home.** Caesarius of Heisterbach (*Dialogus* V.37, c. 1220s) tells of a sick knight carried by a demon to Rome "in spirit, since his body remained at home, pale and bloodless"; he comes back cured and remembers the journey vividly (p. 66). Another tale has a former necromancer ride a demon to hell and come back pale (p. 63).
- **A bodily flight escape.** At Reims c. 1176–80 a woman condemned as a heretic throws a ball of thread out of the window, says "catch", and is said to fly away with the spirits who helped Simon Magus (p. 52). This is an accusers' story.
- **Guibert of Nogent**'s father, made impotent by a stepmother's magic, was cured by "a certain old woman" (p. 61).
- **Sorcery for theft.** Cistercians who hired a sorcerer and buried a black cat at a crossroads were burned with the cat in 1323 (p. 59).
- **"Magic" outside Europe.** Marco Polo saw "magic" wherever he saw idolatry. Jolly concludes that "the application of the term 'magic' to other cultures and beliefs outside of Europe should be avoided" (p. 71).

## 2. Part 2: Raudvere, "Trolldómr in Early Medieval Scandinavia" (pp. 73–171)

**Scope and method.** c. 800–1300, mainly Icelandic and Norwegian texts written in the 13th century (pp. 75–76). Raudvere uses the native terms (*trolldómr*, *seiðr*, *galdr*, *spá*) to avoid "witch" (p. 80). Sagas are social memory and feud narrative, not ethnography (pp. 76–77, 81, 89).

**Concepts** (ch. 1):
- **No sabbat in the North.** The witches' sabbath, "nocturnal gatherings, ritual murder of small children, orgies of perversion or cannibalism, are not found at all in Old Norse literature" (pp. 86–87).
- **Gender.** Both sexes practise; women appear more in *trolldómr* stories than elsewhere in Old Norse literature, but this is no separate women's culture (against Kress), and "Men were apparently equally involved" (p. 81). "Troll is a term in the neuter case and is not, as such, gender specific" (p. 88). Male terms: *galdramaðr*, *vitki*, *skratti*, *trollmaðr*; female: *gýgr*, *seiðkona*, *spákona*, *trollkona*, *vǫlva* (p. 89).
- **Speech.** *Galdr* is from *gala*, "to crow, chant"; spoken words were binding (pp. 90–92). Women were largely shut out of public speech and of trials, so their *trolldómr* is imagined as secret speech (p. 92).
- **Mothers' songs.** In *Grógaldr* a dead völva sings nine charms over her son (p. 93); foster-mothers in the sagas protect their sons with words.
- **Agents of fate are female**: *fylgjur* in women's shape, *dísir* (with a winter *dísablót*), norns; a völva could be called *spádís* (pp. 98–101).
- **The soul leaves the body.** A strong *hugr* could act far away in a *hamr* while the body "lay as if sleeping" (p. 102). "No transformation with a complete disappearance of the ordinary body is told of; some part of the body is always left behind" (p. 104).
- **Night-riders.** *Túnriður*, *myrkriður*, *kveldriður* and the *mara* are women in a temporary shape; Óðinn boasts of trapping and seducing them (*Hávamál* 155, *Hárbarðsljóð* 20), with "sexual overtones" (pp. 104–05, 147–49).

**Seiðr** (ch. 2):
- *Seiðr* is a technique to know or bend what is coming, good or harmful by point of view (pp. 110–11). Divinatory seiðr is collective and semi-public; harmful *trolldómr* is solitary and secret (p. 87).
- **Origin.** Freyja taught it to the Æsir; Óðinn is its master (pp. 112–17).
- **Snorri's *ergi* sentence is over-read.** Snorri says seiðr brought *ergi* and was taught to the priestesses, but the next lines pass Óðinn's skills to the male *blótgoðar*. This "has not received half as much attention as the ergi part" (p. 118). Dillmann's count: "men and women appear equally often as practitioners of trolldomr" (p. 118). "There is no sexual activity or erotic symbolism expressed in the seidr narratives" (p. 119). Raudvere prefers Clover's moral sense of *argr* (cowardice) and Clover's view of a "sex-gender system rather different from our own" (p. 119).
- ***Eiríks saga* ch. 4.** Raudvere uses it "with some hesitation": "too many times this particular account of the ceremony has been read as an accurate anthropological description of a volva's performance" (p. 122). Points of detail:
  - Þorbjǫrg is the last of nine sisters; she walks the farms at the end of winter (p. 122);
  - the women form a ring round the *hjallr* while she sits on it (p. 125);
  - **no trance in the text**: "nothing is mentioned in the text about the volva's soul or any journey of the soul" (p. 125). Raudvere rejects Strömbäck's shamanic reading, and notes DuBois's case for Sámi or Balto-Finnic influence;
  - the völva calls Guðríðr "my daughter" (p. 126);
  - "it is the collaboration between the women that makes it a success" (p. 127).
- **Hospitality buys a better prophecy.** In *Víga-Glúms saga* the housewives must welcome the seeress well because what she says is influenced by her welcome, and a hostess threatens to drive her off for a bad forecast (pp. 128–29).
- **Male seiðr not called unmanly.** The Hebridean Kotkell family (husband, wife, sons) raise a *seiðhjallr* together and sing storms and death. "No action is commented on as unmanly or with any equivalent term, but rather as immoral and, above all, foreign" (p. 140). Ethnicity (Hebridean, Sámi) and old age are the usual markers of otherness (pp. 130, 133).
- **Other rituals:** the *Vǫlsa þáttr* farmwife lifted over a door-frame (pp. 131–33); Busla's *bœn*, a curse called a "prayer" (pp. 133–36); Þuríðr's runes on driftwood against Grettir (pp. 137–39); a völva's grave with a large *seiðstafr* (*Laxdæla* 76, p. 141); birth-runes and the *dísir* in childbirth (p. 145).

**Law and trial** (ch. 3):
- *Eyrbyggja saga* 16: Geirríðr is summoned as a ***kveldriða***. As a woman she cannot speak at the thing, so her brother speaks; a **jury of twelve** clears her by oath on the altar ring, and the accusers are dishonoured (pp. 155–56).
- Informal "trials" are revenge: Katla, Kotkell and Grima are stoned, Hallbjǫrn drowned, each with a skin bag over the head against the gaze (pp. 157–59).
- **The client is liable, the foster-mother is not.** Þuríðr is never charged; the man who hired her, Ǫngull, is outlawed, and *Grágás* makes paying someone to work *trolldómr* a crime. Raudvere quotes Miller: "The many foster mothers in the sagas who were adept at magic and lore seldom suffered sorcery accusations" (p. 160).
- Christian codes keep many saga ideas; sorcery was "evil but real", and the devil is the one new piece (pp. 161–62).

## 3. Part 3: Peters, "The Medieval Church and State" (pp. 173–245)

**Vocabulary** (p. 177). "Witchcraft" is often a poor translation before the late twelfth century; "witch" is better rendered "by the Latin terms of stria or striga". *Maleficium* first meant any crime; the Theodosian Code records "the common people" applying *malefici* to magicians (p. 181).

**Late antiquity** (ch. 1). Augustine folds pagan religion and all magic into *superstitio* and ties it to pacts with demons (*De doctrina* II.20–23; pp. 182–84). Isidore, *Etymologies* VIII.9, gives the master list of diviners (pp. 185–86). Priscillian (385) is the first heretic executed, on a charge of magic (p. 181).

**Germanic laws** (ch. 2):
- *Pactus legis Salicae* (c. 510) §64: calling a free woman a *stria* without proof costs three times the fine for calling a man a sorcerer (*strioportio*, "one who carries the brass cauldron"); and yet "if a witch eats a man" there is a fine (p. 190).
- **Rothair's Edict** (Lombard, 643), c. 376: no one may kill another's servant or slave as a *striga* or *masca*, "it is in no wise to be believed by Christian minds that it is possible that a woman can eat a living man from within" (p. 191). Cc. 197–98 protect a woman falsely called *striga* (p. 190–91).
- Liutprand (727) bans consulting sorcerers and worshipping trees and springs (p. 191). The Alamannic laws forbid private punishment of a woman accused of witchcraft (p. 192).
- Peters lists the night ride with "Diana-Herla, or Holda, or Herodias, the female leader of the 'wild ride'" among Germanic beliefs, "few and scattered until the late ninth century", and from the 13th century one element of the later sabbat (p. 189).

**Canon law and Carolingian legislation to 1100** (ch. 3):
- **Penitentials.** Theodore: seven years for a woman who puts her daughter on a roof or in an oven to cure fever; five for incantations and divination (p. 195). Silos (early 9th c.): one year for "Those who in the dance wear women's clothes and strangely devise them" (p. 196). Egbert (10th c.): one year for a woman's witchcraft, seven if it killed (p. 196).
- **Leptinnes** (c. 743): the list of superstitions condemns "the belief that women control the moon and the hearts of men" (p. 197).
- **Charlemagne.** *Admonitio generalis* (789) orders magicians to amend or die (p. 198). The Paderborn capitulary (785) puts to death anyone who "believes after the manner of pagans that some man or woman is a witch and eats people" and burns or eats her (p. 198). A council near Freising/Salzburg (c. 800) wants weather-makers jailed until they repent, "so that they do not lose their lives" (p. 198).
- **Council of Paris (829):** malefici "trouble the air", bring hail, and "steal the fruit and milk of some folk to bestow it on others" (pp. 199–200).
- **Political sorcery.** Judith and Bernard of Septimania are accused (830); Bernard's sister, the nun Gerberga, is drowned at Chalon in 834 "after the manner of sorcerers" (pp. 200–01). Two people executed for poisoning Arnulf (899); women executed for killing the count of Angoulême (1028) (p. 202).
- **Sceptics and believers.** Agobard mocks the storm-makers from Magonia; Hrabanus Maurus doubts; but Charles the Bald (Metz 859, Quierzy 873) says sorcerers really kill and must be hunted; Hincmar (c. 860) charges Waldrada with causing Theutberga's barrenness (p. 201).
- **Old English laws** (p. 202–03): Wihtred and Cnut call pagan practice devil-worship; Alfred applies Exodus 22:18 to women who receive wizards; Edward and Guthrum exile or fine witches and diviners. Terms: *scinnlāc/scinncræft* (magic), *galdorcræft* (enchantment), *lyblāc*, *lyblǣca* (m.), *lybbestre* (f.) (poisoning), and *wicca* (m.), *wicce* (f.), *wiccecræft* (divination, later "witchcraft").
- **Regino** (c. 906) gives the Canon *Episcopi* in two parts: sorcery is real and its practitioners are expelled from the diocese; the night ride with Diana is a phantasm (pp. 203–04). "At least part of the text probably came from a ninth-century capitulary that is now lost" (Russell 1972; p. 203–04). Rather of Verona echoes it late in the 10th century (p. 204). The whole later history is "a long set of changing interpretations of the canon Episcopi and the problem of diabolically caused flight and sacrilegious assembly" (p. 204).
- **Burchard**, *Decretum* X and XIX (1008–12), sums it up; XIX is nearly the last list of superstitions; after c. 1050 churchmen's attention turns to heresy (pp. 205–06).

**Twelfth and thirteenth centuries** (ch. 4):
- The Canon passes through Ivo to **Gratian** (c. 1140), C. 26 q. 5 c. 12, still credited to the Council of Ancyra; impotence by sorcery goes into C. 33 from Hincmar (*Si per sortiarias*) (pp. 207–10).
- The **gloss on Exodus 22:18**: "sorcerers are to be excommunicated and exiled, as are other heretics, not literally killed" (p. 209). Twelfth-century theologians allow nothing stronger (p. 209).
- Heretics, not witches, are now pictured as night-meeting, orgiastic anti-churches (Russell, p. 211).
- **Lateran IV (1215)** makes yearly confession compulsory; confessors' manuals (Thomas of Chobham c. 1215) stress demons in all magic (p. 212).
- **Alexander IV (1258, reissued 1260; *Liber Sextus* 1298):** inquisitors are to keep out of divination and sorcery "unless these clearly savour of manifest heresy" (p. 213).
- Castile's *Siete Partidas* (1260s–70s) put condemned diviners and necromancers to death (p. 214). Bernard Gui's manual (c. 1323) asks about superstition touching the sacraments (p. 214).
- The *Golden Legend* (c. 1260) spreads saint-against-sorcerer tales; Dante's diviners include poor women who left their families to tell fortunes (pp. 216–17).

**Political sorcery c. 1300** (ch. 5). French court trials (Guichard of Troyes 1308, Marigny), the Templars (1307), Boniface VIII accused of keeping a demon (pp. 219–20). John XXII's panic (Hugues Géraud 1317) and decretals of 1318, 1326 and 1331, "in which the sorcerers suspected were all clergy" (pp. 220–21). John XXII briefly took sorcery from the inquisitors; Gregory XI gave it back in 1374 (p. 222). In 1398 two Augustinians are executed in Paris and the theology faculty condemns sorcery in 28 articles (p. 222). English cases: Alice Perrers (1376), Joan of Navarre (1419), Eleanor Cobham (1441) (p. 221).

**Sorcerer and witch** (ch. 6):
- **Kyteler (Kilkenny, 1324)**, after Davidson and Ward: "the first witchcraft trial in European history to treat the accused as members of an organized sect of heretics", and the first to charge sorcery through sex with a demon; Petronilla of Meath is burned. The bishop brought the ideas from Avignon; the case "started no general hunt" (pp. 223–24).
- Famine, plague and war cannot be shown to cause the new idea (pp. 224–25). The Councils of Constance and Basel spread it (pp. 226–28).
- **Gerson** (pacts begin in the mind), John of Frankfurt (1412, against powers ascribed to infants born with a caul), Nicholas Jauer (1405), Bernardino (1427): what the reformers found was "not paganism, but lay Christianity" (Duffy; p. 229).
- **The new crime.** In the early 15th century, separate offences fuse into one: pact, homage, sex with the devil, flight to assemblies, mark, familiar. The Canon's line between fantasy and reality "disappears" (Paravy; p. 231). Alexander V speaks of a "new heresy" in 1409; Jean Vineti says so in 1450 (p. 232).
- **Where.** Lower Rhône eastward: Dauphiné, Vaud, Piedmont, Savoy, Aosta, western Switzerland; then Lombardy (Como), Tirol, Swabia, Bavaria, the Rhineland. "These were not remote mountain backwaters of residual folk paganism, as some historians once claimed" (p. 233).
- **When.** "The key years appear to be 1430-40" (p. 233). Savoy's statutes of 1430 show no concern yet (p. 233). The *Errores Gazariorum* (early 1430s) first names a sect or "synagogue"; Nider's *Formicarius* (1435–37) retells the Simmental cases of 1392–1406; Fründ (c. 1437); Claude Tholosan's treatise (Briançon, 1437) is the work of a lay judge who had burned people (pp. 233–35). Eugenius IV accuses Savoy under the antipope Felix V of harbouring sorcerers (p. 236). Le Franc's *Champion des dames* (c. 1440) shows women on brooms in its margins (p. 236).

**Eve of the Reformation** (ch. 7). Trials in the Valais, Lausanne, Vevey, Neuchâtel, Bern, Fribourg, Basel after 1440 (p. 238). Adeline at Évreux (1453); Arras (1459–60): "thirty-four people were arrested as witches and twelve were executed by burning", all rehabilitated in 1491; two women burned at Cologne in 1456 (p. 242). The *Malleus* (1487) and later belong after the game.

**Geography (open decision 9).** Peters refines Hutton (p. 174): the first sect trials are in **Savoy, the Dauphiné, Vaud, Aosta and western Switzerland**, and reach **Lombardy** (Como) and the Rhineland from there (pp. 232–33). Milan's 1384/1390 cases remain the pre-sect bridge (from the other notes; Peters only mentions "isolated trials" in late-14th-c. Italy, p. 232).

That may be what Lecouteux's "872, Charles the Bald" garbles (`witch_hutton_lecouteux.md` §5 open question 1). Neither text, as Peters describes it, mentions the night ride. An 867 start is still inference (grade C).

### Names and words

- **Do not adopt "Herla".** Peters's "Diana-Herla" (p. 189) runs the army-of-the-dead king into the Lady. Hutton keeps them apart (`witch_hutton_lecouteux.md` §3 change 7). Keep Herla out of the Lady's names.
- **"Hulda/Holda" is not a witch in the faith's voice.** Jolly's "the Germanic witch Hulda" (p. 18) is a gloss.
- **Hostile vocabulary by region, from Peters:** *stria* (Frankish, c. 510), *striga* and *masca* (Lombard, 643), *malefica*; OE *wicce* (a woman diviner), *lybbestre* (a woman poisoner) (pp. 177, 190–91, 202–03). Good for accusers' text: a Lombard notary writes *striga* or *masca*, not "witch", and the faith never uses either word of itself.
- No new names for the Lady. The book does not mention Abundia, Satia, Abonde, Oriente, Percht or Pharaildis.

### Practices

The book confirms the clerical frame (the ride as dream, penance) and adds the Fates' table. It has nothing on house-visits, uncovered vessels, the greeting or the staff; those rest on the Hutton/Lecouteux note.

## (c) The Anglo-Saxon charms (English content)

The mod's English material is Bædling's (York, the *bædling* of the penitential, Wulfstan). Jolly and Peters give what an English event or a hostile English voice can lean on.

- **Read the charms as Christian.** The *Lacnunga* is "otherwise full of formulas calling on God"; the *Nine Herbs* and *Nine Twigs of Woden* are exceptions inside it (Jolly p. 36). The *Æcerbot*, with four sods, Masses, and *crescite* in Latin and English, is "evidence of the Christianizing of rural practice", not paganism (p. 47). Use: an English charm in game is a household's or a priest's, not a survival of the Bædlings' own rite. The faith may borrow a charm; it does not claim the *Lacnunga* as its scripture.
- **Usable charms** (Jolly; Raudvere):
  - the journey charm: "I chant a victory charm; I carry a victory rod" (Jolly p. 48); good for a Bædling band setting out, said by a Christian ally;
  - *Wið dweorh* ("against a dwarf"): the Seven Sleepers on wafers, then a metrical charm (p. 42);
  - elf-shot and elves in exorcisms (Jolly pp. 31, 35; Raudvere p. 144);
  - betony in the *Leechbook* against nightmares (Jolly p. 33);
  - the delayed-birth charm: step three times over a grave (Raudvere pp. 145–46).
- **A gloss error.** Raudvere calls the grave a "wise man's" and then a "wise woman's" (p. 146), but the Old English they print reads *gewitenes mannes byrgenne* (p. 170 n. 57), usually "a departed person's grave". Do not write "a wise woman's grave" in loc.
- **Galdor** = "song" (Jolly p. 36): supports the warding *songs* as the English word for what the völva needs.
- **Penances** (Peters pp. 195–96): the oven-or-roof fever cure; five years for incantations and divination; Egbert's year for a woman's witchcraft. Hostile voice for an English confessor before 1066, alongside A 127.
- **Old English law terms** (Peters pp. 202–03): *wicce* (f.) is a diviner, *lybbestre* a poisoner, *galdorcræft* enchantment. Alfred's law cites Exodus 22:18 against women who receive wizards; Edward and Guthrum exile or fine. Good for **Wulfstan-era English reaction text** (the TODO item). Note that the English *wicce* is a diviner; an English accuser calling a Bædling völva *wicce* is period-correct.
- **Guardrail:** the *Lacnunga* cattle-theft charm calls the Jews Christ's killers and thieves (Jolly p. 48). Never quote that charm, even in hostile text.

## (d) Other faiths

- **Adelphai Dorleyaiou.** Peters leaves out the Greek East (p. 177). Jolly has only Liudprand's wonder at the automata in Constantinople (p. 67) and a pointer to Byzantine demonology (p. 64). Nothing new.
- **Ha-Nehpakhot.** The Venaissin seat lies beside Avignon. Peters places John XXII's sorcery panic there (1317–31; pp. 220–21) and has Tholosan consult jurists at Aix and Avignon in 1437 (p. 234). A possible **context event** after 1317 ("the pope fears poisoners and image-makers"), aimed at clerics. Guardrails: Jolly records the Jew-as-sorcerer and blood-libel motifs (pp. 21–22, 62; Peters p. 178). None appears in any voice, not even as allegation. The "synagogue" for the sect (Errores, Le Franc; pp. 233, 236) is already banned by rule 25.
- **The Mummers (Good Ladies event 6).** Silos confirms men dancing in women's clothes as a penitential item (Peters p. 196); Burchard's stag and calf (Jolly p. 18). Rule 5 stands.
- **Worn Garland, Yutai Dao, Badenya.** Jolly: "the application of the term 'magic' to other cultures and beliefs outside of Europe should be avoided" (p. 71). Supports the existing rule: no "magic" or "sorcery" in the narrator's voice for Tamil, Chinese or Mande practice.
- **Joan of Arc.** Nider read Joan's case as diabolical sorcery when told of it at Basel (Peters pp. 228, 235). One more hostile reading. Joan stays unclaimed.

## Guardrails

- **No Murray.** Three authors say it plainly: late-medieval magic does "not reflect continuity with some intact pagan religion surviving underground the centuries since conversion" (Jolly p. 23); the first trial regions "were not remote mountain backwaters of residual folk paganism" (Peters p. 233); *trolldómr* was not "underground" hidden lore (Raudvere p. 81).
- **Rule 25 holds.** No sabbat, pact, familiar, broom or bodily flight to a gathering. The Reims "flight" and the *Malleus* stay out. Spirit travel with the body at home has support in Raudvere (p. 104) and in Caesarius (Jolly p. 66), but neither is the Ladies.
- **Pact nuance (not a change).** Augustine already ties superstition to pacts with demons (Peters p. 183), and clerical necromancers are accused of written pacts in the 12th century (Jolly p. 62). Rule 25's "never a pact before c. 1430" is safe for **seers and the Ladies' believers**, which is what it governs. A learned cleric's tract after c. 1250 could speak of a "tacit pact"; the mod chooses not to (open question 6).
- **No real people claimed.** Judith, Gerberga, Waldrada, Alice Kyteler, Petronilla, Alice Perrers, Eleanor Cobham and the Arras dead are victims of accusations, never members of any faith.
- **No Jewish-sorcerer or blood-libel material** (above).
- **Sámi and Hebrideans.** Raudvere shows the sagas use ethnicity to make sorcerers foreign (pp. 130, 140). The mod should not repeat it: no Sámi or Gaelic character is a sorcerer *because* Sámi or Gaelic.
- **No third gender.** Clover's "sex-gender system rather different from our own" (Raudvere p. 119) is about saga honour, not a third sex. The Bædling refusal (`baedling.md`, *No third sex*) stands. Kotkell's sons are men.
- **Rule 5.** The *Vǫlsa þáttr* horse-phallus cult (Raudvere pp. 131–33) is not to be used near a daughter or her body.
- **Rule 24 nuance.** Peters shows Carolingian royal law (789, 785, 859, 873) as well as penitentials condemning sorcery before 1123. Hostile Latin text before 1123 may cite "the emperor's capitulary" as well as the penitentials. Still no canon-law citations before Gratian (c. 1140).

## Open questions

1. **Burchard's Fates.** Exact wording of the *Corrector* question on setting the table for the three *Parcae* (McNeill and Gamer 1938, pp. 321–45; Jolly gives no question number). Does it give a night or date (e.g. New Year)?
2. **Burchard's "straw or wood".** Jolly (p. 18) has the substitute for the heart as "straw or wood"; `witch_hutton_lecouteux.md` §3 left the straw detail unverified. Check McNeill and Gamer.
3. **Charles the Bald and the Canon.** What do the Metz (859) and Quierzy (873) capitularies say? Is either the "lost ninth-century capitulary" (Russell 1972, pp. 75–81, 291–93), and does either mention the ride?
4. **Arras.** 12 burned (Peters p. 242) or "more than 20" (`witch_papers_and_timeline.md`)? Check Hansen 1901 or the Alberta exhibit.
5. **Le Franc.** Peters reads the *Champion des dames* section as describing old women at the "synagogue" (p. 236); our timeline reads it as a debate sceptical of flight. Both may be right (the poem is a dialogue). Check before a Le Franc event.
6. **Tacit pact.** Should a learned cleric after c. 1250 be allowed the phrase "a tacit pact with demons" for a seer, or does rule 25 forbid it outright? The rule now forbids it; Athlone shows it would be period-correct theology. User decision.
7. **John XXII and the inquisitors.** When exactly did John XXII remove sorcery from inquisitorial jurisdiction, and did Gregory XI restore it in 1374 (Peters p. 222)? It changes who prosecutes in Lombardy between those dates.
8. **The 1412 caul.** Does John of Frankfurt's text describe night-time fighting or only luck? If it describes night battles, it is a German pre-1453 parallel to the benandanti and needs its own grading.
9. **Icelandic or Norwegian suit.** Which counties count as "Icelandic" for the jury of twelve and the asker's liability, and which as "Norwegian" for the six housewives and the seeress's liability?
