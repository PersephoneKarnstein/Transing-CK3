# Law for v2: Caviness, "Giving 'the Middle Ages' a Bad Name" (2013), the *Sachsenspiegel* and blood punishments

**Source.** Madeline H. Caviness, "Giving 'the Middle Ages' a Bad Name: Blood Punishments in the *Sachsenspiegel* and Town Lawbooks," *Studies in Iconography* 34 (2013): 175–235. JSTOR stable 23924250. Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo. Pages whose extracted text was cut off (pp. 187–198, 219–226, 230–232) were read from the PDF page images. Page numbers are the printed ones.

Pronouns: Caviness is *she* here. Eike von Repgow is *he*.

Quote rule: every verbatim quote is 25 words or fewer and carries a page. Caviness quotes the *Sachsenspiegel* in Maria Dobozy's English translation of the Wolfenbüttel manuscript (p. 179). Those quotes are marked "Ssp via Caviness". Everything else is paraphrase.

**What was read.** The whole article, pp. 175–226, and the notes, pp. 226–235, including the figure captions.

**Relation to existing notes.** No existing note covers German secular law. The witch notes describe the **Church's** response by phase (`witch_design.md` §5; `witch_papers_and_timeline.md` §3–§4). This note adds the **lay court's** side: its procedures, and its one clause on magic. The Norse comparison is `../norse_mitchell_witchcraft_magic.md` ("Law (ch. 5)"). There is an open lead on a *Sachsenspiegel* *Zwitter* clause in `../comparanda_reading_list.md` (§6, "*Sachsenspiegel*").

**Usefulness: limited but real.** The article is about pictures, not cases. It gives the law of **Saxony from c. 1220–25**, far east of Prüm and after both 1066 and 1178. It is useful for (a) one burning clause, which genders the sorcerer only in the picture, (b) how reluctant lay courts were to shed blood, and (c) pre-1215 procedure (ordeal, oath-helpers, wergeld). It says nothing about cross-dressing, and sodomy only in one secondhand note (n. 97).

## 0. Framing warning

- **Her thesis.** The famous punishment pictures are not records of practice. Read literally, they made "the Middle Ages" look bloodier than they were. In her closing words: "I do not doubt that capital punishment was carried out" (p. 226). But the reputation grew because "images that were informed by anxiety and uncertainty have been taken literally" (p. 226). Her argument has three parts: jurisdiction and Christian teaching were shifting; the texts and pictures were rhetoric meant to show "teeth in the law"; and some pictures go beyond the physically possible (p. 177).
- **What it does not claim.** It does not say executions were rare. There are "almost no detailed case studies" from the lawbooks' period, so frequency cannot be known either way (p. 176). Hanging was "the commonest form of capital punishment" for lower-class men, and most towns had a gallows (p. 211).
- **Method.** Speech-act theory (Austin, Culler) and Butler on performativity (pp. 182–83). It is an interpretive frame, not evidence.
- **Dates (check against the starts).**
  - **Text:** Eike von Repgow, c. 1220–25 (p. 179). The brief's "c. 1220–35" is wider than Caviness's date.
  - **The four picture books:** Heidelberg (H) 1295–1304; Oldenburg (O) 1336; Dresden (D) a few years before W; Wolfenbüttel (W) 1358–62/71 (pp. 179–80, n. 26). They are the reception of the text, not of Eike's time.
  - **Town books:** the Soest *Nequambuch* pictures c. 1315; the Zwickau Red Book 1348/1353 (pp. 198, 200).
  - **For 1066:** none of this exists yet. **For 1178:** still forty years off.
- **Where it applies.** The law is Saxon: Eike worked from Altzelle near Meissen, for a count near Halle (p. 179). He claims relevance beyond Saxony by naming Swabian and Franconian lords (p. 183), and copies spread west, south and east (n. 22, about 460 manuscripts). **Prüm (Lotharingia) is not Saxon-law country.** Use this note for Saxon realms in the 13th–14th centuries, and only as background for the Rhineland.
- **Anti-Jewish material.** The *Sachsenspiegel* pictures draw Jews bearded and off-balance (p. 180) and include a clause hanging a Jew found with church goods (Fig. 2 caption, p. 181). One Zwickau victim has Jewish-coded features (p. 209). **None of this goes into the mod** (standing rule: persecution is never a mechanic against Jewish characters, and they are never the wrongdoers).

## 1. Findings

### 1a. Magic in the *Sachsenspiegel*: one clause, gendered by the picture (p. 187)
- **The clause.** "A Christian man or woman who is without faith and practices magic or mixes potions and is convicted must be burned on the pyre" (Ssp via Caviness, p. 187). It sits with the other death sentences (beheading for murder, arson, rape and adultery).
- **The text is not gendered; the picture is.** In W, "the witch or sorcerer is a woman, the heretic is male" (Fig. 8, register 2; p. 187, caption on p. 188).
- **Three conditions in one line.** The burning is for someone who is (1) a Christian (2) "without faith" who (3) practises magic or mixes potions, *and* is convicted. Unbelief and magic travel together. "Mixes potions" is Dobozy's English. Whether the Middle Low German means poison is not discussed (see §5 lead 1).
- **Section number.** Caviness quotes the clause without a number. Her caption gives 2.14–15 for the page. Do not cite a section number until it is checked in Dobozy.
- **Nothing on night journeys, ladies or spirit travel.** The only other supernatural agent in the Landrecht is a "very tame devil" who makes animals run amok. Their owners pay damages (p. 200, n. 70; Fig. 17, 2.63).

### 1b. Lay judges were afraid to shed blood (pp. 189–92, 224–26)
- **Eike pushes them to sentence.** "A judge who does not sentence a person for a crime draws upon himself the same penalty" (Ssp 2.14 via Caviness, p. 189).
- **Two fears pull the other way** (pp. 189–90):
  - a vendetta from the convicted person's kin (after Whitman);
  - God's judgment. Peter the Chanter and Innocent III worried about spilling blood even indirectly. A judge who condemned the innocent would be "himself a murderer and suffer in hell" (p. 190).
- **The burden moves.** Over the 13th century the spiritual burden shifted from the judge to the witnesses and jurors (*Schöffen*) (p. 190). By Eike's time, with the clergy gone from ordeals, the *Schöffen* "were expected to play God" (p. 225).
- **Church courts give no blood.** Theoretically, "sentences of blood" were not given in church courts (n. 2, p. 226, after Clarke).
- **Torture.** "Torture was little used in the twelfth century" (n. 5, p. 227, after Edward Peters; **secondhand**). Torture to obtain confession belongs to the early modern period (p. 176).

### 1c. Before and after 1215: ordeal, oath and wergeld (pp. 192–98)
- **Ordeals.** Priests had administered the ordeal until Lateran IV (1215) forbade it (p. 192). Eike still admits it, while saying Saxons keep their old law "as long as it does not conflict with the law of Christianity or correct faith" (Ssp 1.18 via Caviness, p. 192).
- **Forms:** hot iron, immersion in cold water, an arm in boiling water. The iron and the water were for people "without legal standing because of prior convictions" (1.39; p. 192).
- **A famous woman's ordeal.** The Ottonian Empress Cunigund cleared herself of a charge of infidelity by the hot iron. A 13th-century manuscript shows her led to it by two bishops (p. 192).
- **Trial by combat.** It was banned by Frederick II in 1231 (p. 192) and by the church in 1215 (p. 194). Pope Gregory XI condemned the *Sachsenspiegel* for it in 1374 (p. 194).
- **Oath-helpers.** A man in good standing could be cleared by six others swearing to his good name (p. 198). Where life or limb was at stake, the judge and six others had to swear as witnesses before judgment (1.8; p. 196).
- **Wergeld instead of blood.** If the killer settled damages with the family quickly, he could not be sentenced to death. The sum scaled with the dead person's rank (p. 194).
- **People with no wergeld (*rechtlos*).** Champions and their children, minstrels, people of illegitimate birth and outlaws (n. 62, p. 232). Minstrels and bondsmen are awarded "the shadow of a man" (Ssp 3.45–47 via Caviness, n. 61, p. 231).

### 1d. Women before the court (pp. 187, 196–98, 215)
- **Women may not plead their own case** before a judge. Eike gives the reason: a certain Calefurnia's anger at the emperor (p. 198; the tradition goes back to the *Digest*, n. 66).
- **Pregnant women.** The law limits their corporal punishment to "hide and hair" (3.3). The Heidelberg picture contradicts this by showing her flogged, and makes her look like Christ at the column (p. 187).
- **Rape.** A woman's case depends on her raising the hue and cry at once and coming to court with torn clothing (2.65). Old customs about the house and all witnesses made reporting costly (pp. 196–98).
- **Adultery.** Lay law treated adultery as a threat to the male line, but "Churchmen regarded adultery much like any other venal sin" (p. 215). In 1256 Louis II of Bavaria had his wife executed on suspicion and had to found an abbey in atonement. A poet mourned her as an innocent (p. 215).
- **Not in the article:** women and sorcery beyond §1a; any cross-dressing clause; sodomy (only Mills's later "hell" iconography, n. 97, secondhand).

### 1e. Penitence and the condemned (pp. 221–22)
- In 1312 Clement V forbade denying penitence to the condemned. Penitence was customary in the Rhineland, and Strasbourg had two chapels for it (p. 222, after Esther Cohen; **secondhand**). This lightened the judges' burden (p. 226).

## 5. Leads not yet followed

1. ~~Dobozy, *The Saxon Mirror* (1999)~~ **Checked 2026-10-27.** The magic clause is Book II, the section beginning "Now hear about criminal acts" (§14 in Dobozy's numbering of the Wolfenbüttel text), p. 97: "A Christian man or woman who is without faith and practices magic or mixes potions … must be burned". Dobozy gives no note on the Middle German (notes to pp. 97–99, p. 222), so whether "potions" means poison is still open. It sits in a list with theft, robbery, arson, rape and adultery, and a judge who fails to sentence takes the same penalty.
2. ~~Ssp Landrecht I.4 in the original~~ **Checked 2026-10-27: no *Zwitter*.** Dobozy p. 70: tenancy and inheritance cannot "devolve upon the feebleminded, dwarfs, or cripples". The Middle German is *uf alwilen unde uf getwerge*. Her n. 28 (p. 211) says *alwil* "has not yet been adequately defined" (Lübben; Janz pp. 69–70). The *Zwitter* lead probably glosses that word. There is no *Zwitter* clause for the mod (`../comparanda_reading_list.md` updated).
3. **Caviness and Nelson, "Silent Witnesses, Absent Women, and the Law Courts in Medieval Germany,"** in *Fama*, ed. Fenster and Smail (Cornell, 2003), 47–72 (n. 32). This is the study of women in the *Sachsenspiegel*, and the best next read for row 4.
4. **Robert Bartlett, *Trial by Fire and Water* (1986)** (nn. 57–60). Pre-1215 ordeal practice in the Rhineland: what a 1066 lay court at Prüm or Trier would actually do.
5. **James Q. Whitman, *The Origins of Reasonable Doubt* (2008)** (n. 16). The judges' "moral comfort" thesis behind row 3.
6. **Agobard of Lyons (769–840)**, quoted on the ordeal (p. 225, after Whitman). Agobard also wrote against belief in storm-makers. That is outside this article: check his *De grandine* separately for a pre-867 lay belief text.
7. **Robert Mills, *Suspended Animation* (2005), pp. 85–105** (n. 97). Hell iconography and the prosecution of sodomites. It is late medieval and secondhand here, so read it before any use.
