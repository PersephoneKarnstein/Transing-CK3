# Witches for v2: Motz, "The Winter Goddess: Percht, Holda, and Related Figures" (1984)

**Source.** Lotte Motz, "The Winter Goddess: Percht, Holda, and Related Figures," *Folklore* 95, no. 2 (1984): 151–166. Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo, checked against the PDF scan for the notes. Page numbers are the journal's printed ones.

Pronouns: Motz is *she*. Hutton is *he*. Waschnitius and others whose pronouns I don't know are left without pronouns.

Quote rule: every verbatim quote is 25 words or fewer and carries a page. Everything else is paraphrase.

**What was read.** The whole article, pp. 151–163, and all its notes, pp. 163–166. **The printed notes skip from 105 to 112** (p. 166; the scan confirms the gap). So pp. 159–160 (Olaus Magnus, the werewolf, Isengrind, *Wolfsmonat*, Yule cakes, the Roman midwinter) have no notes. Note 118's "Höfler, as in ref. 112" points to Lid, so it is a misnumbering for ref. 41.

**Relation to existing notes.** This is the eighth reading for the Good Ladies. It answers the user's question of 2026-10-27: were Holda and Perchta pre-Christian goddesses? The note is checked against:
- `witch_hutton_lecouteux.md` (Hutton 2014; Lecouteux 2011), the current reading;
- `witch_papers_and_timeline.md` §3 (evidence grades A–D);
- `witch_horsley_folk_religion.md` §0 and §2 (Horsley's "surviving fertility goddess", set aside);
- `witch_design.md` §1, §3.1, §10;
- rule 25 in `../../faith_design.md`.

The design is now planned for the 1066 start at Prüm.

**Usefulness: low for building, useful as a warning.** Motz is the clearest statement of the "pagan goddess" case for Holda and Perchta, and it shows how little of its evidence is medieval. It adds a handful of dated clerical witnesses (13th–15th c.) that are not in the existing notes. **Only one item falls at or before 1066 (Burchard), and only one is from the Rhineland (also Burchard).** Nothing is from the Eifel.

## 0. Framing warning

- **Her thesis.** Motz claims four things (p. 151): the figures are "indeed divinities"; one basic form lies behind the local variants; they grew from a hunting people's guardian of animals into a "Lady of Wild Beasts" like Artemis; and they are native to northern and north-western Europe. **She argues for a goddess of the wild, not a fertility or mother goddess.** She ties the midwinter feast to hunting, not to farming or to the death and rebirth of vegetation (p. 160). This differs from Horsley's fertility goddess (Horsley pp. 87–88).
- **Her method is the one Hutton rejects.** She says she turned to "the long memory of folk-belief" (p. 151). Her material comes "on the whole" from Waschnitius's collection, *Perht, Holda und verwandte Gestalten* (1913–14) (p. 151, n. 3). She then reconstructs one ancient form from many recorded variants (pp. 157, 163). Hutton names both habits as the Grimm legacy: treating variants as the decay of one ancient myth, and using 19th–20th-century folklore to fill out medieval texts (Hutton pp. 162–63, in `witch_hutton_lecouteux.md` §1).
- **Her folklore is undated.** Almost every custom and legend is cited only as "Wasch." with a page. She never says when or by whom it was recorded. Read all of it as **"recorded before 1913, date unknown"**. Most of it is very likely 19th-century collecting.
- **Her theory leans on Höfler and Eliade.** She takes the Perchten runs and the Wild Hunt to be "images of one another" from Otto Höfler's *Kultische Geheimbünde der Germanen* (1934) (p. 153, n. 43). The note on Höfler in `witch_hutton_lecouteux.md` §2 applies: he was a Nazi-era scholar with a politically loaded *Männerbund* thesis. The initiation reading (pp. 160–161) is Eliade's typology, with Australian shamans' dreams as the parallel (p. 160, n. 117).
- **Her comparisons are typological.** The comparisons are Artemis (p. 161), Siberian and Central Asian guardians of game (pp. 157–158, after Paulson 1961), and Norse giantesses (pp. 162–163). They show likeness, not descent. Motz half-concedes this for Artemis when she rejects Liungman (p. 161).
- **The modern runs are a living community.** Perchtenlauf, Posterlijagen and Streggelejagen (pp. 152–153) are present-day customs. Per `witch_design.md` §10 they are never the faith or its continuation.

## 1. Findings

### 1a. The argument, step by step

1. **Divinity** (pp. 151–152). Her evidence is (a) clerical condemnations (§1b, items 3–7), (b) food offerings, (c) days, places and customs named after the Lady, and (d) her power over the year's luck. She concludes it would be "difficult to deny her a firm position in the faith of men" (p. 152). Only (a) is medieval.
2. **One form** (pp. 152–157). The traits she gathers from Waschnitius are: winter season, offerings, a noisy following, supervising spinning, punishing and rewarding (including slitting the stomach and filling it with straw), unbaptized infants, children, women, the forest, a double aspect (hag or fair lady), animals (dog, goat, a beak-like "iron" nose), no husband, and swift movement. Her summary: "a virgin deity of the woodland, of dual aspect" (p. 157).
3. **The infants are Christian.** She says plainly that the unbaptized children following Perchta are "a Christian explanation" (p. 155). This agrees with Lecouteux and the existing note: Percht leading unbaptized children is later folklore (`witch_hutton_lecouteux.md` §2).
4. **Lady of Beasts** (pp. 157–159). Guardians of the wild exist worldwide. The Alpine and German "wild folk" (*Holzleute*, *Selige Fräulein*, *Fangen*) survive where an "ancient form of faith is preserved", and Percht and Holda are a "more developed form" of them (p. 158). The evidence is folklore and comparative ethnography.
5. **The midwinter feast** (pp. 159–160). Church councils and penitentials condemn the Kalends of January beast-masquerade more than any other custom (p. 159, n. 105). Motz reads them as native to the north. She then reads the masks as a hunters' rite and puts the Lady at its centre (p. 160). **The penitentials are real early evidence (§1b, item 1), but none she cites names a Lady.** The link to Holda is her inference.
6. **Initiation** (pp. 160–161). She reads stomach-slitting, spinning tests and the "hideous" and "handsome" Perchten as puberty-initiation motifs (after Eliade). The evidence is folklore and modern custom.
7. **Not Diana** (pp. 161–163). She rejects Liungman's thesis (1938, n. 121) that a Graeco-Roman Artemis/Diana cult came via the Balkans and was taken up in 7th-century Bavaria. Liungman's evidence (Diana shrines in the upper Rhineland, **near Trier** and in southern Gaul; Gregory of Tours; Caesarius) "does not pertain to the Germanic provinces", Motz says (p. 161). She argues instead that the many vernacular names point to a native figure (p. 162). She supports this with Norse parallels: name likenesses (Holle/Hulda with the giantess Huld and the Norwegian *huldre*; Harke with Herkja; Gode with Gói), winter, night, wolves, beaks and goats (pp. 162–163).
8. **The night company.** Motz first says there is "some evidence" that the Lady and her followers really went in procession through village streets (p. 153). She cites the Canon and three later documents for this. Then she steps back: "we cannot be sure that men or women did actually ride", only that people believed they did (p. 154). The Canon itself calls the ride an illusion (`witch_papers_and_timeline.md` §3). **Do not cite p. 153 for a bodily procession. Rule 25 allows spirit travel only.**

### 1c. Inside the game, at Prüm, and on the Rhine

- **At or before 1066:** items 1 and 2 only. Item 1 does not mention a Lady. Item 2 is the Canon and Burchard, which the existing notes already hold from better routes (Kors and Peters; McNeill and Gamer; Lecouteux's translations). **Motz adds nothing new for 1066.**
- **Eifel and Prüm:** only indirectly. Regino of Prüm is in her n. 105 list for the Kalends beast-masquerade (McNeill and Gamer p. 318). Liungman's Diana shrine "near Trier" (p. 161) is late antique and is reported without a source.
- **Rhineland:** Burchard (item 2). The folklore places nearest the Rhine are the Westerwald *Hollenabend* (Nassau, n. 18), the Nassau *Hollezopf* (p. 156) and the Heidesheim hunt (p. 153). All are undated. **"Rhingebirge" on p. 152 is the Rhön, not the Rhine:** p. 154 has "Rhöngebirge", and n. 17 places it where Hesse, Bavaria and Thuringia meet. Motz says Westphalia "show[s] little evidence" of the Lady (p. 152).
- **Within 1066–1453 but not at Prüm:** item 3 (13th c., Perchta), and probably items 4 and 5 (15th c., Perchta). All are Bavarian or Alpine and second-hand.

### 1d. Verdict on the user's question

**As far as Motz and the existing notes support: not shown.** Motz asserts that Holda and Perchta were pre-Christian divinities. But no witness she cites names either figure before c. 1010 (Holda, Burchard, Worms). Perchta first appears in the 13th century (item 3, unverified), otherwise c. 1350 (Lecouteux). That is centuries after the Rhineland and Bavaria became Christian. Her case for a pagan origin rests on four supports:
- penitentials that condemn Kalends masks but name no Lady;
- 13th-century Norse name likenesses;
- comparison with Artemis and Siberian guardians of game;
- undated folklore read through Höfler and Eliade.

Her best medieval evidence shows something narrower. **Clerics from the 13th to the 15th century called Perchta and Holda "Diana" and a "heathen goddess"** (items 3, 7, 8). That is clerical *interpretatio*, the same move as Regino's "Diana, goddess of the pagans", not proof of a cult.

This leaves Hutton's position standing. The women's company "may well have pre-Christian origins" (Hutton p. 175), and nothing here proves or disproves it. **Superseded (2026-10-27):** Hutton's later book is firmer. He finds "no known pagan goddess" who is "an obvious ancestress" of the Lady, and judges such figures "products of the Christian period" (*Queens of the Wild*, 2022, pp. 138, 196; `witch_hutton_queens_of_the_wild.md`). Lecouteux finds no basis for a Diana cult surviving into the Middle Ages (ch. 1, n. 8). **For the mod: Holda and Perchta are medieval figures of Christian-era popular belief, attested from c. 1010 and the 13th–14th c. Their pagan roots are possible and unprovable. The narrator never calls her a surviving goddess** (`witch_design.md` §10). Only clergy call her a goddess, as in the sources.
