# Sex workers' own writing (B): Nuttbrock, Gallant & Lam, *Working It*

Companion to `sw_books_a.md` (Mac & Smith, Gira Grant, *We Too*; cited "A §n", its principles "A-P n"). Targets the consolidated Japan note `japan_design.md` ("JD §n", events "JD e n") and `japan_goodwin_kudenshu.md` ("GK §n"). Writing rules are `docs/faith_design.md` §16 ("rule n"). Nothing in the repo was edited. Written 2026-10-24.
Texts in `scratchpad/v2/ocr/sw/`. Quotes are verbatim and 25 words or fewer. Nuttbrock and *Working It* are cited by printed page. Gallant & Lam is an EPUB, so it is cited by chapter and section heading.
Weighting follows the memory rule: insiders outrank outside scholars on a community's own life. Each source below is tagged **insider**, **partial** or **outsider**. Real people in these books are never characters, and their private details are not reused.
Excluded by the guardrails: every passage about minors (Nuttbrock ch. 3–4 and ch. 16 on adolescents; Kulick's childhood accounts; G&L ch. 7, "But what about the children?"; the adolescent parts of Pariah and Naomi in *Working It*), every passage about suicide or self-harm, and any material on trans men, which barely appears in these books anyway (Nuttbrock p. xvii).

## 1. Nuttbrock, ed., *Transgender Sex Work and Society* (Harrington Park 2018) — "N"

**What it is.** A public-health volume of 25 chapters. The core is the New York Transgender Project (NIDA-funded HIV and depression cohort). Section VI covers Turkey, India, Brazil (two chapters), Malaysia, Thailand, the Andes, Spain and China.
**Read:** foreword, introduction, ch. 1, 3, 14, 15, 16, 17, 19, 24 and 25, plus the parts of ch. 18 on kathoey self-views, and the contributor notes. **Skimmed:** ch. 2, 5–12, 13, 20–23 (epidemiology, mental health, HIV, care).

**Who speaks** (contributor notes, pp. 439–48):
| Chapter | Author(s) | Standing | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foreword, ch. 25 | Bockting (clinical psychologist), Nuttbrock (sociologist) | outsider: no stated community standing | low on meaning, fair on data |
| Intro, ch. 2, 3 | Nuttbrock (ch. 3 with Hwahng) | outsider; ch. 3 builds on Bailey | low; see "must not" |
| ch. 1 | Sel J. Hwahng: "transmasculine gender-variant person of color", organiser since 1995 (p. 3) | partial: trans community, not a trans woman or a sex worker | medium |
| ch. 14 (India) | Chakrapani, Newman, Noronha (public health) | outsider, but drawing on hijra and thirunangai self-reports and Indian community research | medium |
| ch. 15 (Brazil) | Don Kulick (anthropologist) | outsider; his frame contradicts the mod (below) | low |
| ch. 16 (Brazil) | Barry Wolfe (lawyer; founder of an NGO for victims) | outsider, with an anti-trafficking lens | low to medium (detail only) |
| ch. 17–18 (Malaysia, Thailand) | Nemoto et al. | outsiders; long quotes from mak nyah and kathoey workers | medium (the quotes are insider) |
| ch. 19 (Andes) | Salazar et al. | outsiders; one LGBTI activist co-author | medium |
| ch. 24 (Canada) | Lyons, **Leslie Pierre**, Krüsi, Shannon | partial: Pierre is a Dakelh trans woman, formerly at the sex-worker-led PACE Society, and a peer co-analyst (pp. 422, 446) | medium to high |

**Summary.** The book documents high rates of sex work among trans women everywhere it looks, and it explains them through exclusion from schooling, housing and other work; family rejection; the cost of transition; racism; and police violence. The best chapters (1, 14, 17, 18, 24) let workers say how they choose, screen, refuse and help each other. The weakest (3, the foreword, 25) frame trans women's sex work through male desire and "affirmation" by clients, and ch. 3 entertains Bailey's claim that some trans women are "well-suited for sex work" (p. 36).

**Key claims.**
- *Sex work as skill.* The editor says the term "points to the skills, labor, emotional work, and physical presentation of sellers" (pp. xv–xvi). Hwahng's Asian workers in New York were paid for "emotional and psychological pampering in the form of soothing dialogue, compliments, and physical touch" (p. 10), and for private fashion shows with no sex.
- *Setting decides safety.* Workers who could screen clients, see fewer and set terms (apartments and hotels, not the street) had more power: condoms "nonnegotiable", drugs feigned and thrown away (pp. 10–11). Rushed negotiation is dangerous, and screening is "a key aspect of managing potential client violence" (Lyons et al., p. 424).
- *Other income gives the power to refuse.* With a full-time job, Rowena says, an arrogant client who will not wear a condom: "I reject" (p. 298). Almost all the mak nyah also worked "bridal assistance and providing makeup and hair services" (p. 298). Andean trans women work in hairdressing, make-up and decoration (p. 341); kathoey "work as hairdressers or in the entertainment business" (p. 331).
- *Womanhood is fate, the trade is chosen.* Most kathoey workers "thought that their gender identity … was fate, but engaging in sex work was their choice, not fate" (p. 331).
- *Community draws women in as much as money.* Mak nyah entry was "tied to both economic need and the longing for friendships and community" (p. 297). In the Andes a senior woman, "usually called 'mother' by the initiates", teaches the trade and is repaid in money, domestic labour and care (p. 345).
- *Houses, gurus and fees.* Among hijras the gharanas "are divided according to the nature of the work done by their members" (p. 215), and only some trade. Gurus take part of a chela's earnings and in return give lodging, food and security (p. 219). A hijra who will not trade may move to another gharana, paying "a fine or 'compensation'" to her guru (p. 223). Hijras call sex work *dhanda*, "literally, 'work'" (p. 218). Their older livelihood was blessing births and weddings and dancing at festivals; the trade grew as that income declined (p. 218).
- *The mother who protects and the mother who owns.* Wolfe's Brazilian *madrinha*/*filha* pairs (pp. 273–74): good *cafetinas* "charge justly for board and lodging", lend without interest, never fine or beat. The exploiting *madrinha* uses the same emotional bond to hold her daughters through debt, fines and fear. Kulick found Brazilian travestis in Milan held by older travestis' charges for a bed, a stretch of pavement, food and clothes (p. 237).
- *Old age is neglected.* "Nobody seems to care much about old travestis" (Kulick, p. 239). Ch. 14's life-stage plan ends in pensions, asset ownership and health cover for old age (pp. 226–28).
- *The data undercut the desire story.* In Nuttbrock and Hwahng's own model, sexual orientation stops predicting sex work once ethnicity is entered; poverty and racism carry the effect (p. 41).
- *Self-naming.* Khartini Slamah: *mak nyah* was coined in 1987 "to define ourselves from a vantage point of dignity rather than from the position of derogation" (p. 313).

**What the mod takes.** The work is a set of skills that the house teaches. Screening and setting terms are the women's protection. Other trades in the same house give a woman the power to refuse. A house may or may not trade, and houses differ by what they do (the gharana model). The mother's office has two faces. The faith cares for its old. Womanhood is the one thing in her life that is not a trade.

**What the mod must not do.**
- Must not use the "affirmation" thesis as mechanic or narrator: "Being admired and desired by male clients may serve as a powerful affirmation of their femininity" (foreword, p. xii; again pp. 225, 345, 435–36). Some trans workers say it of themselves, and that is theirs to say. A patron's desire must never confirm, earn or measure a daughter's womanhood.
- Must not take Bailey's link between desire for men and the trade (p. 36), Kulick's "travestis did not want to be women" (p. 237), or Bockting's "transwomen are not simply women" (p. 436). All three are outsider frames that the mod's rules reject.
- Must not make the trade an initiation. Hwahng's House Ball informants describe sex work "internalized within the community as a rite of passage" (p. 5), and the chapter treats this as a harm.
- Must not use the market's racial taste. Hwahng's informants report white clients choosing Asian workers for "natural femininity" (p. 8). That is the clients' fetish, reported, not a fact about Asian women.

## 2. Gallant & Lam, *Not Your Rescue Project: Migrant Sex Workers Fighting for Justice* (Haymarket 2024) — "G&L"

**Standing: insider-led.** Lam founded Butterfly, the Asian and migrant sex workers' network in Toronto. Both authors write as organisers inside a worker-led movement ("We are activists and organizers", Intro). The book is built on workers' own stories, shared with permission (the four Interludes). The authors' analysis ranks just below the workers' quoted words, and both outrank outside scholars.
**Read in full:** foreword, Introduction, ch. 1–9, Conclusion; the afterword skimmed.

**Summary.** The book's subject is Asian and other racialised migrant women, many of them older and many of them mothers, in massage and sex work in Canada and the US. Its argument is that the "modern slavery" story is a racial story. That story casts Asian women as passive victims or dangerous temptations, their families and men as the villains, and the police and charities as saviours. Its practical effect is raids, seizure of savings, eviction, deportation and isolation. The workers in the book explain why they chose the trade, how they set boundaries, how they help each other, and what "rescue" took from them.

**Key claims.**
- *The Orientalist pair.* The white-slavery panic cast Asian women "as both inherently docile and dangerous criminal temptations, originating from uncivilized cultures where families sell women and girls" (ch. 4, "The White Slave Becomes the Modern Slave"). The Page Act of 1875 excluded Chinese women in the name of fighting trafficking (ch. 4).
- *Who controls the meaning.* "The problem is not that the sex industry inaccurately portrays Asian women as 'too sexual.'" It is that Asian women lack the power "to define their own sexuality, on their own terms" (ch. 2, "Navigating Racism"). Older Asian workers sometimes use clients' racist ideas of their youth to their own profit (same section).
- *Comparative choice.* "It's not that sex work is necessarily good, it's that it can often be better than other work" (ch. 2, "Wages and Working Conditions"). Their list of reasons runs: wages; mobility; flexibility; a trade less disabling for older, sick and disabled women; a way out of marriage; community; sexual exploration for some (ch. 2).
- *Mixed houses give choice.* In massage businesses, where not every service is sexual, workers "have the choice—to sell sexual services or not", "when, to whom, and for how much" (ch. 2, "The Unique Benefits of Massage Businesses"). Living at work is cheap housing, and it also means a raid takes the roof.
- *The workers' own terms.* "We aren't stupid. If they don't have money, then we don't do [the sexual service]" (ch. 2). Lisa, a 61-year-old massage worker: "With the experience, I can tell where my customer hurts just by touch" (Interlude I).
- *Rescue as removal.* After the Seattle raids the "rescued" women "were simply displaced. They lost both their means of living and their housing" (Intro, quoting a Seattle outreach project). Police took savings and then described the women as "freed" (Intro).
- *Name the harm.* A bad boss is bad working conditions, a partner who takes the money is intimate violence, and forced labour is forced labour. "Trafficking" hides all three (Intro, "Against 'Modern Slavery'"). Butterfly's crisis workers avoid the word because it "make[s] invisible the problems workers are facing" (ch. 9).
- *Trans women are cast as menace, not victim.* Police describe trans migrant workers "as dangerous aggressors, not helpless victims". The state pushes cis women out of the trade and keeps trans women locked in it (ch. 3, "State Violence and Migrant Transgender Sex Workers").
- *Care networks come first.* Before any organisation existed, workers were "showing up at the jails for each other", paying rent and minding children: "We are all we have" (ch. 9, quoting Yves Tong Nguyen of Red Canary Song).
- *The adventurer's story.* Empower Foundation–Thailand: a man who sets out to make his family rich "would be the hero. But I am not a man" (ch. 1).
- *Whose story.* "Stories are told about them, not by them" (Intro). "Migrant sex workers are the heroes in their own stories. They are no one's rescue project" (Intro).
- *The pity reflex.* Allies are warned against the idea that the trade is "something that 'no one would want for their own daughter'" (Conclusion, point 8).

**What the mod takes.**
- Concrete anti-Orientalist rules for the singers (Principles 9–11 below).
- The mixed-trade house as the source of a woman's choice. A house of song, boats and lodging lets "the bed" stay optional, so the house's plural trade is a safety, not a cover.
- "Rescue" in 1178 is a man of power gaining control: a governor, an estate official, a preacher with a patron behind him. The cost to the women is counted.
- Harm is named by its period kind: a head's greed, a patron's blow, a husband's command, an official's seizure.
- Trans daughters are never the house's danger, and they are not treated as less rescuable or more culpable than cis singers.

**What the mod must not do.**
- No docile-or-temptress singer.
- No "families sell their daughters" culture-blame. The adult form of this fact does not appear, and the minors' form is already excluded (GK §6).
- No "the god or the Buddha frees her from the river".
- No pity line in the narrator's voice ("a hard life", "poor girls").
- No exotic sensory description wrapped around the trade.
- No modern slogans in game text: "sex work is work", "rights", "consent", "trafficking", "rescue industry". A-P 18 already bans these words; this book is where the ideas come from.

## 3. Bickers, breshears & Luna, eds., *Working It: Sex Workers on the Work of Sex* (PM Press 2023) — "WI"

**Standing: insider.** Every contributor is a current or former sex worker except Aubrey, a social worker (p. 18), and perhaps Ditmore and Kaylor, who are researchers with no stated sex-work history. The trans women are Sarah, "a former trans sex worker from Australia" (p. 219); Alyssa Pariah, a "Black Puerto Rican trans woman" (p. 218); and Jelena Vermilion, a "trans-femme full-service sex worker" who leads SWAP Hamilton (p. 220). Beyondeep is "Black, queer, and trans"; Sage is gender-fluid.
**Read:** Introduction (pp. 1–21); Raven (22–24); Sarah (31–33); Sage (35–38); Vermilion (73–78); Manon (83–86, the craft passages); Luna (129–34) and her interview (194–96); Beyondeep (137–40); Pariah (155–59, the adult reflection only); Bickers, "Intimate Labor" (184–93); Shepard, "1099 Problems" (197–201). **Skimmed:** the rest.

**Summary.** Workers write about the work itself: waiting, boredom, the performance, money, boundaries, managers, teaching, mutual aid, and the ways outsiders dress the work up or down. The editors' line: it is "a job like any other job, with the potential to be exploitative or fulfilling based on the day and interaction and context" (p. 11), and "Defend sex workers and our rights? Yes. The industry? Never" (Bickers, p. 193).

**Key claims.**
- *Skill hidden by design.* The labour is real but the customer must not see it: "no one wants to see the effort going into our production of sexy affection" (p. 18). "Neither whoring nor caregiving can be done well by just anyone at all" (Bickers, p. 191).
- *The skill list.* Vermilion lists what sex workers collectively know (pp. 76–78): de-escalation, negotiation, "cultivating intimacy", entertaining, dancing, massage, "being a muse", storytelling, mentorship, "sensing red flags", record-keeping, herbalism, "hotel wisdom", making escape plans, stamina. She calls full service "an equitable exchange" that "feels like honest work to me" (p. 74).
- *Emotional labour is the trade's centre.* Luna: "We flirt, we suck up, we tease, we cajole, we smooth over, we grin through our gritted teeth; we sell" (p. 133). Sarah, a trans woman, says her clients want "subtle reassurance" about their own desires, and that it is tedious (p. 31).
- *Monotony.* Sarah's essay is about waiting, dead shifts, bare pantries and the same script from every caller. Beside the "irrepressible sisterhood with other workers" sits "the fucking monotony too" (pp. 31–33). Neither glamour nor "pity porn".
- *No respectable cover.* Luna attacks the repackaging of the trade as therapy or athletics, because it treats 'the "sex" part as something to be excused' (Intro, p. 15; Luna pp. 129–34). "I'm not a naked therapist. I'm a stripper at the club and a therapist at the clinic" (p. 134).
- *The whorearchy.* Sage: "the less physical contact you make with people, the more 'valuable' your work is within the sex worker community". Full-service workers rank lowest and burlesque highest (p. 37). Outsiders now dehumanise by "fetishization, appropriation, and voyeurism" (p. 37).
- *Teaching inside the trade.* New dancers are "taken under the wing of more experienced dancers" (Luna, p. 130). Manon's first lesson came from a working friend, about a stage name and "move like the air is honey. If the customers laugh at you, act as though you meant for them to" (p. 85).
- *The client's illusion.* "The delusion clients cling to so fondly: that we do this because we love sex, and not because we need money" (Bickers, p. 187).
- *The trade beside care work.* Bickers compares the bed with paid eldercare: both handle bodies and both are "feminine" care work, but the care job is worse paid, more harassed and more physically damaging (pp. 190–92). Trans women, working-class women and sex workers "already don't meet the Western standard for chastity and womanhood" (p. 192).
- *The boss decides.* Quoted from Shepard: "How well a dancer is treated isn't a consequence of worker status" but of whether the owner is greedy (p. 19; her essay pp. 197–201).
- *Kinship.* Raven: trans and queer people in the trades "forge networks of kinship rooted not in blood relations but in mutual respect, empathy, kindness, and care" (p. 24). Sex workers are "pioneers of mutual aid … we often have only each other" (Intro, p. 11).
- *Trans women's reasons.* Many trans women take up the trade because other work is closed to them (Intro, p. 10). Pariah names the knot: "Sex, gender, money: these three are tangled in knots that I haven't even tried to unravel yet" (p. 157). The rest of her piece concerns her adolescence and is excluded.
- *First colleagues can exploit.* Vermilion's first working partner "became exploitative and was expecting me to pay her a cut", so she went independent (p. 74).

**What the mod takes.** The crafts by name; teaching from elder to newcomer; a persona or working name that is separate from the self; boredom and dry seasons as ordinary; the house's purse and kinship; no ranking of song above bed; no "it's really art, really healing" cover; the head's good or bad character, not the trade, deciding a woman's lot.

**What the mod must not do.**
- No scene of the work (A-P 9).
- No staging of these writers' stories (Pariah's adolescence, the clients in Sarah's and Bickers's pieces).
- No making the trade glamorous or tragic.
- No borrowing the modern idiom: "client", "booking", "whorearchy".

## 4. Principles (complementary to A-P 1–18)

A covered harm-by-power, rescue, mutual aid, sweeps, shares, period idiom and "made for it". These fifteen add craft, Orientalism, the mother's two faces, and the trans-specific frames these three books expose.

1. **Every trade event shows a craft.** Song, drum, talk, poem-capping, hosting, reading a guest, the boats, the house accounts or judging a patron is on the page. The bed is one craft among them, never the hidden truth of the others (N pp. xv–xvi, 10; WI pp. 18, 76–78, 191; G&L Interlude I).
2. **No craft outranks another.** Song does not redeem the bed and the bed does not stain the song. There is no gap in piety, prestige or opinion between a woman who sings only and one who also takes the bed. "Song Is Dharma" covers the house's whole living (WI p. 37; Luna pp. 129–34).
3. **No respectable cover.** The faith does not excuse the bed by calling it healing, art or holiness, just as it does not call it sacred sex (GK §5). The bed is named plainly, rarely and in its place (Luna pp. 133–34; Intro p. 15).
4. **The house teaches its trade.** Craft passes from mother to newcomer like the song line, by example and correction, and the teacher is repaid in care, not in a cut of the newcomer's body (N p. 345; WI pp. 85, 130). Teaching is never initiation into the bed (Principle 12).
5. **Screening is the women's knowledge.** The house keeps word of dangerous guests and refuses them at the landing, and sisters warn each other. Protection is something women do, not something done to them (N p. 424; WI Intro p. 1, the bad-client lists; Vermilion pp. 76–78).
6. **Other trades give the power to refuse.** A woman with song, boat or lodging income can turn away a patron. The house's plural living is her safety, and the design must keep those incomes real (N p. 298; G&L ch. 2, massage businesses).
7. **The mother has two faces, and the house can tell them apart.** The good head charges fairly for board, lends free, protects, and lets a daughter go. The bad head holds by debt, fines and affection used as a leash. Events show both, and the house's remedy is its own (N pp. 223, 237, 273–74; WI pp. 19, 74). This sharpens A-P 3 and 12.
8. **Leaving costs nothing but goodbye.** There is no "compensation" fee on leaving, unlike the hijra gharana fine (N p. 223), and no debt that follows her. Lent robes may be returned. This carries rule 23 into the trade.
9. **No docile singer, no temptress singer.** Singers are never written as soft, yielding or childlike, nor as dangers to men's virtue in the narrator's voice. Both are the one Orientalist pair (G&L ch. 4). Temptation lines belong to a named preacher (A §4a).
10. **No culture-blame.** No text says Japanese families, Buddhism or "the custom of the land" sold or doomed these women. Hardship comes from named persons and from conditions such as floods, war, a patron's fall or an official's seizure (G&L ch. 4, ch. 6 "The New Perpetrator, Victim, and Rescuer").
11. **No exotic garnish.** Trade events get no sensory decoration of bodies, silk, perfume, moonlit water or blossoms for the reader. Work detail fills the frame: oars, the drum skin, the rice measure, the gift tally, the seal (G&L ch. 5, "Sensationalist Imagery"; WI p. 37 on voyeurism).
12. **Womanhood is not the trade.** No client's desire confirms her, no earnings fund her "becoming", and no first night marks her entry. The gate never touches the trade (GK §5), and no event treats a first patron as a milestone (N pp. xii, 5, 36, 331).
13. **Trans daughters are neither the victims nor the menace.** No hostile mechanic or official treats daughters as the dangerous ones of a house while cis singers are the pitiable ones. No patron "discovers" a daughter (rule 5; N p. 424; G&L ch. 3).
14. **Old age is the house's charge.** Old singers teach, judge the gate, keep the death-days and draw on the purse. No youth-and-beauty economy is built into income (N pp. 6, 226–28, 239; G&L ch. 1, "They Are Older, Sick, and Disabled People").
15. **Boredom and dry seasons are ordinary.** Not every trade event is danger or triumph. A slow season on the river, a patron who never pays and a tedious guest are part of the texture, with no despair (rule 17) and no glamour (WI pp. 9–10, 31–33).

## 5. Critiques and suggestions for the song-houses faith (JD; GK §§5–6)

A §5 already covers the per-worker share income, the `lustful` question, the trade as a flag, the letter of leave, the debt event, the preacher, the sweep, the purse, vouching and the "made for it" audit. Those points are agreed and not repeated here. The points below are new.

### 5a. Orientalism

1. **Michichika's foxes: confine them or drop them.** JD §7 gives "like foxes from some old grave mound that take the forms of women" (G 129) to Muro events, and JD decision 2(b) makes the same courtier the "uncanny outsider". This needs care on two counts:
   - It is the most exportable Japanese temptress image (the shape-shifting fox-woman), so it feeds Principle 9 even in a hostile voice.
   - Its logic, a thing that only "takes the forms of women", is exactly the trans deceiver line (rule 3, exposing shape). Aimed near a daughter it becomes the "not really a woman" slur, wearing period dress.
   - *Suggest:* keep it in notes; or use it once, in a Muro event about the whole port's women, never near a daughter, with no fox imagery, and answered materially. Do not make Michichika the decision-2 outsider. If an uncanny voice is wanted, give it to a generic Tendai chaplain whose line is about the drum-diviner's sight, not her body.
2. **"Asobi" untranslated, and glossed.** The word means play. Glosses like "play-women", "pleasure women" or "women of pleasure" exoticise. *Suggest:* loc uses "asobi" sparingly, glossed once as "the singers of the river houses", and otherwise "singers", "women of the house" and "the house". Add "pleasure boat", "floating world", "flower-and-willow" and "geisha" to GK §6's banned list: the first is a Western phrase and the rest are Edo.
3. **The boats are work boats.** Goodwin's singers rowed out to travellers' ships. Write oars, mooring, hailing and the risk of the river, never a drifting tableau.
4. **The capital's gaze is not the narrator's gaze.** JD rule 1 has this; extend it to beauty. The narrator never praises a singer's face or body. Praise belongs to a patron's or diarist's line and is framed as his taste (G&L ch. 2: the right "to define their own sexuality").
5. **No "families sold daughters", even for adults.** GK excludes the bought girls (G 34–35) as minors. Also exclude any adult version ("her father pledged her to the house"). It is the trafficking story in Heian dress (G&L ch. 4). Adult debt stays as A §5.6 frames it: a debt she owes, with remedies.
6. **The emperor's summons is a commission, not a romance.** JD e12 (Summoned to Sing) and the start hook JD e8 must not read as the emperor's favourite. Show the style quarrel, the judging and the brocade as payment for craft, and no private audience. This keeps the "imperial courtesan" fantasy out.

### 5b. Skill and craft (the gap in JD)

7. **The craft is not yet in the mechanics.** JD §3g lists "Diplomacy, stewardship"; add learning for the song line. *Suggest:* a house-craft track as modifiers or an education-style trait, "Taught in the House" (tiers: learning the line, holding the drum, may teach), raised by teaching events and lifestyle XP. House income events should read diplomacy, stewardship and this track. **Never** read `beauty_*`, age or `lustful`. That keeps the youth-and-beauty economy out (Principle 14; N pp. 6, 239).
8. **New event, "The Elder's Lesson"** (invented; WI pp. 85, 130; N p. 345). Trigger: a mother of the house and a newcomer, adult, of the faith. The mother teaches one craft of hosting: how to make a guest ask for the song you meant to sing, how to turn a man away without shaming him, how to read a party's mood from the landing. Options give diplomacy or learning XP and raise the craft track, for teacher and pupil alike. It never touches the bed, and the line "the house does not teach the bed; a woman learns her own" may stand in the mother's voice. Ends at the landing.
9. **New event, "A Guest the Sisters Warned Of"** (invented; Principle 5; N p. 424). A patron known to have struck a sister at another port arrives. Options:
   - refuse him at the landing (prestige risk);
   - receive him for song only, with two sisters present;
   - send word to his proprietor.
   No option sends a woman alone to him. The harm is named as his. Ends at the landing.
10. **Make the persona separate from the self.** Asobi used house names, and Manon was told to pick a stage name close to her own (WI p. 85). The gate's "a name" (JD §4.4) is her name as a woman, and must never double as a stage name, or her womanhood reads as a costume (rule 16; JD's Rokuhara guise logic). *Suggest:* a working name, if one is used at all, is a separate, optional event flavour that any woman of the house may take. The gate name is never called a "house name".
11. **Monotony gets one event.** "A Slow Season on the River" (invented; WI pp. 9–10, 31–33): floods or a patron's fall empty the landing. Options: teach, mend the boats, draw on the purse, travel to Muro. Small stress, and no despair line (rule 17). Pairs with JD e16.

### 5c. The mother's office

12. **Give the good head her texture, not only the bad one her trial.** JD e2 covers abuse of the seal. Add three gestures for the good head, usable in JD e3 (Dividing the Gifts) and in A's debt event:
    - lend robes or a drum free of interest;
    - take a fair, fixed board for lodging;
    - write leave for a woman who wants to go to another house, at no fee.
    Each gives an opinion bonus from members. Their opposites (interest, rising board, a leaving fee) feed A's unseating petition. Sources: Wolfe's just *cafetina* (N pp. 273–74); the hijra fine as the counter-model (N p. 223); Vermilion's exploitative first colleague (WI p. 74).
13. **Kim's protective head is a claim, not a fact.** JD §2 notes Kim's "protected group members from undue exploitation" (*sec.*, "believed"). Wolfe's *madrinha* shows the same protective bond turning into a leash (N p. 274). Loc must not state the head's goodness as the house's nature. Each head is good or bad by her own traits and choices.

### 5d. Trans daughters

14. **The starting set-up quietly decides who works.** JD §5 seeds a cis *chōja*, an *uketsugi*, a Marked trainee and "one singer who does not work the trade". *Suggest:* state in the design which seeded women keep the bed. At least one cis woman should, and the *uketsugi* should not by default. Otherwise the first thing players see repeats the trope that the trans woman is the one in the bed (A-P 15; Hwahng's "rite of passage", N p. 5).
15. **No affirmation-by-patron line anywhere.** This includes a daughter's own voice in a choice option. A single "his eyes told me I was a woman" option would become the faith's implied answer (Principle 12). The house's recognition is the only recognition the faith's text speaks of (JD §4, Mitsuhashi framing).
16. **"Honest work": keep the thought, period the words.** A §5.4 calls "honest work" a modern slogan. Note that a trans insider uses exactly these words (Vermilion, WI p. 74), so the idea is sound and insider-backed. The game text should still be a mother's, for example: *"What the river brings, the house earns. No sister is lesser for her share of it."*

### 5e. Rescue and power, in 1178 forms

17. **The rescuer gains something.** In JD e4 (Horses and Cloth) and A §5.8, give the seizing official a visible gain: the horses, a stretch of landing for his own boats, or credit with his proprietor. Show it beside his pious pretext. That is the G&L pattern of rescue as land-clearing and seizure (Intro, the Seattle raids), in period dress. *Invented*; the 1114 suit (G 136) is the only grounding, so keep the pretext generic.
18. **"Retire" never shows as the good ending.** In JD e6 the woman who takes vows is honoured (good). Make sure the event's result screen and AI weights do not favour that option or give it the largest modifier. Equal value is the rule (A-P 4).
