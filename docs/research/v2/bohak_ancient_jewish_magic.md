## 1. Bibliographic line and verdict

**Bibliographic line.** Gideon Bohak, *Ancient Jewish Magic: A History*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2008; first paperback edition 2011. ISBN 978-0-521-87457-1 (hb), 978-0-521-18098-6 (pb). Contents: Introduction (1–7); 1. Jewish magic: a contradiction in terms? (8–69); 2. Second Temple period (70–142); 3. Late antiquity: the "insider" evidence (143–226); 4. Non-Jewish elements (227–290); 5. How "Jewish" was ancient Jewish magic? (291–350); 6. Magic and magicians in rabbinic literature (351–425); Epilogue (426–434).

**Verdict: strong support for the proposed hard limits. Almost no usable content. Three narrow ideas for the sect's own voice and the physician's.**

- The book covers c. 300 BCE to c. 650 CE, the "western branch" (Palestine, Egypt). It reaches the Middle Ages only through the Cairo Genizah (copied from the 9th century on), a 15th-century Byzantine manuscript, Hai Gaon and Maimonides. **It says nothing about Provence**, Arles, the Tibbonids or 12th–14th-century France.
- **Nothing on sex change or gender crossing.** A full-text search found no sex change, androgynos, eunuch or turning material. Gender appears only as "women and magic", and nearly all of that is a do-not-use list.
- The book is the scholarly case **for** Jewish magic as a real, long tradition. Its rabbis permit amulets, healing charms, the study of magic and some magic outright. The mod cannot follow it there. Shown to a mostly non-Jewish audience, a Jewish sect doing any of this would land on the stereotype that Bohak himself calls a pillar of medieval antisemitism (p. 6). The mod keeps magic out **as a design limit, not a historical claim**. The narrator must not say the opposite either ("Jews had no magic"), which Bohak calls apologetics (pp. 6, 8–10).

## 2. The argument (with pages)

**"Jewish magic" is not a contradiction.** Scholars long denied that it existed, for five reasons: the Torah forbids it; it is irrational; it is un-monotheistic; "magic" is only a label for the other; or all Judaism is magic (p. 10). Bohak rejects all five (pp. 67–69). The Genizah's founding scholars simply skipped its many magical fragments (p. 8).

**What the Torah forbids.** Deuteronomy 18 forbids consulting kinds of practitioner, mostly foreign ones, rather than defined practices. It promises Israel's own prophets as substitutes: "the prohibitions here are not so much on certain practices as on certain practitioners" (p. 14). The terms are vague and the list is incomplete. The "whisper" (*laḥash*) is never forbidden (p. 17). The Bible also offers models: the man of God (Elijah, Elisha) and the power of sacred objects and priests (pp. 19–31). In short, "what was forbidden was the consulting of foreign experts" (p. 428).

**Gender in the biblical model.** No biblical woman revives the dead or heals: "From its very beginning, Jewish thaumaturgy was a deeply engendered affair" (p. 24).

**The Second Temple period.** The sources are thin. Jews hardly used "magic" as a slur against each other, unlike later Karaites against Rabbanites, opponents of Kabbalah, or opponents of Hasidism (pp. 75–76). The Gospels never accuse Jewish opponents of magic, which became "a constant anti-Jewish accusation in later Christian polemics" (p. 76). Medicine was debated there in a way magic was not. Ben Sira defends the physician, so that if doctors heal, "God wants them to and approves of their craft" (pp. 76–77). The Book of the Watchers already presents magic as taught by fallen angels to women, and so as "mostly a female affair" (p. 81).

**Late antiquity.** Jewish magic became scribal: amulets on metal, Babylonian bowls, recipe books. Some of it was borrowed from Greco-Egyptian magic and then made Jewish (chs. 3–5; summary pp. 426–429). Sepher ha-Razim is a learned Hebrew book of angel adjurations with Greek-style rites, such as a lion cub slaughtered with a bronze knife and its blood mixed with wine (p. 171). It is pre-Islamic, of uncertain date and place, and was later translated into Arabic and Latin (pp. 170–175).

**The rabbis: a discourse of licit and illicit magic** (ch. 6).
- The Mishnah stones the *mekhashef* only for a real act: "He who performs a deed is liable, but he who creates an optical illusion is exempt" (m San 6.7, 11; p. 358).
- **Exceptions.**
  - *Study*: "to do you may not learn, but you may learn to understand and to teach" (b. San 68a; p. 359); judges must know magic (p. 360).
  - *The laws of Creation*: Rava's man, Ḥanina and Oshaia's calf, Abaye's three grades (pp. 362–364).
  - *Medicine*: "Anything which heals is not of 'the Ways of the Amorites'" (p. 364), backed by the rule that saving life overrides the Sabbath and Yom Kippur. This was "the widest door" for magic into rabbinic Judaism (p. 365).
  - Unstated but implied: magic against magicians, and magic as social control (pp. 365–369).
- **Amulets were not "magic" to the rabbis.** They "saw amulets as a standard constituent of a person's daily garments" (p. 370). The only question was carrying them on the Sabbath. An amulet that had healed three times was "approved". The rabbis deferred to physicians: "A physician may be trusted if he says, this amulet is approved – I healed with it once and twice and three times" (y. Shab 6.2; p. 371). "The question … is not a religious question but a medical one" (p. 371).
- The Hebrew **Oath of Asaph the Physician** (dated anywhere from the 5th to the 9th century) "explicitly commands physicians to avoid all magical practices (including, we may note, erotic magic!)" (p. 372). Still, "medicine and magic often went hand in hand" (p. 372).
- **Contested practices that the rabbis did not call magic.**
  - Biblical verses as charms, such as Ex 15.26 whispered over a wound (Akiva) and Ps 91 (pp. 378–381).
  - Sacred objects used for protection: a Torah scroll or tefillin on a child (p. 381).
  - Adjuring angels. Rabbi Yoḥanan says the angels do not know Aramaic, and one should cry to God, not to an angel (p. 382).
  - "The Ways of the Amorites": a bag of forbidden gentile customs. Anything that heals is outside it (pp. 382–385).
- **Summary.** The rabbis' stance was "to master some magical techniques, denounce others, and ignore all the rest" (p. 423). "The rabbis developed a discourse of licit and illicit magic" (p. 385).
- **Accusations.** The rabbis accused only two groups: women, "that 'internal other' in the rabbis' male-only religious and cultural elite" (p. 392), and *minim*, Bible-reading heretics, whose books are "books of *qosmin*" (p. 398). They did not accuse Samaritans or pagans (pp. 392–393). Bohak treats "Most women engage in sorcery" as a misogynist dictum. He notes "magic was practiced by men and women alike" and that the "female disease" claim "should not be taken at face value" (p. 393).
- **The road to the stereotype.** Codified rabbinic literature shaped medieval Jewish magic, and "coupled with the Christian discourse of magic as heretical and diabolical, it gave rise to the stereotype of the Jewish magician, the Devil's own henchman" (p. 386, citing Trachtenberg 1943).

**Epilogue.** Within Judaism, "magic" was "only rarely used as a polemical label affixed to the religious practices of one's opponents" (pp. 428–429). Under a belief in providence, "afflictions and misfortunes will lead not to a quest for who is the witch, but for a search for one's sin" (p. 429). The rabbis "developed a discourse of 'licit magic'" (p. 429). Magic is neither universal nor timeless. Parallels and late evidence must be used with great care (pp. 430–433).

## 3. For Ha-Nehpakhot

Project limits applied: Jewish characters are never the wrongdoers; persecution of Jews is never a mechanic; no antisemitic trope in any voice beyond what the hard limits allow; the Turning is never a spell or a cure; the §13 hard limits; rules 3, 9, 10, 25, 26. Every recommendation below is a **proposal**. The default is the conservative one.

### 3.1 What must stay out, and why

**Does Bohak support Stratton's proposed limits? Yes, strongly, and from the Jewish-studies side.**
- He calls "the medieval stereotype of Jews as magicians, the Devil's own henchmen, as a central pillar of some of the most virulent forms of medieval anti-semitism" (p. 6). He names it a constant accusation in Christian polemic (p. 76).
- He names its medieval forms: Jews were "later accused of trying to bewitch Muhammad, or medieval bishops, or the pope" (p. 76, n. 8, citing Trachtenberg 1939 and 1943 and Cohn 1970).
- He traces the stereotype to the meeting of rabbinic magic texts with Christian diabolism (p. 386). Any scene where Jewish characters do anything magic-like, even licit by rabbinic standards, rebuilds that meeting on screen.
- He notes that Christian writers "often insisted that it was better to die than be healed by a Jew" (p. 399 n. 150, citing Wilken). That is the root of the physician-as-sorcerer charge that `jud_shatzmiller_medicine.md` documents for the Middle Ages (sorcery at Briançon 1433; poisoning).

**Proposed hard limits.** These adopt Stratton's §6.3 and sharpen it.
1. **No "Jewish magic" in any voice**: narrator, Christian, Muslim, Jewish or the sect's own. No Christian or Muslim voice calls Jews, the sect, its elders, its *fisica* or its translator a sorcerer, magician, witch or poisoner. The charge does not appear even as a rumour the narrator denies. (Bohak pp. 6, 76, 386.)
2. **No Jewish voice charges the sect with *kishuf***. That includes calling its members *minim* or their books "books of *qosmin*". This is the most historically plausible rabbinic charge against a dissenting sect of women (pp. 392, 398), which is exactly why it must stay out. Jewish opposition stays **law and ruling** (the baylons, *niddui*, a lord's licence), as Stratton proposed. Bohak's p. 429 supports this: Jews looked for sin, not for a witch.
3. **The sect, its daughters and its physician never write, wear, approve or sell amulets.** They use no divine Names, adjure no angels, whisper no verse as a charm (Ex 15.26, Ps 91, Zech 3.2) and use no Torah scroll or tefillin for protection. This holds even though the rabbis allowed much of it (pp. 370–382). A mezuzah, if it ever appears, is a commandment and never protection (compare p. 368).
4. **None of these appear in loc, in any voice:**
   - **Esther's menstrual stains** and the fear that they would be used for *keshaphim* (Esth. R. 6.8; p. 390). Esther is the sect's scroll, and the Three Lamps already have "no blood".
   - **Leah's mandrakes** (*dudaʾim*, Gen 30.14–16), which the rabbis tied to the mandrake used for erotic magic (p. 381). Leah's prayer for Dinah is the sect's core aggada. It must stay prayer only: never mandrakes, never a potion. This matches the hard limit "no knife and no potion".
   - **The golem and creation by the "laws of Creation"** (Rava's man, the calf; pp. 362–364). Any text saying a person *makes* a body would recast the Turning as the rabbis' "magic of creation". Heaven turns; nobody makes.
   - **Fertility and pregnancy amulets**: against barrenness, the "preserving stone" against miscarriage (pp. 373–374), and the Jericho spring (p. 42). Rule 20 ties no motherhood to the womb, and these would tie the sect's "turning" to womb-magic.
   - **"Binding" a bridegroom** (impotence magic: the witch of Rome, p. 396; Geonic responsum, p. 219 n. 208). It is a cousin of rule 25's "stolen member" ban.
   - **Sotah water** (pp. 28–29). It is an ordeal on a woman's body, and it sits close to rule 26.
   - **Sepher ha-Razim** and the *Sword of Moses* (pp. 170–176): lion-cub blood, adjurations, love rites. Out entirely, since they break the "no blood" and "no beast" limits and present Jewish texts as grimoires.
   - **The hyena-skin cure for rabies** (b. Yoma 84a; p. 377). This falls under the hard limit "no hyena or other animal imagery".
   - **The rabbis' witch and *min* stories** (pp. 393–401). Stratton's list already covers Shimeon ben Shetaḥ and Yannai's donkey. Add: R. Yehoshua and the witch of Rome; the Tiberias bath-house duel; R. Ḥanina bar Papi; "Most women engage in sorcery"; women returning from a house of mourning (p. 367); Rav's curse on a man who sowed **on Purim** (b. Meg 5b; p. 403 n. 159). Every one makes a Jewish character the wrongdoer or a woman the witch.
   - **"The curse of a sage"** and rabbis killing with a look (pp. 402–403). Rabbis must not be shown wielding harmful power, against the sect least of all.
5. **No Jesus-as-magician material** (Toledoth Yeshu; b. San 43a, 107b; pp. 76, 364, 399), and no healing "in the name of Jesus" refused (p. 399). This is two-way polemic, and Christian lords protect the sect.

### 3.2 What the sect's own voice could use (conservative)

None of this is needed. Each item is optional and awaits the user.

1. **"A prayer, not a deed."** The Mishnah makes only a real *deed* capital (p. 358). Stratton suggested one line in "Before the Baylons". Bohak gives the same idea in the rabbis' own legal terms. Suggested line, in the elder's voice: *"We do no deed of the hand. We ask, and the Holy One answers in His time."* It names no magic, so it does not raise the subject. It also fits the patience mechanic.
2. **Ask God, not His servants.** The rabbis distrusted adjuring angels and told the sick to cry to God directly (p. 382). The Turning's prayer is addressed only to God, never to an angel or by a Name. That is a design rule rather than text. **Kalonymus's list of God's turnings (Abraham from the furnace, Dinah, the staff, the sea) should be framed as a litany of praise.** Bohak shows that magicians used biblical scenes, the fiery furnace among them, as *historiolae* (p. 421). The narrator should therefore never call the list a "formula", "charm" or "spell". *Comparandum to verify, not in Bohak:* the penitential litany "He who answered Abraham … may He answer us" (*Mi she-ʿana*) is the liturgical form the list most resembles.
3. **The physician's medicine is medicine.**
   - The rabbis treated healing questions as "not a religious question but a medical one" and deferred to physicians (p. 371).
   - Josephus, who believed in demons, had "some physicians, and not an exorcist" treat his broken wrist (p. 44).
   - The Oath of Asaph forbids physicians magic (p. 372).

   Proposed: the *fisica* event (`tm_hipukh.0031`) and any later physician text describe only book medicine (diet, regimen, drugs, the learned authorities Shatzmiller names). It never shows her approving or writing an amulet, though Bohak shows physicians did (p. 371). Ben Sira 38 ("Honour the physician") is the obvious proof text (p. 77). Because it is in the Latin Bible as Ecclesiasticus, it could be quoted by a Christian lord who licenses her. *To verify, not in Bohak:* the Vulgate wording, and that a 13th–14th-century Provençal lord or clerk would cite it. Do **not** give it to the sect: the medieval Hebrew *Alphabet of Ben Sira* is a different book, and it carries Lilith.
4. **"We look for sin, not for a witch"** (p. 429). Optional design principle: when misfortune strikes the street, Jewish voices never blame a daughter's Turning or anyone's magic. An elder may say the street searches its own deeds. This is more a guardrail than a line.
5. **Prayer for the sick is licit.** "Whoever has a sick person in his home should go to a sage and ask for mercy" (p. 403). An elder or daughter may pray for a patron's health, as rabbis were said to pray for Antoninus (p. 403). Optional flavour for the translator or physician at a Christian court. It is prayer only, never a charm.

**Do not use** the rule "anything which heals" for the Turning (p. 364). That agrees with Stratton: it would make womanhood an illness and the Turning its cure.

### 3.3 The Provençal setting: neutral background

- **Provence directly: nothing.** The book's medieval reach is Cairo (Genizah, 9th century on), Hai Gaon writing to Kairouan, MS Sassoon 56 (Greek hand, 1465–68; p. 223), Ashkenaz through the Hekhalot manuscripts, and Maimonides.
- **Continuity in general: yes.** Genizah recipe books copy late-antique Palestinian recipes "copied, translated, re-edited, and 'updated'" (p. 221). Amulets were ordinary wear for rabbinic Jews, children and even cattle (pp. 370, 374). Medieval rabbis wrote amulets (p. 375 n. 73). So a 12th–14th-century Jewish street would plausibly have had amulets in daily life. That is **background knowledge for writers, not content**: by §3.1 no amulet is shown.
- **The Maimonidean stance fits the sect's milieu.** Maimonides attacked "the madness of amulet writers" (p. 37) and condemned angel names on mezuzot (p. 67 n. 140). He still accepted an amulet "tested" on three patients (p. 41), and an incantation over a scorpion bite for its effect on the patient's mind (p. 42). The sect's learned translator and physician family belongs to the Lunel and Montpellier world that read Maimonides, which the faith page already calls "the translators' country". A rationalist physician who uses no charms is therefore historically natural for them, not an anachronism. *Context to verify, not in Bohak:* the Tibbonid translation of the *Guide* at Lunel, and the Montpellier dispute over physicians' astrological talismans in the Rashba's responsa. If the talisman dispute is ever confirmed, **keep it out** for the same reason as §3.1.3.
- **Do not write sect text as an anti-amulet polemic.** That would turn the sect against other Jews and is a short step from making Jewish characters wrongdoers. At most, the sect's self-description is that it "keeps no charm".
- **An error not to copy.** Bohak's p. 224 calls Kairouan "ancient Cyrene". Kairouan is in Ifriqiya (Tunisia); Cyrene is in Libya. Relevant if Hai Gaon's responsum ever reaches the Maghreb content (`docs/wave3/ha-nehpakhot-maghreb.md`). It should not: it is a responsum about magical Names.

## 4. Other faiths

**Adelphai Dorleyaiou (Byzantine).** Late-antique Christian–Jewish magic polemic. Keep all of it out, in every voice:
- John Chrysostom, *Against the Jews*: Christians going to the synagogue at Daphne for incubation near a pit called "Matrona" (p. 321), and Christians buying amulets from Jewish practitioners (pp. 315, 423).
- Justin on Jewish exorcists (p. 90).
- Origen's disdain for pagans who used Jewish divine names (pp. 200, 389).
- Christian sayings that it is better to die than be healed by a Jew (p. 399 n. 150).

No Orthodox hostile voice may call the Sisters "Judaizers" who seek charms in the synagogue. That would bring the Jewish-magician stereotype into a faith with no Jews in it. Coincidence to avoid: do not tie the Adelphai or the name list's *Matrona* (`faith_design.md` §18) to the Daphne pit. Stratton's demon register for Orthodox hostility (§6.6) is unaffected. Bohak adds that the Christian discourse after Justin tied magic to Satan, and that this fed the anti-Jewish stereotype (pp. 81, 386). Hostility against the Sisters must therefore never borrow anti-Jewish forms.

**The Good Ladies (v2).**
- Bohak contrasts the rabbis' witch stories with the Roman and inquisitorial ones: "No child-sacrifice here, no dancing around a satanic figure …, no mad orgies, no flights of fancy" (p. 395). This confirms that rule 25's banned items are a later Christian and Roman package.
- His claim that magic is local, not universal (pp. 430–432), supports the rule of no continuity from antiquity.
- Never cross Jewish women's food and "magic" texts (bread by the road, p. 362 n. 23; two women at a crossroads, p. 366 n. 39; the witches' feast of Ashkelon, p. 394) with the Ladies' laid table, as Stratton §5(a) already says.

**All faiths.** Bohak's emic/etic caution (pp. 3–4) supports Stratton guardrail 7: no narrator label of "magic" or "sorcery" for any faith's rites.

## 5. Guardrails (proposed additions)

1. Adopt Stratton's §6.3 hard-limit candidates for §13, with Bohak (pp. 6, 76, 386) as a second source. Extend them so that **no Jewish voice charges the sect with *kishuf*** or calls its members *minim*.
2. The sect, its daughters, its physician and its translator use **no amulet, divine Name, angel, charm-verse or sacred object for protection or healing**, in text or mechanic.
3. Never in loc: Esther's stains, Leah's mandrakes, golem or "creation", fertility amulets, binding, sotah, Sepher ha-Razim, the hyena cure, the rabbis' witch, *min* and curse stories (§3.1.4).
4. Kalonymus's turnings list is a **litany**, never a formula. The Turning's prayer goes to God alone.
5. Physician text is **medicine only**. Her licensing never turns on a cure that "worked like a charm".
6. Do not state either that Jews practised magic or that they had none.
7. No Christian–Jewish magic polemic in Adelphai text (Chrysostom, Justin, Origen, Toledoth Yeshu).
