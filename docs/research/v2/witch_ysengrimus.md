# Witches for v2: the *Ysengrimus* and the Lady Pharaildis (Ghent, c. 1148)

Scope: a close reading of the Herodias/Pharaildis passage in the *Ysengrimus* and of what two modern editions say about it, as a follow-up to `witch_hutton_lecouteux.md` (which cites "*Ysengrimus* II.71–94") and `witch_papers_and_timeline.md`. The question is the same as in those notes: what can a CK3 "Good Ladies" faith rest on, inside 867–1453? Only the passage, its frame and a few related passages were read, not the whole poem.

Pronouns: I do not know the pronouns of the editors (Jill Mann, Michael Schilling) or of other scholars named here, so they are referred to by surname or as *they*.

Quote rule: every verbatim quote is 25 words or fewer and carries a line or page cite. Everything else is paraphrase. Line numbers are the same in both editions (both follow Ernst Voigt's 1884 numbering). Mann's Latin keeps medieval spelling (*uirgo*, *meste*, *here*). Schilling's Latin is classicised (*virgo*, *maestae*, *herae*). Latin below uses Schilling's spelling unless noted.

How the texts were read: Mann's PDF is a Google scan without a text layer, so it was OCR'd with Apple Vision. Key Latin was checked against the page image (Mann p. 266). Schilling's PDF has a text layer. Page numbers are the printed ones.

## 1. Editions

**A. Mann.** Jill Mann, ed. and trans., *Ysengrimus: Text with Translation, Commentary and Introduction*. Mittellateinische Studien und Texte 12. Leiden: E. J. Brill, 1987. ISBN 90-04-08103-8. Latin facing English prose, with a long introduction (section 10, "St Pharaildis," pp. 89–97) and a line commentary.

**B. Schilling (Tusculum).** Michael Schilling, ed. and trans., *Ysengrimus. Lateinisch–deutsch. Mit einer Einführung und Erläuterungen*. Sammlung Tusculum. Berlin/Boston: De Gruyter, 2020. ISBN 978-3-11-066315-0; e-ISBN (PDF) 978-3-11-066346-4. The Latin is Voigt's text with classical spelling. The German translation is new, made with reference to Schönfelder, Mann and Nieuwenhuis (p. 33–34). Introduction pp. 9–34; notes ("Erläuterungen") pp. 483–508.

**Author, date, setting (both editions agree on the essentials).**
- **Author.** Anonymous. "Magister Nivardus" rests on a single late manuscript heading, a florilegium of the late 13th or early 14th century (Mann p. 157; Schilling p. 9). Schilling says flatly that the author is unknown (p. 9). Mann reviews the candidates (Nivard, Balduin the Blind, Simon of Saint-Bertin, Bruno) and endorses none (pp. 156–70). Both agree on a learned cleric. Schilling says monastic (p. 10). Mann keeps three Ghent possibilities open: a canon of St Pharaildis (Voigt's and van Mierlo's guess), St Peter's (Blandinium) or St Bavo's (pp. 92, 168–69).
- **Date.** Spring 1148 to August 1149. The poem mentions the disasters of the Second Crusade, including the Thracian flood of 7 September 1147, news of which reached the West in spring 1148. Its satire of Anselm of Tournai needs Anselm alive and in office, and he was bishop from 1146 until his death on 24 August 1149 (Mann pp. 160–61, "spring 1148–August 1149"; Schilling pp. 10–11). "c. 1148" in the prior notes is fine.
- **Ghent.** St Peter's (Blandinium, V 447), St Bavo (III 717), the Scheldt (IV 592, V 551), the diocese of Tournai and Reims, and Pharaildis (II 71) all place the poem in Ghent. Schilling calls Pharaildis's cult limited "auf Gent und Umgebung" (to Ghent and its surroundings, p. 10) and uses it as evidence for where the poem was written.

## 2. The passage: II 59–94 (Pharaildis story proper II 71–94)

**Frame and speaker.** This is the end of the wolf-fishing episode. Ysengrimus, the wolf, who stands for a monk-bishop, has his tail frozen into the ice, and the villagers have beaten him. **Aldrada**, an old peasant woman with an axe, means to finish him (II 1–58). Before striking she prays to a litany of saints "Quae plebeius habet nomina nota canon" ("in the popular canon," II 60). The Pharaildis story is **not Aldrada's direct speech**. Her speech ends at II 34. Lines 59–94 are the **narrator's** report of her litany, and the narrator expands its last item into a mock *vita* (saint's life). Mann's summary uses "we are told" (p. 92). The prior note's "II.71–94" is correct. Mann cites the story itself as II 73–94 (p. 93).

**The litany (II 61–72).** It names Osanna and his wife Excelsis, Anna, Alleluia (married to Peter, mother of Michael), Helpvara, Noburgis, Brigid, Celebrant and Pharaildis. Most of these are liturgical words that the unlettered woman has turned into saints. Osanna and Excelsis come from *Osanna in excelsis*, with *in* heard as Dutch *en* ("and"). Celebrant comes from the end of the Preface (*concelebrant*). (Mann's commentary, pp. 264–66, quoting Voigt; Schilling pp. 487–88.) Pharaildis is the one authentic local saint in the list, and she is given a false *vita*.

**The story (paraphrase, with Mann's translation quoted, p. 267).**
- II 71–72: Pharaildis is "the virgin Pharaildis, given over to unjust suffering—but the saints do whatever they want."
- II 73–76: She is **Herod's daughter**, so the poem merges Herodias with Salome. She loved John the Baptist: "This girl, yearning for the bed of the Baptist alone, had vowed to be no man's, if he were denied her." Latin: *nullius esse viri* (II 76).
- II 77–80: Her father, angered at the love, beheads John. Grieving, she asks for the head, and a royal servant brings it on a dish.
- II 81–84: She clasps it, weeps and tries to kiss it: "As she strained after its kisses, the head backed away and blew at her, and she disappeared through the skylight in the eddy of breath." Latin: *caput aufugit atque resufflat* (II 83); *per impluvium turbine flantis abit* (II 84).
- II 85–88: Ever since, John's anger drives her "through the empty heavens." The Latin is *Per vacuum caeli flabilis urget iter* (II 86, *flabilis* confirmed on Mann's page image), which Mann renders "hissing anger." The dead John persecutes her though he never loved her alive. Yet the fates do not let her perish entirely.
- II 89–90: "honour softens her grief, and reverence lessens her punishment. A third portion of mankind serves this melancholy lady." Latin: *Lenit honor luctum, minuit reverentia poenam, / Pars hominum maestae tertia servit herae.*
- II 91–92: "from the second part of the night until the black cock sings Prime, she perches on oaks and hazel-trees." Latin: *Quercubus et corilis a noctis parte secunda / Usque nigri ad galli carmina prima sedet.*
- II 93–94: "Now her name is Pharaildis, whereas before it was Herodias, a dancer neither preceded nor followed by anyone equal to her." Latin: *Nunc ea nomen habet Pharaildis, Herodias ante*; *Saltria* ("dancer").
- II 95–102 return to farce. Aldrada gabbles garbled prayers (*Pater nuster*, *Credinde*, *Miserele*, *Deugracis*). Her prayers fail, and her axe cuts off the wolf's tail instead of his head.

**Latin key terms.**
- ***hera*, not *domina*.** The Lady is *maestae ... herae*, "the mourning mistress" (II 90). *Hera* is the mistress of a household, the woman servants obey. The poem uses the same word for a dairymaid's mistress (VII 326). *Domina* does not occur in this passage.
- ***pars hominum tertia*** (II 90): "a third part of humans." The phrase does not say whether these are men or women.
- ***servit*** (present tense): the third *serves* her now. *honor* and *reverentia* (II 89) are what soften her punishment. That service and reverence are the poem's version of a cult.
- ***quercus*, *corilus*** (oak, hazel; II 91). ***sedet*** (II 92) means "sits" or "perches" (Mann), not "rests."
- ***a noctis parte secunda ... ad galli carmina prima*** (II 91–92). Mann takes *carmina prima* as the hour of Prime (note to II 92, p. 267). Schilling has "ersten Gesängen des schwarzen Hahns" (first songs of the black cock, p. 115). The black cock (*niger gallus*) is a detail of its own.
- ***Saltria*** (II 94): "dancer." This points back to Salome's dance.

## 3. What the editors say

### Mann (introduction section 10, pp. 89–97; also pp. 32–33; commentary pp. 262–67)

- **The *vita* is invented.** The three real *vitae* of St Pharaildis (Veerle in Dutch) bear "no resemblance" to this story (p. 93). In them she is an 8th-century virgin of royal birth who refused to consummate her marriage. Her husband beat her for that, and also because her frequent **night absences**, made to pray at a nearby monastery, made him suspicious (p. 93).
- **Real lore behind the motifs.** Mann accepts "the tradition about Herodias' after-life as a witch" (p. 93). The earliest testimonies for it are Rather of Verona and Burchard of Worms, via Grimm (n. 243). The blowing head also has one earlier parallel, **Ælfric's** Old English homily on the Beheading of John the Baptist (late 10th century). In it, "some erring people said" (*Sume gedwolmenn cwædon*) that the head blew at Herodias, so that she travelled with the winds through all the world. Ælfric rejects this because Herodias lived on afterwards (n. 244). The *Legenda Aurea* (13th century, too late to be a source) has her die when the head blows. A more popular legend has her fall through thin ice into a frozen lake (n. 244). Mann suggests the vagueness of Ælfric's and Jacobus's sources points to **oral** tradition, and that the poet "may likewise have picked up the legend from oral tradition" (n. 244).
- **The Pharaildis–Herodias identification is not folk belief.** There "is absolutely no evidence outside of the Ysengrimus itself that Herodias and Pharaildis were ever identified with each other in popular tradition" (pp. 93–94). Mann rejects Grimm's and Voigt's view of a popular confusion. Grimm's link through Middle Dutch *Verelde* ("Frau Hilde") and a Milky Way name ("Vroneldenstraet") is unsupported. Grimm's star reading also rests on an inferior manuscript reading, *sidus* where *fidus* is correct (nn. 245–48). Mann's verdict: "The identification of Pharaildis and Herodias is, in short, a comic invention of the poet himself" (p. 95). Van Mierlo said the same earlier (p. 95). Pharaildis belongs with Osanna and Excelsis, "the progeny of linguistic confusion" (p. 95).
- **What may have prompted it.** St Pharaildis's royal lineage, and a local tradition of her special devotion to John the Baptist (the church at Bruay, dedicated to both; pp. 95–96). Mann finds no evidence for van Mierlo's claim that John and Pharaildis were joint patrons of Ghent (n. 251).
- **Tone.** The joke lies in pinning "one of the most scandalous figures of the New Testament" on a respectable virgin saint (p. 96). It is probably not an attack on St Bavo's or the canons of St Pharaildis. Instead, "the comic irreverence with which Pharaildis is treated shows a familiar affection" (p. 97). Mann adds a speculation: St Bernard was said to have been in the church of St Pharaildis in 1146, and the poet is hostile to Bernard. But that report comes only from a 17th-century source (p. 97, n. 259).
- **The poem's world.** Mann sets the passage in the poem's "body" theme (pp. 32–33): a severed head with its own power, a body that can be blown away. Pharaildis "now lives a witch-like existence, driven by John's hissing anger" (p. 32). The same theme returns with Gerard the goose's hissing, which seems to blow a wolf's head off (IV 297–332), and with the monks' storm of hissing in Book V.
- **Aldrada.** One gloss calls her a priest's wife. Voigt (after Mone) made her the priest's cook. Mann rejects both: "she is simply a peasant-woman" (note to II 3, p. 262). Of the nine saints she invokes, seven are women (Voigt, quoted in Mann's note to II 61–70, p. 264).

### Schilling (Tusculum)

- **Notes** (pp. 487–88). The note to 61ff explains the pseudo-saints as Mass words misunderstood by an Aldrada who knows no Latin. The note to II 71 gives Pharaildis's **goose** attribute (she sheltered starving wild geese in a hard frost) and her relics at St Bavo's. It says "Pharaildis war wie Johannes der Täufer Patronin von Gent" (Pharaildis, like John the Baptist, was a patron of Ghent, p. 488). It cites the 1882 *Actes de Sainte Pharaïlde* and, as the latest discussion of the "Verwechslung" (confusion) with Herodias, Heizmann 1995. It does not rule on whether the confusion was folk belief or the poet's joke.
- **Introduction.** Pharaildis is used only to locate the poem (p. 10). The litany appears under wordplay, as a joke for a Latin-literate clerical audience, "für eine lateinkundige geistliche Zuhörerschaft" (p. 22).

## 4. Evidence value

- **It is satire.** The passage sits in a Latin beast-epic written for clerics. It mocks an old, toothless, unlettered peasant woman whose saints are misheard liturgy. The narrator, not a believer, tells the Herodias story as a mock saint's life.
- **Not evidence for:** any Flemish belief that the Lady was called *Pharaildis*, or that St Veerle was linked to the night company. Mann (pp. 93–95) is decisive here, and Schilling offers no counter-evidence. Grade **C/D** for "Pharaildis" as a name of the Lady.
- **Fair evidence for:** a Ghent cleric c. 1148 knew, and expected his audience to recognise, a Herodias lore cluster: (a) Herodias carried through the air for ever by the Baptist's breath or anger; (b) a third of humankind serving her; (c) a night schedule ending at cockcrow; (d) her perching on trees. (a) has an older parallel in Ælfric (England, late 10th century). (b) matches the Herodias beliefs Mann traces to Rather of Verona and Burchard (n. 243); Rather's "third" is in the prior note. The joke only works if the audience knows the lore. Grade **B** for "Herodias night-lore was current in 12th-century Flanders among clerics." Grade **C** for it being a *lay* belief in Flanders, which is plausible but rests on Mann's oral-tradition inference (n. 244).
- **Oaks and hazels, the black cock and Prime** have no parallel cited by either editor. They may be the poet's own detail.

## 5. Corrections to the prior notes

1. **`witch_hutton_lecouteux.md` timeline row (c. 1148).**
   - The Lady **sits or perches** (*sedet*) on oaks and hazels. She is not said to "rest."
   - The time runs "from the second part of the night" until the black cock's first song, which Mann reads as **Prime**.
   - Add that the poem makes **Herodias Herod's daughter** (merged with Salome), whose unrequited love for John is the cause of everything.
2. **Regions table, "Flanders (Ghent) c. 1148, Pharaildis (= Herodias)."**
   - Relabel this as *Herodias* lore known in Ghent, with "Pharaildis" a **literary joke** (Mann pp. 93–97).
   - Do not list Pharaildis as a regional name of the Lady alongside Holda, Abundia or Oriente.
3. **"Add Flanders and the name Pharaildis"** (change 4 in the Hutton/Lecouteux note). Keep Flanders, at secondary weight, as a place where the lore was known. Drop "the name Pharaildis" as a believers' name.
4. **The third of humanity.** The Latin *pars hominum tertia* has no gender. Lecouteux's musing on who "the other two-thirds" are misreads the line. The other two-thirds are simply people who do not serve her. The line cannot show men as members, and it cannot rule them out.
5. **The title.** The poem's word for the Lady is ***hera*** (mistress of a household), not *domina* (II 90).
6. **Framing.** The passage is the narrator's mock *vita* inside an old woman's garbled litany in a beast-epic. It is not a churchman's condemnation of belief, unlike Regino or Burchard. It belongs in a separate column: *learned parody that presupposes the belief*.
7. **New early parallel.** Ælfric's homily (England, late 10th century) on "erring people" who say Herodias was blown by the Baptist's head to travel with the winds through the world (Mann n. 244, citing Thorpe's edition I, p. 486). This is a pre-1000 **English** trace of Herodias's endless flight, which neither prior note has. The flight here is driven by wind, with no company and no Lady's rule.

## 6. Design implications for the faith

These are suggestions, not decisions. The narrator never claims St Pharaildis or any historical person for the faith.

- **Region.** Use Flanders (Ghent) as a **minor, late-12th-century "lore known here" county**, not a heartland. It fits a 1178 start, which comes 30 years after the poem. Ghent's church of St Pharaildis (Sint-Veerle), the counts' chapel within their castle complex (Mann p. 81; p. 92, n. 240), is good local colour for a clerical event. It should not be a holy site of the faith.
- **The Lady's names.** Keep *Herodias* (the clerical name) and the regional names already in the notes. "Pharaildis" can appear **only in a hostile or comic clerical voice**, for example a Ghent canon's verse that turns the Lady into a local saint, so that the joke is the cleric's. In the faith's own voice, the poem's *hera*, the mistress of the house, suits the household-visiting and table rite (William of Auvergne) better than *domina*.
- **The third of humanity.** This is a strong, sourced doctrinal boast, supported by Rather (10th century) and the *Ysengrimus*: "a third of all people serve her." It can be a tenet or doctrine flavour line, or a clerical complaint about how many people believe. It is not a demographic mechanic. Per the growth-tuning feedback, do not turn it into a size cap or target.
- **The mourning Lady and reverence as consolation.** *Lenit honor luctum, minuit reverentia poenam* (II 89) gives a doctrine: the Lady is sorrowful, and her followers' honour eases her sorrow. That makes a tender register for rituals, with offerings as consolation rather than appeasement. It fits the "Good Ladies" household tables.
- **Trees and the hours.** Oak and hazel perches, from the second part of the night until cockcrow or Prime, with a **black cock** marking the end. These suit a sacred-tree holy-site flavour and a night-ends-at-cockcrow rule for night events. Pair them with Raoul de Presles (fairies in trees) and the Domrémy ladies' tree (1431) from the prior note, but flag that the *Ysengrimus* trees are a satirist's detail.
- **Flight on breath and wind.** Herodias blown through the skylight and driven through the "empty heavens" gives an origin legend: carried on the wind, not on beasts as in Regino. The Ælfric parallel makes it older and wider than Ghent.
- **The satire register as an event voice.** The poem gives a ready model for a **learned mocking cleric**. It laughs at women's "popular canon" (*plebeius canon*, II 60) of misheard saints and gives the Lady a scandalous *vita*. Mann reads the tone as affectionate rather than aggressive (p. 97), so the voice can be droll, not only persecuting. That makes a useful middle register between Regino's "delusion" and the 15th-century "sect." Handle with care: the joke's target is an old, poor, illiterate woman, and the satire is ageist and misogynist. The event should let the player see that.
- **Renaming.** "Now her name is ..., before it was ..." (II 93) is a ready narrative beat. In the source it is a cleric's joke. It could be used, carefully, for a believer's taking of a new name in the company. That is a design choice, not something the source shows.
- **Smaller flavour from elsewhere in the poem.**
  - **Aldrada** (II 1–108): an old woman with an axe "tonsuring" the wolf-bishop, a comic reversal of clerical power.
  - The wolf's dying curse on a dairymaid (VII 325–60): spilled milk, thin butter, scattered pots, vessels "Integra mane, eadem sint vespere fissa" (whole in the morning, broken by evening, VII 359). It is a demon's curse (Agemund), not the Ladies. But it gives household-misfortune imagery that echoes William of Auvergne's neglected house.
  - **Salaura the sow's** lament (VII 485–504): a female speaker performs feminine modesty, "Femina sit reverens" (VII 495), while delivering the poem's sharpest attack on the pope over the Crusade. It is a model for a woman's voice of critique in clerical satire.
  - St Pharaildis's **goose** attribute (Schilling p. 488) and Aldrada's goose Gerard (II 11) are coincidence-level and not worth building on.

## 7. Open questions

1. **Heizmann 1995** (Wilhelm Heizmann, "Die Pharaildis-Überlieferung im mittellateinischen Tierepos *Ysengrimus*," *Jahrbuch der Brüder Grimm-Gesellschaft* 5 (1995): 107–21) is the latest study, cited by Schilling. Does it argue against Mann for a real Flemish folk identification? Not read.
2. Mann's fuller argument is in "*Luditur illusor*: the Cartoon World of the *Ysengrimus*," *Neophilologus* 61 (1977): 495–509 (Mann p. 95, n. 249). Not read.
3. **Rather of Verona's** exact wording on Herodias and the "third part" (*Praeloquia*), so that the *Ysengrimus* "third" can be tied to it directly. Mann cites it only through Grimm.
4. **Ælfric's** homily: verify the passage in a modern edition (Clemoes, *Ælfric's Catholic Homilies: The First Series*, EETS 1997) and get a translation of "travelled with the winds through all the world."
5. Is the **black cock** or the **oak and hazel** pairing attested in any other pre-1453 Ladies source, or is it the poet's own?
6. Mann (n. 244) notes the popular legend of Herodias falling through **ice**. The Pharaildis digression is set in an episode on ice. Did the frozen pond suggest Herodias to the poet? That is my inference, not either editor's.
7. Real St Pharaildis's **night absences** and beatings by a jealous husband (Mann p. 93) are striking next to the Ladies' night journeys. They belong to a Catholic saint's *vita*, though, and should not be borrowed for the faith without a clear reason.
