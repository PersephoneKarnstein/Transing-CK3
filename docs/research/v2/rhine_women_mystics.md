# Epiney-Burgard and Zum Brunn, *Women Mystics in Medieval Europe*: design reading for the Good Ladies' Rhineland and Low Countries

Source: Emilie Zum Brunn and Georgette Epiney-Burgard, *Women Mystics in Medieval Europe*, trans. Sheila Hughes (New York: Paragon House, 1989; third printing). It was first published as *Femmes troubadours de Dieu* (Turnhout: Brepols, 1988). ISBN 0-913729-16-7.

Read from an OCR text extraction of the Internet Archive scan, kept outside the repo. The OCR is messy, but the running heads are legible. Page numbers are the printed ones, taken from those heads. The table of contents' page numbers are off by two in Part I: it gives Hildegard's introduction as p. 5, but it starts on p. 3. The running heads were used throughout.

**What was read.**
- The introduction in full (pp. xiii–xxxiv).
- Part I, Hildegard: introduction and every excerpt (pp. 3–36).
- Part II, Mechthild of Magdeburg: introduction (pp. 39–53).
- Part III, Beatrice of Nazareth: introduction (pp. 71–79).

- Part V, Marguerite Porete: introduction (pp. 143–159).
- The conclusion (pp. 177–179) and the notes to the introduction (pp. 181–183).
- Keyword searches over the whole text for Sapientia, Caritas, Ecclesia, Wisdom, Elisabeth, Cathar, *magistra*, Jews and Lady Love.
- **Not read:** the excerpts from Mechthild, Beatrice, Hadewijch and Porete, beyond keyword hits.

**Relation to existing notes.**
- The beguines appear so far only in passing: `../book_federici_caliban_witch.md` (Vienne 1312), `../hist_karras_medieval_sexuality.md` E65 and E214, `../comparanda_reading_list.md` candidate B, `contesting_orthodoxy.md` (Vadstena 1506), and `survey_1178.md` and the region note ("too late for 1178").
- This is the first reading of beguine authors' lives.
- For Hildegard it adds the 1178 interdict letter in translation (the region note's row F), her preaching tours, and her noble-only house.
- **Scope gap:** despite the brief, the book has almost nothing on Sapientia, Caritas or Ecclesia as feminine divine figures. The feminine figure it does have is Lady Love (*Minne*), from the 13th century. See §1.6 and the leads.

## 0. Framing warning

- **Devotional, 1980s, partly essentialist.**
  - The authors write as admirers ("our Beguines", "our nuns").
  - The conclusion calls for "a return to the essential values symbolized by woman" and praises "woman" as "receptivity" (pp. 177–178).
  - Do not import that frame: rules 2, 15 and 18, and the mod does not cast womanhood as an essence.
- **Heavy secondhand.** Long passages are quoted from Balthasar (Mechthild, pp. 40–51), Porion (pp. xix, xxx) and Guarnieri (Porete, pp. 145–148). Each is marked *secondhand* below.
- **Dates.**
  - Hildegard (1098–1179) and Elisabeth of Schönau are 12th-century Benedictines.
  - Beatrice (1200–1268), Hadewijch (fl. second quarter of the 13th c.) and Mechthild (c. 1207–c. 1294) are 13th-century.
  - Porete was burned in 1310.
  - For a 1066 start, *all* of this is later content: gate Hildegard from c. 1141–47, beguine material from c. 1210, and Porete after 1306.
- **Joan of Arc.** The authors relay an unproven claim that she was called a beguine (p. xxiii). Joan stays unclaimed (`witch_design.md` §10).
- **Rules.**
  - The narrator never claims these women for any faith. A character may read or cite them.
  - Bride mysticism (Mechthild, Hadewijch) is Catholic voice only (rule 26).
  - Their penances and "suffering to grow" are never a gate (rule 22).

## 1. Findings

### 1.1 The link the authors draw, and its limits (pp. xiii–xvi, xxiii)

- **Contemporary witness.**
  - A 1158 annal says of Hildegard and Elisabeth: "In these days God made manifest His power through the frail sex" (p. xiii; *Annales Palidenses*, p. 181 n. 1).
  - About 1250 the Franciscan Lamprecht of Regensburg marvels that "in Brabant and the land of Bayern" an "art" has arisen among women that an old woman understands better than a learned man (pp. xiii–xiv).
- **Inspiration as the only door.** Direct inspiration "was the only competence recognized to women in the realm of spirituality — and this only in privileged cases" (p. xiv). Hildegard and the beguines alike had to excuse themselves as unlearned (p. xxiii). The exception was a recognised special gift (p. xxiii).
- **Titles.**
  - Such women were sometimes called "mistresses" (p. xiv).
  - Some manuscripts call Porete a "*Beguine clergeresse*" (p. xv).
  - Hildegard became "the sibyl or prophetess of the Rhine" (p. xv).
- **The suspicion behind every ecstatic.** The authors quote an unnamed writer: "an ecstatic must be either a minister of the Holy Spirit or an instrument of Satan" (pp. xxiii–xxiv; *secondhand*, source not named in the extraction).

### 1.2 Hildegard (pp. xx, 3–36)

- **Life.**
  - Born 1098 at Bermersheim near Alzey, of a baronial family (p. 3).
  - Placed at eight with the women's community of Disibodenberg under Jutta of Sponheim (p. 3).
  - In 1148 she "broke free of the authority of the monks" to found Rupertsberg, defending it through the archbishop of Mainz and Barbarossa (p. 3).
  - She founded Eibingen in 1165 (p. 3) and secured the houses' "economic autonomy" by her "competence in legal matters" (pp. 3–4).
- **Mission.**
  - Prophetic calling at 43; approved at the Synod of Trier in 1147 in Pope Eugenius's presence (p. 4).
  - Pilgrims came; demons demanded to be cast out only by "Old Wrinklegard" (p. 4).
  - She warned the Cologne clergy and the monks of St Martin at Mainz against the Cathars (p. 4).
  - **Four preaching tours** to clergy and laity: Franconia, Lorraine, Swabia and the Rhine as far as Werden. "This role was quite exceptional for a woman" (p. 4).
- **The 1178 interdict** (pp. 4–5; her letter, pp. 23–27). The excommunicate had been reconciled and buried "in the presence of all the inhabitants of Bingen" (p. 24). Under the interdict the nuns stopped singing and read the office "in an undertone", and gave up their near-monthly communion (pp. 24–25). She warns prelates against any decree "which closes the mouths of a community singing to God" (p. 26).
- **Illness.** She was ill all her life, and ill until she could speak her visions. She also used illness to press her superiors, for example to leave Disibodenberg (p. 5).
- **Rank.** She took only noble girls. Tengswich (Tengswidis), "Prioress of Echternach", asked why, since Christ chose the poor. Hildegard answered with God's ordered estates: does one put oxen, sheep, asses and goats in one fold? (p. 6). The authors also say she held that heretics should not be put to death, being *forma Dei* (p. xx). The note numbering is unclear in the OCR: it seems to cite Ep. XLVII, PL 197.232–233. **Unverified; check before use.**
- **Self-description.** "*homo simplex*", "*paupercula mulier*", "*indocta mulier*" (p. 7). She saw her visions awake, "the shadow of the living light" (letter to Guibert, pp. 22–23).
- **Gender in the visionary works (the authors' reading).**
  - Before the Fall man and woman behold each other in "complete equality" (p. 14).
  - God's love is "maternal" (pp. 14, 29–30). She favoured the parable of the woman seeking the lost drachma, where the woman stands for God (p. 14).
  - Strength and weakness become "a heritage common to both", woman's strength being "more supple" (pp. 14–15).
  - She also "stigmatize[s] all forms of sexual perversion" (p. 14): Catholic voice, hostile.
- **Medicine.** Her knowledge came from nature and from "the illnesses of those who came to her to be healed" (p. 8). The best remedy is "the doctor's compassion" (p. 16).
- **Song.** "Symphony" says God receives "not only those who stand erect, but also those who fall and those who are bent" (p. 35).

### 1.3 Beguines as a way of life (pp. xviii–xxiii)

- **Who they were.**
  - Women of noble or burgher birth who could not enter orders, for lack of dowry or noble birth, or because orders refused new houses. They lived "without pronouncing perpetual vows" (p. xx).
  - Beguinages answered the *Frauenfrage*, the problem of unmarried women (p. xxi).
  - There was a small male minority, the Beghards (p. xxi).
  - They were not "proletarians". Mostly noble or middle-class in the 13th century, their houses later became "places of refuge for poor women" (p. xxi).
- **Work.**
  - Under a *magistra* they nursed the sick, laid out the dead and did "all kinds of manual work", earning their own living (p. xxi).
  - Without intending it, they competed with the guilds, "especially with that of the weavers" (pp. xxi–xxii).
  - Some begged "*Brot für Gott*", and these were "the most ill-reputed" (p. xxi).
- **Allies and suspicion.**
  - Cistercians and then friars gave pastoral care. Jacques de Vitry (1170–1240) protected them (pp. xix, xxii).
  - Clergy suspected them for two reasons: they were neither cloistered nor vowed and had no official status; and they brought scripture and theology into the vernacular, "encroaching on a domain reserved to the clergy" (p. xxii).
  - "Neither Meister Eckhart nor Marguerite would have been condemned if their works had appeared only in Latin" (p. xxii).
- **Joy.** "Laughter, clapping of hands, volts, and dances" in holy women's lives (Porion, *secondhand*, p. xix). With Beatrice and her friends this was tolerated unless it upset the liturgy (p. 74).
- **Orthodoxy.** Most beguines were "perfectly orthodox" (p. xviii). They tended "to minimize the necessity of recourse to the ecclesiastical hierarchy" (p. xviii).

### 1.4 The beguine authors, briefly

- **Beatrice of Nazareth** (1200–1268):
  - At seven she was sent to the beguines' school at Zoutleeuw (Leau) and followed Latin liberal arts there (p. 72).
  - She was a Cistercian from 1215 or 1216, and prioress of Nazareth from 1237 (pp. 72–73).
  - Her penances were extreme (p. 72), followed by three years of "passive trials" (p. 75).
  - After a vision in 1232 her devotion turns outward to neighbours (p. 76). As prioress she is a "patroness" to people "of every age, every condition and profession" (p. 77).
- **Hadewijch of Antwerp** (second quarter of the 13th c.):
  - "Mistress" to "an unorganized group of Beguines" (p. 98).
  - She met opposition from inside and outside, was threatened with banishment and perhaps prison, and was separated from her friends Sara, Emma and Marguerite (p. 98).
  - She urged her friends to study and to ask questions "in Latin and in Flemish" (pp. 98–99).
  - Her "List of the Perfect" names a beguine put to death by the inquisitor Robert le Bougre (active in Flanders 1235–38) "on account of her just love" (p. 97; "doubtlessly Aleydis", p. 144).
  - Her counsel: untimely zeal "may upset everybody"; do not expect fidelity from others (p. 106).
  - Also: "Unless you suffer, you do not grow" (p. 106). See rule 22.
- **Mechthild of Magdeburg** (c. 1207–c. 1294):
  - Joined a Magdeburg beguine community about 1230 and began her book in 1250 under the Dominican Henry of Halle (p. 39).
  - She attacked clergy, Empire and even her own Dominicans, and drew "considerable clerical antagonism" (pp. 39–40).
  - She was warned that unless she buried the book it would be burnt (p. 41, Balthasar, *secondhand*).
  - She called herself "the last of the foolish Beguines" (p. 46). She retired to Helfta about 1270 (pp. 40–41).
  - Her Joachite hopes (pp. 51–52) and her attitude to Jews are flagged by the authors themselves (p. 52).
- **Marguerite Porete** (d. 1310): **outside 1066–1250**, recorded only as an endpoint.
  - The bishop of Cambrai burned her book at Valenciennes before 1306 (p. 143). She was imprisoned 18 months for refusing to swear before the inquisitor, and burned in Paris on 1 June 1310 (pp. 143–144).
  - Her fellow accused, the Beghard Guiard de Cressonessart, retracted and got life imprisonment (pp. 145–146).
  - Three clerics had approved her book, two of them only for a few readers (pp. 144–145).
  - Vienne (1311–12) then confused her book with the "Free Spirit" (p. 147). The Church's case was "directed against the idea of freedom" (p. 158).

### 1.5 Bride and courtly mysticism (pp. xxv, xxx, 45–49, 99–106) — rule 26 material

- **Sources.** Bernard's Sermons on the Song and courtly love shaped the beguines' language (p. xxv). They are "troubadours of God", serving Lady Love as a knight serves his lady (p. xxx).
- **Mechthild.** The soul is betrothed to the Son "from before time" (p. 45). She wants to "love to death"; God is "sick with love" for the soul (pp. 46–47, *secondhand*). Love's third stage "demands and desires to be rejected" (p. 49).
- **Hadewijch.**
  - Love (*Minne*) is a lady and queen (p. 99), "sometimes gracious and sometimes cruel" (p. 101).
  - The soul is "the bride in the City", "spouse and mother" (p. 104).
  - Spiritual growth is likened to a pregnancy that must not be stopped (p. 106).

### 1.6 Feminine divine figures: what the book does and does not give

- **Hildegard.** God's maternal love (pp. 14, 29–30); the drachma woman as God (p. 14); *viriditas*, "greenness" (pp. 21, 28, 36). No Sapientia or Caritas figure appears in these excerpts or the introduction. The Church appears only in a one-line summary of *Scivias* II (p. 10).
- **The beguines.** Lady Love, "the feminine aspect of God" in Mechthild (p. 53); Hadewijch's *Minne* (pp. 99–100); Porete's *Amour*, who says "I am God" (p. 152).
- These are 13th-century and courtly.

## 2. Cross-checks

- **Rupertsberg date: three sources, three dates.**
  - Founded in 1147 and settled "a few years later" (Cadden, `rhine_cadden_hildegard_types.md` §1.1).
  - "In 1148 she broke free" (here, p. 3).
  - The move in 1150 (`rykener_and_mobilities.md`, Cole and Victoria row).
  - Write "c. 1150" in any loc.
- **Birthplace.** Bermersheim near Alzey (here, p. 3) vs. "near Sponheim" (Cadden p. 150). Loc should avoid naming the village.
- **Tengswich / Tenxwind.** Here she is "Prioress of Echternach", a canoness, objecting to noble-only admission (p. 6). Clark (`rhine_clark_priesthood_virgin.md` §1.2) calls her "Abbess Tengswind". `rykener_and_mobilities.md` has "Abbess Tenxwind" objecting to "vain and rich devotional performances".
  - The objections are complementary: the same exchange may cover both.
  - Her house is **unverified**. Other scholarship (not read here) places her at Andernach, not Echternach. Check before naming a place.
- ***Scivias* dates.** Clark dates it 1151 (`rhine_clark_priesthood_virgin.md` §1.2). Here it is "between 1141 and 1158" (p. 9). The 1158 end date looks wrong, since the next book is dated 1158–63 (p. 9). Use c. 1141–51.
- **`../later_starts/region_latin-christendom-jewish-europe.md` row F and item 6.**
  - Row F (the 1178 interdict) is confirmed and enriched (pp. 4–5, 23–27).
  - Item 6 ("beguines too late for 1178") is confirmed: Beatrice was at a beguine school c. 1207 (p. 72), Mechthild joined c. 1230 (p. 39), and the movement peaked mid-13th century (p. xiii).
- **`survey_1178.md` (Latin Europe rows).** Consistent. The book does not mention Lambert le Bègue.
- **`../book_federici_caliban_witch.md` row 7 ("Women Without a Rule", after 1312).** Supported: no vows, no official status, suspicion (p. xxii); Vienne 1311–12 (p. 147).
- **`../comparanda_reading_list.md` candidate B.** "No beguine source attests male-to-female crossing": none here either. The book adds the vernacular and the weavers as friction points (p. xxii).
- **`witch_even_ezra.md` (Hildegard and the masters).** Consistent. A canon of Toul contrasts Hildegard with "the subtle French masters", who lose themselves in disputes (p. xv, *secondhand*).
- **`witch_design.md` §5 (Church response by date).** Robert le Bougre's execution of a beguine in Flanders (1235–38; pp. 97, 144) is a lethal precedent early in the "Tools forged" phase (c. 1230–1320). It concerns heresy, not the Ladies. It may appear as off-screen news only: no violence on screen, and no link to the Ladies in the narrator's voice.
- **Rule 22.** Beatrice's penances (p. 72), Hadewijch's "unless you suffer" (p. 106) and Mechthild's longing to be rejected (p. 49) are Catholic devotion. None may become a gate, a trial or a merit for daughters.
- **Rule 23.** Guiard retracted, and that was honoured with life rather than death (pp. 145–146). In any later trial event, retracting (stepping back) must carry no narrator shame.
