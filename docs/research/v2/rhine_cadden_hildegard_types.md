# Cadden, "It Takes All Kinds" (1984): Hildegard's types of men and women, design reading

Source: Joan Cadden, "It Takes All Kinds: Sexuality and Gender Differences in Hildegard of Bingen's 'Book of Compound Medicine'," *Traditio* 40 (1984): 149–174. JSTOR 27831151.

Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo. Page numbers are the journal's printed ones. Hildegard's text is cited by Cadden from Kaiser's edition of *Causae et curae* (1903), given here as "K" page numbers.

**What was read.** The whole article, pp. 149–174, including Table 1 (pp. 161–164) and all footnotes with their Latin. The table's layout is damaged in the extraction. Its cells were read against the running text (pp. 165–172) and the notes.

**Relation to existing notes.**
- `../wsq_libby_wondrous_bodies.md` reports Charmaille's intersex reading of *Cause et cure* (Libby p. 167). `../tsq_lane_trans_biology.md` says not to use Hildegard's bodies to explain the Marked.
- `../cmp_vanderlugt_sex_difference.md` is van der Lugt's tribute to Cadden's later book. It covers beards and Huguccio's behavioural test.
- `../hist_trans_historical.md` P48 names Hildegard as a woman physician.
- Cadden is the first direct reading of what Hildegard actually says about types. It answers the intersex question: the types are temperaments of men and of women, not intermediate bodies.

## 0. Framing warning

- **Authenticity and text.**
  - The work survives in one 13th-century manuscript (Copenhagen 90b). Kaiser's edition "contains numerous errors" (p. 149 n. 1).
  - Its authenticity has been disputed: Singer argued against, Schrader and Führkötter for (p. 149 n. 1).
  - The rubrics naming the four temperaments are probably later additions, though by the 13th century (pp. 165–166).
  - Treat "choleric", "sanguine" and so on as the manuscript's labels, not certainly Hildegard's own.
- **Nothing heterodox.** Cadden: "nothing she says about gender, sexuality, or reproduction contradicts standard assumptions" (p. 152). This is mainstream 12th-century learned opinion, written with unusual breadth (pp. 152–153). It is not a dissident or proto-trans text.
- **Not intersex, not trans.** Every type is a kind of man or a kind of woman. Where a type takes the other sex's traits (phlegmatics, and sanguine men's "prudence, continence of women"), it is a temperament inside a sexed body (pp. 162, 164, 166).
- **Rules for use.**
  - Period theory only, in characters' mouths. Never the narrator's truth about daughters (rules 3–4). It never gives the Mark a cause in the narrator's voice.
  - No examination scenes: a physician talks from the book, not from her body.
  - Some table entries are violent or degrading (melancholy men's hatred of women, p. 163; women made "to serve men", p. 154). They are not for loc.

## 1. Findings

### 1.1 Hildegard and the book (pp. 150–152)

- **Life.** Born 1098 "near Sponheim" (p. 150) to local nobility. Sent to a nunnery at eight. Head of the community in 1136. She founded Rupertsberg near Bingen in 1147, settled there a few years later, and died there in 1179 (p. 150).
- **Standing.** Acknowledged by Pope Eugenius III (p. 151). She treated the sick, "reportedly successfully" (p. 151). She preached to clergy and laity "in the Rhine, Main, and Moselle valleys" and corresponded with Bernard and Barbarossa (p. 151).
- **The medical works.** She wrote the *Book of Simple Medicine* and the *Book of Compound Medicine* "in her fifties", that is, around the 1150s (p. 151).
- **The cosmos.** The *Compound Medicine* opens with Creation and treats divine creation and human procreation as the joint basis of human difference (p. 151).

### 1.2 Creation and procreation (pp. 153–156)

- **Adam and Eve.** Adam was made "from the vigor of the virile earth" and is strong. Eve was made from his marrow and is "soft" (p. 153).
  - Eve fell first, which was good: Adam's sin would have been harder to correct (p. 153).
  - Earthiness gives man his beard. Woman is beardless and her mind "airy" (p. 154).
- **Work.**
  - Man was transformed from clay to flesh, so he makes the earth bear fruit.
  - Woman was made from flesh that "remained flesh", so "the skilled work of the hands" was given to her (*artificiosum opus manuum*; p. 154 n. 22; repeated as Cadden's closing line, p. 174).
- **Conception.**
  - Conception comes from a man's and a woman's "foam" (*spuma*) of agitated blood (p. 154). Sometimes she allows woman a little weak seed, "like a crumb to his loaf" (p. 155 n. 25).
  - The strength of the seed decides the sex. The parents' love decides the character: strong seed and mutual love make a virtuous boy; weak seed and no love make a "bitter daughter" (p. 155).
- **Not Aristotelian.** Hildegard "neither states nor implies" hot male against cold female, or active against passive (p. 155).
  - Plump women's children look like their mothers (p. 155).
  - Male and female adultery are treated symmetrically (p. 156).
  - Women suffer less from hernias and gout (p. 156; p. 171).

### 1.3 Desire (pp. 157–159)

- **Desire as wind.** In both sexes desire is a wind from the marrow (p. 157).
  - In men it is forced into a narrow place, the loins; in women it spreads in the broad womb (p. 157).
  - Men are "tossed by a storm"; women's waves are milder, so they control themselves "more easily … though it takes work" (p. 158).
- **Degree, not kind.** These are "differences of degree, not differences of kind". Hildegard "does not regard women and men as opposites" (p. 158).
- **Moral differences.** They are "even slighter" than the physical ones (p. 159).
- **The less misogynist side.** Between "men are more passionate and women more prudent" and "women are lustful and men their victims", she takes "the less misogynistic option" (p. 173).

### 1.4 The four types of each sex (pp. 159–166; Table 1, pp. 161–164)

- **Order.** Men: choleric, sanguine, melancholy, phlegmatic. Women: sanguine, phlegmatic, choleric, melancholy. The two lists are given in different orders with pages between them (p. 160).
- **No system.** Men and women of the same type are rarely parallel or opposite (pp. 160, 165).
  - Cadden's conclusion is "a striking conceptual independence of the sexes" (p. 166).
  - Women "are not the same as men of their kind, nor are they a pale imitation" (p. 166).
- **Choleric** (pp. 161, 166–167):
  - Both sexes are prudent and feared.
  - Choleric men cannot restrain themselves.
  - Choleric women are "chaste, faithful wives", charming to men, and suffer without a husband to keep faith with (p. 168 n. 62).
- **Sanguine** (pp. 162, 170):
  - Men are moderate, honourable and sober, and have "the prudence, continence of women" (Table 1, p. 162).
  - Women are "skilled", with a good memory, and are "unhealthy without husbands" (pp. 162, 170 n. 77).
- **Melancholy** (pp. 163, 170):
  - The men are bitter and violent toward women (p. 163; not for use).
  - The women are "stronger and happier without husbands" and weakened by marriage (*saniores, fortiores et laetiores … absque maritis*; p. 170 n. 75). They are usually sterile (pp. 163, 169).
- **Phlegmatic men** (pp. 164, 168, n. 61):
  - Body: "ample, soft, womanly flesh", "womanly color", "no virile beard", too little fire for an erection, sterile.
  - Temper: not mean and without hatred; "faithful".
  - Their slight desire is "like Adam before the Fall".
  - They "can live with men or with women" (*et viris et feminis cohabitare valent*). Cadden's gloss is "live comfortably with". Do not read it as sexual.
- **Phlegmatic women** (pp. 164, 168, n. 63):
  - Body: "somewhat manly"; "a little beard sometimes".
  - Mind: a "somewhat virile mind" (*aliquantem virilem animum*, p. 166 n. 55).
  - Desire: they can refrain from marriage, but with men they are "incontinent … like men". They "draw and lead men after them". Very fertile.
- **The cross-sex pairing.** Phlegmatics "share a tendency to resemble the opposite sex" (p. 166). Hildegard "sees the tepid phlegmatic man as womanly, the active phlegmatic woman as manly" (p. 172).

### 1.5 What she includes and leaves out (pp. 167–172)

- **Gaps in her own system.**
  - Only men's brains are described (p. 167).
  - Women's desire is described only where women are "like men", the phlegmatics (p. 168).
  - Only women's attractiveness to men is noted, never the reverse (p. 169).
  - The man chooses the time for procreation (p. 169).
- **Health.** The uterus is described for all four women's types (pp. 169–170). Abstinence harms most types, and women more than men (p. 170). Menstruation is a purgation, and its stopping causes illness (p. 171).
- **Overall.** She does not adopt any of the three classical models: Galenic parallel, Aristotelian opposite, or Hippocratic womb-dominated (p. 173).

## 2. Cross-checks

- **`../wsq_libby_wondrous_bodies.md` §1 ("Other points") and §2 ("Intersex is not trans").** Libby reports Charmaille reading *Cause et cure* through intersex (Libby p. 167). Cadden's direct reading finds no intermediate bodies. The cross-sex traits are temperaments of men and of women (pp. 164, 166, 172). This is a **difference of reading**, not a factual clash, and Cadden is the closer reading of the text. Both notes agree on the rule: keep Hildegard's types apart from daughters.
- **`../tsq_lane_trans_biology.md` ("Intersex conflation").** Confirmed. A physician's "phlegmatic" diagnosis of a daughter is the hostile "inner" family of rule 3 ("the seed's heat") in period form.
- **`../cmp_vanderlugt_sex_difference.md` §B.** Van der Lugt: beards counted because they could be seen "without invasive examination". Cadden's types also lead with face, beard and colour (p. 160; Table 1). Both support the rule that a learned voice reads the face and the book, never the body.
- **`../hist_trans_historical.md` P48.** Hildegard as a woman physician is supported (p. 151). Narrow the claim: it is "reportedly successful", from the *Vita*.
- **`rykener_and_mobilities.md` (Cole and Victoria row).** That note dates the move to Rupertsberg to 1150. Cadden: founded 1147, settled "a few years later" (p. 150). These are compatible (founding vs. move). See `rhine_women_mystics.md` §2 for a third date.
- **Rule 20 ("no line ties motherhood to the womb").** The uterus-centred health entries (pp. 169–171) are hostile or physician voice only.
- **Rule 18.** "Every woman is a little phlegmatic" or similar universalising lines are out.
