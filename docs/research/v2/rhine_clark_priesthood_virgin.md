## 0. Framing warning

- **Clark's thesis.** Twelfth-century Rhineland women "were not arguing for an empowered laity" nor "for the ordination of women" (p. 24). They produced a "religious discourse that could not be easily wedged" into Gratian's "two kinds of Christians" (p. 24). Never make Hildegard or Elisabeth into advocates of women priests, in any voice.
- **Against a unified Hildegard.** Clark reads tension where Augustine Thompson (1994) reads a coherent system (p. 12 n. 24). Her readings of tension are her interpretation. The texts she quotes are the evidence.
- **All three artefacts sit inside established communities.** None of them is a sect or something the Church condemned (p. 8). Nothing here is evidence for the Good Ladies or for any folk practice. Use it only as the Catholic neighbourhood around them.
- **Rules.** The narrator never claims Hildegard, Elisabeth, Ekbert or the Cologne chalice for any faith. Bride images in the sources (p. 15) are Christian voice only (rule 26).

## 1. Findings

### 1.1 The setting: reform and the clergy–laity line (pp. 6, 8–11)

- **Reform.** From the mid-11th century reformers pushed clerical celibacy, against simony and against lay investiture. Clerical marriage and "concubinage" were "still widely practiced and defended" (p. 9).
- **The effect.** The priest was set apart as the "exclusive mediator" of the sacraments (p. 9). From the 12th century, ordained priests faced "a dependent laity obligated spiritually and materially" to them (Van Engen, quoted p. 10). Clergy were also set apart in dress, training and language (Rubin, via Clark, p. 10).
- **Gratian's Decretum (1140).** *Duo sunt genera Christianorum*, "there are two kinds of Christians" (p. 10). It forbids consecrated women (nuns) to touch the altar's vessels and vestments (p. 11). Women may not take communion to the sick or baptise, except in grave necessity (p. 11).
- **Women and the altar.** Women had been barred from approaching the altar "since at least the ninth century" (p. 6 n. 5).
  - A 9th-century Paris council condemned women who handled the sacred vessels, helped priests vest and gave communion to the people. *Secondhand:* Clark quotes Macy, who quotes Hochstetler (p. 11 n. 20).
  - The condemnation implies that some priests allowed it (p. 11 n. 20).
- **When the question was first asked.** The Sentence commentaries take up women's ordination explicitly only from Bonaventure (1250–54) (p. 11 n. 23, citing Cardman). Hildegard raised it a century earlier (pp. 11–12).
- **Marian devotion.** Devotion to a "maternally loving Mary" grew from the 11th century (p. 8). But neither Hildegard nor Elisabeth wrote of a "sweet mother" Mary (p. 9 n. 10, citing Newman and Clark).

### 1.2 Hildegard: the priesthood without the office (pp. 12–17)

- ***Scivias* II.6 (1151).** The female figure Ecclesia stands at the altar and receives Christ's blood in her chalice (p. 12).
- **The ban.** In the same vision Hildegard bars the bodily "defective" from ordination (p. 14). She says women may not "approach the office of the altar", being "an infirm and weak habitation, appointed to bear children" (II.6.76; p. 14).
- **The plough.** A woman conceives through a man as the ground is ploughed by a farmer. "Just as the earth cannot plow itself, a woman must not be a priest" (p. 14).
- **The strain in it.** The same vision repeatedly likens the consecration to Mary conceiving Christ without a man (p. 14). Mary is offered as the model for the priest: her faithful word made the Lord's body (p. 14). Hildegard makes the same link in a letter to priests (p. 15).
- **The virgin "has" the priesthood.** A virgin betrothed to Christ "has the priesthood and all the ministry of My altar" (*habet sacerdotium et omne ministerium altaris mei*; II.6.76, p. 15). Clark: this is priesthood "without the notion of office" (p. 15).
- **"Daughters of Zion."** In *Scivias* II.5, mitred clergy with the episcopal pallium stand among the "spiritual people" and are called "daughters of Zion" (*filiae Sion*). The laity stand lower, at the level of Ecclesia's womb, because they reproduce (p. 16). Monastics rank above clerics (p. 16 n. 39).
- **The usurper.** Whoever comes to the altar without anointing "does not fear to wound My Son" (II.6.61; p. 17). Clark notes that Hildegard puts the butchery of the Son where the usual image was the rape of the Bride (p. 17).
- **Sacerdotium and officium.** Clark's key point: women may "have" the priesthood, yet may not approach the office of the altar. Virginity gives a standing outside the social order, unlinked to office (p. 17).
- **Dress.** The bar on women at the altar is tied to not taking "the masculine role" (*virilem personam*) in clothes or tonsure. Clothes and hair are the public markers of office (p. 17 n. 44).
- **Rank.** Hildegard's reply to Tengswind: social subordination to men does not bind professed virgins (Ep. 52r; p. 17 n. 43).
- **Later Hildegard** imagined a violent upheaval of church order carried out by laypeople (p. 16 n. 40). *Secondhand* (Kerby-Fulton); not checked.

### 1.3 Elisabeth of Schönau: Mary as priest, then the abbot's Mass (pp. 17–21)

- **The two women knew each other.** They met at least once, exchanged letters and read each other's works. Both were Benedictine nuns from childhood (p. 7).
- **First visionary diary.**
  - Elisabeth is tormented by the devil into doubt and despair (p. 18).
  - On a Saturday Mass of the Virgin she sees a regal woman in white with a purple mantle. "My lady" blesses her "with the sign of the cross" and reassures her (p. 18).
  - After communion she sees "my Lady" by the altar "in a garment like a priestly chasuble" (*veste qualis est casula sacerdotalis*), crowned (p. 18).
  - Clark reads the crown as possibly evoking tonsure. The *Summa Coloniensis* (c. 1169, perhaps Rhineland) calls tonsure a crown: "we must be kings" (pp. 18–19).
- **The women's own rites in the first diary.**
  - At the nuns' service for a dead sister, an angel stands beside the *magistra*, "under whose ministry that office was being celebrated", and Mary watches (p. 20).
  - At Pentecost a dove sets small flames on the priest, the altar ministers "and each nun's head" alike (p. 20).
- **Second diary: Mary withdraws.**
  - Mary is "angry" with her. St Peter, and then her angel, tell her to ask the abbot to say Mass for Mary (p. 19). Mary returns only after the abbot's Mass and praises *him* (p. 19).
  - Three dead women's sufferings are eased by the abbot's Mass, not by the visions, prayers and mortifications of the Schönau women (p. 19).
  - Male saints and the angel now predominate (p. 19).
- **Clark's cause.** In between, Abbot Hildelin took a closer interest and her brother Ekbert arrived. Ekbert was a cleric of SS. Cassius and Florentius at Bonn, then a monk of Schönau and her secretary and editor (pp. 20–21).
- **Later texts.** In the third diary Mary saves the world from her Son's anger (p. 21). The priestly Mary of the first diary is gone; Mary "recedes into the heavenly distance" (p. 21).

### 1.4 The Cologne chalice (pp. 8, 21–23)

- **What it is.** A silver chalice from the Cologne region, perhaps from Nicholas of Verdun's workshop or circle. Its date and place of use are unknown (p. 8 and n. 8). It is now in the Cologne diocesan museum (p. 8 n. 8).
- **The bowl.** Four labelled figures: Abel with a lamb, Melchizedek with bread and wine, Mary enthroned with hands raised in prayer, and Christ (p. 21).
- **Who saw it.** By the mid-12th century the chalice was often withheld from the laity. This cup was probably made "for an exclusively clerical gaze" (p. 23).
- **Clark's reading.** Mary as priest fits easily into a rhetoric of male priestly prerogative: she is "alone of all her sex" (p. 23, after Warner).

## 2. Cross-checks

- **`witch_design.md` §4 (the gate).** The design already has recognition come from the *hera* of a household, "not by a priest". Clark's split between *sacerdotium* and *officium* (p. 17) is the period's own way of naming a standing without office. It supports the design as it stands. The faith still claims no priesthood: §2 lists "any priesthood" as invented, and that stays.
- **`witch_design.md` §5 (Church response).** The "Illusion" phase (to c. 1230) coincides with the reform's hardening of the clergy–laity line (pp. 9–11). A 1066 start opens at the height of the celibacy campaign (p. 9). §5 has no event for it yet: see row C1 below.
- **`../cmp_devun_shape_of_sex.md` §2.2 and item 6.** Devun has Elisabeth's Christ as a "female virgin" (Devun p. 192). Clark adds Elisabeth's Mary in a chasuble at the altar (p. 18). These are complementary Catholic images, for Catholic speakers only.
- **`../book_ford_dress_codes.md` ch. 4.** Ford quotes Hildegard: no exchange of clothes "except in necessity". Clark gives the reason she ties to the altar: clothes and hair are the markers of office (p. 17 n. 44). Rule 24's list of outward change (hair, veil, girdle) matches what a 12th-century cleric would read.
- **`../later_starts/region_latin-christendom-jewish-europe.md` row F.** No conflict. Clark does not cover the 1178 interdict.
- **`../tsq_lane_trans_biology.md` and `../wsq_libby_wondrous_bodies.md`.** Clark's "defective" bodies barred from ordination (p. 14) are canon-law impediments. They are not intersex material and are not about daughters.
- **Rule 26.** "A virgin betrothed to My Son … has the priesthood" (p. 15) is bride mysticism. It is for a Catholic nun's voice only, never the Good Ladies' own voice.
- **Rule 7.** Clark's evidence concerns holy standing only. It must not become the daughters' only road. The design's non-holy roads (steward of a well-kept house, the healer or finder) stay.
