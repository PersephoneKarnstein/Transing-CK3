# Eleanor Rykener (Henningsen 2019, Bychowski 2026) and *Medieval Mobilities* (2023)

Notes written 2026-10-25. All quotes are 25 words or fewer and carry page numbers. Everything else is paraphrase. No book text is kept here.

**Pronouns.** Eleanor Rykener is *she*, following her own name in the record and all three readings below. Scholars are *they* throughout, because none of these texts states their own pronouns. (Khoury uses *he* for Henningsen and *she* for Bychowski. These notes do not rely on that.) Note that **Kadin Henningsen** (author of A) is a different person from **Gustav Henningsen**, the witchcraft historian ("The Ladies from Outside") cited in `witch_papers_and_timeline.md`. ## 1. Eleanor Rykener: the record and how it is read

### 1.1 The record

**Document.** The only source is one entry at the head of London Plea and Memoranda Roll A34, membrane 2 (1395), now London Metropolitan Archives CLA/024/01/02/035 (A p. 250 n. 5; B p. 275). Ruth Mazo Karras and David Lorenzo Boyd found it through A. H. Thomas's *Calendar of Select Plea and Memoranda* (vol. 3, p. 228). Thomas had summarised it in one sentence as an "examination of two men charged with immorality" (C p. 28). The proceedings were in English, but the scribe wrote Latin (C p. 33). Every scholarly English quotation comes from the Karras and Boyd translation (A p. 249 n. 1).

**Arrest and hearing (London).**
- On **11 December 1394**, John Britby "of the county of York" and Rykener, "calling [herself] Eleanor, having been detected in women's clothing," were brought before Mayor John Fressh and the aldermen (A p. 249). B also gives 11 December 1394 (p. 273).
- They had been found "last Sunday" between 8 and 9 in the evening, lying by a stall in **Soper's Lane**. Britby had met her in **Cheap** (Cheapside) (A pp. 249, 260; C p. 28).
- **The date is muddled in the scholarship.** Khoury gives 11 December *1395* (C p. 28), apparently from the roll year. Henningsen calls the night of the encounter "Sunday, December 11" (A p. 260), which contradicts A's own p. 249. My own check: 11 December 1394 (Julian) was a Friday. That fits a Friday hearing after a Sunday (6 December) arrest, and Fressh's 1394–95 mayoralty. Use **1394** and verify against the translation.
- Britby says he approached her "thinking he was a woman," asked her for sex "as he would a woman," and agreed a price. The amount is not stated (C p. 28; A pp. 260–261). B says she negotiated the pay before agreeing (p. 273).
- The court asked who had taught her this "vice," for how long, where, and with whom, "masculine or feminine" (A p. 259; C p. 28).
- **No charge or verdict survives** (A p. 262; C pp. 33, 36). Karras and Boyd suggest she fitted neither offence. In law "prostitute" meant a woman, and sodomy accusations fell mainly on the penetrative partner (A p. 262). Khoury adds that the court seems unable to decide her gender, which may explain the missing verdict (C p. 33).

**Who taught her (London).**
- **Anna**, "the whore of a former servant of Sir Thomas Blount," first taught her to have sex "in the manner of a woman" (A p. 259; C p. 28). B calls Anna a sex worker who trained her in the trade (p. 274).
- **Elizabeth Brouderer** (B and Khoury spell it *Bronderer*) first dressed her in women's clothing and called her Eleanor (A pp. 252, 259).
- Elizabeth also brought her own daughter Alice to men at night "without light." She then showed them Eleanor in the morning and said they had been with *her* (A p. 259). B reads this as a farcical story told at the Brouderer house (p. 274). The record frames it as Elizabeth's trick on clients.
- Elizabeth's house was **outside Bishopsgate**. There, **Phillip, rector of Theydon Garnon** (a parish in Essex), had sex with her "as with a woman," and she "took away two gowns of Phillip's" (A p. 261). Khoury: she "absconded with two gowns (filched from a priest, no less)" (C p. 32).

**Work and travel, late summer and autumn 1394.**
- **Oxford**, for five weeks before Michaelmas (29 September). She lived "in women's clothing and calling [herself] Eleanor," working as an **embroideress**. She had sex often with three "unsuspecting scholars" (A pp. 252, 261; C p. 29).
- **Burford, Oxfordshire**, from the Friday before Michaelmas, for six weeks. She worked as a **tapster** (ale-server) for John Clerk at **the Swan** (A p. 251). Clients there were two Franciscans who gave her a gold ring, a Carmelite friar and six foreign men, nine in all (C p. 29; A p. 261).
- **Beaconsfield** (Buckinghamshire). "As a man" she had sex with a certain Joan. "As a woman" she had sex with two foreign Franciscans (C p. 29; A pp. 261, 263).
- **Back in London.** Three chaplains, including Sir John, once chaplain of **St Margaret Pattens**, had sex with her in the lanes behind **St Katherine's by the Tower**. "Many priests" did the same (A pp. 261–262; C p. 29).
- **Women.** She said that "as a man" she often had sex with "many nuns" and with women married and unmarried. No payment is mentioned for any of these (A p. 263; B p. 274).
- **Ending.** The record ends with her saying she took priests more readily than other clients because they paid more (C p. 29). B: "Rykener confessed that she preferred priests and monks because they paid better than other clients" (p. 274).

**Errors in the Palgrave entry (B).** Where B differs from A and C, check the translation before using it.
- B makes "Theydon Garnon" the rector's name. It is his parish. His name was Phillip.
- B says he *gave* her a pair of dresses and that she refused to return them (p. 274). A and C have her taking them.
- B calls the Burford ring-givers "a pair of monks" (p. 274). C calls them Franciscans, who were friars.
- B's "seamstress" and "bartender" (p. 273) are modern glosses for embroideress and tapster.
- Khoury lists Theydon Garnon among her travel stops (C p. 43). The record seems only to name it as Phillip's parish.
- The first Karras and Boyd article is cited as *GLQ* 1 (1995): 459–465 (A p. 249; B p. 275) but as 479–485 (C p. 45). The second article's title is garbled in each source ("Ut cum mulier", "Et cum muliere"). The correct title is "'Ut cum muliere'." The book chapter is in *Premodern Sexualities* (1996), 99–116.

### 1.2 Henningsen: gender labour and becoming a woman (A)

- **Thesis.** Rykener "ought to be understood as a transgender woman" because she lived and worked as one and her milieu accepted her (p. 250). The scribe records her "calling [herself] Eleanor" even in a hostile court. Henningsen reads this as inscribing herself as a woman "strategically—and perhaps even defiantly" (pp. 250–251).
- **Pronouns in the Latin.** The record has 35 pronouns: 2 feminine, 13 masculine and 20 indeterminate or missing. The scribe was unsure how to gender her. Karras and Boyd chose to render the indeterminate ones as masculine (p. 251 n. 6).
- **Women's work.** Embroidery and sewing were "the quintessential modes of women's labor" (p. 252). Tapster work was a common living for single or widowed women (p. 251). She mentions no men's work at all (p. 260).
- **Seriality** (Iris Marion Young). "Women" is treated as a *series*, a collective defined by orientation toward objects and structures, not by shared biology (pp. 255–256). That lets her belong to the series without biological criteria.
- **Gender labour** (Jane Ward): "the affective and bodily efforts invested in giving gender to others" (p. 257). Ward names three kinds, all applied here (pp. 257–264):
  - *Alliance.* Anna and Elizabeth name her, dress her and teach her. John Clerk employs her as a woman (pp. 252, 258–260). She was "part of a community of women who not only accepted her as a woman but helped her live as a woman" (p. 252). In modern terms they helped her socially transition (p. 252).
  - *Being "the girl" / "the man."* Clients like Britby take the man's role, approaching her "as he would a woman." Britby's own testimony slides from masculine to feminine pronouns (pp. 260–261, 263).
  - *Forgetting.* Partners set aside what they know of her body to treat her as a woman (p. 263).
- **Against Karras and Boyd.**
  - Karras and Boyd hold that the case "stands practically alone" for male transvestism in medieval England, and treat her as a man in women's clothes (p. 250).
  - They suggest Elizabeth proposed the cross-dressing for profit. Henningsen says this "strips Eleanor of any agency for self-fashioning" and erases the women's gender labour (pp. 259–260).
  - Henningsen adds a caution (n. 34, citing Alina Boyden). Shut out of marriage and motherhood, she "might not have had a choice" about sex work as a way to survive (p. 259).
- **Against Karras and Linkinen (2016).** Karras later regretted "transvestite" and preferred "transgender person." Henningsen calls this a half-measure ("transgender-like," ze/hir pronouns) that still does not call her a woman (pp. 253–254). Karras and Linkinen also suggested making her case "via fiction." Henningsen rejects this because it echoes the trope of trans women as pretenders (Bettcher's "evil deceivers and make-believers"): "We do not, however, need to resort to fiction to write Eleanor's history" (p. 254).
- **Sex with women.** "As a man" may only describe a penetrative position. That she was unpaid with women leaves open that she was a bisexual or lesbian trans woman (pp. 263–264). Note 45 rejects the idea that a woman must be dysphoric about her penis (p. 264).
- **Conclusion.** Naming herself in court is "a defiant refusal" of erasure (p. 265). The record is set beside Thomas(ine) Hall (1629), Mary Jones (1836) and Mary Ann Waters (1851). All are post-1453 and outside the mod.

### 1.3 Bychowski's Palgrave entry (B, pp. 273–275)

- **Framing.** Rykener "was a trans woman who lived as a seamstress, bartender, and sex worker." The record shows "practices of gender transitioning, the negotiation of paid sexual exchanges, as well as the community life of working women" (p. 273).
- **The source is hostile.** It was written "according to cisgender perspectives." The scribe repeatedly deadnames her as "John." Even so, "the text nonetheless archives an oral history of a medieval trans woman's life told primarily from her own perspective" (p. 273).
- **Allies.** Elizabeth gave clothing and affirmed the name. Anna introduced and trained her (p. 274).
- **The court's sexual fixation.** The court asks about many acts unrelated to the arrest. This "speaks to the ways trans women have been viewed and sexualized by the dominant cisgender society" (p. 274). That is a direct design warning (see §2.3).
- **Historiography** (p. 275).
  - Karras and Boyd (1995/96) published "male transvestite."
  - Dinshaw (1999) used "John/Eleanor."
  - Holsinger's novels (2014, 2016) gave her the invented slang "swerver."
  - Linkinen made a puppet show.
  - Trans scholars' work came later: Bychowski (2012 blog paper), Henningsen (2016 conference paper, 2019 article) and Bychowski, "The Transgender Turn: Eleanor Rykener Speaks Back" (*Trans Historical*, 2021).

### 1.4 Khoury's historiography and "journey" reading (C, pp. 27–47)

Khoury surveys about 25 years of scholarship and tracks pronoun choice. Of nine works, three use *he/him*, two *he/she*, one avoids pronouns, one uses *ze/hir* and two use *she/her*, and the most recent are the most affirming (pp. 41–42). Khoury's own position is to "pay Rykener her dues as a trans woman, author of her own text, and sex worker with agency" (p. 28 n. 4).

- **Law context** (pp. 31–32). Sex work was tolerated and regulated as a "necessary evil," and sex workers were made visible through prescribed dress such as striped hoods. Khoury argues the court validated her femininity by treating the offence as women's, yet withheld a prostitution charge.
- **Dinshaw** (pp. 33–34). Dinshaw stresses Britby's "dogged repetition of *mulier*" as self-protection. Anna and Elizabeth "exploit the dominance of gender expectations." Khoury ties the clergy's generous pay to the 1395 Lollard attacks on clerical sodomy (p. 34; see open question 4).
- **Evans** (2006, p. 34). Evans reads her as a disorderly "imitator" and "false trader" who changes jobs and blurs categories. Karras and Henningsen rebut this: women commonly changed trades (pp. 34, 40).
- **Bennett** (2003, p. 35). Bennett reads her through women's labour after the Black Death: "change without transformation," with women's work still low-status and poorly paid. Bennett suggests she struck her own "patriarchal bargain."
- **Goldberg** (2014, p. 36). Goldberg reads the record as civic satire aimed at Richard II. Khoury rejects that reading but notes two real contributions. Goldberg tried to identify every named person in the archives, and mapped her exact itinerary down to particular lanes. He also flags oddities: the entry's odd position on the roll, the missing charge, and a London clerk recording events outside the city's jurisdiction.
- **Linkinen** (2015, p. 37). Linkinen calls it the only known English legal action against same-sex intercourse.
- **Henningsen** (pp. 37–39). Khoury praises the reading but disputes one point. Khoury would read Anna and Elizabeth not as "confederates" but as "her queer, or chosen, family" (p. 38), and objects to setting her against "cisgender women friends."
- **Karras and Linkinen** (2016, pp. 39–40). Khoury criticises the puppet show. It invents a childhood to "explain" her, and it presents her as not understanding love, which repeats transphobic narratives of self-confusion.
- **Bychowski** (2021, p. 41). Three demands:
  1. affirm her body;
  2. use her name;
  3. read her exchange with Britby as a collaboration between trans and cis subjects.
- **Khoury's proposal** (pp. 43–44). Read her movement (Oxford → Burford → Beaconsfield → London) as a trans *journey narrative*, one of "pilgrimages of and to the self." Karras notes that "the most independent sort of prostitute" was transient and found her own clients (p. 43). Leaving home may have been necessary to live as herself. Khoury contrasts urban "passing" with small-town imperceptibility (Crawford), and notes that Britby was a mover too, from York. The period's pilgrimage traffic and post-plague displacement made movement thinkable (p. 44).

**Disagreements in brief.**
- *Who she was.* Karras and Boyd (1995/96): a male transvestite prostitute. Dinshaw and Bennett: "John/Eleanor," moving between genders. Karras and Linkinen (2016): "transgender-like," ze/hir. Henningsen, Bychowski and Khoury: a trans woman.
- *The women around her.* Karras and Boyd: profit-seekers who suggested the dressing. Karras and Linkinen: she named them to shift blame (C p. 40). Henningsen: allies doing gender labour. Khoury: chosen family.
- *Her jobs.* Bennett: genuine women's work. Goldberg: fronts for sex work.

## 2. What the Rykener case could give the mod

**Ground rule.** Rykener is never a member, saint, founder or ancestor of any faith. She can be *cited*, for example in a design note or an in-game codex line attributed to the 1394 record. She can also be *echoed*: an event in which a generated character lives a similar life. No generated character should be named "Eleanor Rykener." "Eleanor" alone is a common English name and is fine in name lists.

**Placement.** The case belongs to no Good Ladies region or witchcraft context (see `witch_papers_and_timeline.md`). Options:
- (a) generic content for *any* daughter in England from about 1350;
- (b) flavour for a Good Ladies presence in England, since the *bonae dominae* and Abundia are attested in France and England;
- (c) material for a later English daughter-faith if one is ever made.

This is the user's decision.

### 2.1 Event and flavour ideas (design, not documented fact)

1. **"Who taught her" (mentor chain).** An older woman, perhaps a broiderer, alewife or sex worker, gives the daughter a woman's name and clothes and teaches her a trade. This draws on Henningsen's *labour of alliance* (A pp. 252, 258–260) and Khoury's *chosen family* (C p. 38).
   - Mechanically it could be a friend or mentor relation and a "named by her sisters" modifier.
   - The name is given by *others*. That is the point of the source.
   - Options should let the daughter choose her name too. In the record she *calls herself* Eleanor (A p. 250).
2. **Women's craft as a path.**
   - *Embroideress*: high skill and a respectable cover. It also opens a link to *opus anglicanum*, the famous English embroidery trade. That trade is outside these sources; see §4.
   - *Tapster or alewife*: modest income, gossip and contacts (A p. 251; Bennett).
   - Each is a way of living as a woman *through work*, not a disguise. Do not adopt Goldberg's "front for sex work" reading (C p. 36) as the game's voice.
3. **The road: London, Oxford, Burford, Beaconsfield.** A travel event chain in which an English daughter moves between town and countryside. Each stop offers a job and a risk. This follows Khoury's journey reading (C pp. 43–44).
   - City: anonymity. Small town: being "imperceptible." The road: freedom and danger.
   - Use CK3's travel system if it fits. Check with `ck3_vanilla` which vanilla baronies cover Oxford, Burford (Oxfordshire) and Beaconsfield (Buckinghamshire).
4. **The Mayor's court.** One possible end of the chain, or a random danger for an English daughter, is arrest after the night watch finds her with a client.
   - The scribe's Latin misgenders her. The narrator does not.
   - Options: name herself before the court; let the client's testimony stand (Britby's "mulier," C p. 33); or say nothing.
   - The historical outcome is **no recorded sentence** (A p. 262). An open, non-punitive ending is authentic.
   - Avoid making arrest the default fate.
5. **Clergy clients and discretion.** Priests paid better (C p. 29; B p. 274), and Khoury links this to a climate of clerical scandal (C p. 34). This could become a small hook or secret mechanic in which she knows things about churchmen. Handle it as worker's knowledge and leverage, not blackmail played for laughs. It also works as anticlerical flavour without mocking faith.
6. **Women who love her.** The record's unpaid relationships with women (A pp. 263–264; B p. 274) support trans-lesbian and bisexual daughters as ordinary. Do not treat "as a man" in the record as evidence against her womanhood (Henningsen's second reading, A p. 263).
7. **A quotable echo.** A loading tip or codex line could read: *In 1394 a London clerk wrote that she was "calling herself Eleanor."* The quotation is cited and she is not claimed for anything.

### 2.2 Guardrails: sex work

Follow `sw_books_a.md` and `sw_books_b.md` and the memory note on sex work in faiths.
- **Sex work is one path of several.** Embroidery and tapster work stand beside it, as in the record (A pp. 251–252). Never make it the defining or inevitable fate of daughters. That would be the "trans women belong in the trade" trope.
- **Workers have their own voice.** She negotiated her price (B p. 273), chose her clients, and preferred the better-paying ones. Do not write rescue arcs, shame arcs, or "redeemed by marriage" endings.
- **Acknowledge constraint without fatalism.** Henningsen notes that survival pressures were real (A p. 259 n. 34). Event text can admit that choices were narrow. It should not turn that into a tragedy about her identity.
- **No titillation.** Do not reproduce the record's list of sex acts in loc. The court's fixation on her sexual history is the hostile part of the source (B p. 274; Bychowski's critique of cis sensationalising, C p. 41).
- **Don't treat Anna and Elizabeth as villains.** The "procuress who led her astray" reading is Karras and Boyd's and has been contested (A p. 259; C p. 38).

### 2.3 Guardrails: the court record as a hostile source

- The record was produced under interrogation by a scribe hostile to her gender. It was translated with masculine pronouns that the Latin mostly does not require (A p. 251 n. 6; B p. 273). In-game, the *court* can speak in the court's terms, framed as the court's voice. The narrator always uses *she* and her name.
- Do not build "deception reveal" events, where a client discovers and reacts with violence or disgust. Henningsen's critique of the make-believer trope (A p. 254) applies to game writing as much as to history.
- Do not adopt Goldberg's satire reading or Evans's "false trader" as narration (C pp. 34, 36).
- Keep all of this **pre-1453** and English. Henningsen's later comparisons (A pp. 265–266) are outside the mod.

The volume came out of the 2020 Gender and Medieval Studies conference (p. v). It has three parts, Bodies, Spaces and Transcendence, each opened by a senior scholar's short introduction. Its stated method is to read gender and movement together, using trans studies' sense of "trans" as motion (Introduction pp. 6–8, citing Kim and Bychowski 2019).

### Usable material (beyond Rykener)

- **Rhineland women's house (Cole and Victoria).**
  - Hildegard's 1150 move from the double monastery at Disibodenberg to her own community at Rupertsberg, against the monks' resistance, freed her music and writing (pp. 64–66).
  - Her play *Ordo virtutum* has 18 women's roles and one man's, roughly the size of her community (p. 66).
  - Abbess Tenxwind objected to the "vain and rich devotional performances" at Rupertsberg (p. 58 n. 30).
  - *Causae et curae* (from c. 1152) covers menstruation, conception and childbirth (p. 66).
  - **Use**: flavour for the Rhineland as a region where women's religious houses held cultural authority. That is useful background for Good Ladies content near Worms and Mainz (Burchard). It is a Catholic setting; do not make Hildegard part of any mod faith.
- **Side-wound amulets (Sexon).** Images of Christ's side wound were rolled up and tied to the body against illness, period pain, danger in childbirth and sudden death (p. 88). This is a candidate "Christian neighbours" artifact or household-charm flavour. Sexon also cites DeVun on an early-15th-century German alchemical *Book of the Holy Trinity* (Frater Ulmannus) with a "JesusMary" hermaphrodite (p. 84).
- **Norse law on gender insult (Price).** Strong hostile-voice material for any Norse daughter content:
  - *Gulaþingslǫg* (13th-c. manuscript) outlaws *ýki* such as saying a man "is a woman every ninth night and has borne children" (p. 236 n. 56).
  - *Grágás* lets a man kill someone who calls him *ragr*, *stroðinn* or *sorðinn* (p. 237).
  - *Gulaþingslǫg* lists comparing a man to a *portkona* (harbour-woman, a sex worker) among the gravest insults (p. 238).
  - Ármann Jakobsson's gloss on *ergi* covers "the blurring of gender role," "shape-shifting and sorcery" (p. 236 n. 55).
  - Hallr says it is unclear whether Þorgils is "either a man or a woman" (p. 236).
  - **Use**: hostile and legal voice and persecution events. Þorgils is a father who nurses and is not a daughter, so he is not mod material.
- **Volga and steppe flavour (Upham).**
  - Ibn Faḍlān reports Ghuzziyah men and women washing naked together in the river without adultery (p. 125).
  - The Rūs ritual leader called the "Angel of Death" is linguistically ambiguous in gender (p. 127). This is **not** evidence of a daughter, so do not use it as one.
  - The Rūs funeral involves the killing of an enslaved woman. **Exclude.**
- **Anchoresses (Johnson).**
  - The *Ancrene Wisse* triad is obedience, chastity and stability of place (p. 172).
  - Enclosure followed a funeral-like rite (p. 172).
  - Johnson argues the anchorhold let a woman "construct her own version of femininity" away from patriarchal policing (p. 194, paraphrased).
  - **Design idea (speculative)**: enclosure as a refuge path for a daughter in a Christian realm. No medieval case supports a male-assigned anchoress. Label it invented if used.
- **Fairy ladies (Bonsall).** Fairy spaces in Middle English romance shine with gems in the dark and are ruled by powerful ladies: Melior; the fairy queen of *Thomas of Erceldoune* (pp. 145–146). ## 4. Open questions and further sources

**Open questions.**
1. **Exact wording of the record.** Were the Burford ring-givers friars (Khoury) or "monks" (Bychowski)? Did Phillip give her the gowns or did she take them? What did she say when he asked for them back? Check the Karras and Boyd translation (1996, pp. 111–112), Bychowski 2021, or the LMA image.
2. **Date.** Was it 11 December 1394 or 1395? A, B and my weekday calculation favour 1394. Confirm Fressh's mayoral year.
3. **Elizabeth Brouderer.** Does her surname mean she was herself a broiderer (embroiderer), which would tie her to Eleanor's Oxford trade? This is my speculation, not in the sources. Goldberg (2014) tried to identify her in the archives (C p. 36).
4. **Lollard Conclusions.** Khoury puts the attack on clerical sodomy in the *Twelfth* of the 1395 Twelve Conclusions (C p. 34). My recollection is that it is the *Third*. Check Anne Hudson, *Selections from English Wycliffite Writings*.
5. **Where does an English daughter belong?** Is this Good Ladies-in-England content, generic content for any daughter, or a seed for a separate English faith? Note that v2 starts in 1178. Rykener-style events fit about 1350–1453.
6. **Trade mechanics.** Should "embroideress" and "tapster" be modifiers, a lifestyle focus, or event-only? Check what vanilla offers (`ck3_vanilla`, `ck3_docs`).
