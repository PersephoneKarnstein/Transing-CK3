## 0. Framing warning

- **An "initial" and "necessarily superficial" survey.** The author says the material is "neither comprehensive nor representative" (p. 151) and the conclusion is "a cautious affirmative" (p. 159).
- **Almost wholly second-hand.** The medieval Latin evidence comes through Skemer (2006), Harmening (1979), Flint (1991) and Kieckhefer; the objects through Spier, Pitarakis and Bierbrauer. Regino and Burchard are cited, not read in context.
- **Most of the objects are eastern.** Section 3 (Byzantine amulets, gems, bronze crosses) does not bear on the Rhineland.
- **Jewish material.** The holy-rider amulets (Solomon, Sisinnios) are cited partly to Bohak (p. 151 n. 24). Under `bohak_ancient_jewish_magic.md` they stay out of every faith of the mod.

## 1. Findings

### 1a. The Church's line, with dates

- **Condemnations.** Laodicea (364) called phylacteries poison for the soul; Pope Gelasius I (d. 496) prohibited them, confirmed by the Council of Ratisbon (742); Augustine made magic a traffic with demons; Caesarius of Arles warned against clergy who used amulets (p. 149). Boniface brought one Adalbert before the pope in 745 for turning liturgy and angel-names to magic (p. 152).
- **Regino of Prüm (d. 915).** In his "book of rules" (*De ecclesiasticis disciplinis*, i.e. *De synodalibus causis*) he summarises Gregory the Great: all who consult sorcerers or use amulets are to be banned (II.356). He also gives a later penitential's **three years' penance, one on bread and water, for making amulets** (II.444) (p. 152). He keeps Augustine's view of magic as demonic remnants of pagan practice (p. 153).
- **Burchard of Worms** carried Isidore's history of magic on, with Rabanus Maurus and Hincmar (p. 153), and like Regino prescribed penance for making amulets (p. 161).
- **Magic and heresy** were often used interchangeably in medieval treatises (p. 153). Charlemagne's capitulary of 789 forbade baptising bells against hail (p. 153). Magic was also practised at courts and among nobles, not only by the poor (p. 153).

### 1b. The exception: the cross

- Chrysostom praised the cross as the only true protection; Eligius (588–660) the sign of the cross with the Creed or Pater Noster; at Cluny the cross was said to have more intercessory power than any saint (p. 150). Aquinas still allowed amulets bearing the cross (p. 150).
- Harmening's summary, as Korsvoll gives it: every amulet except the cross was "to be considered demonic" (p. 152).
- **Caesarius of Heisterbach** (13th c., a Cistercian) counted the Ave Maria, the Benedicite and the sign of the cross as *medicina* against the Devil (p. 160, via Skemer).

### 1c. The West and the Rhineland

- **Little early material.** Between the 5th and 8th–9th centuries the Latin West has so few amulets that Skemer wondered whether Augustine and Caesarius had suppressed them (pp. 157–58).
- **Women's brooches.** North of the Alps, Christian motifs (crosses, doves) appear on grave goods only in the 7th century. In the 8th and 9th centuries bronze or copper fibulae with Christian decoration became common **in women's dress** across the Carolingian empire (p. 158).
- **A cross with old words.** A Greek-style silver pectoral cross (8.2 cm) found at Lausanne cathedral in 1910 carries crosses and variants of ABRAXAS and ABRACADABRA (p. 158). Deonna reported similar crosses from early medieval Germanic burials (p. 159).
- **The Synod of Trier, 1227.** It ordered baptismal water, holy oil and the Host locked away "so as to avoid abuse in magic" (p. 155, via Harmening).
- **Clergy as makers.** Until literacy spread, only clergy had the letters, tools and models to make written amulets (p. 161). Saints made and handed out amulets (p. 154). Devotional books were cut up for parchment and holy text (p. 155).
- **A 12th-century English case.** Durham, Hunter MS 100: a blood-staunching charm written on a leaf with liturgical phrases, and a lead-cross fever amulet with nonsense rhymes. The page was kept, with a marginal warning against using it (pp. 159, 161).

### 1d. Conclusion

Over the centuries amulets moved toward the cross shape and church imagery, in East and West alike, while older signs survived in a few cases into the 12th century (pp. 159, 161–62).
