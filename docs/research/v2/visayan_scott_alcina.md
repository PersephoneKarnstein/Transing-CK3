**Framing warning.**
- Both books are built on Spanish colonial texts and friar dictionaries of 1521–1668. Scott says his question is what Spaniards "actually say" (foreword, p. vii).
- Scott writes from the dictionaries of Sánchez (Leyte–Samar, 1617) and Méntrida (Panay, 1637). He notes, **"No early Cebuano lexicon is known"** (n. 10, p. 278). Every Visayan word used for Cebu is therefore borrowed from Waray or Hiligaynon.
- Alcina was a missionary who reads the priestesses as the devil's instruments. Everything he says of the asog comes through that frame and through informants recalling "ancient times" from forty to a hundred years after conversion.

## 1. Verdict update

1. **Still viable as a marked reconstruction, now better grounded.** Scott and Alcina both put the asog inside the Visayan priesthood (Scott p. 84; Alcina Bk 3 ch. 13 via vol. 1 pp. 155–156). Neither gives any datable material for 1178.
2. **Alcina's own words fit the gate.** The asog "considered themselves more like women than like men" in life, relationships and occupations. They wore the women's foot-length *lambung*, did women's work, danced as women, and were the only Visayan men who went untattooed (vol. 1 pp. 141, 155).
3. **The lambon is not an asog garment.** It is the ordinary Visayan foot-length wrap, recorded at **Cebu in 1565** (Scott p. 29; Alcina vol. 1 p. 123). Wearing it is wearing what women wear, which suits the faith's reading.
4. **The Call by illness is in the source** (Scott p. 84), but rule 22 still bars it as a gate. The source's other roads, apprenticeship "frequently [to] a relative" and being chosen by a diwata, carry the gate.
5. **The doctrines are well sourced**: bilateral equal inheritance, divorce, a chief wife with secondary wives, uncle–niece marriage, no adultery crime, wailing funerals, and capital punishment for witches. Section 5 maps them to CK3.
6. **Still missing**: Alcina vols. 3–4 in full, Loarca's text checked directly, and any 12th-century archaeology (Junker, Nishimura).

### Trade, raiding and anything older than 1521

- **Women's trades** (ch. 3, pp. 72–73): spinning, weaving, sewing "were all women's work, sometimes for hire, and so was the marketing of their products". Pottery was "also a female craft". Firm.
- **Cloth for China** (pp. 70, 76): the Chinese called Mindoro abaca *yu-da* "in the thirteenth century", and Visayan cotton and thread "had a steady market in China". Firm for the 13th century, the nearest dated hook to 1178.
- **Song iron** (p. 54): Song traders were delivering cauldrons and pig iron "at least by the thirteenth century", and Visayan smiths broke up Chinese pans for iron. Firm.
- **Who traded** (p. 74, "The Chinese Enigma"): Spaniards never saw Chinese ships in the Visayas before 1569. Bornean and Luzon traders were "called Chinese among these islanders". This is a caution for any in-period "Chinese trader" event: in the Visayas the trader is more likely Bornean.
- **Butuan** (ch. 9, p. 164): direct contact with Champa in the 10th century. Envoys petitioned for tribute status on **3 October 1003**. The dossier (row 3, from Scott 1983) has a first mission in 1001; flag the discrepancy.
- **Raiding** (ch. 8, pp. 153–157):
  - Raids aimed "to control people, not territory": slaves, bahandi, and the abduction of brides of rank (pp. 153–155).
  - Before sailing, raiders asked the diwata to rock a small boat (p. 83).
  - Scott himself answers the "pirate" label by comparison with Charles V's orders to Magellan: "Mangayaw was the Visayans'" maritime law (p. 157). **Use this framing**: raiding is told as the record tells it, not as a people's nature.
- **Forgeries** (n. 1, p. 277): the *Maragtas* is "a copyrighted 1907 local history" whose legend "has carelessly been considered a pre-Hispanic document". This verifies the dossier's caution on Balengkaka.
- **Nothing in Barangay** treats the P'i-she-ye raids or any Visayan event before the 10th century.

**Translators' notes worth keeping (2002, scholarly, not 17th c.):**
- **Lisboa's Bikol dictionary** (cited at p. 155, *date and page per translators*): asug "dressed like women and imitated them in all their words and acts; usually they did not get married."
- **Sánchez's Waray dictionary** (1711 printing; first compiled before 1617) (p. 489, n. 10):
  - *bayug* is "a man who behaves like a woman and dresses as a woman", with *asug* and *bantut* as synonyms;
  - asug is also used of animals and trees that bear no fruit;
  - the recorded insult is "*Babayinun ka*. You are nothing; you are a woman."
  - This is an **in-culture hostile voice**: a Visayan put-down recorded by a friar.
- **Kobak and Gutiérrez's own gloss** (p. 489): "today we would term [them] as transvestites". They also note *bayut* and *bakla* as present-day Samar–Leyte words. That is living usage and **not** for the faith (dossier risk 2).

**Alcina on what came before Spain (Bk 1 ch. 1, p. 75):** Bornean ships came yearly to trade cloth and food for gold and, in famine, for children sold to them. The Chinese traded "even many years earlier" in cloth, gold and porcelain. These are his elders' memories, firm only as 17th-century tradition.

- Bk 4 chs. 12–16: marriage, property and the Sumangga romance. Scott quotes 4:59, 4:218, 4:227 and 4:248–56.

## 4. Corrections

### To the first-pass dossier

1. **Row 14 (Loarca's afterlife).**
   - Scott confirms the gist but says nothing of a mountain in Borneo. Siburanen takes Cebuanos and Boholanos to "Borneo"; the mountain (Madyaas) is the Kiniray-a destination.
   - Magwayen is the *Panay* ferryman. The dossier's "Maguayen ferries them" for Cebu needs Loarca checked directly (B&R vol. 5).
2. **Row 3 (Butuan).** *Barangay* p. 164 dates the first tribute petition 3 October 1003; Scott 1983 gives 1001. Cite both, or say "c. 1001–1003".
3. **Row 22 (Alcina via Brewer).** The dossier's wording is now verified at vol. 1 p. 155 (the Kobak–Gutiérrez rendering). Add three things the dossier lacked:
   - the asog went untattooed (A1);
   - the Sulat witness danced, made pots, embroidered and sewed (A10);
   - Alcina's motive-language ("hide their shortcoming", A5–A6) is hostile and body-aimed, so it must stay out of loc.
4. **Row 23 (Sumangga).**
   - Scott spells the princess *Bugbung Humasanun* (not "Humayanun"; Alcina's own spelling *unverified*).
   - The song has **six** forays in order: Tandag in Caraga; Yambig and Camiguin; Siquijor and Dapitan; Mindanao and Jolo; "Grand China"; heaven, which fails (pp. 101–103).
   - It ends with the hero threatening to strip her hairpiece, and she yields "terrified in her heart lest he dishonor her". Scott calls Alcina's telling ironic. **This is not a romance to celebrate.** See event 7.
5. **Row 27 (three orders).** Confirmed (S26, S34–S36). Add that the timawa were the datu's marriage envoys (p. 131). That is the in-source model for an envoy office, beside Méntrida's rower-of-the-diwata gloss (row 19, not in these books).
6. **Vanilla localisation (dossier §2).** Confirmed. The Visayan term is *babaylan* (Scott p. 84), *baylan* in Mindanao and the epics (p. 168), *baliyan* in Bikol (p. 185) and *katulunan* in Tagalog (p. 238). Vanilla's `dayawism_priest_male: "katalonan"` is the Tagalog word.
7. **Dossier §2 "Balengkaka from the Maragtas … *unverified*".** Now **verified**: Scott n. 1, p. 277.
8. **Sikatuna.** He is not merely "a historical 1565 Bohol name". He is "the celebrated Si Katuna", a known datu with vassals in Leyte (p. 128). This raises the bar for leaving him unconverted and untouched by the seed.
9. **The Call.** Scott does give illness as the road in (S10). The dossier's design stays, but the note should say so honestly: the source's commonest road is *excluded by the mod's rule*, not absent from the record.
10. **Witchcraft.** Vanilla Dayawism's `doctrine_witchcraft_accepted` is contradicted by S25: Visayans executed suspected witches and sorcerers. The babaylan are not those people. For the faith, see §5.

### To the Lambon sketch (`south_reading_list.md` §2A)

1. **"The lambon is the long skirt … the asog wore"** → the lambung was the ordinary Visayan foot-length garment, women's housewear in Alcina (p. 123), described at Cebu by Juan de la Isla in 1565 (Scott p. 29). The asog wore *the women's* garment. The faith name still works, and better: it names womanhood, not a costume.
2. **"Asog attested in the Visayas only in 1668"** → also Scott pp. 20, 69, 84 (synthesising Sánchez 1617, Méntrida 1637 and Alcina), plus the dossier's Méntrida and Diaz.
3. **"Sickness event: catalepsy, mutism or madness … Alcina's mute indian"** → the Sulat witness's muteness is not a call or a sickness in Alcina, and nobody fell mute to become asog. **Remove** the mutism link: it turns a disabled man's life into a mechanic.
4. **"Married or widowed before she can teach" (Bolinao)** → Zambal. The Visayan record says apprentices joined "some older babaylan, frequently a relative" (p. 84). Use that and drop the marriage rule.
5. **"Mark her forehead with the blood among the women"** → in Scott, the beneficiaries' foreheads (p. 85); in Pigafetta, husbands first. Mark it as adaptation.
6. **"Inheritance follows bilateral kinship (Brewer ¶4)"** → now sourced directly: equal inheritance by both sexes (p. 143), a wife's own property (p. 142).
7. **"Late pressure: Islam reaching Sulu"** → out of range for 1178, as the dossier said.
8. **"Bolinao holy site"** → remove (Zambal). Bohol is now better attested: the Baclayon jar burials, the golden-boat seer, the Sumangga princess and Si Katuna.

## 5. Revised design

### Name and voice

- **Working name:** keep *tm_lambon* (Lambon), glossed in the README as "the women's long wrap".
- **Daughters' word, in the faith's own voice:** *daitan*, "befriended", from Scott's *daitun* and Alcina's *daitan* (S9, A10). It means befriended by a diwata. It is Waray, not Cebuano, and the note must say so (n. 10, p. 278).
- The faith calls its daughters women; the narrator never calls them "asog" or uses "transvestite". The faith's voice may say: *"The old women said the asog lived as women. We say they were."* That is a claim in the faith's voice, not the narrator's (rule 19: kin, not proof).

### Gate: the Road, the Lambong, the Hog

1. **The Road (choose one; each can be declined with "Not today.").**
   - *The kinswoman:* an older babaylan relative takes her as alabay (S11).
   - *The friend:* an unrelated babaylan teaches her (Chirino, dossier row 18).
   - *The diwata's choosing:* a dream in which a diwata names her (A11).
   - Illness is **not** a road. A world-sent fever may be flavour, never a requirement (rule 22; S10 noted).
2. **The Lambong (a season of women's work, measured by work done).**
   - She weaves, sells cloth, makes pots and works the swidden (S36; pp. 72–73; A9).
   - She dances *taruk* and *kigal* in the women's line at a feast (S21).
   - She dresses her hair in the women's rosette with switches (A14).
   - She stays *mapuraw*, uninked (A1, S2). In the faith's voice: *she was never owed the warrior's ink, and never wanted it.*
3. **The Hog (recognition by women).**
   - Two elder babaylan (S15) with horned headdress, trumpets and dance; the spear stroke (S16); the meat to the shore raft (S17).
   - Her forehead is marked among the women (adaptation, marked as such).
   - The rite waits if two elders are absent and never fails.
   - The flesh the spear touched is hers if she is to be babaylan (S17).

### Offices

- **Babaylan / daitan (holy).** Healing for fees: choice cuts, the head, heirloom porcelain from datus (S12, S19). Paganito for crops, newlyweds, war and the dead (S20). The cleansing of bones at secondary burial (S30).
- **Mourner (non-holy).** The old women who sing dirges addressed to the dead (S27) and the female eulogists at funerals (p. 98). This is a road to standing that is not holy office (rule 7).
- **Weaver-trader (non-holy).** Cloth sold in market and to Chinese buyers (pp. 72, 76).
- **Envoy (non-holy).** The timawa's marriage-envoy duty (S36), joined to Méntrida's rower of the diwata (dossier row 19). **Invented:** a daughter in a timawa's role.

### Events (each tied to rows)

1. **The Hog of the Two Old Women** (S14–S17, dossier rows 9–11). The gate rite.
2. **The Bones in the Chest** (S30). A year after a death, the family asks her to cleanse the bones. Quiet, kin-centred, a babaylan's ordinary work.
3. **Gold for the Crossing** (S32). A poor family's dead went without gold, and Pandaki takes rich paganito. The House pays for the rite. The faith's voice offers no promise, only the rite done this year (rule 15).
4. **The Rocking Boat** (p. 83; S20). Before a raid a datu asks her to sit in the outrigger-less boat and ask the diwata. She can read it as yes, read it as no, or refuse to ask. The raid stays off-screen.
5. **Mapuraw** (A1, S2; Sánchez insult p. 489). At a feast a tattooed timawa (Dayawist, named) sneers "*Babayinun ka*", meaning "you are a woman", as an insult. The old women answer for her in her own words. **Hostile line**: in-culture, attributed, aimed at her standing, not her body.
6. **The Women's Line** (S21). At a datu's wedding feast (S37) she dances *kigal* with the women. A choice of partner closes the dance; men and women do not touch.
7. **Sumangga's Song** (Alcina via Scott pp. 101–103). A bard sings the six forays at court, and the faith keeps its distance from the ending.
   - A datu asks her to bless a raid "like Sumangga's". She may bless, refuse or bargain.
   - The princess's threatened dishonour is **not** sung in the event. Only the buyo riddles and the forays are.
   - The captives are numbers in a song, never shown.
8. **The Mourning Wine** (S31). As the ranking woman of a house she carries wine to allies after a death. The source's killing that ends mourning is excluded; here mourning ends with the wine voyage alone (an adaptation; say so).
9. **Cloth for the Junks** (pp. 70, 74, 76; dossier rows 5–6). In 1178 a Bornean trader, "called Chinese" (p. 74), buys her cotton. A trade event that carries the dossier's "world ends east of Java" line.
10. **Cover Your Head** (S23). An elder tells her, "Do not uncover your head lest you be desired." It is the first time anyone has warned her as a woman. She may laugh, or keep the kerchief.

### What is invented (to state in the README and design note)

- The community, its House, its gate and the word *daitan* used for its daughters.
- Any 1178 rite. Every rite text dates 1521–1668.
- The daughter as envoy, as ranking woman carrying the mourning wine, and as babaylan who ends a mourning without a killing.
- The forehead marked among the women.
- The Bornean holy site.
