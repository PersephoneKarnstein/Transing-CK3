# Witches for v2: two papers read, plus a pre-1453 evidence timeline

Scope: could Crusader Dolls build a faith on *actual historical witches* inside CK3's timeline (starts 867, 1066, 1178; game ends 1453)? This note covers (1) and (2) close readings of two student papers the user supplied, (3) a timeline of pre-1453 witch-adjacent evidence from brief open-web checks, (4) a verdict, and (5) guardrails, open questions and books.

Pronouns: both paper authors are referred to as *they* (pronouns not stated in the papers). Historical people are given the pronouns their own sources support where that is clear (Eleanor Rykener: she). Joan of Arc is called *they* here because trans readings of Joan are contested; the trial record says *she*.

Quote rule: every verbatim quote is 25 words or fewer and carries a page or URL. Everything else is paraphrase.

## 1. Paper A: Hollister, "Lenses, Focus, and Fluidity" (2022)

**Bibliographic line.** Reese Hollister, "Lenses, Focus, and Fluidity: Lessons from Medieval Queer History," *The Gettysburg Historical Journal* 21 (2022), article 6, journal pp. 45–62. Undergraduate paper (Manhattan College '23). https://cupola.gettysburg.edu/ghj/vol21/iss1/6

**Argument.** A short historiographical essay. Hollister argues that reading medieval sources through queer and trans lenses shows gender and sexuality were more fluid than assumed, and that this supports present-day trans and queer rights. It compares Bullough and Brundage's *Handbook of Medieval Sexuality* (1996/2000) with Spencer-Hall and Gutt's *Trans and Genderqueer Subjects in Medieval Hagiography* (2021). It then surveys "lesbian-like" women (Bennett; Jacqueline Murray) and cross-dressing (Bullough), and ends with Eleanor Rykener. It is almost entirely secondary. Nearly every claim comes through four edited-volume chapters, and it does not deal with witchcraft directly.

**Usable points (journal page numbers).**

- **Joan of Arc (p. 45).** The paper opens with Joan and follows Bychowski (via Spencer-Hall and Gutt) in arguing that masculine dress and a masculine social role drove Joan's execution. It quotes the line that Joan's death was "the direct result of medieval transphobia" (p. 45). It also notes that queer historians are not trying to prove Joan transmasculine (pp. 45–46). This is useful for framing Joan's trial (see timeline), but it is secondhand.
- **Method: read acts, not identities (pp. 54–56).** Bennett's "lesbian-like" category applies definitions to practices, not persons (p. 54). Hollister extends this to gender: study gender-nonconforming *actions* because medieval people lacked our identity vocabulary (p. 56). This is the right method for the mod. A witch faith should be built from documented practices and beliefs, not invented identities.
- **Asymmetry of cross-dressing (pp. 57–59).** Following Bullough, female-to-male dress could earn respect, while male-to-female dress lowered status and was read as a sexual ruse (p. 58). Hollister says Gregory of Tours treated male cross-dressing that was not sexual as paganism linked to witchcraft (p. 58). **Flag:** this claim is secondhand through Bullough and has no Gregory reference. The well-attested early condemnations of men in women's dress at the January Kalends come from Caesarius of Arles and Frankish councils, not clearly from Gregory. Verify in Bullough (*Handbook*, pp. 223–242) before using it. It matters because it is the one place in either paper that links male-to-female dress with pagan or witch coding.
- **Penitentials (p. 58).** The penitential of Silos sets one year's penance for male cross-dressing, and "St. Hubert's" penitential sets three years (via Bullough). This is usable as evidence that men in women's dress were a pastoral concern before 1000. It does not show a cult.
- **Knights in women's dress at a 13th-century tournament (p. 59).** This was comic or shock display, an exception to the rule.
- **Eleanor Rykener (pp. 59–60).** Through Boyd and Karras (1995), Hollister presents the London interrogation of Rykener, who called herself Eleanor and worked as an embroideress in Oxford, "she lived and worked with women" (p. 60). Hollister also hedges that the record covers acts, not feelings (p. 60). This is the strongest pre-1453 English trace of a person assigned male who lived as a woman. It is **not witchcraft material**, but it is good context for how a "daughter" might appear in a 14th-century record.
- **Lesbian-like material (pp. 51–56).** Augustine's letter to nuns (c. 423), the Penitential of Theodore (three years for a woman who "practices vice with a woman"), and the 1405 Laurence/Jehanne case. This is context for women-only spaces, not witchcraft.

**Weaknesses.**
- Donatus is dated "d. 355" (p. 53). The rule for nuns cited by Jacqueline Murray is by Donatus of Besançon (7th century). This is a factual slip.
- The claim that "about twelve" medieval lesbians are confirmed, nearly all executed in the 1400s (pp. 51–52), compresses Bennett; check the original.
- Its conclusion that the past was "more fluid" is asserted rather than shown.
- Treat it as a reading list (Bullough, Bennett, Murray, Boyd and Karras, Spencer-Hall and Gutt), not as a source to cite in-game.

## 2. Paper B: De Lis, "Witches as Queer(ed) Comrades" (2021)

**Bibliographic line.** Iris S. De Lis, "Witches as Queer(ed) Comrades: How Patriarchy, Sexism and Religious Fanaticism Fueled the Early Modern European Witch Hunts," University Honors Thesis 1138, Portland State University, 2021. Adviser Kathleen Merrow. https://doi.org/10.15760/honors.1169. Page numbers below follow the thesis's own "DE LIS n" running heads (56 PDF pages).

**Argument.** The thesis is a literature review through a feminist and queer lens. It adapts Cathy J. Cohen's "Punks, Bulldaggers, and Welfare Queens" (1997; and the 2019 follow-up) to argue that accused witches were *made* "queer subjects" by church, state and neighbours, whatever their sexuality or practice (pp. 3–4, 12–13, 40–44). The thesis argues that gender has "fallen out" of witch-hunt historiography (Whitney, p. 8) and centres on women historians (Barstow, Wiesner-Hanks, Purkiss). It traces the feminisation of witchcraft through Nider and the *Malleus* (pp. 16–22) and covers male witches (Kounine, Apps and Gow, pp. 25–29). It then widens to link the hunts to colonial genocide, Atlantic slavery and Baconian science "feminising" nature (pp. 32–40). The author describes themself as queer and trans (p. 14). The focus is **early modern** (post-1450). Little bears directly on 867–1453.

**Usable points.**

- **Medieval chronology of feminisation (pp. 18–21), the most useful part for the mod.** Via Michael D. Bailey (2002):
  - Clergy in the 10th and 11th centuries readily linked women with magic. They saw it as harmless "superstitious belief and susceptibility to demonic deception" (Bailey 125, quoted p. 19).
  - From the 12th to 14th centuries, learned necromancy, a male and clerical practice, made clergy take *maleficium* seriously (pp. 19–20).
  - Nider's *Formicarius* is the "earliest appearance of a strong association between women and witchcraft in authoritative literature" (Bailey 121, quoted pp. 18–19).
  - In-game, this supports a phase shift: before about 1300 the "night ladies" are treated as delusion and penance; after about 1400 they become a demonic sect.
- **Witches as dependent agents of a male devil (p. 20).** Via Wiesner-Hanks: witches came to be seen as people "used by the devil" rather than people commanding demons, which fit gender norms. This fixes the stereotype the mod should *not* adopt as the faith's self-understanding.
- **Gender rather than sex as the core of "witch" (pp. 26–28).** Apps and Gow argue that male witches were implicitly feminised: "biological sex was not, at the conceptual level, the primary characteristic of the witch; gender was" (p. 27). De Blécourt, Kent, Gaskill and Rowlands push back: male witches failed *masculine* codes, or "bad neighbour" is the better category (pp. 27–28). This is a useful frame for male-assigned members of a witch faith being coded as feminised by accusers. It is early modern evidence, however.
- **Kounine on Württemberg, 1497–1750 (pp. 28–29).** Kounine found no "good nomadic woman," and independent women without male protection were likely to be accused (p. 29). Post-1453, but it sets the tone for persecution events.
- **Saint/witch mirror (pp. 29–32).** Herzig shows that Krämer promoted Italian women mystics after the *Malleus*. The holy woman and the witch were mirror images (Herzig 24, quoted p. 32). This is a good design hook: the same visionary woman can be read as saint or witch depending on who sponsors her.
- **Murray via Purkiss (p. 9).** The thesis reports Purkiss's point that Murray's critics gendered their attacks. It also quotes Purkiss that Murray's thesis is "intrinsically improbable" and "commands little or no allegiance within the modern academy" (p. 9). So De Lis does **not** endorse Murray.

**Reliance on Federici, Murray and the "nine million" myth, and other weak points.**

- **Nine million:** **not used.** De Lis explicitly adopts Wiesner-Hanks's 100,000–200,000 tried and 40,000–60,000 executed (p. 7). This is the mainstream range.
- **Murray:** cited only via Purkiss and labelled improbable (p. 9). No reliance.
- **Federici:** relied on for the old woman as inverted wise woman and the broom as phallus (pp. 23–25, citing *Caliban and the Witch* pp. 192–193). Federici's primitive-accumulation thesis is contested by witch-trial historians and is about c. 1550–1650. It is not usable for pre-1453 design. Note too that the "wise woman" premise is itself a romantic trope.
- **Barstow:** *Witchcraze* (1994) is a key source. Barstow's own death-toll estimates sit at the high end and her framing has been criticised. De Lis uses her for gender ratios (about 80–85% women executed), which is broadly mainstream.
- **The *Malleus* "cited in virtually every subsequent witchcraft trial" (p. 7, quoting Schuyler):** an overstatement. Recent work (Broedel, Mackay) stresses its uneven influence, and many regions and courts never used it.
- **The "Haeresis est maxima…" motto on the *Malleus* "title page" (p. 17, via Neave):** dubious. The early incunables lack such a title-page line. The text's Part I Q.1 makes a narrower claim that obstinate disbelief savours of heresy. Verify before repeating.
- **The 1486 and 1487 dates for the *Malleus* both appear (pp. 16, 30).** The first edition is usually dated 1486/87.
- **Agnes Sampson and torture (pp. 38–39, via Merchant):** this was a **Scottish** case (North Berwick, 1590, James VI). The thesis frames it beside the claim that torture was banned in England, which conflates jurisdictions.
- **Pages 32–40 (colonialism, slavery, Bacon, climate change)** are argumentative and analogical, not evidence about witches. Pages 12–13 and 42–43 are present-day political digressions. Nothing there is usable for the mod.
- **Overall:** a sincere, readable undergraduate synthesis with sound headline numbers. Its medieval content is all secondhand from Bailey, and it has no primary sources before the *Malleus* except via Bailey's summary of Nider. **It names no gender-nonconforming or male-to-female accused witches.** Its "queer" is Cohen's positional sense, not identity.

**Why historians reject Murray.** Norman Cohn (*Europe's Inner Demons*, 1975) showed that Murray quoted trial confessions selectively. She cut the impossible elements (flight, transformation) to make the rest look like real ritual, then read confessions extracted under torture and leading questions as ethnography. Her "coven of thirteen" rests on essentially one Scottish confession (Isobel Gowdie, 1662). Her horned god is the inquisitors' devil renamed. The sabbath stereotype (child-eating, orgy, animal-shaped god) was recycled from accusations against early Christians and medieval heretics, not observed. Keith Thomas, Hutton (*Triumph of the Moon*, 1999; *The Witch*, 2017) and others concur, and De Lis concedes it via Purkiss (p. 9). The pre-1453 record contains **no** organised witch religion. It contains (a) clerics describing and dismissing women's beliefs about a night-faring Lady, and (b) from about 1400, clerics inventing a diabolical sect out of trial confessions.

## 4. Verdict

**Viable? Yes, in a narrow form, without inventing a Murray-style secret pagan cult.** The single best-evidenced witch-adjacent thing inside 867–1453 is **a belief, held mostly by women and described by churchmen, that on set nights they join a great company of women following a Lady.** She is called Diana, Herodias, Holda, Abundia/Habonde, Satia or the *bonae dominae*. The company travels far, visits houses that leave out food and drink, and brings abundance. Bishops from Regino (906) to William of Auvergne (1230s) call this a demonic dream and give penance, not death. That is at least 350 years of consistent, edited, primary attestation. It is attested as **belief and household practice** (offerings, night vigils, the ladies' tree), not as a priesthood, temple or organised church.

**Most defensible form:**
1. **A folk devotion of the "Good Ladies,"** a night company of women around a Lady. In CK3 terms, a small folk faith or a hidden or secret faith sitting beneath a Christian majority. Its doctrine flavour would cover offerings left at night, ecstatic sleep and dream travel, abundance and fertility of the household, and healing. Church doctrine in-game should treat it the way the sources do: **delusion needing penance** (low hostility) until about 1320. Then escalate: *Super illius specula* 1326, Kyteler 1324, Milan 1390, Valais 1428, Nider 1430s. In that phase the same beliefs are recoded as diabolical heresy and persecution events fire. The Milan case (1384 penance, 1390 execution, same story) is the ideal template for this flip.
2. **Heresy coding as an overlay, not the base.** "Vauderie" shows clerics fusing witchcraft with Waldensians after 1430. Use this for persecution events and the accusers' vocabulary, not for the faith's own self-image.
3. **A secret-cult mechanic** (hidden faith, secret members) fits the record better than a public church. It is how accusers imagined witches, and how a lay devotion under a hostile church would really survive. But **do not** give it covens of 13, a horned god, sabbath orgies or child sacrifice. Those are the accusers' fantasy and Murray's error. Vanilla CK3 already has a Murray-flavoured witch secret and coven content (verify with `ck3_vanilla`), so the mod's version should differ deliberately.

**Where the "daughters" fit.** Directly: nowhere in the pre-1453 night-company sources does a male-assigned person appear as a member. The company is a "multitude of women." Adjacent material:
- the Kalends men in women's dress, condemned as pagan (6th–11th c.);
- Eleanor Rykener (1394, not witchcraft);
- Joan's trial (1431), where gender-nonconforming dress was the capital issue;
- post-1453, the Sicilian companies that included men among the *donas*, and the Romanian Mute in women's dress inside a Herodias-linked fairy troupe.

A defensible design is that the company is *of women*, and that "who counts as a woman of the company" is the faith's own answer. That is design, not a documented fact. Label it as such in the faith's description or design doc.

**Best start date: 1066.** Burchard's *Corrector* (c. 1008–12) gives the fullest pre-hunt text, with Diana, Herodias and Holda, and his Rhineland/Worms diocese gives a concrete home region. The church is in its "delusion and penance" phase, so the faith can exist at small scale without immediate hunts. **1178** also works and adds John of Salisbury (1159) and, soon after, Abundia and the *bonae dominae*, in France and England. **867** is possible but thinner. Regino writes about 40 years later, so placing the faith at 867 rests on reasonable inference that the belief predates its write-up, not on a document.

## 5. Guardrails, open questions, books

**Guardrails.**
- **No Murray.** No covens of 13, horned god, surviving Neolithic religion, or "Old Religion" framing in loc or design docs.
- **No living community as the faith.** Contemporary Wicca, Stregheria, Heathenry, the Aradia tradition (Leland 1899), feminist Goddess movements and Romanian căluș practitioners are living traditions and must not be presented as the mod's faith or as its continuation. Leland's *Aradia* in particular is a 19th-century text, not medieval evidence.
- **No "nine million."** If a death toll appears anywhere, use about 40,000–60,000 executions for 1450–1750 (Wiesner-Hanks via De Lis p. 7). Nearly all of these are post-1453 anyway.
- **Do not put accusers' fantasies in the faith's mouth.** Child-eating, devil-sex and cannibal feasts are inquisitorial stereotypes, also used against Jews, early Christians and heretics. They can appear in *persecution* event text as what clerics allege, clearly framed as such.
- **Keep Joan's gender unresolved.** Do not claim Joan as a "daughter." Joan's documented gender nonconformity was female-to-male. The trans readings in Spencer-Hall and Gutt are transmasculine-leaning and contested.
- **Names and glyphs:** Holda, Abundia, Habonde, Satia, Herodias and Diana are ASCII-safe. Run `tools/check_glyphs.py` on any Hungarian, Romanian or Old High German forms (e.g. ő, ș, ț).
- **Growth:** per the existing feedback, keep it a small, slow folk faith with no transparent caps.

**Open questions.**
1. Does Henningsen state that male members of the Sicilian companies were themselves called *donas*? (Check the chapter in Ankarloo and Henningsen, *Early Modern European Witchcraft*.)
2. Is the "Gregory of Tours" link between male cross-dressing and witchcraft (Hollister p. 58, via Bullough) real, or a slip for Caesarius of Arles or a council? Check Bullough, *Handbook*, pp. 223–242.
3. What is the exact wording of Burchard's Corrector questions on *Holda/unholda* and the closed-door night killers? Use the McNeill and Gamer translation or the Schmitz edition.
4. What is the gender ratio of the Valais accused in the primary sources? The "two-thirds men" figure comes from secondary summaries; check Ostorero et al. 1999.
