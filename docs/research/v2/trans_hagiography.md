# Spencer-Hall and Gutt, *Trans and Genderqueer Subjects in Medieval Hagiography* (2021): a full reading for v2

Scope: the whole volume read again, chapter by chapter, for the six existing faiths and the three v2 candidates (Imayō no Ie, the Lambong, the Good Ladies' night company), inside 867–1453. Two earlier notes cover parts of it, and this note does not repeat them in detail:

- `../byz_spencer_hall_gutt_hagiography.md` read the Introduction, chs. 4, 6, 8 and 10 and part of the Appendix for Adelphai Dorleyaiou. Its Ignatios event is already built (TODO.md, TO-TEST.md N4).
- `../norse_colwill_hagiography_chapter.md` read ch. 7 for Bædling.

What is new here: chs. 1, 2, 3, 5, 9 and the Epilogue, which the earlier notes did not read; a check of the earlier notes against the text; and a per-faith pass that includes the three v2 candidates.

Pronouns: the book's own text gives *she* for Alicia Spencer-Hall (p. 40), M.W. Bychowski (p. 22) and Martha G. Newman (p. 27), and *he* for Blake Gutt (p. 40). Everyone else (Szabo, McLoughlin, Elphick, Sexon, Wright, Colwill, Ogden, van Dijk) is *they*.

Quote rule: every verbatim quote is 25 words or fewer and carries a page cite. Page numbers are the print pages (the PDF's page index matches them). Everything else is paraphrase.

## 1. Bibliographic line

Alicia Spencer-Hall and Blake Gutt (eds), *Trans and Genderqueer Subjects in Medieval Hagiography*. Hagiography beyond Tradition. Amsterdam: Amsterdam University Press, 2021. ISBN 978-94-6298-824-8; e-ISBN 978-90-4854-026-6; DOI 10.5117/9789462988248. Ten chapters, an Epilogue (Mathilde van Dijk), and a "Trans and Genderqueer Studies Terminology, Language, and Usage Guide" (pp. 281–330).

## 2. Verdict

**Low for trans-feminine content; high for method; medium as a store of in-period Christian voices.**

- The volume has **no chapter on a person assigned male who lived as a woman**. The earlier note said this, and a full read confirms it. Its saints are AFAB monks (Joseph, Marinos, Euphrosyne/Esmarade), a woman made a man by an angel (Blanchandin·e), a eunuch read as a man (Ignatios), Christ's body, and AFAB-sexed Norse graves. The editors themselves say almost all their sources are Western European (p. 28).
- The nearest trans-feminine material is all **indirect**: a Cistercian monk "assigned female at death" whose abbot switches the Latin gender of the prayers (ch. 1); Cistercian tales of a monk unmanned by an angel and an abbot found smooth at death (ch. 1); Christ's feminised side wound on birthing girdles (ch. 5); a Spanish abbess re-formed from male to female in the womb (ch. 3, but born 1481, outside the window); the humoral idea that sex "could slide either way" (Epilogue).
- Wilgefortis and the *Roman de Silence* are **not discussed**. Wilgefortis gets one sentence, through DeVun (p. 137). *Silence* appears only in a bibliography title (Campbell 2019). Eugenia gets one footnote (the Vézelay capital, p. 172 n. 63). Nothing here changes beliefs.md F6 or `wsq_libby_wondrous_bodies.md`.
- **The new find is ch. 1's Cistercian material.** It is Rhineland, Franconia and Brabant, c. 1188 and after, almost exactly the v2 start, and it is the kind of story monks told one another about men becoming women. It is the best thing in the book for the Good Ladies candidate, as a Christian voice around them.
- Nothing for Yutai, the Pāvai faith, Badenya, Imayō no Ie or the Lambong, beyond the Appendix's guardrails.

## 4. Usable trans-feminine material (with page cites)

None of this is a trans woman in the record. Each item is a *motif* the faiths can meet, quote, or argue with.

**4.1 "Assigned female at death": the abbot changes the gender of the prayers (ch. 1, Rhineland, after 1188).**
- Engelhard opens: "This man was a woman although no one knew it" (p. 44). Joseph, born near Cologne, went on pilgrimage, served the archbishop of Cologne, carried a letter to the pope at Verona, was hanged and held up by an angel, and entered Schönau (pp. 48–49).
- At the funeral the body is seen by only a few. For everyone else the change is *verbal*: the abbot "changed (transivit) the gender (genus) from masculine to feminine" in his commendation (p. 53). "All those who are literate are astounded, hearing a woman commended" (p. 53). Every version keeps this detail (p. 53 n. 34).
- The monks refuse the name Joseph in their obituary, because "Joseph signified the masculine and not the feminine" (p. 55), and settle on "maid of God". The name Hildegund comes only in later versions (p. 55).
- Newman's second reading: the monks experience "a miracle, in which Joseph only becomes a woman after death" (p. 54). She asks whether his perfected soul, as Bride of Christ, became female (p. 54).
- **Why it matters for the mod.** This is a 12th-century Latin story in which the *community's words* make a woman: the abbot's grammar, the obituary's name. That is close to Ha-Nehpakhot's Turning (a prayer said aloud) and to "Declare Before Witnesses". It is still an AFAB story told by a cis author who insists on the body, so it is a motif, never a precedent.

**4.2 Cistercian tales of unmanning (ch. 1, p. 54).**
- Herbert of Clairvaux's *Liber* (1.3): a monk asks an angel for help with desire; in a dream the angel removes his penis and the temptation goes (p. 54).
- The *Cronica Villariensis* (Villers, Brabant): abbot William's body at death showed "no vestige of [male] genitals", only "a clear smooth area, bright and pure" (p. 54).
- Bernard of Clairvaux's Song of Songs sermons had monks perform the Bride, so that "they may ultimately become better men" (Engh, via Newman, p. 54).
- Byzantine parallel: Patriarch Methodios was counted a eunuch after being "castrated in dreams or visions" (Szabo, p. 112).
- **Read with care.** These are tales of *chastity*, not womanhood. Their value is that 12th-century monks themselves told stories in which a holy man's body stopped being male, and called it grace.

**4.3 Male-to-female in the womb (ch. 3; 1481, outside the window).** God was "already forming a male child when the Virgin Mary intervened", and left the Adam's apple "as witness to the divine re-gendering" (pp. 94–95). It is the book's only clear male-to-female transformation of a holy person. It is Castilian and late; use it only as a pointer for later reading, not in game.

**4.4 Christ's body, mother and wound (chs. 3, 5; Intro).**
- Bynum's "Jesus as Mother": Christ's body, "born of the Virgin Mary alone", is "fundamentally female" (Intro, p. 24).
- Francis of Assisi wrote "I am speaking to you, my son, in this way as a mother" and told the pope a parable in which he is "a poor, but beautiful woman" (Elphick, p. 92). Bonaventure c. 1260–63.
- Sexon: from c. 1320 the side wound is drawn alone, vertical, vulva-like; amulets of its "true measure" were tied on women in labour and for menstrual pain (pp. 138–40). Christ "knows the pains of childbirth in his capacity as mother of the church" (p. 139).
- Use: late Latin Christian devotion (after c. 1320). A birthing girdle is the Church's rival to any women's house-blessing; a daughter may note that the Church draws its God with a woman's wound.

**4.5 The humoral scale (Epilogue).** Sex was a matter of humours; "individual men or women could be closer or further away from the opposite sex" (p. 273). Asceticism raised fire and made "both men and women more manly" (pp. 273–74). Van Dijk's summary: sex "operated on a scale and, by manipulation, could slide either way" (p. 277). The book gives this one-way in practice (towards man). It is the physicians' and clerics' frame, a hostile or neutral voice the faiths can argue with.

**4.6 Eunuchs, and the AFAB saint "taken for a eunuch" (chs. 4, 10).** Most of this is in the earlier note. New or worth keeping:
- Ignatios' posthumous miracles are women's miracles: water touched by "relics of Ignatios' hair" restores two mothers' milk; oil from his tomb ends a woman's barrenness; a breech birth is saved (p. 124). His mother Prokopia and sisters Georgo and Theophano comfort the persecuted with him (p. 124).
- Marinos' brothers "considered her to be a eunuch, for she was beardless and of delicate voice" (Constas, quoted p. 257). The eunuch was the category a Byzantine monastery reached for when it saw someone it could not sort.
- Davis counts "around a dozen trans hagiographies that blossomed from the ninth to eleventh century" (Bychowski, p. 249): the AFAB monk-saints' Lives were being copied and revived in exactly the Adelphai window.

**4.7 Seiðr (ch. 7).** Covered in `../norse_colwill_hagiography_chapter.md`. For trans-feminine use, the relevant pieces are Ynglinga saga's "thought shameful for men" (p. 182), Lokasenna's Odin who "beat a drum like a witch" (p. 183), and Klinta 59:2, sexed male, cremated with "a single oval brooch, a bone needle, and sixteen beads" (p. 190).

## 6. Method: reading hagiography as trans history

1. **Hagiography is not biography.** It shows "what medieval people were told to believe" and that "trans lives could be envisioned and, therefore, trans lives could be lived" (p. 27). Use saints' Lives as evidence of what could be *imagined*, never as records of a person. This matches the mod's rule that no known historical people held these beliefs.
2. **Disguise is usually the critic's reading.** The disguise trope is "often a product of the critic rather than the text itself" (p. 26). Newman retracts her own 2003 "woman in disguise" reading of Joseph and its reliance on Garber's "third gender" (p. 44 and n. 3). Write the faiths' stories as lives, not masquerades.
3. **"Transvestite" is a 1910 medical word, not a neutral one.** Bychowski notes the irony that scholars reject "transgender" as anachronistic while using "transvestite": "Cis scholars seem at times to prefer their own anachronisms" (p. 250). The Guide: a trans woman wearing women's clothes "is not a transvestite" (p. 322).
4. **Authenticity over artifice.** Bychowski's *imago transvesti* reads the trans saint's life as a projection of the soul's own truth, and *imitatio transvesti* as the effect that truth has on others (pp. 250–53). Useful as a tone check for event text: daughters are not tricking anyone.
5. **Euphoria, not only dysphoria.** "Being trans is not only about what feels wrong; it is about what feels right" (p. 15). The faiths' rites should give joy, not only escape.
6. **Social gender, read from what communities did.** Colwill uses "social gender", a role needing "both performance and interpretation" (p. 180), and warns that sexing bones or goods is circular (p. 181). A faith's daughters are defined by how their House treats them, which the mod already does.
7. **The funeral as the test.** In Joseph, Marinos and Esmarade, the body is stripped at death and re-gendered against the life (pp. 53, 247, 156). The earlier note proposed a "washing of the body" event for Adelphai. The same pattern fits any faith in a hostile court.
8. **Literary characters and real lives differ.** The Guide says it may be fine to analyse divine transformations in fiction "in ways which would not be appropriate" for real lives (p. 283). For the mod: invented daughters can be written freely; real historical figures stay unclaimed.
9. **Pronouns.** Bychowski uses the saint's lived pronouns but leaves sources' pronouns in quotation unchanged, to show the inconsistency (p. 249). Wright uses they/them for Esmarade (p. 156); Gutt uses "Blanchandin·e" with they/them (p. 224). For mod docs: daughters are she/her; quoted hostile sources keep their own words.

**Terminology from the Guide** (for docs and README, not for in-world speech):
- "Trans woman" (two words), never "transwoman" (pp. 318, 322).
- "MTF" only if someone uses it of herself: it can suggest trans women are "really" men (pp. 303–04).
- "Transmisogyny": transphobia and misogyny together; trans women face "high levels of abuse, violence, and murder" (p. 320).
- "Eunuch": an AMAB person moved "from one form of masculinity to another", "within the spectrum of masculinity" (p. 293). This supports the court's view, not the Sisters' (see the earlier note, row 1).

## 7. Guardrails

- **Do not claim.** The book openly calls Marinos, Joseph and Esmarade trans saints; the mod's narrator must not, and the Sisters explicitly do not claim Marinos, Euphrosyne or Pelagia.
- **Trans men stay out.** Chs. 1, 6, 8, 9 and 10, Paula Barbada (p. 91), Oseberg's older occupant and Birka Bj. 581 (pp. 186, 192) are transmasculine or AFAB material. Use them only as Christian context or as the double standard the faiths name.
- **No suicide or self-harm.** The Introduction mentions trans people "lost to violence or suicide" (p. 14) and a study on religion and suicidality (p. 17). Carry none of it into the game.
- **No "third gender" romance.** The book cites Ringrose's eunuch "third gender" (p. 27) and the saints-beyond-gender line (p. 25). Colwill criticises seiðr "third gender" parallels drawn from Indigenous American systems (p. 183), and Newman withdraws her own (p. 44 n. 3). The faiths' daughters are women.
- **No living community as a faith.** The Appendix's Hijra and Two-Spirit entries (pp. 301, 324) and the editors' comparative wish (pp. 28–29) are not design input.
- **No Attis.** The book has none; keep it that way.
- **The knife is never imposed.** The Cistercian angel and Methodios' vision are unmanning *by grace* to stop desire; Ignatios' castration was political mutilation (p. 118). None of these is the Sisters' consensual rite, and event text should not blur them.
- **Watch the dates.** Juana (born 1481), Wilgefortis (14th c. and later), the wound-girdles (after c. 1320) and *Tristan de Nanteuil* (14th c.) are late. Joan of Arc (d. 1431) is in window but AFAB, and the book notes the far right's use of her (pp. 12–13).
- **Far-right appropriation.** Both the Introduction (pp. 12–13, 19) and Colwill (pp. 177–78) warn about white-supremacist use of the medieval and Norse past. The Good Ladies and Bædling designs should keep the plural, non-*völkisch* tone already set.

## 9. Open questions and further sources

**Open questions**
1. Is there any medieval Latin, Old French or Occitan *narrative* of a person assigned male who lives as a woman, outside court records like Rykener? Gutt's "Medieval Trans Lives in Anamorphosis (Pregnant Men and Backward Birth)" (*MFF* 55.1, 2019) is the likeliest lead.
2. Herbert of Clairvaux's angel tale and the Villers chronicle: exact dates and wording. Are there other Cistercian or Premonstratensian "body changed at death" tales in the Rhineland c. 1150–1250? Caesarius of Heisterbach's *Dialogus miraculorum* also tells the Hildegund story (cited p. 51 n. 24) and should be checked for other gender miracles.
3. Do Ignatios' women's miracles (p. 124) reflect a real cult? Szabo says there is no evidence of a "meaningful cult" (p. 126). If the mod uses them, it should be as a story told, not a shrine.
4. For the Good Ladies: did any Rhineland or Lombard night-company account include men who rode as women? Not answered here; see `witch_hutton_lecouteux.md` §5.
