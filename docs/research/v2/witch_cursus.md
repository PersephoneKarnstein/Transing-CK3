# Witches for v2: Even-Ezra, "Cursus" (2017), Roland of Cremona on night flight and ointment

Scope: the fourth reading for a possible v2 "Good Ladies" faith (the night company, mostly of women, around a Lady: Diana, Herodias, Holda, Abundia, Oriente). It answers open questions 1–2 of `witch_even_ezra.md` §7. Related notes: `witch_hutton_lecouteux.md`, `witch_papers_and_timeline.md`, `witch_studies_reader.md`. Writing rule: `docs/faith_design.md` rule 25 (amended 2026-10-25).

Pronouns: Ayelet Even-Ezra is *they* here. Roland of Cremona is *he*, as in the article.

## 1. Bibliographic line

Ayelet Even-Ezra (Hebrew University of Jerusalem), "Cursus: An Early Thirteenth-Century Source for Nocturnal Flights and Ointments in the Work of Roland of Cremona," *Magic, Ritual, and Witchcraft* 12, no. 3 (Winter 2017): 314–330. University of Pennsylvania Press. DOI: [10.1353/mrw.2017.0034](https://doi.org/10.1353/mrw.2017.0034). Project MUSE article 685465.

Page range confirmed from the PDF: the 18-page file (cover sheet plus pp. 314–330) carries printed folios 314 to 330. Read in full, including notes and the epilogue.

## 2. Verdict

**High value as a guardrail and a timeline fact. Low value as material for the faith itself.**

- Roland's *cursus* (Summa, c. 1232–34) is the **earliest known flying-ointment testimony**, about 200 years before Nider (p. 324). It is a real, pre-sabbat, non-tortured source.
- But it is **not the Ladies**. No Lady is named. Men and women both go. The centre is an ointment, bodily flight and sex ("orgy", including with beasts). Roland himself says the cursus belief must not be confused with the belief about the *fatae* ladies (p. 322). Even-Ezra stresses that it lacks "feminine dominance, visiting houses, or feasting and reviving animals" (p. 328).
- Under rule 25 the cursus is almost entirely **unusable in any voice**. Its claim is bodily flight to a sexual gathering, which is the core of the later sabbat. It may at most supply a hostile *rumour of an ointment*, with no flight and no orgy, or a physician's doubt.

## 3. The source: what Roland says

**Where.** Roland of Cremona OP (d. 1259), *Summa*, in the quaestiones on demons (pp. 317–18, 327). Even-Ezra cites Vatican, BAV Barb. Lat. 729, fol. 86b, checked against Paris, Mazarine 795 (n. 13). The demon quaestiones follow William of Auxerre's order, i.e. the angels-and-demons part of *Sentences* book 2 (pp. 317–18). Only book 3 is edited (Cortesi, Bergamo 1962, from the Bergamo MS, which has book 3 alone; n. 8). **So the passage is unedited**, in book 2. (The "book 2" label is my inference from n. 8 and the subject; the article does not name the book.)

**When and where written.** The *Summa* was "probably completed between 1232 and 1234, after his Paris regency" (pp. 316–17). Roland was regent master at Paris 1229/30, then went to Toulouse and later Italy (p. 316). The article does not say where the *Summa* was finished.

**Context in the Summa.** It comes straight after Roland rejects incubi (demons "have no sex organs", p. 318). He then turns to a belief "better known (*famosa*)" and defends discussing popular opinion. He cites Aristotle and the *Recognitiones*: "the opinion of the people holds the status of prophecy" (p. 318). He claims the belief exists "among the Saracens, the Jews and the Christian peoples" (p. 319).

**The core passage** (Even-Ezra's translation, p. 319; Latin n. 14):
- "Nor am I able to believe in what the common people call the cursus" (p. 319).
- "at night both men and women run and smear themselves with a certain ointment by which they fly" (p. 319). Latin: "*de nocte currunt et homines et mulieres et ungunt se unguento quoddam per quod volant*" (n. 14).
- They "copulate with each other, as well as with beasts" (p. 319; "*invicem coeunt, et cum bestiis*").
- The source chain: "So they tell to their confessors, as I have heard from monks who admitted such people in confession" (p. 319). The penitents "cried bitterly for this sin and for others, and were ready to be penitent" (p. 319).

**Demons.** "If there is any such thing as a cursus, it was invented by the art of demons to advance lust" (say those who go on it) (p. 319). Demons in bodily form "lead people to this mockery (*ludificationem*)" (p. 319; n. 15).

**The ladies passage (obscure).** Some people link *fatum* and being *fatatus* (bewitched to do only what is ordered) to "a certain other cursus". "And certain ladies are called fatae (fairies). This is absolutely nonsense, and this opinion is not as popular" (p. 319). Latin: "*Et vocantur fate quedam dominae. Hoc omnino fictio est*" (n. 15).

**The ointment.** The most impossible part for Roland is its speed: "just as quickly as they are anointed with that ointment, as rapidly do they fly" (pp. 324–25; n. 30). Yet he does **not** call the flight an illusion. He asks whether an ointment really could lift bodies. His argument is natural magic by analogy (p. 325, Latin n. 31):
- "by the skill of demons or the ingenuity of philosophers" an ointment was invented that makes iron unable to cut the anointed, "as the Book of the Cow says" (p. 325).
- Lodestone attracts iron, so why not things that repel it? "All things are twofold" (Ecclesiasticus 42).
- So why not an ointment "that suspends anointed bodies in the air and moves them quickly wherever the person wishes?" Stones make people invisible, after all (p. 325).
- **No ingredients are given.** The *Liber vaccae* invulnerability ointment is made from the guts of an artificial homunculus. Roland "entirely obscures the main ingredient" (p. 326).
- Conclusion: "That this cursus exists, I cannot assert … I cannot entirely deny that it might be possible for such an unguent to be created" (p. 326; Latin n. 35).

**Medical or demonic?** Both, but neither is pharmacological. The frame is demonic (the quaestio sits among demons; demons "invented" any cursus). The ointment's mechanism is natural-philosophical: hidden properties of matter, like a magnet. **There is no hallucinogen or sleep theory in Roland.** The "deep sleep, then vivid dream" model belongs to Nider (1430s) and Laguna (1555), and the drug theory to modern scholars such as Bever (p. 323).

## 4. Even-Ezra's argument

1. **Roland the physician-theologian.** He was "a renowned master and a practicing physician" when he joined the Dominicans in Bologna (p. 315). He records treating patients in the *Summa* (BAV Barb. Lat. 729, f. 84a; n. 3). His teacher and ally John of St Giles was also a physician, so "the physicians [were] the first two Dominican masters of theology in Paris" (p. 316). He mixes theology, natural philosophy and field information from "monks he spoke with, patients, laypersons" (p. 318). He was an early reader of the *Liber vaccae*, an Arabic-derived book of organic magical "experiments" (pp. 325–26).
2. **Independent of the Canon Episcopi.** "There is no indication here of influence from either Burchard or the canon Episcopi" (p. 321). The differences: the name *cursus*; the ointment; the orgy; flying, not riding (*volant* is "unquestionable", p. 322); the claimed interreligious spread; no Diana; no violence; men as well as women; beasts as sexual partners, not mounts (pp. 321–22). Even-Ezra concludes it is "an independent account based on confessions" to his fellow friars (p. 322).
3. **The word.** No other Latin use of *cursus* in this sense is known. It may translate a vernacular "journey" word. Even-Ezra compares the *Roman de la Rose* (souls "par le monde ainsine s'en corent", ll. 18414–15) with *currit per mundum*, and the 16th-c. Bavarian "Fahrt" of Chonrad Stoeckhlin's "phantoms of the night" (p. 322). Because people "walk (*vadunt*)" on it, "trail" is also possible (p. 322).
4. **Distinct from the Ladies.** Roland "instructs his reader not to confuse the belief about the cursus with the belief about the ladies" (p. 322). "The cursus, in his eyes, is a different practice, whose center lies in the ointment and the orgiastic flight" (p. 322). It is closer to Nider's lone woman with the ointment than to the night-women tradition (p. 328).
5. **Not yet heresy.** The account "lacks the concern with demonic pact and conspiratorial, anti-Christian heresy" (p. 328). Demons want to corrupt morals, not "defame Christianity, murder babies, or endanger society"; "the Devil as a specific persona and leader remains a blurred shadow" (p. 328). Roland, an anti-heresy preacher in Toulouse, does not link it to heresy or call the goers sorcerers (p. 328). So "such practices were still not closely tied with heresy" in the early 13th c. (p. 328).
6. **Ointments not purely learned fiction.** Against Cohn and Ostling, who place the flying ointment in early modern learned discourse (p. 323), Roland shows the idea is much older and may have travelled orally (pp. 323–24, 329). "More elements of the sabbath were present in the early thirteenth century than has been supposed" (p. 328).
7. **Reliability.** The information is "thirdhand (citing confessors)" but "fairly reliable" because isolated, free of torture and "too early to be contaminated by fifteenth-century stereotypes" (p. 328). Roland had no influence on later writers (p. 329).
8. **Learned magic opened eyes.** William of Auvergne (Abundia) and Roland are among the first Latin readers of occult literature and "two of the very few Latin authors" on Abundia or the cursus (p. 330). Reading necromancy may have made masters notice folk beliefs.

**Context the article gives on the Ladies tradition (useful dates):**
- Regino 906 / Gratian: women ride with Diana "and with an innumerable multitude of women" (p. 320).
- Burchard: "Hulda" and a demon throng "transformed into the likeness of women"; elsewhere a leaderless female ride as the wandering of the soul, killing and reviving men (p. 320).
- William of Auvergne: "Lady Abundia", who visits houses and grants prosperity; women who think they fly at night with "good things" (p. 321).
- *Roman de la Rose* (Jean de Meun): some say a third of humanity believe their souls leave their bodies thrice a week, ride with good women and "lady Habonde", and enter closed houses (p. 321).
- Milan 1390: "Madona Oriente"; Nicholas of Cusa 1457: two old women of Val di Fassa in the "society of Diana", called Richella (p. 321).
- Avraham ben Yitzhak Halevi of Gerona, Hebrew essay, 1380: *bruxas*, "good women walking at night", who leave their sleeping bodies, enter houses, dance, eat animals and revive them; also said to strangle babies (p. 327).
- Der Stricker (fl. 1215–50; MS 1260): a learned traveller never heard a master say what an *unholde* is; a woman riding a calf, a stick or a broom (pp. 329–30). **The broom is barred by rule 25.**

## 5. Answers to `witch_even_ezra.md` §7

**Q1. What does the cursus passage say?**
- **Who goes:** "both men and women" (*et homines et mulieres*). No gender is stressed. Even-Ezra notes the absence of "feminine dominance" (p. 328). No gender transgression of any kind appears in the article.
- **A Lady?** No. Roland never names Diana, Holda or any leader, and no company is named. The only ladies are the *fatae* in a side remark that Roland calls "absolutely nonsense" and keeps apart from the cursus (pp. 319, 322).
- **Ointment:** discussed as possibly real by natural magic or demonic art, by analogy with the *Liber vaccae*. Not medical in a drug sense. No ingredients. Not a dream or a sleep.
- **Edited?** No. It is in the unedited part of the *Summa* (BAV Barb. Lat. 729, fol. 86b; Mazarine 795), almost certainly book 2. Cortesi's 1962 edition is book 3 only.
- **Date and place:** written c. 1232–34, after the Paris regency, by a master trained at Bologna and teaching in Paris, then Toulouse. The informants are unnamed "monks"/religious who heard confessions. No place is given for the penitents.

**Q2. Did a 13th-c. master apply "soul away, body alive" to the Canon Episcopi riders?**
- **Not Roland.** He does the opposite. He does not even raise illusion, "unlike his contemporaries" (p. 325), and treats bodily suspension in the air as the thing to test. He also keeps the cursus apart from the ladies.
- The article gives no scholastic master who applies the rapture/abstraction theory (Roland's Paul quaestio) to the riders. The soul-leaving-body reading appears in **Burchard** (11th c.), the vernacular **Roman de la Rose** (c. 1275, Natura on dreams) and the Hebrew **Avraham of Gerona** (1380). William of Auvergne discusses Abundia but the article does not say how he explains the flight.
- **So Q2 stays open for Latin masters**, with a firm negative for Roland. Within his own *Summa* the two topics (rapture of the soul, the cursus) stay apart.

## 6. Implications for the timeline and rule 25

- **New dated row:** c. 1232–34, Paris/Toulouse, Roland of Cremona's *Summa*: the *cursus*, an ointment-flight and sexual gathering of men and women, heard from confessors; Roland neither believes nor denies the ointment. Grade B (thirdhand, unique, but untortured). Mark it **not a seed** for the Good Ladies.
- **Flying ointment is pre-1430 in the record.** This moves the *earliest* ointment from Nider (c. 1435–37) to c. 1232. It does not move the "flip" to diabolical heresy (still c. 1320–1430): Roland ties the cursus to no pact and no heresy.
- **Is ointment flight bodily flight?** In Roland, **yes**. The claim he reports and tests is that bodies are lifted "in the air" and carried "wherever the person wishes" (p. 325). The drug-induced vision with the body at home is a 15th–16th-c. learned reading (Nider's exemplum, where the friar tells the woman "she had not moved from her place", p. 324; Laguna 1555) and a modern hypothesis. So a cursus-style ointment is exactly what rule 25 bars: bodily flight to a gathering.
- **The "illusion" clause has a historical outlier.** Rule 25 lets clergy speak of spirit travel only as "illusion" before c. 1430. Roland is a real clergyman who did *not* call it illusion. This does not weaken the rule (it is a design rule, not a claim that all clergy agreed). But any note saying "all medieval clergy called it illusion" should be softened to "most", citing Even-Ezra p. 325.
- **No change needed to rule 25's wording.** The cursus gives no reason to loosen it and one more reason to keep "no bodily flight" at all dates.

## 7. Design implications

All are suggestions. Any churchman or physician in an event is a generic, unnamed figure.

1. **Keep the cursus out of the faith's own voice.** It is not the Ladies by Roland's own account, and its content (bodily flight, orgy, beasts) is barred or unsuitable. The faith's travelling stays spirit travel with the Lady while the body stays home.
2. **Ointment only as a hostile or learned claim, never as practice.** Option: a low-hostility event, before c. 1430, where an unnamed physician-friar asks a daughter of the faith whether the company uses "a salve". She can deny it ("we go in sleep; the body stays in bed"). The friar may wonder aloud that some salve "might" exist, without saying it makes anyone fly. Do not have him describe flight, a gathering or sex. No ingredients, no method, no effect description.
3. **A physician-master voice** fits well for curiosity rather than hostility: someone who has treated patients, reads Arabic natural-philosophy books, gathers what penitents tell confessors, and ends "I cannot assert it, nor wholly deny it". This is the tone of the source; the person must be fictional.
4. **Region.** The article gives **no Lombard or Cremonese detail** for the cursus; penitents are unlocated. Its Italian Ladies material is Milan 1390 (Madona Oriente) and Val di Fassa 1457 (Richella). The Bologna link is Roland's own training, not the belief. A Lombard Ladies region rests on Milan, not on Roland.
5. **"Confession and tears" as the low-hostility outcome.** Roland's penitents "cried bitterly … and were ready to be penitent" (p. 319). This supports the existing design: before c. 1320, the outcome is penance, not punishment.
6. **Jewish neighbours.** Roland claims Jews and Muslims share the belief; Even-Ezra finds no Muslim parallel and no direct Jewish cursus evidence (p. 326). Avraham of Gerona (1380) shows Catalan Jews knew the Christian "good women walking at night". Do not build a Jewish or Muslim version of the Ladies on this.

## 8. Guardrails

- **The orgy framing is the accusers'.** "Orgiastic" is Even-Ezra's word. The sexual content reached Roland through confessors, framed as sin and the work of demons; it is not a neutral description of anyone's practice. Never give the faith a sexual rite from this source. No bestiality in any voice.
- **The cursus is not the Ladies.** Do not cite Roland or Even-Ezra for the Lady, the company of women, Diana, Holda, Abundia or Oriente. Even-Ezra's Ladies material is a literature review of other sources.
- **No recipe.** The ointment has no ingredients in the source; do not invent any. Do not mention the *Liber vaccae* homunculus.
- **Rule 25 holds:** no sabbat, pact, familiar or broom (Der Stricker's broom included); no bodily flight to a gathering, in any voice, at any date.
- **No living community.** Nothing here is about present-day witches, pagans or Reclaiming practice.
- **Historical people stay historical.** The narrator never claims Roland, Nider, Avraham of Gerona or anyone else for the faith. Roland may be named only in this research note.
- **Thirdhand source.** Roland reports what friars said penitents said. Do not present it as eyewitness evidence of a real ointment.

## 9. Open questions

1. Does Roland's text at BAV Barb. Lat. 729 fol. 86b say more (the "short discussion of this ointment's efficacy" that Even-Ezra summarises)? Van der Lugt, "'Abominable Mixtures'", *Traditio* 64 (2009): 264, quotes the passage "extensively" (p. 315). Read that next for a fuller Latin text.
2. How does William of Auvergne (*De universo*, 1674 ed., 1:1006) explain the flight with Abundia: illusion, soul travel or demons? This is the best remaining test of Q2.
3. What is the vernacular word behind *cursus* (Italian *corsa*? Old French *erre*/*oirre*?) and where did the confessing penitents live?
4. Does any 13th-c. confessors' manual pair the ointment with the Canon Episcopi? Even-Ezra found nothing.
5. The fuller Milan 1384/1390 records (Madona Oriente) remain the key Lombard source for the Ladies; check whether any ointment appears there.
