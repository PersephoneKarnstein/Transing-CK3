# Euro scan: three queer-studies volumes, read for Crusader Dolls

Written 2026-10-25. Read from the text extractions in `scratchpad/v2/ocr/euro/`. Page numbers are the printed ones, rebuilt from the OCR's running heads. Where a page is inferred from the nearest running head, it is marked "c.". Nothing in the repo was edited.

**The verdict in one line.** None of the three books has a trans-feminine person, community or institution dated 867–1453 that the repo lacks. They add **hostile voices** (Castilian law, Florentine and Sienese preachers, a Venetian law of 1467, an Abbasid caliph's rebuke), a little **flavour** (Torelore next to Arles, a 1445 Sienese *Paradise*, the Bologna Bollette), and two **date checks**. No new faith. One small new hub, Beaucaire as flavour for Ha-Nehpakhot, rates *plausible as flavour*. Everything else is *thin* or *exclude*.

**Insider weighting.** None of the contributors writes as a trans scholar on trans subjects, as far as these volumes show (*unverified*: the contributor notes give no identities). The books are gay, lesbian and queer studies, and their "queer" is an umbrella that sometimes folds gender crossing into sodomy (Rojas and Thompson, pp. 2–3; Friedrich, pp. 117–130). Treat their readings of gender as outside readings. Where the repo already holds trans-authored readings (Henningsen, Bychowski, Betancourt, Wingard), those come first.

## 1. The books

### 1.1 Murray and Terpstra (2019)

Jacqueline Murray and Nicholas Terpstra (eds), *Sex, Gender and Sexuality in Renaissance Italy* (Abingdon and New York: Routledge, 2019). ISBN 978-1-138-54244-0 (hb), 978-1-138-54245-7 (pb). A Festschrift for Konrad Eisenbichler. It covers 1300–1700, but almost every chapter is after 1500.

**Contents.** 1 Murray and Terpstra, introduction (pp. 1–17). Part I, "Sex, order, and disorder": 2 Ruggiero on Boccaccio's Griselda (21); 3 Brizio on sexual violence in the Sienese state (35); 4 McCarthy and Terpstra on the sex trade in Bologna (53); 5 E. Cohen on adulteresses in Rome (75). Part II, "Sense and sensuality": 6 Simons on Benedetta Carlini (97); 7 T. Cohen on Ludovico Santa Croce, 1557 (125); 8 Milligan on dress and masculinity in Castiglione (141); 9 Giannetti on sausage and *carne* (160). Part III, "Visualizing sexuality": 10 Saslow on "Il Sodoma" (183); 11 Moulton on Piccolomini and Aretino (211); 12 Kodera on della Porta (227); 13 Tylus on Tasso's women (247).

**Read in full:** the introduction. **Read in part**, for the material dated before about 1460: chs. 2, 3, 4, 6 and 8. **Skimmed:** chs. 5, 7 and 9–13, which are all 16th–17th century.

**Relevant to the mod:** nothing on gender crossing in range. The chapters that touch the period are Ruggiero (Florence, 1342–50), Brizio (Siena, 1378 and 1460), McCarthy and Terpstra (Bologna, 1376 and 1462), Simons (Siena, 1445) and Milligan (Siena, 1424–41).

### 1.2 Rojas and Thompson (2021)

Felipe E. Rojas and Peter E. Thompson (eds), *Queering the Medieval Mediterranean: Transcultural Sea of Sex, Gender, Identity, and Culture*, The Medieval Mediterranean 121 (Leiden and Boston: Brill, 2021). ISBN 978-90-04-31515-0 (hb), 978-90-04-46532-9 (ebook). DOI prefix 10.1163/9789004465329. The brief's subtitle, "Transcultural Sea of Desire", is the cover form; the title page has the longer subtitle given here.

**Contents.** Introduction (Rojas and Thompson, pp. 1–12). 1 Filios, "Anomalous al-Andalus" (15); 2 Lledó-Guillem, the masculine body in *El Monserrate* and *Tirant lo Blanc* (35); 3 Amer, "Bad Girls and Gender Trouble in the Thirteenth-Century Mediterranean" (55); 4 Rouhi, the *alcahueta* (70); 5 Hayes, Ibn Dāniyāl's shadow play (95); 6 Friedrich, queer names in Old French (117); 7 Sturges, *Aucassin et Nicolette* (155); 8 Burshatin, Alfonso X on captives (175); 9 Hutcheson, Spain's *pecado sodomítico* (191); 10 Clark, Usama ibn Munqidh's bathhouses (220).

**Read in full:** the introduction and chs. 3, 5, 7, 9 and 10. **Read in part:** chs. 1 (the gender argument, pp. 22–26), 4 (Trotaconventos and the nun), 6 (the eunuch and castration passages, pp. 117–130) and 8 (pp. 175–178). **Skimmed:** ch. 2 (*Tirant* is 1460s–1490).

**Relevant to the mod:** every chapter is in range (8th–15th century), but the volume is literary and legal. The editors say so themselves: "This collection does not contain contributions that discuss the Italian city-states, Greece or its northern neighbors" (p. 10). It has nothing on Sicily, Byzantium or the Ottomans.

### 1.3 Goldberg (1994)

Jonathan Goldberg (ed.), *Queering the Renaissance* (Durham, NC: Duke University Press, 1994). The brief gives 1993; the series *Series Q* volume is usually catalogued as 1994 (*unverified*, because the OCR lacks the copyright page).

**Contents.** Goldberg, introduction. Halley, "Bowers v. Hardwick in the Renaissance"; Bray on male friendship in Elizabethan England; Traub on "lesbian" desire in early modern England (62); A. K. Smith, "Fraudomy: Reading Sexuality and Politics in Burchiello" (84); Freccero on Marguerite de Navarre (107); Stevens on Erasmus's letters (124); Mager on John Bale; Pittenger on Nicholas Udall (162); Stephens on Spenser's Amoret; Goldberg on *Romeo and Juliet* (218); Hammill on Bacon; Rambuss on 17th-century devotional lyric (253); Masten on Beaumont and Fletcher (280); Frank on Dryden (310); Warner, "New English Sodom" (330); Hunt, afterword (359).

**Read in full:** Smith on Burchiello (Florence, 1404–49), the only chapter in range. **Read in part:** the introduction, and the passages in Halley, Traub and Rambuss that touch the Middle Ages. **The rest** is English, French or American, 1520–1700, and out of range.

## 2. Numbered findings

Ratings: **new pathway**, **flavour** (with the faith named), **hostile voice**, or **not usable** (with the reason).

### Mamluk and Ayyubid Egypt, Syria, the Crusader Levant

**1. Shajarat al-Durr, Sultan of Egypt, May–July 1250** (Amer, pp. 56–60). A freed slave of Armenian origin, she hid al-Salih's death in November 1249, ruled in his name, and was then proclaimed sultan with the khutba and coins in her name. Ibn Wasil: "An event like this was not known to have occurred previously in Islam" (p. 56). The Abbasid caliph's rebuke, via al-Maqrizi: "If you do not have men there [to reign as sultan], tell us so we can send you men" (p. 59). She abdicated by marrying Aybak on 31 July 1250. **Hostile voice**, for any woman, cis or trans, who rules in a Sunni realm after 1250: the caliph's line is a ready model for a rebuke event. **Not a trans case.** Amer also lists Qutlugh Khatun of Kirman (r. 1256–82) and Radiyya of Delhi (p. 64 n. 25). Radiyya likely overlaps `sea_jackson_delhi_sultanate.md` (not checked).

**2. Al-Tifashi's *Nuzhat al-albab*, mid-13th century, Tunisia and Egypt** (Amer, p. 63; Hutcheson, p. 214). Amer reads it for its chapter on women's same-sex practice. Hutcheson lists its types: "*lāṭā* (pl. of *luṭī*), *muʾājirīn* (beardless youth), and *khināth* (effeminate men)" (p. 214). Al-Tifashi is already in the repo (`hist_karras_medieval_sexuality.md` E163; `isl_tor_violent_order.md`). **Not usable as new material**: the *khināth* are only named here. The lead stays open: the French translation (Khawam, *Les délices des cœurs*, 1991) would show what the *khināth* chapter says. *Unverified* whether that chapter describes people who live as women.

**3. Ibn Dāniyāl, *Ṭayf al-Khayāl*, Cairo, late 13th or early 14th century** (Hayes, pp. 95–117). The author was an eye doctor who had fled Mosul in 1261, aged 14 (p. 95). The play is set after **Baybars's 1267 campaign against vice** (p. 96). Its world is "pimps, prostitutes, rent-boys … sodomites, catamites, and those who switch between sexual roles" (p. 96). "Effeminates" appear only as a rung in the hero's list of declining sexual partners (p. c. 110). **Date check:** Hayes's 1267 confirms the correction already made in `isl_islamicate_sexualities.md` §11 (Rowson prints "665/1257"; AH 665 is 1266–67). **Hostile voice / setting** for the existing "Anti-Vice Campaign" pulse idea (`isl_islamicate_sexualities.md` l. 88). **Not usable as a trans source**: the effeminate is an object of a joke, and the "rent-boys" material involves youths (excluded).

**4. Usama ibn Munqidh, *Kitab al-Iʿtibar*, written c. 1180s about the 12th-century Levant** (Clark, pp. 220–235).
- *(a)* The Maʿarra bathhouse. A Frankish knight has his own and his wife's pubic hair shaved by the Muslim attendant Salim (Cobb trans., p. 149; Clark, pp. 225–226). This is about Frankish "jealousy", not gender. **Not usable.**
- *(b)* The Tyre bathhouse. Usama "couldn't be sure if she was a woman", and his companion lifted her hem (pp. 228–229). The scene turns on doubted sex, but it describes a forced exposure of a girl whose age is not given. **Exclude** (possible minor; non-consent).
- *(c)* Usama's insinuation about Nasr and the caliph al-Zafir (pp. 234–235). This is already in `isl_islamicate_sexualities.md` l. 32.
- **Date slip in the book:** the introduction gives Usama as "1095–1118" (p. 10). Derenbourg's title, cited by Clark, has 1095–1188 (p. 226 n. 23).

**5. Al-Bakri (d. 1094) on the Galicians**, via Hermes and al-Azmeh (Clark, p. 227 and n. 28). Andalusis despised the Galicians for their "subversion of the proper order of gender relations". **Hostile voice**, Andalusi-on-Christian. It is a trope about the northerners' women, not about gender crossing. **Flavour at most**, for an Andalusi ruler's opinion of the Latin north. Ibn Faḍlān on the Oghuz (921–22) is in the same passage (p. 227).

### Castile, Aragon, al-Andalus, Jewish Iberia

**6. Alfonso X's *Fuero Real* (early 1250s), 4.9.2** (Hutcheson, p. 195). "They both be castrated before the entire population, and afterwards, on the third day, that they be hanged by the feet until dead." The law is filed with monks who leave their order (p. 195). **Hostile voice** for Castile, 1252–84: a pulse that raises the stakes for any daughter found out in Castile. Hutcheson notes that the age clause (boys of fourteen or younger spared) belongs to the *Partidas*, not the *Fuero Real*, and that this is often confused (p. 195 n. 11). Keep that straight in any event text.

**7. *Siete Partidas* 7.21 (c. 1265)** (Hutcheson, pp. 191–219; Spanish text in the appendix, pp. 215ff.). Sodomy brings "mala fama non tan solamente á los facedores, mas aun á la tierra do es consentido" (appendix). Hutcheson's thesis is that the law was drafted to be read by Jews and Muslims too: it renders *mishkav zakhar* and echoes the Qurʾān's Lot story (pp. c. 203–208). It engages the Mālikī debate over the passive partner, setting Ibn Ḥazm's Ẓāhirī leniency against Ibn Abī Zayd of Qayrawān's hard line (pp. c. 209–210). It also cites Maimonides's *Mishneh Torah*, *Issurei Biʾah* 1:14 (p. c. 210). **Hostile voice** for Castile, al-Andalus and the Maghreb. Ha-Nehpakhot's Maghreb notes may already hold Maimonides on this point (not checked in detail).

**8. Jacob ben Elʿazar's Hebrew tale of Sapir, Shapir and Birsha, Toledo, 13th century** (Hutcheson, pp. 213–214, via Decter 2011). This is a male–male love tale that recasts Sodom. **Not usable** for trans women: it is about desire between men, and the beloved is a "youth" (exclude). It is still useful as a sign that Toledan Hebrew *maqāma* writing handled Sodom with irony. Decter, *Journal of Medieval Iberian Studies* 3.2 (2011), is a **lead** for Ha-Nehpakhot's reading list.

**9. *Amor de voluntad*: *Partida* II on ransoming captives, 13th century** (Burshatin, pp. 175–190). The law names, among those bound to redeem a captive, friends joined by "love freely given". Burshatin links this to Hinojosa's 10th-century Iberian *fraternidad artificial*, the *hermanos de elección* (p. 176). **Flavour, Ha-Nehpakhot (Maghreb branch) or Adelphai**: chosen, non-blood kin with a legal duty to ransom. It parallels `hist_rapp_brother_making.md` and the Adelphai sponsor. **Thin**: the law is about men and says nothing about gender.

**10. The *alcahueta*: Trotaconventos in Juan Ruiz's *Libro de buen amor* (1330/1343)** (Rouhi, pp. 70–94). The old go-between courts the nun doña Garoza for the Archpriest: "You should seek the love of some nun, believe me" (p. c. 86). She also courts a Moorish woman (p. c. 87, via Barletta). Rouhi reads the go-between as a queer third party. **Not usable**: no gender crossing. At most, it is colour for a convent-visiting old woman.

**11. Pedro II of Aragon "tanquam effeminatum", 1213** (Sturges, pp. 165–166, citing Lipton). Crusade polemic called the king effeminate for loving a noblewoman of Toulouse said to be a heretic. Guibert of Nogent says of the "Manichaeans": "men are known to lie with men, women with women" (p. 166). **Hostile voice**: the effeminacy slur as crusade propaganda, 1209–29. It is close to the Cathar material the 1178 survey excluded (rules 18 and 25).

### Provence and Languedoc

**12. Torelore in *Aucassin et Nicolette*, early 13th century, Picard, set at Beaucaire** (Sturges, pp. 155–174). The king of Torelore "was lying in childbed. 'And where is his wife, then?' … she was with her army" (section 28; p. 160). Aucassin beats the "feminized king" (p. 161). The war-lord is "Bougars de Valence", and *bougre* means heretic, then sodomite (p. 162). Sturges ties the text to the **siege of Beaucaire, May 1216** (p. 163). **Flavour, Ha-Nehpakhot**: Beaucaire is about 15 km up the Rhône from Arles, and Beaucaire's fair is a known Jewish trading venue (*unverified* for the 13th century from this book). A carnival-inversion event ("the King of Torelore") could fit the Purim-season Turning: a Picard joke heard in the Arles street, mocking but also enjoyed. Rate it **plausible as flavour**, not as a new faith. **Guardrail:** this is a male couvade joke and a cis woman queen at war; never present it as a trans text. Nicolette's minstrel disguise (p. 158) is a cis woman disguised as a man: **not usable**.

**13. Arnaud de Verniolle, Pamiers, 1323** (Sturges, p. 166 n. 55, via Arnold). The inquisitors linked a man's sex with men to heresy. **Not usable**: it is about male–male sex, and the Fournier register's Arnaud material involves youths.

### Latin West: eunuchs and names

**14. Pseudo-Ovid *De Vetula* (13th century) and Jean Le Fèvre's *La Vieille* (c. 1370s)**, via Clark in Tracy (ed.), *Castration and Culture* (2013) (Friedrich, p. 119). Of the eunuch they ask "Quid est?" ("what is it?") and "what gender of pronoun" to use; the eunuch is a "demi homme" or *semivir*. **Hostile voice, Adelphai Dorleyaiou**: a Latin commonplace that a Frankish or Venetian character could voice to a daughter of the knife. It pairs with `byz_ringrose_perfect_servant.md` and `byz_tougher_*`. Clark's 2013 chapter is a **lead** (not in the repo).

**15. Friedrich on the *Roman de la Rose* (c. 1230)** (pp. 117–154). Oiseuse's "castrated" grammatical ending, the beaver, Bel Acueil "le travesti" (Zink). **Not usable**: philological play, and the reading equates hermaphrodite and eunuch with homosexual (pp. 124–126). That conflation is the kind the mod's writing rules avoid.

### Italian city-states, 1342–1467

**16. Florence: Burchiello (1404–49), the Ufficiali di Notte and ten thousand charges** (Smith, in Goldberg, pp. 84–106). "Greeks, Italians, and especially Florentines were reckoned as notorious practitioners of sodomy" (p. 90). There was a night "vice squad"; Bernardino and Savonarola preached against sodomy as "a specifically Florentine vice"; about 10,000 charges were brought over the century (p. 91). The Office of the Night was created in 1432 (Rocke; *unverified* here, since Smith does not give the year). **Hostile voice** for Florence after 1432. **Excluded content:** Rocke's Florentine cases are mostly adult men with adolescent youths. Do not mine them.

**17. Siena: Francesco Pontano (fl. 1424–41) and San Bernardino (1380–1444) on men's dress** (Milligan, in Murray and Terpstra, pp. 144–145). Pontano says ornament is to be disdained by all "who want to be called real men" (p. 144). Bernardino attacked short tunics and tight hose (p. 145). Villani (c. 1280–1348) is in the same passage. **Hostile voice**: a "gender-policing preacher" event for a Tuscan court, 1420s–40s. Bernardino is a real saint, so voice him as a type ("a Franciscan preacher in the piazza"), not in his own words.

**18. Venice, 1467: the Council of Ten orders doctors to report "anyone treated for damages resulting from anal intercourse"** (Brizio, p. 51 n. 42, citing Ruggiero, *Boundaries of Eros*, p. 117). **Hostile voice / date** for `later_starts/venice.md`. That note lists the Signori di Notte and the Council of Ten on sodomy (1406–18) and Rolandina (1354) as "attested but late". **Add 1467** to that list, with the Ruggiero page, which is *unverified* until Ruggiero is read; this is second-hand via Brizio. The venice note's open item 5 (Ruggiero for Rolandina's page) is **not** resolved: these books cite Ruggiero but never mention Rolandina.

**19. Bologna, 1376: the Ufficio delle Bollette registered "Foreigners, Jews, and Whores"** (McCarthy and Terpstra, pp. 53–54). The 1462 statutes set a civic brothel by the Piazza Maggiore (p. 56). **Flavour, Ha-Nehpakhot (comparison only)**: a magistracy that licenses and fines outsiders "living and working in the local body social while remaining legally outside the body politic" (p. 54). It is a model for "the street's licence" as a fee-paying status, but it is Italian Christian civic law, not Jewish. **Thin.**

**20. Siena, 1378 and 1460** (Brizio, pp. 35–36, 40). The Otto di custodia was created in 1460 to oversee behaviour. In 1378 a district judge was dismissed for raping a girl (p. 40). **Not usable**: sexual violence against a minor.

**21. Giovanni di Paolo, *Paradise*, Siena, 1445** (Simons, pp. 104–105, fig. 6.2; Metropolitan Museum, open access). In it "two Dominican nuns entwine in one unit"; same-sex pairs of the blessed embrace. **Flavour** for any convent-based house, such as the Adelphai daughter-house idea in `venice.md`: a loading-screen or event image of women embracing in paradise, with no claim that it shows trans women. Simons also notes the androgynous Gabriel, since for Aquinas angels "had no natural bodies" (p. 107). Use with care (rule 18).

**22. Boccaccio's Griselda (*Decameron* X.10, c. 1350) and Walter of Brienne, Florence 1342–43** (Ruggiero, pp. 21–34). A political reading. **Not usable.**

### Out of range or excluded, noted briefly

- **Benedetta Carlini, Pescia, 1619–23** (Simons, ch. 6). Out of range. A cis nun; the debate is about the "lesbian" label.
- **Bologna, 1553: nuns "cross-dressed for the male roles" in convent plays; Caterina de' Ricci played Jesus** (Simons, p. c. 118). Out of range, but it is a precedent for convent theatre with women in male roles. Not trans.
- **Montaigne's weaver of Vitry, 1580** (Traub, in Goldberg, p. c. 66). Assigned female, lived as a man, married, and was hanged. A **trans-masculine case: out of scope**, and out of range.
- **Princess Boudour in the *Nights*** (Amer, pp. 64–65). A cis woman disguised as her husband rules a kingdom. Out of scope.
- **Hermaphrodite medicine** (Traub's notes on Daston and Park, and Duval, 1603). Out of range. The repo has DeVun for the medieval material.
- ***Tirant lo Blanc* (Valencia, 1460s; printed 1490)** (Lledó-Guillem, ch. 2). The hero saves Constantinople from the Ottomans in fiction; Muslim desire for his body is cast as unmanning. This is the only "Ottoman" item in the three books, and it is fiction about 1453. **Exclude.**
- **Mūsā b. Nuṣayr, al-Andalus, 711–15** (Filios, ch. 1). Before 867, and "queer" means anomalous, not gender crossing. **Exclude.**
- **Il Sodoma (1477–1549), Ludovico Santa Croce (1557), Erasmus and Servatius (1487)**. Out of range, and/or about youths. **Exclude.**

## 4. Concrete uses, if any are wanted

1. **"If you have no men" (Islamic rulers, after 1250).** When a woman or a daughter holds a title in a Sunni realm, the caliph or a rival sends this rebuke (Amer p. 59). It is a hostile opinion modifier, and it ends when she marries or abdicates. It fits only if the mod adds Islamic-realm reactions.
2. **Castilian law pulse (1252–1284+).** In Castile and León, an exposed daughter faces *Fuero Real* 4.9.2 (Hutcheson p. 195). Rate it as severe as the Florentine pulse; voice the law, not the king.
3. **"Quid est?" (Latin courts, any date).** A Frankish noble asks what pronoun to use for an Adelphai eunuch-daughter (Friedrich p. 119, via Clark 2013). A hostile flavour line with a reply option.
4. **The King of Torelore (Ha-Nehpakhot, Arles, carnival season).** A Picard minstrel's tale of the king in childbed reaches the Jewish street. Options: laugh with it, or turn it ("the king had a better winter than the queen"). Flavour only; the source is mockery (Sturges p. 160).
5. **Date additions for `venice.md` §"Attested but late":** 1467 (Council of Ten, the doctors' duty to report; Ruggiero p. 117 via Brizio, *unverified*).

## 6. Guardrail log

- **Minors:** Florentine youths, the Tyre bath girl, Siena 1378, Sapir and Shapir's "youth", Ibn Dāniyāl's rent-boys, Il Sodoma, Erasmus. All excluded.

- **Living communities:** none presented. Egypt today appears only in Amer's account of the 2011 mausoleum restoration (pp. 66–68). Not usable.
