# Witches for v2: Hutton (2014) and Lecouteux (2011) on night processions

Scope: a close reading of two sources on medieval night processions, done as a follow-up to `witch_papers_and_timeline.md` (the "prior note"). The question is the same: what can a CK3 faith of the "Good Ladies" night company rest on, inside 867–1453?

Pronouns: Ronald Hutton and Claude Lecouteux are *he* (their own author notes use it). The translator, Jon E. Graham, and others whose pronouns I don't know are *they*.

Quote rule: every verbatim quote is 25 words or fewer and carries a cite. Hutton is cited by journal page. The Lecouteux EPUB has no print page numbers, so it is cited by chapter and section heading (for example "ch. 1, The Feast of the Ladies of the Night"). Hutton's own page references to Lecouteux (for example "Lecouteux 2011, 153") point to the print edition and are passed on as given. Everything else is paraphrase.

## 1. Hutton, "The Wild Hunt and the Witches' Sabbath" (2014)

**Bibliographic line.** Ronald Hutton, "The Wild Hunt and the Witches' Sabbath," *Folklore* 125, no. 2 (August 2014): 161–78. DOI 10.1080/0015587X.2014.896968.

**Argument.** Hutton argues that "the Wild Hunt" is not a medieval category. He calls it "essentially a modern construction, derived largely from the work of Grimm" (p. 175), dating from Grimm's *Deutsche Mythologie* (1835). Two habits of method came with it, and later scholars kept both: treating every recorded variant as the decay of one ancient myth, and using 19th- and 20th-century folklore to fill out and interpret medieval texts (pp. 162–63). Hutton names Ginzburg, Pócs and Lecouteux as the current holders of the "cult of the dead" reading (pp. 161–62). His own method does the opposite. He uses only sources written before 1600, assumes no unified older system behind them, and keeps the different kinds of procession apart (p. 164). He says plainly that he is not denying that popular beliefs, some probably ancient, fed into early modern witchcraft (pp. 162, 175). His target is the narrower claim that processions of the dead were the core of an ancient pagan myth that led straight to the sabbath.

**How he separates the strands.** Hutton argues that the modern Wild Hunt is "primarily a conflation of two different kinds of nocturnal procession or cavalcade" (p. 164). In his conclusion he adds a third component (p. 175):

1. **The women's company.** This is a procession "composed mainly of female spirits," often visiting homes "to bless them if the inhabitants were clean and hospitable" (p. 164). Living people claimed to join it, often in spirit while their bodies stayed in bed. It is led by a supernatural woman whom clerics called Diana or Herodias, and who also went by local names: Holda, Abundia, Satia, Percht (p. 164).
2. **The lone spectral huntsman.** Following Lecouteux's own typology (2011, 56–84), there are three of these: a demon chasing sinners, a sinful hunter doomed to ride forever, and a wild man chasing otherworldly prey. None normally has a retinue (p. 164). Herne the Hunter goes entirely: he is first recorded in Shakespeare and was attached to the Hunt by Grimm, Harrison Ainsworth and then Murray (p. 164).
3. **The army or procession of the dead.** These are human dead, mostly penitents, noisy, "rarely regarded as attractive or benevolent" (p. 164), and usually people who died early and violently (p. 175).

On how these relate, Hutton grants that Ginzburg and Lecouteux saw the difference between the strands, but treated them as two faces of one primeval myth (p. 164). He shows that the medieval authors Lecouteux gathers to prove the strands were one (Guillaume de Guillerville, the *Roman de Confession*, Raoul de Presles) only *list* the two together, usually while condemning both. Being listed together is not being the same thing (p. 171). Real blends are rare:

- Adam de la Halle's play (13th c.) mixes them, but Hutton reads this as one writer reworking the material, not wider belief (pp. 171–72).
- Two 14th-century inquisition records do mix them: the Pyrenean sacristan of 1319 and the Milan women (pp. 172–73).
- In later trials the strands are usually kept apart. A Dolomites woman tried in 1506–10 set the "good game" against "the game of the devil," which included the dead (p. 173).

Hutton's overall finding is that "the armies and parades of the dead rarely feature in witch-trials" (p. 174), while the women's company appears in them often (p. 174).

**Dating of each strand.**

- **Women's company:** Hutton says it is "recorded relatively early in the Middle Ages, from the ninth century onward" (p. 174). It lasted strongly in popular belief across most of German-speaking Europe and parts of Italy, and trial cases cluster in the Alps and northern Italy, with an isolated outpost in Sicily that "lacked the dominant and leading female figure" of the Alpine form (p. 174). He leaves its pagan origin open but plausible: it "may well have pre-Christian origins" and "probably contributed directly" to the sabbath (p. 175). In particular it was likely "critical to … the flight to and from it" (p. 174).
- **Army of the dead:** Hutton finds no clear ancient evidence for roaming companies of the dead (pp. 165–66). He dates the strand to an 11th-century turn toward interest in the fate of Christian souls (p. 166), with a 12th-century distribution "from England across northern France to the Rhineland" (p. 168). In the 13th century it spread into German lands as *das wütende Heer* and was turned demonic by churchmen (pp. 168–69). It faded from England and France in the 15th century but stayed strong in German lands (pp. 170–71), even though it was not linked to witches there (p. 174). Its seasonality, with the dead riding at Christmas and the Ember Days, is "not found earlier" than the 16th century (p. 170).
- **Lone huntsman:** medieval in origin, with "no connection with the witch-trials" (p. 175).
- **A third night-travel tradition:** the benandanti-type night battles. Hutton places them only in a zone from north-east Italy through the Balkans to Hungary (p. 173).

## 2. Lecouteux, *Phantom Armies of the Night* (2011)

**Bibliographic line.** Claude Lecouteux, *Phantom Armies of the Night: The Wild Hunt and the Ghostly Processions of the Undead*, trans. Jon E. Graham (Rochester, VT: Inner Traditions, 2011), eISBN 978-1-59477-806-3. Originally *Chasses fantastiques et cohortes de la nuit au Moyen Âge* (Paris: Imago, 1999). Read as the EPUB, without print pagination. Lecouteux is a former Sorbonne professor of medieval literature. The publisher is a popular esoteric press. The book prints long translated primary texts but runs a strong reconstructionist argument alongside them. Hutton supplied a jacket blurb calling it "a viable working hypothesis" (front matter), then took apart its central claim three years later.

**Structure.**
- **Introduction.** His working definition is: "the Wild Hunt is a band of the dead whose passage over the earth at certain times of the year is accompanied by diverse phenomena" (Introduction).
- **Part One, "The Hosts of the Night."** Ch. 1 covers the Good Women who roam the night, ch. 2 the demon troops, ch. 3 the troops of the dead, ch. 4 phantom armies.
- **Part Two, "The Supernatural Hunters."** Chapters 5–7 cover the diabolical huntsman, the wild huntsman and the cursed hunter.
- **Part Three, "The Wild Hunt."** Ch. 8 covers Herla, ch. 9 Orderic, ch. 10 later medieval developments, and ch. 11 the 14th–15th-century blends. This is the key chapter for the Good Ladies, with the sections "Dame Holle," "Percht and the Host of Children," "Dame Abundia" and "The Game of Diana." Ch. 12 covers masks and charivari, ch. 13 Scandinavia, ch. 14 calendar dates.
- **Part Four, "Odin and the Wild Hunt."** Ch. 15 surveys the scholarship, ch. 16 argues for Indo-European roots, ch. 17 tests the Odin and warrior-brotherhood hypotheses, and ch. 18 concludes.
- **Six appendices.** These include a 14th/15th-century German night charm, Cysat's Lucerne testimony, Risco's Galician "Society of the Bone" and a list of names.

Just past 1453 (useful for late-game events, not for the faith's founding):
- **1457, Val di Fassa:** Nicholas of Cusa examines two women who followed **Richella** (*quasi Fortuna*, "she who presides over fortune") (ch. 11, The Game of Diana).
- **1468:** the Munich *Thesaurus pauperum* describes the fullest table rite: bread, cheese, milk, eggs, wine, water, spoons, plates and knives, set for "Fraw Percht" and her troop between Christmas and Epiphany (ch. 1).
- **1485, Bormio:** Bernard of Como records a *ludus bonae societatis* (ch. 11).
- **c. 1497, Fiè:** Zuanne della Piatte speaks of the Venusberg "where Dame Herodias lives" (ch. 11, The Mountain of Venus).

**The Milan testimony in detail** (Lecouteux, ch. 11, The Game of Diana, via Behringer). This is the richest single pre-1453 description of what the company *did*:

- **Greeting.** Members greet their mistress with "May you fare well, Lady Horiens," and she answers "May you fare well, good people."
- **Form.** They go to the game as animals (a donkey, a fox) or as people, living or dead.
- **The executed dead.** Those who were beheaded or hanged are present but do not dare lift their heads.
- **Animals.** The company kills and eats animals, puts the bones back in the skins, and the mistress strikes them with her staff, which has an apple on it, and revives them. They are never much good for work afterwards.
- **House visits.** They go through people's houses, eat, drink and "rejoice" in spacious, well-ordered ones, and "the mistress then gives her blessing to this house."

*B. The army of the dead and its "Hellequin" names* (ch. 3, 8–10).

- **Otloh** (c. 1010–70); **Wipert** c. 1060 (Narni).
- **Ekkehard of Aura, 1123, Worms province.** Dead knights include Count Emicho, who asks for prayers and alms.
- **Hugh of Mans, 1135.** A ghost travels "in the company of" a wicked throng.
- **Peter of Cluny, 1135–42.** The knight Guigo in Dauphiné.
- ***Miracles of Sainte-Foi*** (1108–55, Alsace). Hordes of white pilgrims and red knights.
- **Orderic Vitalis.** This is the night of 1 January 1091 (or 1092) at Saint-Aubin in the diocese of Lisieux, as told to Orderic by the priest Walchelin. The column includes looters, biers carried by dwarves, **women riding side-saddle on saddles studded with red-hot nails as punishment for lechery**, clergy, and black knights. The priest is told it is the *familia Herlechini*. Lecouteux judges the story "quite unreliable," a clerical "fabrication" built from older traditions, partly to serve Saint-Évroul's quarrel with the bishop of Lisieux and Robert of Bellême (ch. 9, A Reversed Vision).
- **Peterborough, 1127** (Laud/Peterborough Chronicle and Hugh Candidus). Black, ugly huntsmen on black horses and black goats, with black wide-eyed hounds. They were seen in the deer park and the woods towards Stamford, and horns were heard at night from the arrival of Abbot Henry of Poitou through Lent to Easter (ch. 8, end).
- **William of Malmesbury, c. 1125.** A procession of both sexes and every rank, led by a lord in a chariot (ch. 10).
- **Walter Map, *De nugis* (dated by Lecouteux 1180–86).** Herla visits a pygmy king's hollow mountain and comes back two hundred years later. His troop never dismounts and was last seen plunging into the Wye in Henry II's first year. A second passage describes the *Herlethingi* as a silent troop with "a concourse of men and women" and carts, seen at noon in the Welsh March (ch. 8).
- **Herbert of Clairvaux** (c. 1178), Yonne. A **living village woman** tells a young guard she is "to meet it that very night." Then a troop of dead **artisans** passes, never touching the ground (ch. 10, A Reduction of Oderic Vitalis's Narrative).
- **Helinand of Froidmont** (c. 1200). The *familia Hellequini* has finished its penance, and the name is glossed as *Karolus Quintus*. Helinand also tells his uncle's story of a forest full of the dead and demons (ch. 10).
- ***La Relation de Rein*** (1185–1200), the ***Chanson d'Antioche*** (the spectral army at Antioch in 1098), and **John of Viktring** (1343, Carniola) (ch. 10).

*C. Huntsmen* (ch. 5–7). These are 13th-century exempla:
- Caesarius of Heisterbach's priest's concubine, in the archdiocese of Mainz;
- Helinand's charcoal burner at Nevers;
- Gervase of Tilbury's Arthurian hunt;
- Stephen of Bourbon's Arthur's knights at Mont du Chat.

All are clerical moral tales.

**Gender content.** I have gathered this here because the book is spread out.
- **A woman leads, and women are the majority, in the Ladies strand.** Every pre-1453 leader name is female: Diana, Herodias/Pharaildis, Holda, Satia, Abundia/Abonde, the *bonae res* or *felices dominae*, Percht, Madonna Horiens, Richella. Clerics assign the belief to women ("many women," Burchard; "old women … especially women," William of Auvergne; "foolish peasant women," Berthold) (ch. 1).
- **Men in the Ladies strand.**
  - **Directly attested:** Stephen of Bourbon's man who goes with the *bonae res* (ch. 13). John of Salisbury's "simpleminded … men" are attested as **believers**, not as members.
  - **Implied:** Jean de Meun's one-in-three of the land's people (ch. 1), and Lecouteux's own question whether "men and children make up the other two-thirds" of Herodias's following (ch. 1, Diana and Herodias).
  - **Hybrid case:** Hutton's 1319 sacristan, whom Lecouteux does not name or cite as a participant.
  - **Post-1453:** Cysat's elderly *couple* (c. 1530) and the "men and women still living" (ch. 11; Appendix 2); Stoeckhlin (executed 1587); Zuanne della Piatte.
- **The dead who died before their time.** William of Auvergne (1230s) already gives the idea that souls killed by violence wander out their allotted term (ch. 4, A Critical Piece of Evidence). Geiler (1508), Crusius (16th c.), Cysat and Milan's executed dead show the same theme later (ch. 11).
- **Unbaptized children.**
  - The **Byland** monk (c. 1400) tells of a stillborn child, unnamed and unbaptized, riding in a procession of the dead. Its father names it and clothes it in his shirt (ch. 11, Percht and the Host of Children).
  - Crusius (16th c.) puts all unbaptized infants into the army.
  - **Percht as leader of unbaptized dead children** is **19th- and 20th-century folklore** (Rumpf; Waschnitius), not medieval. Lecouteux says himself that medieval witnesses portray Percht as equal to Abundia (ch. 11).
- **Women punished for sexual sin in the army of the dead.** This is Orderic's red-hot saddles (ch. 9). The priest's concubine is hunted in Caesarius and Helinand (ch. 5). It is the clerical, misogynist strand, and has nothing to do with the Ladies.
- **The meaning of "witch."** Jean de Meun's followers believe they are *estries* (ch. 1). Lecouteux reads John of Viktring's wounded "old women" (1343) as witches whose Doubles fought in the air (ch. 10). That is his interpretation, not the text.
- **Gender crossing.** There is almost none, and none at all in the Ladies strand.
  - The giant huntsman Fasolt "wears his hair braided like a woman" (*Eckenlied*, c. 1250; ch. 6).
  - The 1316 *Fauvel* charivari men wear "garments backward," monks' robes and cow udders sewn on (ch. 12). This is carnival inversion attached to Hellequin, not to the Ladies.
  - Lecouteux calls Hellequin's herald Crokesou "her" (ch. 11, Dame Abundia). Hutton calls Crokesou "a wild-haired male" (p. 172). This is an unresolved discrepancy, and minor.

**Where Lecouteux leans on post-1453 folklore or speculative pagan continuity.** Flag all of these if the mod cites him.
- **The Wild Hunt as a band of the dead.** His definition merges the strands that Hutton separates. He then reads the Ladies' feast as "in reality a repast of the dead" (ch. 11, New Assessment) and folds Abundia, Satia, the Good Folk and Percht into "the cult of the dead" (ch. 11, Requests and Gifts). This is the move Hutton rejects (Hutton pp. 164, 171).
- **Indo-European origin.** He makes Vedic Indra's Maruts and Rudra the "archetype" (ch. 16; ch. 18). He also leans throughout on Dumézil's "third function" (fertility) as the key that unlocks everything (ch. 1, A Rite of the Third Function; ch. 16). This is typology, which he concedes is not genetic (ch. 16, The Troop of the Maruts).
- **Shamanism and the "Double."** He reads Burchard's women as people with an external soul (*hamr*) that clerics failed to understand. He links Burchard's cloud battles to the 16th-century benandanti (ch. 1, The Journey of the Double; ch. 10). Hutton places the night-battle tradition only in the Friuli–Balkans–Hungary zone (p. 173).
- **Pagan goddesses behind the names.**
  - Diana as a blend with a Celtic "Di Ana / Anu" (ch. 1). Unsupported.
  - Matronae and Bede's *modranecht* (ch. 1).
  - The Roman "table of souls" as the source of the table rite (ch. 1). Plausible but undemonstrated.
  - The Dagda and Philippe Walter's cock-and-dog etymology of Hennequin (ch. 6; ch. 7).
- **Warrior brotherhoods.** He finds Otto Höfler's masked warrior brotherhoods (*Kultische Geheimbünde der Germanen*, 1934) "right on the mark" (Appendix 4). Höfler was a Nazi-era scholar whose *Männerbund* thesis was politically loaded. He also uses a 20th-century Galician "Society of the Bone" (Risco 1946) as a model.
- **Calendar, place names and other late evidence.**
  - The Christmas–Epiphany timing, the Loyal Eckhart, the Hörselberg and Venusberg, and Dame Holle with Eckhart all come from Agricola (1534), Praetorius (1663) and Cysat (1545–1614) (ch. 11).
  - The Scandinavian Oskoreia and Guro Rysserova are recorded from the 18th to 20th centuries (ch. 13).
  - Most of his chapter 14 calendar rests on 19th-century folklore (the *Handwörterbuch des deutschen Aberglaubens*).
- **Odin.** To his credit, Lecouteux dismantles the Odin attribution: "we can find no connection between Odin and the Wild Hunt" (ch. 15, False Testimonies). He also rejects any survival of a Diana cult: "I can find no basis for postulating the survival of her worship into the Middle Ages" (ch. 1, note 8). He explicitly distances himself from Margaret Murray.

**Factual slips in Lecouteux to watch.**
- Burchard is dated "around 1066"; it should be c. 1008–12.
- Regino is dated "899"; the usual date is c. 906.
- The Canon Episcopi is said to appear first in 872 "in the capitularies of Charles the Bald." This is not the standard view, and his only support is a cite to Harmening, *Superstitio*, p. 97.
- He places Juliana Winkler's child-revival testimony "in the trial conducted by Fra Beltramino." It actually comes from the Fiè/Völs trials of 1506–10, which he cites to Behringer.
- Polotsk is placed in "Ukraine"; it is in Belarus.
- Stephen of Bourbon's "Cevennes diocese" is not a real diocese; check the Lecoy de la Marche no. 97 original.
- Both authors call Peter of Blois "archbishop of Canterbury." He was a secretary to archbishops of Canterbury, never archbishop himself.
- "Bensozia" is listed among the Ladies' names with no source (ch. 11; ch. 12). Do not use it until it is sourced.

## 3. Corrections and additions to the prior note

**Confirmed.**
- **The core claim.** The best-evidenced pre-1453 witch-adjacent phenomenon is a mostly female night company under a Lady, who visits and blesses households. Hutton (pp. 164, 174–75) and Lecouteux (ch. 1, ch. 11) both support this.
- **No Murray.** Hutton removes Herne (p. 164) and treats "the Wild Hunt" itself as Grimm's construct (p. 175). Lecouteux removes the Diana cult and Odin (ch. 1, note 8; ch. 15). Neither offers covens, a horned god or an organized pagan church.
- **"Delusion and penance" before c. 1300.** The clerical sources in both books condemn the belief as demonic illusion and preach against it: Regino, Burchard, John of Salisbury, William of Auvergne, Berthold, Guillerville. Lecouteux notes that Gratian's *Decretum* (c. 1142) spread the Canon Episcopi into confessors' manuals and, eventually, inquisitors' manuals (ch. 1, Diana and Herodias).
- **The Milan case as the hinge from folk belief to trial.** Hutton (pp. 172–73) and Lecouteux (ch. 11) both rank it as the key pre-1453 bridge.
- **The prior note's 1159 John of Salisbury entry.** Confirmed, including that men as well as women held the belief (Lecouteux, ch. 1).
- **Domrémy's "ladies' tree" now has older parallels.** Raoul de Presles (1370s) places fairies "in stables and in trees." The *Ysengrimus* (c. 1148) has Herodias resting on oaks and hazels (Lecouteux ch. 11; ch. 1).

**Changes.**
1. **Milan date.** Hutton gives "a pair of women at Milan in 1370" (p. 172). Lecouteux gives "around 1380–1390" (ch. 11). The prior note's 1384 (Sibilla) and 1390 (Pierina) matches Lecouteux and the standard literature (Muraro; Verga 1899, cited in Lecouteux's notes). Treat Hutton's "1370" as a slip. Lecouteux's "Petrina … since sixteen" is Pierina, the 1390 case. The name "Madonna Oriente" in the prior note appears as *Horiens/Horiente* in Lecouteux. **Settled (2026-10-27):** Hutton himself now gives 1384 and 1390 (*Queens of the Wild*, 2022, p. 123).
2. **The company is not exclusively women.** The prior note's statement that "the company is a multitude of women" and that no male-assigned person appears needs softening. Men appear as **participants**, not only as believers:
   - the Pyrenean sacristan of 1319, who travelled with the good ladies (Hutton p. 172);
   - Stephen of Bourbon's man with the *bonae res* (13th c.; Lecouteux ch. 13);
   - the executed dead men in the Milan company (Lecouteux ch. 11).

   The leader and the core are female, and clerics gender the belief as women's. **No pre-1453 source shows a male-assigned person joining *as a woman* or in women's dress.**
3. **Add the 10th-century Italian evidence.** Rather of Verona (d. 974) knows the Herodias belief (Lecouteux ch. 1). This adds Lombardy/Verona as a pre-1000 region and slightly strengthens an 867 start. Hutton also says the women's company is recorded "from the ninth century onward" (p. 174), though he cites no 9th-century text. Lecouteux's 872 Charles the Bald claim would help an 867 start if it were verified. As it stands it is unverified. **Update (2026-10-27):** Rather says many people, women and men, held Herodias "as a queen, even as a goddess" with "a third of the world" her realm. He does not mention night rides (Hutton 2022, pp. 114–15; *Praeloquia* I.10, PL 136).
4. **Add Flanders and the name Pharaildis** (*Ysengrimus*, Ghent, c. 1148).
5. **Add the Bavarian/Austrian line.** Berthold of Regensburg's table "for the blessed ladies" (13th c.) gives a pre-1300 **table rite east of the Rhine**. **Percht** is first named c. 1350, and there is a Viennese reference with salt among the offerings (c. 1440s; Lecouteux ch. 1). **Update (2026-10-27):** Percht's bare name appears in the 13th century, "Lady Perchten" (Hutton 2022, p. 122, via Rumpf 1991, p. 61); the developed figure dates from c. 1350.
6. **Seasonal timing.** The prior note does not fix dates. The earliest sources give only "fixed nights" (Regino) or "three nights a week" (Jean de Meun). The **Twelve Nights / Epiphany** timing is attested for the Ladies from **c. 1350** in German lands (Lecouteux ch. 1). Hutton's point that seasonality is "not found earlier" than the 16th century (p. 170) applies to the army of the dead, not to the Ladies. **Update (2026-10-27):** northern Italy too, before 1453: at Padua in 1423 Bernardino of Siena attacked old women who said they travelled "in the ride of Herodias on the night of Epiphany" (Hutton 2022, p. 123).
7. **Separate the army of the dead.** The prior note mixes Holda with the night-ride complex. Following Hutton, the Hellequin/Herla/*wütendes Heer* tradition is a **separate, chiefly 11th–12th-century clerical penitential strand**. It is centred on knights, is mostly male, and has a different geography (England, northern France, the Rhineland). It "rarely" appears in witch trials (Hutton pp. 166–69, 174).
8. **Where the persecution phase happens.** Hutton places women's-company trial cases in the Alps and northern Italy, the cradle of the early 15th-century sabbath (p. 174). The prior note's escalation chain should centre the flip on **Lombardy and the Alpine dioceses**, not on the Rhineland where the belief was first written down.
9. **What the sabbath took from the company.** Hutton narrows it: the company most plausibly gave the sabbath the **flight** (p. 174), not the whole stereotype. This supports the prior note's "accusers' fantasy" framing for everything else.
10. **One flavour item for the Valais escalation.** Lecouteux is hesitant, but his reading of John of Viktring (1343, "old women … wounded") is not evidence of witches. Do not use it as a pre-Valais witch trace.

**Partly answers open question 3 from the prior note (Burchard's wording).** Lecouteux gives English versions, via Lecouteux and Marcq 1990, of three Burchard passages:
- the closed-doors killers lying beside their husbands;
- the cloud battle "by virtue of other limbs provided you by the devil";
- the Holda host of "devils transformed into the likenesses of women" (ch. 1).

The straw-for-heart detail is in Lecouteux's paraphrase, not in his quotation. Still check against McNeill and Gamer.

## 4. Design implications for a CK3 "Good Ladies" faith

**Names of the Lady, by region and period** (all pre-1453 unless marked):

| Region (CK3) | Period | Name(s) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lotharingia (Prüm, Trier) | c. 906 | Diana, Herodias (clerical) | Regino |
| Rhineland / Franconia (Worms) | c. 1010 | Holda (vernacular); Diana, Herodias | Burchard |
| Lombardy / Verona | 10th c. | Herodias | Rather (via Lecouteux) |
| Flanders (Ghent) | c. 1148 | Pharaildis (= Herodias) | *Ysengrimus* |
| England / northern France | 1159 | *noctiluca*, Herodias | John of Salisbury |
| Île-de-France | 1230s–1370s | Domina Abundia, Satia; Dame Abonde / Habonde; *bonae dominae* | William of Auvergne; Jean de Meun; Raoul de Presles |
| South-east France | mid-13th c. | *bonae res* ("Good Things") | Stephen of Bourbon |
| Bavaria / Austria | 13th c.; c. 1350+ | *felices dominae*, *nahtvrouwen*; **Percht / Perchta** (bare name 13th c.; developed c. 1350); Habundia (Vienna) | Berthold; *Mirror of Souls*; Ebendorfer |
| Lombardy (Milan) | 1380s–90 | Madonna Oriente / Horiens ("Lady of the East") | Beltramino trials |
| Tyrol / Fassa | 1457 (post) | Richella, *bona domina* | Nicholas of Cusa |

A good in-game approach is a single deity with **regional names**: Holda in German culture areas, Abonde or Habonde in French, Herodias or Oriente in Lombard, Percht in Bavarian after 1350. Clerical-facing text should use Diana or Herodias. All of these names are ASCII-safe for the glyph check.

**Practices, with their evidence.**
- **Leaving tables set and vessels uncovered at night** for the Ladies, who eat without diminishing anything and bless the house. Covered vessels mean the Ladies withhold their gifts. Sources: William of Auvergne 1230s; Berthold 13th c.; Adam de la Halle 1276; Thesaurus 1468.
- **Travelling in spirit while the body stays in bed**, on set nights. Sources: Burchard; Jean de Meun ("three nights a week"); 1319; Milan.
- **Visiting houses and blessing the clean, orderly, spacious and hospitable ones.** Sources: Hutton p. 164; William of Auvergne; Milan; Fournier c. 1320.
- **Abundance and satiety as the Lady's gift**, which is what the names Abundia and Satia mean. Food, drink and salt are offered for a good year (Ebendorfer, c. 1440s).
- **Ritual greeting of the Lady** ("May you fare well…", Milan).
- **The Lady's staff reviving slaughtered animals** (Milan). This is a strong miracle or event hook.
- **Knowledge for healing and divination gained from the company.** The 1319 sacristan and the Milan women were consulted as cunning folk (Hutton pp. 172–73).
- **Twelve Nights / Epiphany timing**, only after c. 1350, in German lands, and in northern Italy by 1423 (Hutton 2022, p. 123). Before that, use "fixed nights."
- **Trees as the Ladies' resting places** (*Ysengrimus*; Raoul de Presles; the Domrémy tree, 1431).

**Regions.** The core regions are the Rhineland and Lotharingia (Worms, Trier, Prüm), Île-de-France, and Lombardy. The secondary regions are Flanders, Bavaria and Austria, south-east France, and the Pyrenees (Foix, 1319). The Alps are best treated as the **persecution theatre after c. 1400** rather than as a founding region.

**Best start date.** **1066** stays the cleanest choice: Burchard is fresh, it sits in the "delusion and penance" phase, and Rather gives the belief an Italian foothold. **1178** is the richest choice for practice flavour. John of Salisbury (1159) and the *Ysengrimus* (c. 1148) are recent, and William of Auvergne's house-visiting and table rite follows within about 60 years. Use 1178 if tables and house-blessing are the faith's centre, which the evidence suggests they should be. **867** rests on Hutton's "ninth century onward" (p. 174) and Lecouteux's unverified 872, so it is still inference.

**What stays out.**
- **The army of the dead / Hellequin / Herla / *wütendes Heer*.** Keep it out of the faith's doctrine. Hutton shows it is a distinct, mostly male, knightly and penitential strand that rarely appears in trials. At most it belongs in *event* flavour: a night host of penitent dead seen in Normandy or the Rhineland, or something a cleric lumps in with the Ladies (Guillerville). The one hybrid allowed by evidence is the **executed dead within the Lady's company**, from Milan.
- **The Wild Hunt as a term, Odin or Wotan as leader, Herne, and the lone huntsman.** Out. None has pre-1453 support in this form (Hutton pp. 164, 175; Lecouteux ch. 15).
- **The sabbath stereotype.** Child-eating, devil-worship and the orgy stay in accusers' and persecution text only. Burchard's closed-door killers and John of Salisbury's devoured infants are clerical framings, not the believers' own account. Hutton argues that only flight passed from the company to the sabbath.
- **Indo-European, Dumézilian, shamanic or "cult of the dead" framing in loc.** This is Lecouteux's speculation. Do not put it in the faith description as fact.
- **Percht leading unbaptized children, Dame Holle with the Loyal Eckhart, and Oskoreia.** All post-1453 folklore.
- **No living community.** Modern heathenry, Wicca, folk-horror Perchten runs and Alpine Krampus/Perchtenlauf groups are living or modern practices. They are not this faith and not its continuation.

**Gender transgression and male-assigned participants: evidence versus design.**
- *Evidenced:* the Lady is female. The company is mainly female spirits with mainly female human followers, and clerics gender the belief as women's (Hutton p. 164; Lecouteux ch. 1). Men are attested as participants, as men: 1319 (Hutton p. 172), Stephen of Bourbon (Lecouteux ch. 13), the Milan dead (Lecouteux ch. 11).
- *Not evidenced:* any male-assigned person joining the Ladies *as a woman*, any cross-dressing in the Ladies strand, or any gender change granted by the Lady. Neither source has it. The only dress inversion in either book is the 1316 *Fauvel* charivari (Lecouteux ch. 12), and it belongs to the Hellequin/mockery strand.
- **Design choice (label it as such):** "The company is of women, and the Lady decides who her women are," so daughters ride as women among the Ladies. This is invention, made more plausible by the fact that the record does not keep the company sex-exclusive, but it is still invention. Put that statement in the design doc. Do not imply in-game text that it is documented.

## 5. Open questions

1. **Canon Episcopi before Regino.** Is there any real text behind Lecouteux's "872, capitularies of Charles the Bald" (via Harmening, *Superstitio*, p. 97)? If so, an 867 start gets a document. *Hutton 2022 (p. 111) dates the canon to Regino's compilation for the archbishop of Trier, soon after 900, and gives no support for 872.*
2. **Rather of Verona.** What is the exact text and date of his Herodias passage (the *Praeloquia*, c. 935?), and does it say "a third" of humanity serves her? **Answered:** *Praeloquia* I.10 (PL 136); yes, "a third of the world"; believers women and men; no night ride (Hutton 2022, pp. 114–15).
3. **The 1319 sacristan.** He is unnamed in Hutton; elsewhere he is identified as Arnaud Gélis of Pamiers. Check Duvernoy 1:137–39 for his exact words about the "good ladies," whether he describes women or the dead, and any detail on tidy houses.
4. **Hutton's "Milan 1370."** Confirm against Muraro (*La signora del gioco*, 1976) and Verga (1899) that the two trials are 1384 and 1390. **Answered:** 1384 and 1390 (Hutton 2022, p. 123).
5. **Stephen of Bourbon no. 97.** What are the diocese and the exact words for the man who travelled with the *bonae res*? This is the clearest pre-1453 male participant in the Ladies strand.
6. **"Bensozia."** What is the source (a Couserans synodal text?) and the date? Do not use the name until then. **Answered:** a council of the bishop of Couserans in 1280 (Hutton 2022, p. 120). Hutton doubts the bishop met the name locally, so use it only in a cleric's voice.
7. **Crokesou's gender** in Adam de la Halle: male (Hutton) or female (Lecouteux)? Check the text if the mod uses the herald figure.
8. **Twelve Nights gating.** Does any pre-1350 source tie the Ladies to Christmas–Epiphany, or should a seasonal mechanic unlock only after about 1350 and only in German-speaking areas? **Partly answered:** Padua 1423 adds northern Italy; no pre-1350 source found (Hutton 2022, pp. 122–23).
9. **Persecution geography.** Should the escalation chain fire mainly in Lombardy and the Alpine dioceses, following Hutton p. 174, with the Rhineland and France staying in the "penance" register throughout?
10. **Event flavour from the army-of-the-dead strand.** Is it worth an optional "the Host of the dead passes" event in Normandy, England or the Rhineland, clearly separate from the faith, or does that blur Hutton's distinction for players?
