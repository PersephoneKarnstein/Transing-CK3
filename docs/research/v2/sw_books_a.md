# Sex workers' own writing (A): Mac & Smith, Gira Grant, *We Too*

Purpose: ground how Crusader Dolls writes sex work into a faith's religion and mechanics, after the user's decision (2026-10-24) that it may appear "if handled well". First use: the 1178 song-houses faith (`japan_goodwin_kudenshu.md`, cited "JG §n"; event numbers are JG §5's). Writing rules: `docs/faith_design.md` §16 ("rule n").

Texts, from `scratchpad/v2/ocr/sw/`:
- **RP**: Juno Mac and Molly Smith, *Revolting Prostitutes* (Verso 2018). EPUB, read in full (intro to conclusion; notes skipped). Cited by chapter and section heading.
- **PW**: Melissa Gira Grant, *Playing the Whore* (Verso 2014). Read in full. Printed pages.
- **WT**: Natalie West with Tina Horn, eds., *We Too* (Feminist Press 2021). Read: foreword, introduction, Paige, Raphael, Sacco, Schulte/Szurgot/Walker, Almodovar (part), Gira Grant (New Orleans), Aragon, Rebelle Cunt, Anonymous, Lee, Rivera, Doroshow, Carlisle (Hookers Army), the contributor biographies; Kiley and Horn in part. Skimmed or not read: the rest. Printed pages (each page number in the OCR follows its page).

Rules for this note:
- Quotes are verbatim, 25 words or fewer, and few. Everything else is paraphrase.
- The anthology's writers are living people telling their own lives. The note uses their published arguments only. No writer, and no person they name, becomes a character or a model for one. Personal details are left out except where an argument needs them.
- Several essays recount abuse in childhood or adolescence. None of it is used, cited or paraphrased here. Suicide and self-harm mentions are skipped too.
- Insiders first. All three books are by sex workers or former sex workers. Where they quote outside commentators (MacKinnon, Dworkin, Raymond and others), this note treats those lines as the hostile voice they are.
- Trans men are out of the mod's scope. Cyd Nova's essay (WT 125) is noted as read, and that's all.

**Relation to existing notes.**
- `book_serano_whipping_girl.md` row 16 already says that trans sex work comes from poverty and exclusion, not from a wish to be objectified, and that any sex-work institution should be a guild of mutual protection, never a calling. These books confirm both points from inside the trade, and Principle 15 below builds on them.
- `book_revathi_truth_about_me.md` is an insider account in which sex work happens under a guru's house, with shares, watching and flight. Its warning about houses that confine their workers is the same as Goodwin's warning about the *chōja* (G 34, 122). Critiques 1, 5 and 6 below follow from it.
- JG §6's "premise risk" (QV pp. 26–27): never "trans women belong in the trade". This note adds the movement's own version of that warning (PW pp. 19–20; RP ch. 1) and a mechanical audit (critique 11).

## 1. *Revolting Prostitutes* (RP)

**Bibliographic line.** Juno Mac and Molly Smith, *Revolting Prostitutes: The Fight for Sex Workers' Rights* (London and New York: Verso, 2018). Two UK sex workers and organisers (SWARM). Their stance is Marxist-feminist and labour-centred.

**Summary.** The book is explicitly not a memoir and not an "empowerment" argument (Intro, "This Is Not a Memoir"). The authors say the sex industry is sexist and harmful. They place the harm in what the law, the police, borders and poverty do to the people who sell sex: assault, arrest, eviction, deportation, theft of earnings. Chapters 1–3 make the theory (sex, work, borders). Chapters 4–8 test it against legal models: Britain's partial criminalisation, full criminalisation in the US, South Africa and Kenya, the Nordic "end demand" model, German, Dutch and Nevada licensing, and decriminalisation in Aotearoa and New South Wales. The conclusion argues that power comes from solidarity among workers and from giving people resources, not from rescue.

**Key claims (with refs).**
1. *Sex workers are ordinary members of every community, and the "prostitute" is mostly a symbol for people who have never sold sex.* Debates use "The Prostitute" to say things about all women, and they let the actual workers drop out of view (Intro; ch. 1 "Is Sex Good?").
2. *Neither sex positivity nor victimhood.* The authors claim a right to be "sex-ambivalent" (ch. 1 opening). They criticise the "Erotic Professional", who presents the work as a calling with little to do with pay, and the "Exited Woman", whose harm is turned into a demand for criminal law. Both pictures leave out the *unhappy* worker who still needs the income. Quoting Sarah Mann, they say she is caught between a camp that denies her experiences and a camp that denies her rights (ch. 1 "Is Sex Good?").
3. *People sell sex to get money* (ch. 2 "Is Work Bad?"). If the money is left out of the account, workers look broken. The book rejects the idea that sex workers act out their trauma (ch. 2). A Scottish minister heard that one worker was a mother, one a migrant, and one a trans woman who lost her job when she transitioned, and she treated them all as mercenary (ch. 2).
4. *Calling it work does not mean calling it good work.* To name something as work is the first step toward refusing it on your own terms (Federici, Wages for Housework). "You don't have to like your job to want to keep it" (ch. 2, closing).
5. *Consent is not bought.* Workers set boundaries, and the trade's own vocabulary shows it. Rape of a sex worker is rape. A worker named Nikita: "Part of believing me when I say I have been raped is believing me when I say I haven't been" (ch. 2 "Is Work Good?").
6. *The rescue industry.* The term is Agustín's (Intro, "All Hell Broke Loose"). Philanthropy gave middle-class women a public role at the cost of the working-class women it policed. Women "rescued from brothels" in the UK are still sent to live with nuns and are taught feminine crafts such as sewing, baking and candles (ch. 1). The Magdalene laundries became the anti-prostitution charity Ruhama (ch. 1). An "exit scheme" is named for what a woman must leave behind, not for where she wants to go (ch. 6 "The Exit Services"). Help that is withheld until a woman has already quit is no help (ch. 6, the Stockholm case). The Cambodian slogan sums it up: "Don't Talk to Me about Sewing Machines, Talk to Me about Workers' Rights" (Conclusion, "First, Do No Harm").
7. *Third parties and coercion are real, and law against "pimping" hits the workers.* Two workers sharing a flat for safety count as a "brothel". A cleaner who called an ambulance was convicted (ch. 4 "Indoors"). Managers can extract more because the law pushes workers toward them (ch. 4; ch. 7 "The 'Charmed' Circle"). Debt to a smuggler turns into bondage when the worker has no legal recourse (ch. 3 "The Role of the Border").
8. *Remedies for coercion that come from the community.* In West Bengal, DMSC's self-regulatory boards screen for "minors or unwilling adults", help them leave if they want to, run a workers' bank against debt bondage, and forced fairer commissions on brothel keepers. Empower in Thailand helped a migrant renegotiate her debt to her boss: the same repayment, but she worked independently. She did not want rescue (Conclusion, "First, Do No Harm").
9. *Mutual aid is old and is political work.* Medieval brothel workers formed guilds and struck. In the fifteenth century, Bavarian prostitutes told city councils their activity was work, not sin. Nineteenth-century workers shared a common purse and childcare. In colonial Nairobi, women paid each other's fines and left each other their goods. Workers protested together after the Thika murders (Intro, "It Takes about Two Minutes to Politicise a Hooker").
10. *Migrants.* Borders make people vulnerable, and "trafficking" talk hides that. The migrant sex worker is the archetype of the stigmatised migrant, so solidarity with migrants and with whores go together (ch. 3 "Where Next?").
11. *Trans women.* Trans women are at the centre of the authors' feminism (Intro). The book's account of the trans woman's position: she is cast as a deceiver who "traps" men, or else as a threat (ch. 1 "Is Sex Bad?"); she is shut out of formal work (ch. 2); she is arrested for "walking while trans" (ch. 5 "Organising", Monica Jones); she is barred from Turkey's state brothels (ch. 7). Sharmus Outlaw's line "The girls need jobs" frames the work as survival, not as nature (ch. 5).
12. *Regulation can be harm too.* Licensing draws a "charmed circle". Workers inside it are confined, tested and registered. Workers outside it, often the poorest, migrants and trans women, are criminalised (ch. 7).

**What the mod takes.**
- Write the work as a *trade with conditions*: pay, shares, debts, who can refuse whom, who can be thrown out. Don't write it as a moral state or a sexuality.
- The unhappy worker is a legitimate figure. A woman may dislike the trade and still keep it for her mother's rice. Nothing in the faith asks her to love it.
- Coercion comes from a person or an office using power: a head of house, a debt-holder, a husband, a lord's official. The faith's remedy is renegotiation and the sisters' backing, as with DMSC and Empower. It is not abolition and it is not a rescuer from outside.
- Mutual aid is attested in the movement's own account of its history, so the house purse, paid fines, burials and care in old age are well grounded.

**What it must not do.**
- No "rescue into a convent and needlework" ending. Note that the Buddhist nunnery is the period analogue of "sent to live with nuns".
- No origin story of trauma, and no text that explains a woman's entry into the trade by past abuse (ch. 2).
- No "calling" or "vocation" framing (the Erotic Professional), and no text where the faith says patrons have a right to the women.
- No modern slogans in loc ("sex work is work", "rights", "decriminalise", "empower").

## 2. *Playing the Whore* (PW)

**Bibliographic line.** Melissa Gira Grant, *Playing the Whore: The Work of Sex Work* (London and New York: Verso / Jacobin, 2014). The author is a journalist, an organiser and a former sex worker. 135 pp.

**Summary.** Ten short chapters move the reader's gaze from sex workers onto the people who watch, police, rescue and debate them. These include the vice cop's sting video (ch. 1), the invented "prostitute" (ch. 2), the debate as spectacle (ch. 4), the rescue journalist (ch. 9) and the "other women" who fear being mistaken for whores (ch. 8). The book is short on testimony by design. Gira Grant declines to tell her own story because telling it would be "a peep show" (p. 34).

**Key claims (with refs).**
1. *The "prostitute imaginary".* Policing produces "a prostitute where before there had been only a woman" (p. 4). The imaginary is a fantasy of degradation that only noble rescuers attend to (p. 15).
2. *The identity is recent. The practice is old.* Before the nineteenth century no word meant only the sale of sex. "Whore" branded any woman outside respectability (pp. 14–15, quoting Agustín). The nineteenth century turned an occasional behaviour into a person and a class that the law could manage (p. 15). "Sex work" was coined by a worker, Carol Leigh, in 1978. It names labour, not a state of being (pp. 13–18).
3. *The carceral and sexual eye.* To see a sex worker only as her work, always available, is the policeman's eye. Even locked "rehabilitation" shelters use it: "This isn't compassion. This isn't charity. This is control" (p. 11).
4. *Discretion is not deception.* Workers conceal their work for the same reasons a therapist or a priest keeps boundaries. "Double life" talk hides why the concealment is needed (pp. 29–30).
5. *Storytelling as striptease.* The public wants the confession, the origin story and the bed. Telling the truth includes refusing the roles "virgin, victim, wretch, or whore" (p. 33). Outsiders know the worker only in "the red light, the bed, the men, the money" (p. 60).
6. *Refuse the debate.* Workers shouldn't have to defend the trade's existence in order to work free from harm. Their complaints about work are not evidence that they want out. Sarah Jaffe: "No one ever wanted to save me from the restaurant industry" (p. 39).
7. *There is no single sex industry.* Workers in a dungeon, an escort line, a porn site or a club move between sex work and "straight" service work. The commonest picture of a street worker is a "lazily constructed composite" (pp. 49–56). Sex work overlaps with ordinary service work and shares its workforce (pp. 55–56, 98–99).
8. *Rescue employs rescuers.* Rescue projects sell candles and jewellery made by the women they rescued (p. 57). Rescuers' jobs come out of workers' lost incomes (Goldman, p. 56).
9. *Whore stigma* (Pheterson, Nagle; pp. 75–77). The stigma attaches to "illicit femaleness". Any woman who breaks with "compulsory virtue" can be branded with it, so solidarity between women inside and outside the trade is possible. "If woman is other, whore is the other's other" (p. 77). Whore is "maybe the original intersectional insult" (p. 128).
10. *Sex work is performance and skill.* It means playing a role, showing a skill and offering empathy within professional limits, which would be recognised as labour in a nurse or a nanny (p. 90). Consent is negotiated over price, time, place and acts. Money does not remove consent (p. 92). Charlotte Shane's "unenthusiastic consent" is consent without desire. Bad sex at work is not rape, and rape at work is rape (p. 93).
11. *Rescue as eradication.* Cambodia's US-driven raids sent women to detention camps where people died. The US saw "no meaningful difference between the elimination of sex work and the elimination of sex workers themselves" (p. 109). Before the raids, Gira Grant's visit was by invitation, with condoms and talk (pp. 105–110).
12. *Trans women.* Media show trans women in the trade only as stereotypes, and anti-sex-work feminists (Raymond, Jeffreys) deny they are women (pp. 19–20). Trans women are profiled as workers whether or not they work (p. 9, Queens). Sylvia Rivera said trans women did street work because "the laws do not give us the right to go and get a job" as themselves, and STAR was funded partly by that work (pp. 116–117).
13. *Solidarity, not support* (p. 130). Turn the questions outward: what are the police, landlords and neighbours doing to sex workers? Cops are management under criminalisation (p. 131).

**What the mod takes.**
- Premodern fit. In 1178, "asobi" named a house of singers and its women. It did not name a sex act. Gira Grant's history means the bed-trade should be modelled as an *activity some women do*, not as an identity or a trait. The house name stays the women's identity.
- Write events from inside the house: shares, notes about patrons, the boat schedule, the head's ledger. The view in the dungeon chapter is the view you get "when you work there, not when you're paying for it" (p. 51).
- The trade is one of several tasks for the same women: song, lodging, boats, hostessing, teaching. This is attested for asobi (JG §2b) and matches PW ch. 5.
- The "other women" theme suits courtly hostility. Court ladies and diarists keep their distance from asobi to protect their own respectability. Write the hostility as theirs.

**What it must not do.**
- No confessional event that asks a woman why she entered the trade. No event that "reveals" her bed.
- No raid-and-rescue victory screen. When an official clears the landing, the event text counts what the women lose.
- No camera that dwells on bodies. Gira Grant's own rule of "not a peep show" is the standard.

## 3. *We Too* (WT)

**Bibliographic line.** Natalie West with Tina Horn, eds., *We Too: Essays on Sex Work and Survival*, foreword by Selena the Stripper (New York: Feminist Press, 2021). 30 essays by current and former strippers, escorts, dommes, porn performers and street workers, in sections called Stigma, the State, the Workplace, Family, Survival and Healing.

**Summary.** The anthology answers #MeToo. Sex workers suffer workplace sexual violence too, and they are kept out of the movement against it, because their harms are written off as "part of the job" or else used to justify taking their income away (Foreword; Intro pp. 9–13). The editors ask readers not to "use our stories as symbols" for criminalisation or rescue (Intro p. 11). The book is for workers first. It speaks frankly about bad managers, bad sets and bad clients because it refuses both the happy-hooker story and the victim story (Intro pp. 11–12).

**Key claims (with refs).**
1. *Harm without surrender of the income.* Selena: a worker can say she was raped, and that still gives no one licence to take away her workplace and her independence. Even people who have been trafficked can speak for themselves (Foreword, pp. xii–xiii). West: "we are not asking for rescue" (Intro p. 13).
2. *Dirty and holy are the same mistake.* Ashley Paige compares her mother's legal, exploitative job with sex work: "It's just because it's sex work that we're deemed dirty—but also holy and sanctified at the same time. No, it's a job" (p. 31).
3. *Needing the money is not the same as having no choice.* Lorelei Lee: "To say that I needed the money is not the same as saying I could not choose" (p. 232). She also says that choosing does not mean it was always good, and that she was both harmed and healed in the work. Walking away afterwards was what set the work apart for her (p. 226). Anti-sex-work "feminists" flatten such stories into symbols of a womanhood she does not see herself in (pp. 231–232).
4. *Perfect victim and wrong kind of victim.* Rivera says sex workers are assumed to be survivors already, and also assumed to be unable to be violated at work (p. 237). Schulte: "Victimhood is conditionally offered to sex workers when society deems us in need of rescue" (p. 78).
5. *Consent under pressure still exists.* In survival work, consent shifts and narrows under threat but does not disappear. Workers set hard limits (Schulte with Szurgot and Walker, pp. 73–74, 79). The coercion in that essay came from a husband who forced the work. That is the harm. The trade is not.
6. *Rescue as a pipeline.* In Los Angeles, "trafficking" services require police referral and testimony. The label of victim is granted by the police. Refusal costs shelter (Sacco, pp. 68–70). In New Orleans, a Catholic charity's reporting led to raids. No trafficking arrests followed, hundreds of dancers lost their work, and they marched chanting "I am not a victim! I do not want to be saved!" (Gira Grant, pp. 95–96). The district attorney called prostitution something that "progressively rapes their spirit" (Almodovar, p. 89).
7. *Religious hostility, as insiders saw it.* Rebelle Cunt describes a self-described "God-fearing man" who afterwards speaks of her as a temptress sent to test him and wants her to absolve him (p. 171). Aragon describes the Contagious Diseases Acts, under which the woman was "both sick and responsible", the soldier only tempted (p. 110). A Catholic youth shelter turned away the young Ceyenne Doroshow for wearing a bra (p. 261).
8. *Trans women.* Doroshow, a Black trans woman, was put out of home and refused by shelters and employers, and she says plainly that her sex work then "was definitely survival" (p. 266). She also held a shelter job and later ran a group that gives holistic care to trans sex workers. What carried her was chosen family: the house mother Flawless Sabrina, Miss Major, elders who corrected her (pp. 257–267). Her advice is to employ and build up the young, not to rescue them (pp. 266–267).
9. *Organising.* The Hookers Army of Los Angeles is a sex-workers-only group. New members join by vouching, it meets twice a month, and it teaches self-defence and peer care, "in a spirit of honest, open kinship building and mutual aid" (Carlisle, p. 299). Members keep "hooker" as an insider word (p. 302 n. 4). Kiley describes workers housing each other, getting each other work and paying into each other's crisis funds, borrowing "tactics from church groups" (p. 139). Others mention bad-client lists and checking in with each other (Intro p. 12; Rivera p. 235).
10. *Insiders disagree.* Aragon distrusts "sex work is work" as assimilation and prefers a politics of outlaw kinship and mutual aid (pp. 113–114). Raphael says clients show prejudice "without their social masks", and she hopes coalitions of the marginalised will include sex workers (pp. 46–47). The movement is not one voice.

**What the mod takes.**
- The faith speaks with Paige's plainness: the house's trade is a living. It is neither defilement nor sacrament. This is independent confirmation of Goodwin's "no sacred sex" (JG §6).
- Show harm and keep the income. An event can contain a bad patron or a bad head, and its resolution must not take the woman's livelihood away.
- Chosen family and the house mother are the strongest insider analogue for the song-houses' adoption line (K 277). Doroshow's account shows that a house can be a refuge without the refuge depending on the trade.
- Vouching, a common purse, care in sickness and burial, and a word kept for insiders are period-portable mechanics.

**What it must not do.**
- No story from the anthology, and none of its people, becomes an event, a name or a character.
- No content drawn from the childhood material in these essays.
- No "temptress" or "test of the monk" line in the faith's voice. It belongs only to a named hostile speaker (rule 3).

## 4. Principles for writing sex work in Crusader Dolls

1. **A trade, not a nature.** The bed-trade is something a woman does on some nights, under conditions. It is never what she is. No trait, title or nickname marks a woman by it (PW pp. 14–15; RP ch. 2).
2. **Neither filth nor sacrament.** The faith's voice treats the trade as a livelihood under its god, like boatmen or smiths. It is never defilement and never holy sex (WT p. 31; JG §6).
3. **The harm sits with the hand that holds power.** Coercion, debt, theft and violence come from a named person or office: a head of house, a debt-holder, a husband, a lord's man, a preacher with a magistrate behind him. The act itself is never written as the injury (RP chs. 3–4; WT pp. 73–74).
4. **Her yes, her no, her leaving are equal.** A woman may enter, decline, stop, return or leave. No state outranks another in piety, prestige or opinion, and stepping away is honoured equally with staying (rule 23; RP ch. 2; WT p. 232).
5. **The unhappy worker is real.** A woman may dislike the work and keep it. Her dislike is never a cue for someone else to end her income (RP ch. 1; PW p. 39).
6. **No rescue arc.** No event "saves" a woman from the trade by removing her: not to a nunnery, not to needlework, not to a lord's house. Leaving is something she does, with her own means and with the house's help (RP ch. 1, ch. 6, Conclusion; PW pp. 57, 109).
7. **Remedies come from inside.** When a woman is coerced, the answer is the sisters' backing: renegotiating the debt, unseating the head, a petition, the house purse, working under another house. An outside saviour is not the answer (RP Conclusion, DMSC and Empower; WT p. 299).
8. **Count what the sweep costs.** When a lord, governor or official "cleans" a landing, the event text counts the women's losses: goods, boats, shelter, shares. The official's piety is his own claim (PW ch. 1; WT pp. 95–96; RP ch. 4).
9. **Scenes end at the door.** No text depicts the work. Events stop at the landing, the boat, the gate or the screen. No body is described for a reader's eye (PW p. 34, "not a peep show"; JG §6).
10. **No confession and no origin story.** No event asks why she entered the trade. No text ties her entry to past abuse, and no event "reveals" her trade as a twist (PW pp. 33, 60; RP ch. 2).
11. **Hostile voices are named and kept to their own idiom.** Moralist, reformer, preacher, lord and court diarist each speak in their own period terms (sin, temptation, shamelessness, foxes, pollution) through a named speaker of a vanilla faith or court. The narrator never agrees (rule 3; WT pp. 110, 171).
12. **Money is shares and debts, not price tags.** Show who keeps what: the gift, the share, the head's cut, the debt. A head who takes everything is the abuse (RP ch. 4, ch. 7; JG §6, "rewards, not prices").
13. **Mutual aid is the faith's ordinary practice.** It includes a common purse, paying a sister's fine or debt, nursing, burial, keeping death-days, care in old age, and vouching for newcomers (RP Intro; WT p. 299).
14. **Strangers are kin when vouched.** Women from other ports, provinces or troupes may join through a sister's word. Hostile lines about outsiders belong to the hostile speakers (RP ch. 3).
15. **Trans women are not "made for" the trade.** Daughters do not enter the trade more often than other women of the house. No AI weight, event or loc line links a daughter's womanhood to the bed. No text says a daughter became a woman to lure men or to sell herself, except in a named hostile voice tagged under rule 3 (PW pp. 19–20, 117; RP ch. 1; Serano note row 16). No "trap" or "deceiver" reveal by a patron (rule 5).
16. **The house is a refuge for her whatever she does.** A daughter taken in by a house mother is housed, taught and fed whether or not she ever works the bed. Chosen family is the bond (WT pp. 257–267; K 277).
17. **No despair, no salvation.** No text calls the trade a life of despair or offers a refuge as rescue from death (rule 17; WT p. 78).
18. **Period idiom only.** The faith speaks of the house's rice, the river, the seal, the share, her own leave and the god's ear. It never uses "work", "rights", "choice", "consent", "empowerment" or "trafficking" in the modern sense. These are principles to translate, not words to quote.

### 4a. Hostile voices: what each one says, and why the narrator stays out

The books show that the hostility has a few recurring shapes. Each shape has a period speaker who can voice it honestly. The narrator reports these lines and never endorses them (rule 3).

| Shape (books) | Modern form | Period speaker and idiom (1178 Japan) | Faith's answer, in its own voice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sin and loss (RP ch. 1; WT p. 89) | "an immoral life", the spirit worn away | Tendai or nenbutsu preacher: "sunk in sin" (K 297), karmic weight | Miyagi's "anything at all becomes Dharma"; the god hears singers |
| Temptress (WT p. 171) | the whore as a test of a good man | Preacher or monk: the woman as an obstacle on a man's path | Any blame for a man's lapse is his own; the house does not absolve him |
| Shamelessness (PW pp. 75–77) | whore stigma, "compulsory virtue" | Court diarist (Masafusa type): "lose all sense of shame" | The house's honour is its song line and its fair shares |
| Contagion (RP ch. 1; WT p. 110) | the disease vector | Courtier or physician in a plague year: the landing as a source of sickness | Sisters nurse their sick; the purse pays for medicine |
| Foreignness (RP ch. 3) | the migrant as threat | Michichika type: "foxes from some old grave mound" (G 129) | A vouched stranger is a sister |
| Rescue (RP ch. 1, ch. 6; PW ch. 9) | raid and rehabilitation | Pious lord or governor clearing the landing "for their sake" | Count the boats and robes lost; petition the proprietor |
| Deceiver (RP ch. 1; Serano) | the trans woman as a trap | Court gossip: "he became a woman to sell himself" | The house mother answers; the narrator never confirms (rule 5) |

Notes on the table:
- The "pollution" idea (*kegare*) concerns death and blood in Heian sources. Do not attach it to the trade without a source. ***Unverified***; no hostile *kegare* line about asobi until one is found.
- "Base women" (G 82) is late Kamakura law. Use it only after 1210 (JG §5 epigraphs).

### 4b. A test for any event that touches the trade

An event fails the test if **any** answer is yes:
1. Does an option remove a woman from the trade without her own choice, and reward someone else for doing it?
2. Is leaving, or entering, worth more piety, prestige or opinion than the alternatives?
3. Does the text depict the act, or describe a body for the reader?
4. Does the text explain her entry into the trade by trauma, or ask her to confess it?
5. Is the injury placed in the act rather than in a person's use of power?
6. Does a daughter's womanhood make her more likely to enter, or explain why she does?
7. Is a hostile line in the narrator's voice, or unnamed?
8. Does the "good" outcome for an official or preacher leave the women poorer, without the text saying so?

### 4c. Consent and choice as mechanics

- **Who sets the flag.** Only the woman sets her trade state: through her own decision ("Keep the Bed", "Set It Down", "Take It Up Again"), or through an event option in her own voice. A head's interaction can *ask*. The woman's AI answers using her traits and her house's gold, and a player woman always gets the choice.
- **What changes nothing.** Her state never alters her piety, her opinion of the faith, other members' opinion of her, or her right to house seats and offices (rule 7).
- **What it does change.** Gold, a small "patron's favour" prestige trickle, and exposure to the trade's own events (patrons, debts, a bad night on the river). It is a livelihood with its own risks, and so are the boats and the lodging.
- **Unenthusiastic consent** (PW p. 93). A woman may keep the bed and take a small stress gain in a hard season. That stress comes from the conditions (cold, a mean patron, no share), never from the act as such. The fix is better conditions, not her removal.
- **Leaving.** "Set It Down" is free. A woman who leaves keeps the purse's care and her song. Marrying out keeps the house trait (JG §5).
- **Coercion is a crime against the house.** If a head or a husband forces a woman, the faith counts it as abuse. The woman gets a strong opinion malus toward the forcer, and the house gets an unseating petition. This is the faith's internal justice, with no outside rescuer (RP Conclusion).

### 4d. Where the modern argument does not translate

- **No police and no "criminalisation" in 1178.** The analogues are an estate official's seizure (*Chūyūki* 1114, G 136), a governor's displeasure, a proprietor's court and Kamakura law after 1185. Write power through these.
- **No wage.** Payment was gifts and a patron's obligation (G 32). The labour argument translates as shares and debts, not wages.
- **No borders.** Migration translates to movement between ports, provinces and troupes, and to the outsider's lack of a patron or proprietor.
- **No identity politics.** Translate solidarity into kinship: mothers, daughters and sisters of the house, adoption, vouching.
- **The religious frame is the faith's own.** The modern books are secular. The house's god and the Dharma lines (K 296–97; Miyagi) carry the dignity claim in period terms, and the Paige line keeps it from turning into sacred sex.

## 5. Critiques of and suggestions for the song-houses faith (JG §§5–6)

**Already sound** (keep): the trade is never part of the gate. There is no sacred sex. Retiring is honoured as the woman's choice. Work events end at the boat or door. The design admits that the *chōja*'s office can be used for control (G 34, 122) and includes an event where a head abuses the seal. Hostile lines go to named speakers. Minors are excluded.

**Problems and fixes.**

1. **"House's share income per working member" (tenet mechanics).** This pays the head in proportion to how many women work the bed. That is the exact structure RP ch. 4 and ch. 7 warn about: the person with power profits from the women's work. It also gives the AI a reason to push women into the trade. *Fix:* the head's yearly income comes from the house as a whole (song, lodging, boats, gifts) at a flat rate per member, whatever each woman does. The "shares evenly" opinion bonus (event 4) stays. Add a hidden check: a head who takes more than her share gets a "Seal Held Too Tight" opinion malus from members, which can feed the unseating in event 3.

2. **"`lustful` neither sin nor virtue."** Tying the trade to a desire trait implies that women who work are lustful, which is the Erotic Professional and hostile "voracious" picture at once (RP ch. 1). *Fix:* make `lustful` and `chaste` both neutral for this faith on doctrinal grounds ("the god asks no vow of the body"), and keep the trade a separate flag. AI weights to enter the trade read greed, poverty (low gold or an indebted house) and the woman's own `content`/`ambitious` traits. They never read lust or the daughter mark.

3. **Model the trade as an activity flag, never a trait.** Use a character flag or variable, for example `asobi_keeps_bed`, set *only* by decisions the character takes herself or by event options that are hers. It needs three states: keeps, does not, has set down. It must not show in the character's name or title, and other women of the house have no opinion modifier over its state. This is Gira Grant's behaviour-not-identity point (PW pp. 14–15) and fits "asobi" as the house's name for its women.

4. **Tenet text and voice.** "Honest work" reads like the modern slogan. *Suggest* a mother's voice that speaks materially, for example: *"The river feeds this house: song, a roof, the boats, and the bed for those who take it. Hyakudayū hears every daughter's asking. No sister eats less for what she will not do."* No talk of empowerment, calling or holiness (Paige, WT p. 31). Check whether a medieval term for people of a trade, such as *michimichi no mono*, is attested for asobi before using any (Amino and Wakita, JG §7 item 4). It is ***unverified***.

5. **Event 3 (letter of leave): make her answer the hinge.** The woman is asked first, and her "no" closes the event with no cost to her or the house beyond the patron's displeasure. The option "send a willing sister instead" must check that another woman *actually* volunteered (her own flag), not that she merely exists. The abuse branch, where a head sends a woman against her will, should be a separate event in which the other women can petition the house to unseat the head (RP Conclusion, DMSC).

6. **Add a debt event that is about the debt.** Goodwin's bought girls are excluded as minors (JG §6). An adult version is still needed, because debt is the main period-plausible coercion (RP ch. 3). *Event idea, "What the House Is Owed":* a grown woman owes the house or an outside lender for robes, passage or her mother's illness, and the lender presses her toward the bed. The options carry no moral rank: the house purse pays it down; she renegotiates the same sum over song and lodging work only (the Empower model); she works it off her own way; she leaves for another house that takes the debt. *Invented.* There is no rescue option and no option where "the lord frees her".

7. **Event 8 (preacher): answer with a material reply.** Keep the "In Settsu anything at all becomes Dharma" answer. Add an answer modelled on RP's 1920s worker and Doroshow's "employ them" (RP ch. 2; Intro), in period terms: *"Will your temple feed my mother when the river is cold?"* The woman who retires to vows keeps her house tie, her song and her share of the purse. She is not "saved" (RP ch. 1, the nuns). No outcome gives the preacher prestige for a "conversion". The preacher's "sunk in sin" (K 297) and any line about women as a temptation to monks stay his, in a named vanilla Tendai or nenbutsu voice (WT p. 171).

8. **Event 6 (horses and cloth): write it as a sweep.** It is the period analogue of a raid, so give the official a pious or orderly pretext ("clearing the landing of disorder"). The event text then lists what the women lost. The house's means are petition and the purse. A result that is "good" for the official ends badly for the women (rule 8 above; WT pp. 95–96).

9. **Add mutual-aid mechanics.** A yearly "House Purse" contribution, as a small gold sink at the head's court. It funds illness, fines, debts, burials and old age, and it can drive event 11 (bedside song) and event 12 (old age). Add a burial and death-day event for a sister who dies of age or illness. Violent deaths stay out (JG §6). The mutual aid is attested for the movement's history (RP Intro), so the practice is grounded even where the specific Heian form is invented.

10. **Strangers and vouching.** Add an arrival event: a woman from Muro, Aohaka or a kugutsu troupe asks to join, and a sister vouches for her (WT p. 299). Hostile outsider lines, such as Michichika's "foxes", go only to the named court voice (JG §5 epigraphs). Joining by vouching never requires the trade.

11. **Daughters and the trade: audit for the "made for it" trope.** Check every AI weight and event for a link between the daughter mark (or the rite) and the bed-trade. There must be none, and daughters should be *no more* likely than other women of the house to keep the bed (Principle 15). Add one daughter-specific line in a *hostile* voice, for example a court diarist claiming "he became a woman to sell himself on the river". Tag it under rule 3 and give the faith a reply from the house mother rather than the narrator. Never write a patron discovering a daughter (rule 5; RP ch. 1).

12. **Refuge first.** The adoption door ("by taking", JG §5) should open into song, lodging, boats and teaching. Put at least one daughter event in each of those roles before any event touches the bed, to meet JG §6's "at least as often" requirement mechanically: weight the event pool, not just the loc.

13. **Event 5 (patron's debt): keep the risk with the patron.** Pressing a patron is fine. Make sure no option shifts the loss onto the woman who hosted him (RP ch. 6: the person with less need sets the terms).

14. **Words.** Avoid "courtesan", "prostitute", "whore" and "sex work" in the faith's voice and in the narrator's. Use "asobi", "the house", "the bed" (sparingly) and "the trade". Reclaimed words only in a daughter's or a sister's voice, as an insiders' word (rule 9; WT p. 302 n. 4). Masafusa's "lose all sense of shame" stays the courtier's.

**Suggested order of work** (planning only; no repo file is touched by this note).
1. Settle the flag model (critique 3) and the head's income (critique 1). Every other change depends on them.
2. Rewrite the tenet text (critique 4) and the `lustful`/`chaste` stance (critique 2).
3. Revise events 3, 5, 6 and 8 against the test in §4a–4b.
4. Add the debt, purse, burial and arrival events (critiques 6, 9, 10).
5. Run the daughter audit (critique 11) on AI weights and loc before any observe run.
6. Check every new line of loc for glyphs (`tools/check_glyphs.py`) and against rules 3, 5, 17 and 23.

**Open questions for the user.**
- Whether the tenet should make `lustful` *and* `chaste` neutral (critique 2). A vanilla tenet check is still needed (JG §7 item 10).
- Whether the "House Purse" (critique 9) is a new mechanic or folds into the existing house share.
- Whether the debt event (critique 6) can use a lender from outside the house, or should keep all coercion inside house politics.
