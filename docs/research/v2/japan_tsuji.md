## 1. Verdict

1. Tsuji gives **one** case in the window, and it falls in the start year itself: on 1178.11.10 (lunar; c. 20 Dec 1178, conversion *unverified*) the empress's household men danced as the Itsukushima shrine maidens at Rokuhara, Kyoto, praying for Tokuko's birth.
2. All his other cases of men in women's dress are Muromachi to early Edo (1416–1603), **240–425 years after 1178**. Use them only for late-era events, never for 1178.
3. He reads every case as *a guise*: an emergency stand-in for a ritual form (1178), or a festival costume (*furyū*) "given no special meaning" (p. 24). No case is a person living as a woman. He gives no trans woman and no community.
4. So Tsuji supports a Japanese faith at 1178 **as world, not as foundation**: the faith's gate, office and line must still rest on the song-house sources (the *Ryōjin hishō*, Ōe no Masafusa) in the survey.
5. Where: he puts the Itsukushima cult on the Taira axis **Kyoto (Rokuhara) → Fukuhara → Itsukushima**, i.e. `b_kyo`, `b_fukuhara` (`c_settsu`) and `b_itsukushima` (`c_aki`). This fits the survey's Settsu hub well; it says nothing about Eguchi, Kanzaki or Aohaka.
6. Treat him as an outside scholar on this question: he explicitly argues against Mitsuhashi Junko's "dual-sex power" reading (pp. 9–10, 27). Read Mitsuhashi in her own words before taking his framing into the faith's world.

## 3. The Itsukushima question

### What Tsuji says the *naishi* were
- The *Sankaiki* calls them "the shrine maidens (*miko*) of Itsukushima, who are called *naishi*" (伊都伎島の巫、内侍と号す, p. 11).
- *Dengaku* was "normally performed by men", and the *naishi* were women who performed it, attested in "several sources" (p. 11).
- In 1179 and 1180 they danced in men's jackets and trousers over hanging hair: Tsuji's "incomplete male dress" (p. 12).
- "Yaotome" ("eight maidens") in the 1178 entry means them, because the Jōkōin shrine was a branch of Itsukushima (p. 11). Eight dance in 1180 (p. 12).
- Michichika's reaction: 「女の遊びとも見えず」, "it did not look like women's play" (p. 12); at the shrine, "I felt that heavenly beings come down to play must look like this" (p. 12).

### The Taira link (what is in Tsuji, and what is not)
- In Tsuji: Kiyomori's own visit to Itsukushima in 1179 (6.7–22), the *naishi* dancing for him at Fukuhara (p. 11); Kiyomori bringing them to Takakura in 1180 (p. 12); the Jōkōin branch shrine inside the Rokuhara palace (p. 11); the *hitatare* spreading into court society "under the Taira regime" (p. 12).
- Not in Tsuji (from general knowledge, all *unverified* here): the Taira's decorated Lotus Sūtra scrolls given to the shrine (1164); Kiyomori's rebuilding of the shrine (c. 1168); the chief priest Saeki Kagehiro as a Taira man. **Vanilla CK3 has Kagehiro** (`japanese_saeki_sanyo_1`, b. 1140, rite `shinto`) holding `c_aki` at 1178 under feudal government; `b_itsukushima` is already a Shintō holy site (`itsukushima`, `00_holy_site_types.txt`, used by `shinto_faith` and `utaki`).

### The men who stood in (1178)
- *Sankaiki* 1178.11.10: 「おのおの髪を乱し、面に粉黛を施し、南庭に渡る」, "each let his hair down, put powder and brow-paint on his face, and crossed to the south garden" (p. 11).
- "This, they say, was prayer for the birth" (p. 11). Tokuko bore the future Antoku two days later (date from general knowledge).
- Tsuji: a man's topknot and cap marked an independent adult man; undoing it in public was "a great disgrace" (大変な恥辱, p. 11), even a fall in status.
- Why men: the prayer to the Itsukushima god had to take the *naishi*'s form of offering (法楽 *hōraku*); there were none in the capital and no time to fetch them or train other women; men of this rank knew *dengaku* (p. 13). Tsuji calls this "presumed" (推測される, p. 13).
- He calls the act "somewhat convoluted" (いささかややこしい, p. 13): men copying women who were themselves in incomplete men's dress. The female sign was the **hair let down and the painted face**, not the clothes.
- "Of course this cannot be called a general case" (もちろん一般的な例とは言えない, p. 13).

### Testing it as an anchor
- **For:** exact year, exact place (Kiyomori's palace), named source, a named officiant, and a kami already on the map as a holy site. It shows that at the Taira court a woman's form of offering could be required, and that men could be told to take it on for one night.
- **Against:**
  - one entry, one night, under orders, for a ritual form, with Tsuji's reason inferred;
  - the men were officials of the empress's household, not a community, and nothing says any of them lived as women before or after;
  - the women they copied were in men's dress, which this mod excludes as a subject;
  - Tsuji stresses the shame of the act, so a faith reading it as honour would be against its source.
- **Result:** a strong **era event** for the 1178 start (Kyoto, December), told as the world's record. Not a gate, not an office, and the narrator must not say the men were daughters (narrator rule; rule 7: no "accepted for a sacred role").

## 4. Miko, kugutsu, asobi, the shaman-entertainer thesis

- **Miko.** Only the Itsukushima *naishi* (pp. 11–13). Tsuji treats their *dengaku* as ritual offering, not entertainment.
- **Kugutsu, asobi / yūjo.** Not discussed. The one mention is a 1432 diarist praising women *sarugaku* players' voices as "like *yūkun*" (遊君, p. 26). His book 『中世の〈遊女〉』 (2017) is not cited.
- **Women's voices.** Court women (*nyōbō*) could not raise their voices before others, so could not sing *imayō* and were kept out of poetry contests and linked verse (p. 10, citing Okimoto 2006 and Tabuchi 2019). Men could perform in both the "front" and "inner" spaces; women in principle only in the inner (p. 10). Useful for the song-house premise: *imayō* belonged to professional women singers and to men, not to court ladies.
- ***Yamai no sōshi*, *Torikaebaya*:** not mentioned.
- **Mitsuhashi Junko.** Tsuji summarises her essay in the 2022 Shōtō Museum catalogue (p. 9): (1) almost all premodern Japanese performing arts have cross-dressing because audiences loved it, a sense of "a supreme beauty beyond sex" still alive today; (2) a Japanese idea that holding both sexes (双性 *sōsei*) gives unusual power and sacredness, which people sought by cross-dressing themselves.
- **His view.** He "hesitates" to treat men's and women's cross-dressing together under the abstract "dual sex" (p. 10), because medieval men and women had wholly different social places. He closes by warning that treating them alike as "a taste for cross-dressing", "crossing of sex" or gaining "dual-sex power" "may rather harm understanding" (p. 27).
- **The shaman-entertainer thesis.** He does not address it by name. His method (performance shaped by rank, place and audience, not by an abstract sacred androgyny) is skeptical of it, like Goodwin's. He does accept a ritual function in the one 1178 case.

## 5. Tsuji's framing and where it pulls against a trans-women reading

- **His thesis** (abstract, pp. 6–7, 27): men's female dress in *furyū* was chosen freely among relative equals and shared delight (興 *kyō*), keeping and strengthening relationships; women's male dress was asymmetric, serving the audience's, especially men's, delight. The difference reflects the gender structure of society.
- **No inner meaning.** In *furyū odori* "no special meaning was given to female dress" (p. 24); it was one design among many, treated like the male disguise of the *komosō* (p. 24). The men show "no resistance at all" (p. 24).
- **Tensions:**
  - Tsuji reads every case as a costume worn by men who stay men. That is right for his cases, and the faith must not claim them.
  - His argument is aimed partly at Mitsuhashi, a trans woman historian (pp. 9–10, 27). Under this project's rule, she outranks him on her community's own identity; on medieval performance records he is the specialist. Keep both: his records and dates, her framing in her own words once read.
  - His contrast (men's guise = play among equals; women's = gaze) has no place for someone whose dress is neither play nor gaze but her own life. That gap is where the faith speaks, **in its own voice**: e.g. a mother of the house says the festival men "put on a woman's hair for a night and took it off by morning; we were not asked to take ours off" (invented line). The narrator says none of this.
  - Rule 16: the faith's dress is neither defiance of nor surrender to the order of the sexes. It should not be framed against the *furyū* dancers either: no mockery of them.

## 6. Design hooks (all **invented** unless marked as sourced)

### Gate
- Tsuji supports no gate. Keep the survey's "The Song Received" (*imayō* lineage). Okimoto's point (p. 10) helps: court ladies could not sing aloud, so the song-houses' voice was their own.
- Do **not** build a gate from letting the hair down or painting the face. In 1178 those were a one-night guise and, for the men, a disgrace (p. 11): a gate built on them would make her womanhood a costume.

### Office
- Nothing in Tsuji. Keep the house head (*chōja*) from the survey.
- Optional **flavour office:** "the Eight" (*yaotome*), a troupe of a house's daughters who dance the women's *dengaku* at Itsukushima's feasts. *Invented*; the number eight and the *sasara* and drums are sourced (pp. 11–12).

### Holy sites (vanilla map names checked in `common/landed_titles/01_japan.txt`)
- `b_itsukushima` (`c_aki`): sourced to the *naishi* (pp. 11–13); already a vanilla Shintō holy site, so the faith shares it. 1178 holder Saeki Kagehiro.
- `b_fukuhara` (`c_settsu`): the 1179 *naishi* *dengaku* before Kiyomori (p. 11).
- `b_kyo` (`c_yamashiro`): Rokuhara, 1178 (p. 11). No Rokuhara barony exists.
- `b_uji` (`c_yamashiro`): Uji Rikyū festival *dengaku*, 1153 (p. 13 n. 5).
- From the survey, not Tsuji: `b_watanabe` (Yodo mouth), `b_kumano` (`c_kii`). Kojima (1180) has no barony in Bizen (`b_joto`, `b_oku`, `b_tomata`, `b_mashima`).

### Event ideas
1. **"Hair let down at Rokuhara"** (Dec 1178, Kyoto; *sourced*: *Sankaiki*). News reaches the house that the empress's men danced as the Itsukushima maidens for the birth. Options give the faith's own reading. The men are never called daughters.
2. **"The maidens at Fukuhara"** (1179, Settsu; setting *sourced*, encounter *invented*). A daughter meets the *naishi* on the quay after they dance for Kiyomori. Their dress is theirs and the narrator does not comment on it.
3. **"Pilgrimage to the floating shrine"** (holy-site event, `b_itsukushima`; *invented*). She learns the *sasara* rhythm from the shrine's women and brings a song back.
4. **"Uji's festival levy"** (flavour, `b_uji`; *invented* from the 1153 levy). The lord of Uji calls the villages to dance *dengaku*; the house is asked to send singers.
5. **"The god's patrons fall"** (1180–85 Genpei war; *invented* consequences). Taira patronage of Itsukushima ends; the house seeks a new patron. Avoid Dan-no-ura and the child emperor's drowning (suicide and minors guardrails).
6. **"The bon dance"** (late era only: not before c. 1500 for *furyū odori*, c. 1400 for banquet *furyū*; *sourced* setting). Townsmen dance in aprons and head-cloths for a night; a mother of the house says, in her voice, what is and is not the same. No mockery of the dancers.
7. **"A song not for the court ladies"** (*invented*, Okimoto via p. 10). A court lady who may not sing aloud asks a daughter to sing an *imayō* behind the blinds for her.

## 7. Risks

- **The anchor is thin.** One diary entry. If the faith's case for 1178 is built on it, the case fails. Keep it as world.
- **Men in a guise ≠ trans women.** Never present the 1178 men, the Fushimi retainers or the *furyū* dancers as daughters or foremothers (narrator rule; rule 19).
- **Rule 7.** The Rokuhara night must not read as "the court accepted women like her for a sacred role".
- **Rule 3.** A hostile line about a man's unbound topknot would aim at clothes and status, not the imagined body; if used, it needs a named vanilla speaker and should probably go to world flavour, not a hostile family.
- **Women's male dress.** The *naishi*'s own dress is male; *shirabyōshi* likewise. Do not make either a subject.
- **Late material.** Muromachi *furyū* and Edo *kabuki* are 240–425 years off; no Edo imagery at 1178 (no geisha, no *kabuki*, no "floating world").
- **Minors.** Excluded: involves minors (Tsuji's modern Yase parallel, p. 22). *Chigo* excluded as in the survey.
- **Suicide.** The Taira end (1185) includes drownings: keep out.
- **Scholar weight.** Tsuji argues against Mitsuhashi. Do not adopt either's framing as the narrator's; give the faith its own.
- ***Unverified*:** the Western date of 1178.11.10; the instrument list of 1179 (characters illegible in print); Kiyomori's 1164/1168 Itsukushima works and Kagehiro's office; whether `b_fukuhara` vs `b_watanabe` better fits the Yodo-mouth ports.
