## 1. Bibliographic line and verdict

**Bibliographic line.** Kimberly B. Stratton, *Naming the Witch: Magic, Ideology, and Stereotype in the Ancient World*. Gender, Theory, and Religion. New York: Columbia University Press, 2007 (pbk 2022). xviii + c. 290 pp. (312 PDF pages). ISBN 978-0-231-13836-9 (cloth), 978-0-231-13837-6 (pbk).

**Verdict: no medieval material at all. High value as theory and as a guardrail source; low and risky value as content.**

- The book stops at about 400 CE. It never mentions Diana, Herodias, the Canon *Episcopi*, the night company, Byzantium (beyond one note on p. 239) or anything after Jerome. For the Good Ladies it gives the **classical stock** that a 12th-century cleric could have read (Hecate, Thessalian moon-pulling, Horace's Canidia, Lucan's Erictho, Apuleius). It does not give **evidence** for the Ladies.
- Its core claim, that "magic" is a label rivals give each other and not a kind of practice, supports three existing design choices: the Ladies never call themselves witches; the Church's response is an overlay, not the faith's self-image; and rule 3's hostile voices are named speakers with a motive.
- Its rabbinic chapter is the best single survey this project has of **Talmudic texts on women and *keshafim***. Nearly all of them break the Ha-Nehpakhot hard limits or the rule that Jewish characters are never the wrongdoers. §5(b) is therefore mostly a **do-not-use list**, with three small and optional exceptions.
- Its early Christian chapter gives the Adelphai a strong hostile pattern (heresy = Simon = magic; women's presence as proof of heresy) and one usable precedent for the Sisters' own polemic (Firmilian's woman prophet, p. 139–40).
- **Not Murray.** The "Murray" in the notes is Oswyn Murray (*Early Greece*, p. 194 n. 1) and Michele Murray ("The Magical Female in Graeco-Roman Rabbinic Literature", p. 242 n. 130), not Margaret Murray. The cover art is Baldung Grien's *Witches' Sabbath* (1510). Use neither (rule 25).

## 2. The argument

**Magic as a discourse of othering.** Stratton treats magic as a "discourse" in Foucault's sense: a cluster of words, stereotypes, accusations, laws and some real practices. People use it to argue about who holds legitimate power.
- It is local and has a history. The discourse "emerged in Greece in the fifth century bce" (p. 1), as part of Athens's new talk of barbarians after the Persian wars (pp. 39–44). From there it travelled to Rome, to the Christians and to the rabbis, and changed shape in each (pp. 17–22, 177).
- The practices are not the point: "The practices themselves are neutral" (p. 11). The same rite is miracle for one writer and magic for another. In Remus's phrase, quoted by Stratton, "your magic is my miracle" (p. 8). Which one it is "resides primarily in the eye of the beholder and the authority of the one wielding the label" (p. 114).
- Naming has effects. "Once the notion magic exists, it takes on a social reality: it can operate as a form of social control through the fear of accusation" (p. 18). Accusing, performing and redefining magic "all constitute forms of social action" (p. 18).
- Stratton refuses to dismiss practice altogether. Curse tablets and the magical papyri show that some people did what their society called magic, sometimes on purpose as a subversive stance (pp. x–xi, 15, 37).

**The gendered stereotype.**
- The literature makes women the witches. The finds do not: about "86 percent of erotic binding spells are performed by or on the behalf of men" (p. 24; also p. 22).
- Stratton's explanation follows Sherry Ortner: women are cast as dangerous where men's status depends on them. Their example is Athens after Pericles' citizenship law of 451/0 BCE (pp. 64–68, 178).
- Magic also works on men. It is an effeminate kind of power, so accusations "can delegitimize men by associating them with women and women's wily ways" (p. 25).
- Each culture builds a **different** witch:
  - Athens: the jilted wife and her *pharmaka* (Medea, Deianeira).
  - Rome: the predatory old *saga* who hunts young men (Canidia, Erictho), the mirror of Augustus's chaste matron.
  - Pre-Constantine Christianity: male magicians and female victims.
  - The rabbis: women, food and magic.
- The pattern turns when the writers themselves are marginal. Where men "see themselves as marginal vis-à-vis other larger powers, women will operate as a mirror for Self" (p. 179). This is why the early Christians, as an embattled minority, cast women as victims (pp. 140–41, 179).
- Stratton warns against universal explanations: they naturalize "the stereotype rather than interrogates it as artificial and historically determined" (p. 178).

**Two cautions the book makes about its own field, which also apply to us.**
- To "discover magic in non-Western cultures involves importing Western categories and modes of conception" (p. 27). Colonial writers used "primitive magic" to deny people self-rule (pp. 3–4).
- Old anti-Jewish stereotypes are long-lived, but each outbreak of violence used them locally: "the implementation of the discourse is local" (p. 23).

## 4. Chapter notes (the parts read closely)

**Greek (ch. 2).**
- Homer's *pharmakon* is neutral: it is both Circe's drug and Hermes' antidote. The epithet *polupharmakos* ("skilled with all kinds of drugs") also describes physicians (p. 26). Circe's power comes as much from her half-divine status as from any rite; in Homer the concept "magic" does not yet exist (p. 43).
- **Hecate.** By the classical period she is a goddess of the underworld and sorcery. People left offerings at crossroads to keep her "hordes of restless spirits at bay" (p. 42). Her rites "involved placation or purification (katharsion) rather than worship or invocation, seeking to keep her and her itinerant ghosts away" (p. 42). Hesiod still gives her a positive role (p. 196 n. 20).
- **Medea.** Euripides likely invented her barbarian origin and the infanticide to mark her as Other. Corinthian epic had a Medea without magic (pp. 49–50). In tragedy, women's *pharmakeia* answers abandonment and is defensive (pp. 46, 62, 71–72).
- **Gender inversion.** Dionysos is a Lydian "goēs epōidos", effeminate in hair, perfume and eyes (p. 47). Heracles, dying, "finds [himself] female" (p. 63).
- **Phrygia.** A fragment of the *Phoronis* calls *goētes* "mountain-dwelling men from Mount Ida in Phrygia" (p. 28). By the 4th century, *magos* and *goēs* are general insults, "connoting deception, beguilement, and fraud" (p. 44).

**Roman (ch. 3).**
- The witch arrives together with Augustus's marriage and adultery laws (18 and 17 BCE): "the image of the witch emerged as the antithesis" of the chaste matron (p. 99).
- Horace's Canidia and Sagana tear a black lamb in a pauper's graveyard, invoke Hecate and Tisiphone, and use wool and wax effigies (pp. 80–81). In *Epode* 5 they starve a boy for a love potion (p. 82).
- Lucan's Erictho strips corpses and gibbets (pp. 90–91). Apuleius's Pamphile keeps body parts and summons wineskins by mistake (pp. 91–93).
- *Saga*, "wise woman", slid to "procuress" and "witch". It "usually connoted an old woman and was, in this way, employed as a term of abuse" (p. 33).
- Stratton rejects the idea that a universal "night-witch" archetype explains these portraits; such views "suffer from overgenerality" (p. 72).
- Thessalian women pull down the moon: Aristophanes, *Clouds* 749–50 (p. 215 n. 99); Horace, *Epode* 5; Virgil, *Ecl.* 8.69 (p. 185 n. 55). Propertius's procuress consults *striges*, screech owls (p. 85).
- **Roman law.** The Twelve Tables ban charming a neighbour's crops away and the *malum carmen*, which was read as either curse or slander (pp. 30–31). Under Tiberius and Claudius, astrologers and *magi* were expelled (p. 34). By the 2nd century magic was prosecuted as *maleficia* under the *lex Cornelia de sicariis et veneficiis* (p. 34). Treason (*maiestas*) charges carried magic charges in the Tiberian trials: Libo Drusus, Piso and Plancina, Numantina (pp. 99–104).
- **Apuleius's defence** (*Apol.* 25–26): either *magus* means a Persian priest, and the charge is praise, or it means a man of fearful power, and then why do his accusers dare accuse him? (pp. 32–33, 115–16).

**Early Christian (ch. 4).**
- **Outsiders' view.** To Pliny and Celsus, Christians looked like magicians: "The Christians met in secret and at night" (p. 118); they invoked a man who died violently, as curse tablets did; and women, even slave deaconesses, led them (pp. 117–18).
- **Origen's defence:** no spells, only the Name and scripture; no fee; a moral life (pp. 118–19).
- **The Christian counter-charge.** The apologists answered by calling the pagan gods demons (Justin, Tertullian). With that, magic "was now identified with Satan and cosmic dualism" (p. 122), the root of the later witch-as-devil-worshipper.
- **Heresy.** "Once the connection between heresy and magic became established through Simon, other heretical leaders could also be denounced as magicians" (p. 129). Irenaeus admits the "heretics" never claimed Simon; he draws the line himself (p. 129).
- **Gender.** "Early Christian accusations of magic target primarily men" (p. 130); women appear as the magician's victims (Marcus's women, Helena, the virgins of the *Acts of Andrew* and Jerome). Irenaeus's women prophets are dismissed as babblers, and "Women's participation in Christian ritual renders any church “heretical,” whatever its teachings or doctrines might have been" (p. 134).
- **Firmilian** (Cappadocia, letter of 256). He judges a woman prophet whose baptism and eucharist follow the proper forms to work by demons: "one has to assume that the single problem with her miracles and sacraments is her sex" (p. 140). From the 3rd century women are again the witches. Chrysostom scolds women for amulets (p. 140).
- **Montanists** tied prophecy to "dry fasts and sexual continence" (p. 151).

**Rabbinic (ch. 5).**
- The Bavli is ambivalent: "magic in the Babylonian Talmud could connote either divine power or subversive danger, depending on context" (p. 143).
- **Licit.** Rav Ḥisda and Rabbah bar Rav Huna undo a matron's binding of their boat with a "word" (*milta*) of their own (b. Ḥul. 105b, pp. 145–46).
  - Abaye's three grades of *keshafim*, modelled on the Sabbath laws: an act, stoning; sleight of hand, forbidden but exempt; the study of the laws of creation, by which Ḥanina and Oshaia made and ate a calf, permitted (b. Sanh. 67b). "It constitutes, however, a “permissible” (muttar) form of magic" (p. 148).
  - Amulets are proven by three cures. The "ways of the Amorite" are allowed if they heal, and "the only way to know the difference is to ask a rabbi" (p. 155).
  - Learning magic "to understand and to instruct" is allowed (b. Sanh. 68a, pp. 149–50), yet that very excuse shows that "rabbinic magic needs to be justified" (p. 150).
- **Illicit / other.**
  - R. Ḥanina is immune to a woman's attempt by his "great merit" and Deut. 4:35, "there is none other besides Him" (b. Sanh. 67b, pp. 150–51).
  - Healing in the name of Jesus is refused, even at the cost of death (y. Shab. 14:4; t. Ḥul. 2, pp. 152–53).
  - Bread left by the road is passed over because "the daughters of Israel" practise magic (b. Eruv. 64b, p. 153).
  - "The best of women" is a sorceress (y. Kid. 4:11, p. 153). Incense is unblessed (b. Ber. 53a, p. 154).
  - "Most women engage in magic", the gemara's explanation of the feminine *mekhashefa* in Exod. 22:17, said "perhaps partly tongue in cheek". Even so, "it serves to reinscribe an association of women with magic" (b. Sanh. 67a, p. 154).
- **Regional reading.** The positive stance is Babylonian: Sassanian magi, knowledge as power, Torah study. The negative stance is Palestinian: Hellenistic, with power in piety and renunciation (Ḥanina's *nezirut*; m. Sotah 9:15's ladder from scrupulousness to the Holy Spirit; Akiva on fasting) (pp. 157–66). The Bavli puts "Torah" at the head of Pinḥas ben Yair's ladder (Avod. Zar. 20b, p. 164).
- **Women, food, magic**: "If there is a single ideology regarding magic in rabbinic writings, the association of women, food, and magic might be it" (p. 169).
  - Yannai turns an innkeeper woman into a donkey and is "seen riding on a woman in the market" (b. Sanh. 67b, pp. 169–70).
  - Amemar's charm against "the women who practice magic" (b. Pes. 110a, p. 170).
  - Shimon ben Shetaḥ "executed eighty women of Ashkelon for practicing magic". In the Yerushalmi's legend they conjure bread, stew and wine and lose their power when lifted off the ground (y. Sanh. 6:6; m. Sanh. 6:4; y. Ḥag. 2:2, p. 170, n. 128–30).
  - Rav Ilish abandons Rav Naḥman's captive daughters and then calls their stirring with bare hands "magic" (b. Git. 45a, pp. 166–68).
  - Their mother Yalta resists rabbinic rulings: she takes her blood to a second rabbi (b. Nid. 20b) and smashes 400 jars of wine when left out of the blessing (b. Ber. 51b) (pp. 168–69).
  - Stratton's reading: the rabbis could not police kashrut in women's kitchens, and food stood for sex and for the community's borders (pp. 171–76).

## 5. Implications by faith

### (a) The Good Ladies

**What Stratton gives.**
1. **The frame of `witch_design.md` §1 and §5, confirmed.** "Witch" is the accusers' word. Stratton's whole method is to ask whose interest a label serves (p. x). The faith's voice should keep refusing the word, and the Church's phases (illusion, recoding, sect) read as a *discourse* changing over time, as Stratton's chapters show for antiquity.
2. **Men in the company.** That the literature makes women the witches while the finds show men commissioning most spells (pp. 22, 24) is a useful comparison for why the clerics' "mostly women" need not be the whole truth. It is support by analogy only: the attested medieval men come from the Pyrenees and Milan sources, not from here.
3. **Women cast as dangerous where the house's honour depends on them** (Ortner, pp. 66, 178). This fits a household devotion: the clean, open-handed house is the Lady's, and a husband's or priest's suspicion of it is a fear about who controls the house.

**Classical antecedents, and what a learned cleric could say.** Medieval Latin clerics read Lucan, Horace, Ovid and Virgil at school. A Latin-educated hostile voice (rule 3: a named speaker of a vanilla faith) may reach for them. All of the following are **proposals**:

**What is out.**
- **Any claim of continuity from antiquity** (Hecate, Diana or the Bacchae to the Ladies) in the narrator's or the faith's voice. Stratton's own method rejects it: each witch is local, and she declines the universal "night-witch" archetype (p. 72). This is also the Murray line (`witch_design.md` §10).
- The Roman predatory-hag package and the Christian demon dualism (p. 122) as the faith's self-image.
- Cross-pollination with the rabbinic "women, food, magic" material. The Ladies' laid table and the Talmud's warning about bread left by the road are structurally inverse, but setting them side by side would tie Jewish women to the witch faith. **Never do that**, in any voice or in design notes that might leak into loc.

**Does the licit/illicit distinction give the sect anything?** A little, and mostly as a design discipline rather than as text:
1. **The Turning is not an act.** For Abaye only an *act* (*maʿaseh*) of *keshafim* is capital (p. 147). The hard limits already make the Turning a body-free gate: no knife, no potion (Issurei Bi'ah 16:12), heaven's turning "as Dinah was". Stratton's material supports keeping it so. If anyone in a Jewish voice asks whether the Turning is *kishuf*, the sect's answer is that it does nothing: it waits, learns and prays, and heaven turns. This fits `doctrine_witchcraft_shunned`, already on `tm_nehpakhot_rite`. Proposed: one line at most, in the elder's voice in "Before the Baylons".
2. **Classification is authority.** "The only way to know the difference is to ask a rabbi" (p. 155); the power to classify a practice *is* the rabbis' jurisdiction (Janowitz via Stratton, p. 155). The sect already meets the community through rulings (the baylons and the council, *niddui*, the lord's licence). Keep Jewish resistance to the sect framed as **law and ruling**, never as an accusation of sorcery. That is a constraint, not new content.
3. **"Learning to understand and to instruct"** (b. Sanh. 68a) could license, in-voice, the elder reading what Christian physicians write about daughters. Low value; optional.
4. **Do not use the pragmatic test "if it heals, it is permitted"** (p. 155). Applied to the Turning it would make womanhood an illness to be cured and its benefit the proof. That breaks rule 17 in spirit and the mod's whole stance.
5. **Babylonian against Palestinian.** Provençal Rabbanites read the Bavli. Stratton's "knowledge as power" against "merit as power" (pp. 157–66) maps onto the gate's two weights (her learning and the elder's; patience). It is flavour for a design note, not for loc.

**New hard-limit candidates (from ch. 4 and ch. 5).** Proposed for §13:
- **No Christian voice calls Jews, the sect, its elders or its *fisica* sorcerers, magicians or witches**, and no "Jewish magic" in any voice. Luke "invokes a stereotype of Jews as magicians" (p. 124). Justin and Celsus show the charge flowing both ways (pp. 118–20). The medieval charge against Jewish doctors is in `jud_shatzmiller_medicine.md`. This sharpens the existing "Christian hostile text attacks doctrine, never Jews as a people".
- **No Jewish woman linked to food and harm**, in any voice (Stratton's "women, food, and magic" cluster, pp. 169–76).
- **No Simon Magus comparison for any Jewish character.** Simon is Samaritan in Acts, and the slur's later use is Christian heresiology.

### (c) Adelphai Dorleyaiou

Stratton ends before Byzantium, but the Greek patristic texts in ch. 4 (Irenaeus, Hippolytus, Origen, Justin, the *Acts of Andrew*) were read in Byzantium, and two of her figures are Anatolian.

**Hostile Orthodox voice (rule 3: a named speaker; tag family and shape).**
1. **Heresy as Simon's line.** A metropolitan or monk may say the Sisters' teaching comes from Simon. Irenaeus himself admits his targets never named Simon (p. 129). Shape: *exposing* (a deceiver). Family: *trace* or *inner*, depending on the line. The narrator never agrees.
2. ***Goēs* as the Greek word for the exposing shape.** By the 4th century BCE *goēs* means a charlatan and deceiver (p. 44). In patristic Greek, *goēteia* is what heretics and seducers do (*Acts of Andrew*, p. 135; Irenaeus's *magikē kubeia*, p. 131). Rule 9 asks for a Byzantine source before a Greek "monster" word is used. Before putting *goēs* in loc, find it in a Byzantine (not only patristic) text against heretics; likely places are the canons (Ancyra 24, Trullo 61, Basil 65 on magic), not checked here.
3. **"Beguiled women" and the male magician.** Heresiologists cast women in rival churches as victims of a male seducer (pp. 130–35). Turned on the Sisters, this frame **misgenders**: the daughters become either the male *goēs* or his dupes. That is exactly rule 3's *unpersoning* and *exposing* shapes. Allowed only in a named hostile speaker's mouth, and the faith answers it.
4. **"The Phrygian heresy."** Montanism is already the faith's claimed ancestry (§12 item 6: Pessinus, Pepouza, Dorylaion). Stratton adds two hostile hooks. Montanists tied prophecy to "dry fasts and sexual continence" (p. 151), and a *Phoronis* fragment calls *goētes* "mountain-dwelling men from Mount Ida in Phrygia" (p. 28). A learned Constantinopolitan sneer could join Phrygia, *goēteia* and Montanus. Rule 22 applies: fasting must never become a gate test.
5. **Dionysos the effeminate Lydian *goēs*** (p. 47) is the classical template for a Byzantine scholar's sneer at a "Phrygian" man in women's dress. Rule 3 says hostile lines aim at the imagined body, not the clothes, so if this is used, aim it at the body ("a man unmanned"), not at hair and perfume. Rule 24 already sets dress by date.
6. **Christian dualism.** For an Orthodox hostile voice, magic is demon worship (p. 122). This is the strongest charge available against the Sisters, and it is distinct from the Latin "illusion" register of the Good Ladies. That keeps the two faiths' hostile overlays different (uniqueness memory).

**The Sisters' own voice.**
1. **Firmilian's woman prophet** (Cappadocia, 256): sacraments correct in every form, judged demonic because a woman performed them (pp. 139–40). This is the same double standard the Sisters already name over the monk-saints (§16 "Saints"). Proposed: in the Sisters' own voice, as the Church's habit of judging the hand and not the rite. The woman is unnamed in the source; do not claim her as a foremother (rule 19).
2. **Origen's three answers to a charge of magic** (pp. 118–19): no spells, only the Name and scripture; no fee; a moral life. These are a ready template for an event in which the Sisters answer a bishop's charge of *goēteia*. Caution: `doctrine_clerical_function_alms_and_pacification` and the galli alms economy could be turned against "no fee". Let the answer say that the alms feed the poor.
3. **"My miracle, your magic."** In the Sisters' voice, their rites are signs (*sēmeia*) and powers (*dunameis*), as Acts says of Philip and Peter (p. 13), never *mageia*.

**An observation, not a proposal to change.** `transbian_maximalist_rite` (the Adelphai) carries `doctrine_witchcraft_accepted`. The Good Ladies design recommends `shunned` so as to keep vanilla's coven events and "Witch God" out (`witch_design.md` §3.2). After v2 the Byzantine Christian heresy would accept witchcraft while the witch-coded Latin faith shuns it. Stratton's ch. 4 shows why an Orthodox voice would treat "accepted" as demon worship. Whether that is intended (the Sisters as the Church paints them) or an accident of the 1.x build is a question for the user (§7).

### (d) Other faiths

- **Bædling (seiðr).** Stratton's "magic as effeminate power that delegitimizes men" (p. 25; Dionysos p. 47; Heracles p. 63) is the Greek and Roman cousin of the Norse *ergi* charge. It is comparison only. Do not import Greek or Roman tropes into Norse hostile voices; the Norse notes already carry their own law and words.
- **Worn Garland, Badenya, Yutai Dao.** Stratton's warning that finding "magic" elsewhere imports Western categories (p. 27), and that colonial writers used "primitive magic" to deny self-rule (pp. 3–4), supports rule 12 (Badenya: no race-curse rhetoric) and the rule against modern framings for the Garland. The vanilla doctrine names cannot be avoided, but the narrator and the faiths' voices should use emic words (for example the smith's craft, the *pāvai* vow, Daoist ritual terms) and not "magic" or "sorcery". This needs a loc check, not a design change.

## 6. Guardrails (additions to rules 1–26, proposed)

1. **No continuity from antiquity** for the Good Ladies in any voice (Hecate, Diana, Bacchae, the Thessalians). Classical figures appear only as a cleric's learned gloss, in the "illusion" register, with a suggested date gate after c. 1120.
2. **Roman witch material is banned beyond names.** Canidia, Erictho and Pamphile's corpse, child and sex details fall under `witch_design.md` §10's "accusers' fantasies".
3. **No "Jewish magic"** in any voice. No Christian voice calls Jews, the sect or its *fisica* sorcerers. No Jewish woman tied to food and harm. No Simon Magus for Jewish characters. (Candidate §13 hard limits.)
4. **None of Stratton's ch. 5 women-and-magic texts appears in loc** (b. Sanh. 67a "most women"; Exod. 22:17; Shimon ben Shetaḥ; Yannai's donkey; Rav Naḥman's daughters; b. Eruv. 64b; y. Kid. 4:11; b. Ber. 53a; b. Pes. 110a; b. Ḥul. 105b; y. Shab. 14:4; b. Nid. 20b), with the narrow optional exceptions in §5(b).
5. **The Turning is never a spell.** No text frames it as *kishuf*, as countermagic or as a cure. The rabbinic "if it heals" test is never applied to it.
6. **Orthodox hostility against the Adelphai uses the demon register; Latin hostility against the Ladies uses the illusion register** (until c. 1430). This keeps the overlays distinct.
7. **No "witch", "magic" or "sorcery" as the narrator's label** for any faith's rites. These are always someone's accusation (Stratton's "naming").
8. **Never cite Murray**, and do not confuse Oswyn or Michele Murray in Stratton's notes with Margaret Murray. Do not use the book's cover art.
