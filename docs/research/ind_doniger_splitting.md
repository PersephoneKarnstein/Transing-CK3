## 0. Framing warning

Doniger's vocabulary is **not the mod's**:
- She calls the crossings "transsexual" throughout, the gods "serially transsexual" and "bisexual" (p. 260), and Ilā "s/he" (p. 271).
- She uses "MTF"/"FTM" (p. 260), calls the hijras "transvestite eunuchs" (p. 265), and calls Śikhaṇḍin "a murderous transsexual" (p. 285).
- She claims that "the perception of the self as transsexual ... is attested by very early mythological sources" (p. 260).

The mod should not repeat that claim or these labels in loc. Two of her own points cut the other way, and are worth keeping:
- These myths "do not precisely represent the self-perception of transsexuals, who feel that they are not changing to a new sex, as the gods seem to do, but changing back" (p. 261).
- On the third sex: it is "often correctly pointed out that Hindus recognize a third sex", but this "should not be adduced to imply that Hindus approve of this third sex". *Klība* is a "homophobic rubric" for "a man who fails to be a man" (pp. 279–280). That is close to Bhatt's dumping-ground argument.

Her summary verdict on the corpus: "By and large, these are not happy stories" (p. 301).

## 1. The argument in brief

### Viṣṇu as Mohinī (pp. 261–265)

- MBh 1.16–17 has three verses: Viṣṇu takes "the marvelous body of a woman" to cheat the demons of the ambrosia, then "gave up his incomparable female form" (p. 261).
- Doniger reads Mohinī as a **masquerade**: Viṣṇu "never forgets that he is Vishnu; he retains his male memory and his male essence" (p. 263).
- The Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa (4.10) adds Śiva's assault on Mohinī (pp. 263–264). **Sexual violence; do not use.**

### The forest where men become women (pp. 266–270)

The forest appears in several forms:
- **Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.1:** sages interrupt Śiva and Pārvatī, and to spare her shame Śiva says "Whoever enters this place will become a female" (p. 266).
- **Brahma Purāṇa 108.26–30:** Pārvatī asks for her own forest where any man "will become a woman", with Śiva's family excepted (p. 267).
- **The earliest version is the Rāmāyaṇa** (7.87–90, n. 20). No one interrupts. Śiva "took the form of a woman to please Parvati, and everything in the woods, even trees, became female" (p. 267). Doniger notes that later texts reversed the causality: in the Rāmāyaṇa, Śiva's own change makes the forest female.
- **Sāyaṇa** (14th c., on RV 10.95) has the goddess say "Let any man who enters here become a woman" (p. 267). Doniger calls Sāyaṇa "tenth-century"; Sāyaṇa is usually dated to the 14th century (*her slip, unverified*).
- **Skanda Purāṇa, Sahyādrikhaṇḍa (Śivālaya Māhātmya):** after a dice quarrel Pārvatī curses her forest so that males become female. Śiva answers with a forest that turns women into men. The nymphs go there to escape "birth on earth" as women, saying that she who becomes a man by a curse "will not be an object of lust" (pp. 269–270). Female to male; **reaction only**.

### Ilā / Sudyumna (pp. 270–278)

- **Genealogy.** The Mahābhārata (1.70.16 in the Critical Edition, n. 35) makes Ilā Manu's daughter and "both his mother and his father" to Purūravas (p. 271).
  - This settles the numbering question in `ind_mahabharata_bhangasvana_ila.md`: Ganguli's Ādi LXXV = CE 1.70.
- **The Rāmāyaṇa version** (Uttarakāṇḍa 7.87–90, n. 36; Doniger's summary pp. 271–273):
  - Ila is a king, son of the sage **Kardama** (not Manu). He is a hunter whose "lust for hunting was unsatisfied" (p. 271).
  - He enters the place where Śiva has become a woman, and is changed.
  - **Śiva refuses**: "Ask for any boon except manhood" (p. 271).
  - **Pārvatī offers half**: "you will be half female, half male" (p. 271).
  - **Ila proposes the monthly alternation himself**: "let me be a woman for a month, and then a man again for a month" (p. 271).
  - **Pārvatī adds the forgetting**: in each state Ila "will not remember" the other (pp. 271–272).
  - As Ilā, she meets **Budha**, son of Soma, meditating in a lake.
  - Budha turns her former-male attendants into "female Quasi-men" (*kimpuruṣī*) and they run away (p. 272).
  - Ilā and Budha are lovers for a month. When Ila wakes as a man, Budha lies to him: his servants died in a hailstorm (p. 272).
  - Purūravas is born in the ninth month (p. 273).
  - At the year's end Budha has the sages propitiate Śiva with a **horse sacrifice**. Śiva "gave Ila his manhood" for good.
  - "King Ila ruled in the middle country of Pratishthana", and his son Śaśabindu ruled in Bāhlī (p. 273).
- **The *kimpuruṣīs*.** Doniger glosses them as "creatures half-horse and half-human, literally 'sort of men'", "a living oxymoron" (p. 274).
- **Budha as rapist.** Doniger reads Budha's withholding of Ilā's memory and his seduction as "in effect raping a sleeping woman", the "marriage of a ghoul" of Manu 3.34 (p. 275). Ilā has lost "political power, class (servants), and gender" in one stroke (p. 275).
- **Ilā and Bhaṅgāśvana contrasted.** Ilā's greater pleasure as a woman "does not ultimately weigh in the scale of gender against the disadvantages of being a woman: he chooses not to remain female" (p. 276). Bhaṅgāśvana is the contrast case (below).
- **The Bhāgavata Purāṇa version (9.1.18–42, n. 46; pp. 276–277).**
  - The parents wanted a boy, but "the priest had made a mistake, and so a girl was born", named Ilā. The priest "rectified his error" and she became a man (Sudyumna).
  - The forest makes her a woman again. She marries and bears Purūravas. She begs Śiva for help and is allowed alternate months. She goes "to heaven as someone who had the distinguishing signs of both men and women".
  - Doniger: since "his first physical form is that of a female", the text "constantly fights its way upstream against the current of Ila/a's tendency to revert to female type, and requires constant interventions from male powers (gods or priests) to keep making her male" (p. 277).
- **The Bhaviṣya Purāṇa version (3.4.17, n. 33; p. 271).** Ila himself, out hunting, sees Śiva naked. Śiva curses the place: "all creatures but me will become women". Ila's "stallion became a mare".
- **The Sahya mountain version (Skanda Purāṇa, Sahyādrikhaṇḍa, n. 47; pp. 277–278).**
  - Ilā keeps her memory, becomes Pārvatī's companion and servant, and learns that bathing in the river as a woman would release her.
  - She says she "preferred to be a slave to Ganga and Gauri". The goddesses answer: "To hell with birth as a woman; it's nothing but pain and grief."
  - She bathes and becomes a bearded man (p. 277).
  - Doniger: he "chooses to be a woman, but the women around him quickly enlighten him and he rejoins his true self" (p. 278).
- **Two dynasties.** Ilā founds both "the lunar and the solar". Doniger suggests the myth joins two male-descended houses "without demoting either partner to the inferior status of a female" except "only temporarily, of course" (p. 273). She also reads the monthly cycle as "biological and female" against the solar, "dharmic" year (pp. 273–274).

### Bhaṅgāśvana (pp. 276, 301)

- Doniger gives the story only a paragraph, citing MBh 13.12.1–49 (CE, n. 43). Asked which sex s/he would keep, Bhaṅgāśvana "would prefer to remain a woman, since as a woman s/he had greater pleasure in sex—which also makes her love the children she had as a woman more" (p. 276).
- Doniger **links the pleasure and the children**, which the text keeps apart. `ind_mahabharata_bhangasvana_ila.md` shows the children answer a different question.
- Parallels: the Buddhist Soreyya also prefers the children of his female life (p. 276). Cūḍālā says a woman has eight times the pleasure (p. 276). Teiresias (Greek) says the same (p. 293).
- In the conclusion: "empathy can be used as a weapon: 'Which way was the sex better?' they asked Bhangashvana, and answer came there, 'As a woman'" (p. 301).

### Arjuna as Bṛhannalā (pp. 280–281)

- A *klība* "masquerade". "This is a paper-thin masquerade, meant to be funny, because we all know how virile he is" (p. 281).
- This matches the reading in `ind_mahabharata_bhangasvana_ila.md` §6.

### Conclusion (pp. 295–302)

- **Memory and power.** Whichever way the change goes, "the transformed person usually forgets the former gender and identity". But "there is often another man present, untransformed, who remembers, and who therefore has power over the transformed person. For Ila/a, it is Budha; for Amba, Bhishma" (p. 295).
- **Two Hindu views of gender**, both held at once. Gender is "fluid and superficial", changing with the body; or "astonishingly durable", so that the male "retain[s] his male essence" (pp. 268, 295–296).
  - "The texts that view gender as fluid generally depict the transformed male as happy in her female form", and those where memory "lags stubbornly behind" show "him as miserable" (p. 298).
- **Rebirth rarely changes gender** in Hindu texts. The exception she gives is the men "who wanted to become women to make love with Rama", who were reborn as cowherd women (p. 298). This is the same story as Ramanujan's sixty thousand sages (`ind_ramanujan_bhakti.md`).
- **"How deep is gender?"** "Hinduism answers Yes." Many single texts answer "Deep and shallow" (p. 301).

### Later and modern material (flag)

All of these are modern; none may be presented as medieval:
- Krishna's marriage to Araka/Aravāṉ as a hijra origin story (pp. 265–266; Nanda 1990, *India Today* 1990);
- the Bahucharā-type goddess and prince story, and "Ram and the transsexuals" (pp. 278–279; Nanda);
- the Gaṅgamma festival at Tirupati (p. 286, female to male);
- Śiva as Rādhā (p. 270; Brahmavaivarta and Mahābhāgavata, late).

## 2. What matters for the design

1. **The grove story is sourced.** Rāmāyaṇa Uttarakāṇḍa 7.87–90 (CE numbering), with Purāṇic variants: Bhāgavata 9.1.18–42, Bhaviṣya 3.4.17, Brahma 108, Skanda Sahyādrikhaṇḍa. The Rāmāyaṇa and Bhāgavata are well before 1453. The Bhaviṣya's *pratisarga* and the Sahyādrikhaṇḍa may be late (dates *unverified*).
2. **In the Rāmāyaṇa the cause is a god becoming a woman.** "He took the form of a woman to please Parvati" (p. 267). In the faith's voice: the Lord of the grove made himself a woman first, and the grove followed. This is a myth of a god's crossing as the **source** of the change, not a curse. The faith can reclaim the grove in its own voice, though the narrator should flag that the Rāmāyaṇa's Śiva is a Śaiva figure in a Vaiṣṇava-adjacent faith.
3. **Ilā is cured, not chosen.** Every version ends with Ila made a man by male powers: Śiva's gift after a horse sacrifice, a priest, or the goddesses' advice. Doniger says the Bhāgavata "requires constant interventions from male powers ... to keep making her male" (p. 277). **The faith's reading of Ilā is a polemic against the cure, not a claim that Ilā chose to stay.** Bhaṅgāśvana remains the only one who chose.
4. **The alternation is Ila's own bargain, inside Pārvatī's offer of "half".** The amnesia is Pārvatī's condition (pp. 271–272). The reading list's "Pārvatī softens this to alternating months" is half-right. The "third thing" is the half-and-half state that Śiva's refusal and Pārvatī's compromise leave Ila in.
5. **The *kimpuruṣī* are made by Budha, a man**, to strip Ilā of witnesses (p. 272). Read with Bhatt, this is a good find: the "sort-of-men" are created by the male who wants the woman alone and without memory. The "third thing" is a tool of his power over her.
6. **Memory belongs to the man who holds power** (p. 295). The Garland's gate can invert this: the **companions** (*tōḻi*) keep the bride's memory, and no man holds it.
7. **On the Sahya mountain even Ilā chose to stay**, and goddesses talked her out of it with "To hell with birth as a woman" (p. 277). That is a ready **hostile line**, and it gives the faith a martyr-like claim: "She chose, and they shamed her back."
8. **The bath unmakes as well as makes.** Bhaṅgāśvana's lake made a woman. The Sahya pool made a man (p. 277). The dawn-bath step needs care: the faith claims the first water, and its opponents claim the second.
9. **The sixty thousand sages reborn as cowherd women** (p. 298, with Ramanujan) is the cleanest myth of men who wanted to be women for the Lord and were made so. It sits alongside Bhaṅgāśvana.
10. **Pratiṣṭhāna is confirmed** for Ila's rule in the Rāmāyaṇa version (p. 273). The c_prayaga holy site can rest on it. The Rāmāyaṇa gives Ila, as a man, rule of the "middle country of Pratishthana". Which Pratiṣṭhāna is not specified in Doniger; that Prayāga is meant is *unverified*.

## 3. Corrections to the candidate

- **Source of the grove.** "Rāmāyaṇa, Uttarakāṇḍa; Mahābhārata 1.75" → the grove, curse and alternation are **Rāmāyaṇa 7.87–90** (CE) and the Purāṇas. The Mahābhārata gives only the genealogy, at CE **1.70.16** (= Ganguli Ādi LXXV).
- **Who Ila is.** In the Rāmāyaṇa he is the son of **Kardama**, not Manu (p. 271). In the Bhāgavata, Ilā is **born a girl** to Manu's line and made male by a priest (p. 276). The Mahābhārata has Manu's daughter (p. 271).
- **The alternation.** It is not simply "Pārvatī softens this to alternating months". Śiva refuses manhood, Pārvatī grants "half", Ila asks for the monthly alternation, and Pārvatī adds mutual forgetting (pp. 271–272).
- **The ending.** Ila is **restored to manhood permanently** by Śiva after a horse sacrifice (Rāmāyaṇa), or goes to heaven with the signs of both (Bhāgavata), or is talked into manhood by goddesses (Sahya) (pp. 273, 277). "Ilā" is never a figure who remained a woman. The faith's line "half the kings of India descend from a woman who was a man" is still sound for the genealogy ("both his mother and his father", p. 271). The narrator must not imply Ilā stayed.
- ***Kimpuruṣa*.** In the Rāmāyaṇa the *kimpuruṣīs* are Ila's former-male attendants, turned by **Budha** into half-horse "sort of men" (p. 272). They are not "Pārvatī's half-mercy". If the faith's polemic names the *kimpuruṣa* as the cage, it should be the cage **Budha builds**, while Pārvatī's "half female, half male" offer is the compromise. Both can be refused.
- **Bhaṅgāśvana: Doniger is thin.** She gives a paragraph and links the pleasure and children answers (p. 276), which the Mahābhārata keeps separate. Keep the reading in `ind_mahabharata_bhangasvana_ila.md`.
- **Budha is no romance.** Doniger reads the episode as rape of a woman deprived of memory (p. 275). Any Budha content must be off screen, and framed as harm.
- **Sāyaṇa** is 14th c., not "tenth-century" as Doniger writes (p. 267). This is a flag only.

## 4. Design implications

- **Scripture list (doctrine screen), faith's voice.**
  - Bhaṅgāśvana (MBh 13.12): the one who chose.
  - The sixty thousand sages reborn as cowherd women (later tradition): the ones who were granted.
  - Ilā (Rām. 7.87–90; Bhāg. 9.1): the one who was cured against her nature. Faith line: "It took a priest and a god to keep Ilā a man" (from p. 277).
- **Polemic against the third thing ("the cage of the grove").** Split it into two refusals:
  - **Pārvatī's half**: "half female, half male" by the month, with forgetting (pp. 271–272), named as the compromise the faith refuses.
  - **Budha's *kimpuruṣī***: the "sort of men" a lover makes of a woman's companions to leave her alone (p. 272), named as the regime's tool.

  This keeps the faith on Bhatt's side: the third thing is made by power, not found in nature.
- **Gate step 1 (companions), inversion of Doniger's rule.** "The man who remembers has power over her" (p. 295). In the Garland, **her companions keep her memory**. Mechanic: with no surviving companion, success is impossible, or the change comes with the "forgetting" stress trait.
- **Gate step 2 (dawn bath), two waters.** Flavour on success: "the lake that made Bhaṅgāśvana". Hostile event on a bath at a rival shrine: "the Sahya pool that unmade Ilā" (p. 277). Add an option to refuse the second bath.
- **Hostile lines.**
  - Goddess-priestess or Śaiva ascetic: "To hell with birth as a woman; it's nothing but pain and grief" (Sahya, p. 277). This is the strongest in-myth hostile line found so far; keep it as a quote in a hostile mouth.
  - Śaiva lord: "Ask for any boon except manhood" (p. 271), reversed as mockery ("Ask any boon but womanhood").
  - Rival sage: "Every Ilā goes back to being a man at the year's end."
- **Event, "The Horse Sacrifice".** A Lunar-descended ruler's priests propose a horse sacrifice to "restore" a converted kinswoman, as Kardama and Budha did for Ila (p. 273). Options: refuse (faith piety, a dynastic quarrel); accept (she loses the faith or is forced out); or turn the rite into a feast for her instead. Off-screen coercion only.
- **Dynastic claim, c_prayaga holy site.** Narrator: "The Rāmāyaṇa says King Ila ruled the middle country from Pratiṣṭhāna; his line, through the child he bore as Ilā, became the Moon's kings." The faith's voice: "Ilā was both his mother and his father." (pp. 271, 273).
- **Mohinī.** If a Mohinī reference appears (the festival costume, Narayanan p. 117), the faith says Mohinī was a disguise, "the Lord kept his own memory" (p. 263). The Garland's change is not a costume.
- **Do not use:**
  - the Śiva–Mohinī assault;
  - Budha's seduction on screen;
  - Ambā's death or Śikhaṇḍinī's despair (self-harm content, p. 282);
  - the hijra origin stories (modern, living community);
  - Doniger's "transsexual" and "bisexual" labels in loc;
  - her claim that "perception of the self as transsexual" is ancient (p. 260).
