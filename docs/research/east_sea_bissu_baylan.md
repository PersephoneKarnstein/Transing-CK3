# Southeast Asian gender-variant priesthoods: bissu (Sulawesi), baylan/babaylan and asog/bayog (Philippines)

Research pass for the Adelphai Dorleyaiou mod. Purpose: (1) material for a possible later Southeast Asian branch or neighbour faith; (2) on-map precedents for a transfeminine priesthood with ritual, royal and funerary functions; (3) how such roles were gendered and succeeded. Design of the main faith is locked and not relitigated here.

Sources (all read in full):
- A = Alfred W. McCoy, "Baylan: Animist Religion and Philippine Peasant Ideology", *Philippine Quarterly of Culture and Society* 10.3 (1982), pp. 141-194. Cited "McCoy p. N".
- B = Matthew Kennedy, "Clothing, Gender, and Ritual Transvestism: The Bissu of Sulawesi", *Journal of Men's Studies* 2.1 (1993), pp. 1-13. Cited "Kennedy p. N".
- C = Leonard Y. Andaya, "The Bissu: Study of a Third Gender in Indonesia", in Zamfira, de Montlibert, Radu (eds), *Gender in Focus* (Budrich, 2018), pp. 64-87; a reprint of Andaya 2000 in B. Andaya (ed.), *Other Pasts*. Cited "Andaya p. N" (2018 pagination).
- D = Petsy Jessy Ismoyo, "Decolonizing Gender Identities in Indonesia: A Study of Bissu 'The Trans-Religious Leader' in Bugis People", *Paradigma* 10.3 (2020), pp. 277-288. Cited "Ismoyo p. N".
- E = Carolyn Brewer, "Baylan, Asog, Transvestism, and Sodomy: Gender, Sexuality and the Sacred in Early Colonial Philippines", *Intersections* 2 (May 1999). Web text with numbered paragraphs, no pages. Cited "Brewer ¶N" and "Brewer n.N" for endnotes.

Date discipline. The game runs 867-1453. Not one of these sources has a dated primary witness earlier than 1521 (Pigafetta, Cebu) for the Philippines or 1545 (Antonio de Paiva, Siang) for Sulawesi. Everything older is reconstruction from the I La Galigo (undated oral/manuscript epic), from lontara' chronicles written down in the 17th-18th centuries, or from 19th-20th century ethnography projected backward. Each entry below states the date of the evidence and what the author claims about pre-1600 practice.

Cross-source frictions to keep in view:
- Kennedy's "order of ... male priests" (B p. 2) vs. Andaya's demonstration that in the I La Galigo "they are almost all prominent women" (C p. 79) and that a 17th-century Puang Matoa was "a very old woman" (C p. 78).

## 2. Bissu dossier

### 2.1 The five Bugis genders

Ismoyo gives the canonical modern list: "oroané (male), makkunrai (female), calalai (masculine female), calabai (feminine male) and Bissu (androgini or intersex who become religious leaders)" (Ismoyo p. 283). Andaya glosses the bissu-language and vernacular terms: "the bissu (known among themselves by the bissu language term of saladéwi = 'false women') and a homosexual (calabai' = 'false women') or lesbian (calalai' = 'false men')" (Andaya p. 80).

The relation between calabai and bissu is contested in the sources and matters for design:
- Ismoyo (via Lathief): "A calabai or calalai may become a Bissu. However, there are more calabai than calalai who became Bissu" (p. 283); three grades of calabai, "only those who have been in the calabai tungke'na lino group can call themselves Bissu" (p. 283).
- Andaya: "Many of the Bugis are beginning to equate the calabai' ... and calalai' ... with the bissu, a misconception which is reinforced by the necessity of using calabai' and calalai' to act as bissu in important ceremonies requiring forty bissu ... while some of the calabai' are bissu, not all bissu are calabai'. Some bissu have wives and children and lead a life of a male when not in a bissu role" (p. 82).
- Flag: Ismoyo's "intersex" and the unqualified "five genders" are the decolonial-essay generalisation; Andaya's source-based view is that the Bugis had two mortal genders and a sacred, non-mortal category: "it was proper in South Sulawesi that they should be classified not as mortal Bugis but as a unique third category of being" (p. 84).

### 2.2 Neither, both, or "combination"

Three competing framings, all citable:
- Composite/bisexual: "They have been described as male and female existing in one being (van der Kroef, 1956, pp. 189-190), and as bisexual agents that endow objects with spiritual force (Hamonic, 1975, p. 131)" (Kennedy pp. 4-5).
- Neuter/pre-difference: Hamonic "sees the bissu representation as rather more neuter in their unification of masculine and feminine" (Kennedy p. 6); Errington: "Beings that are sexually ambiguous ... are considered potent not so much because they combine or cordate the duality of sexes but because they are as yet pre-difference, they embody an unbroken unity" (Kennedy n.3, p. 12).
- Outside: "The third sex/gender group is regarded as being neither male nor female nor a composite of both. It is their ambiguous status that locates them beyond society's more conventional sexual and gender dualism" (Andaya p. 71).
- Cosmogonic basis: "the creator is an androgynous deity who gives birth to a male sun and a female moon ... the female moon conceived from within herself. The ensuing offspring, among whom were the bissu, albinos, dwarfs, negroids, and other asexual beings, were considered to be powerful because they originated from the most sacred unity before the separation" (Andaya p. 73). "The Bugis cosmogonic myth counts the bissu among the 'monstrous' creations, which are all replete with sacred powers" (p. 73).
- Kennedy's reading of why: "Only the bissu, of symbolically ambiguous gender, have the authority to consecrate such objects, since they alone possess male and female energy" (p. 6).

### 2.3 Recruitment: born, called, trained

- Marked at birth: "It is believed that the gods mark the bissu sexually from birth as a sign that they are destined to become bissu. Something of the nature of this sexual malformation is conveyed in the concept of koro, a pathological fear among Bugis males of the retraction of the penis into the groin. The Bugis in Bone attribute the sign of 'bissu-ness' to the very first bissu who was born with an umbilical cord in the shape of a sacred naga serpent which could not be severed from the body" (Andaya p. 80).
- Called: "It is believed that bissu have both a male and a female spirit spouse who were responsible for calling them" (Andaya p. 82); "The call from the spirits is manifested in a physical way through catalepsy or mutism. This state can only be overcome through special ritual cures, which precede a full initiation ceremony" (Andaya n.28, p. 82). Ismoyo: "a calabai must get a 'spiritual call', after which Puang Lolo will provide guidance and live in Arajang's house to learn the maBissu etiquette" (p. 283).
- Presented by parents (Brooke, Wajo' 1840): "the parents of a boy, upon perceiving in him certain effeminacies of habit and appearance, are induced thereby to present him to one of the rajahs, by whom he is received. These youths often acquire much influence over their masters" (Brooke in Ismoyo p. 284; paraphrased Andaya p. 79).
- Raised among women, tutored by a bissu: "Once a child is chosen by the gods, he is raised among women but tutored by a bissu. He is taught the bissu language or 'language of the gods' (basa déwata), the sacred bissu texts (which he must commit to memory), bissu dances and chants, preparation of offerings, and the precise steps in rituals" (Andaya p. 80).
- Class rule, contested: Hamonic was told "that the kunéng lolo were not permitted to become true bissu because this role was reserved for commoners" (Andaya p. 80), but "this is contrary to the I La Galigo ... In the I La Galigo many of the bissu are of noble or royal stock with female names" (pp. 80-81).
- Definition: "A true bissu is one who has been tutored by a bissu and undergone special initiation rites" (Andaya p. 82).

### 2.4 Apprenticeship and initiation: irebba / ma'rebba (the trance-death) and mabbissu

Andaya (from Hamonic 1977): "From the stage of being a 'bissu mamata', i.e., an unripe bissu, he progresses until he becomes a 'bissu tanré' (high bissu). In that final stage he must undergo the ma'rebba (to fall, die) ceremony, in which the soul leaves the initiate's body and wanders in the other world for one, two or three days, during which time the bissu maintain a constant vigil of singing and dancing. At a given moment, the Puang Matoa, or leader of the bissu, provides a sign to indicate the return of the soul to the body and the creation of a new bissu. The initiate thus becomes a bissu nasolo' ridéwata ('bissu blessed or acknowledged by the gods'). During the Dutch period the ceremony was banned and Indonesian authorities continue to regard it with disapproval today, although it is conducted clandestinely on very special occasions" (Andaya p. 80).

Ismoyo (from Suliyati 2018, Lathief 2004) gives a six-step version: "(1) calabai/calalai must undergo the 'mutih' fast, where they only eat white rice without side dishes and water for 7 (seven) days or fast for 40 days at the bola arajang (house for storing relics); (2) Mattinja' (vows) and must write and pronounce the Sureq Galigo smoothly ...; (3) The Bissu candidates are then laid in the bola arajang attic for 3 or 7 days in the irebba process; (4) The last day, the Bissu candidate is bathed, shrouded and laid down for a day from morning to dusk, on top of his body a water-filled jar which has been purified (mabessi) during the sakkatolo (agreed) ceremony is hung, during this process, Sureq Galigo is sung to the prospective Bissu; (5) At dusk, the jar is broken by Puang Matowa until the body of the prospective Bissu is wet at irebba; (6) The Bissu candidates is officially a Bissu if they have passed this process" (Ismoyo p. 283). Before it: "A calabai must receive the blessing of Puang Matowa and Puang Lolo, then swear to obey all the pangaderreng (traditions and customs) to become a Bissu" (p. 283).

Mythic charter for initiation-as-animation: in the I La Galigo a child "is dressed as a bissu but lies motionless. The bissu fetch their musical instruments and perform a dance which enables the child to gain movement. She then undergoes the proper initiation and becomes a bissu" (Andaya p. 75; again p. 79: "It requires intercession by the bissu and their sacred instruments before the child can be infused with the spirit and initiated to full bissudom").

Word-play on death and generation: "In the bissu language, the same written characters can be transcribed as matelo ('to die') or mattelo ('to make love') ... matelo/mattelo expresses the double meaning of death/love leading to new life/creation" (Andaya p. 81).

Note for the mod: there is no castration anywhere in the bissu material. The bodily rite is a *shrouded ritual death and re-animation*, not a surgical one. The Iban manang bali (Andaya p. 68) is the only "sexually disabled" prerequisite in this corpus, and Roth "does not explain how" (n.4).

### 2.5 Dress and regalia

- Kennedy: "clothing for the bissu functions to represent a trans-gendered state of being: they wear the jacket, white cloth, and adornments connoting the feminine and the kris or sword and head cloth that are distinctly masculine" (p. 6); "the bissu do not create any illusion of overt or exaggerated femaleness" (pp. 5-6). "In Indonesia the knife, kris, and the cloth or garment appear over and over as symbols of male and female, respectively" (p. 6); "an association appears to exist between the cloth and the female sexual organ, just as the spear (kris) is regarded as identical with the male sex organ" (Gerlings via van der Kroef, Kennedy p. 7). Photo captions: "Large held prop referred to as alosoe in nineteenth century Dutch account (Matthes, 1872, p. 9)" (p. 5); "The kepala (head) bissu in performance with kris (dagger) in hand" (p. 6).
- Andaya, 17th-c. Lamatti: the Puang Matoa "dressed in red, was borne on a palanquin"; her bissu carried "swords (alameng) and weaver's wooden shuttles (walida) while chanting their sacred spells (memmang)"; these "are the sacred male and female symbols used in ceremonies to ward off evil spirits (Matthes 1943: p. 502)" (pp. 78-79).
- Paiva 1545: "They grow no hair on their beards, dress in a womanly fashion, and grow their hair long and braided ... they have their teeth covered in gold" (Ismoyo p. 284).
- Matthes-era procession: "led by the Puang Matoa, followed immediately by female bissu carrying a betelbox and a cuspidor, and a large number of dancing and singing bissu" (Andaya p. 80).
- Modern: Puang Matowa Saidi and bissu "dressed in white with a head-covering similar to Hajj" (Ismoyo p. 286).

### 2.6 Functions

Guarding the regalia (arajang). "The bissu, along with the regalia (arajang), are believed to have descended from the Upperworld to accompany the first white-blooded rulers to the earth" (Andaya p. 72). "The sacred objects become the regalia (arajang), and the bissu the priestly guardians of both these new rulers and the arajang" (p. 76). "Until the mid-twentieth century, bissu were found in every royal court serving as advisors and as keepers of the regalia" (p. 74). Some bissu "eschew any sexual contact with others, living in the regalia house as guardians of the arajang" (p. 83). Kennedy: "royal court priests of the former Bone Kingdom of Sulawesi, have been guardians of sacred objects, performers of formal ritual, and healers" (p. 4). Objects: "swords, shields, pottery, jewels, inlaid boxes, gongs, drums, cloth, and spears" (p. 5). The consecration rite: "Regarding the human being who is brought here near the sacred object and who touches it, it is not known of him nor of the object who is the man and who is the woman. If the holy object is a man, then this human is a woman and vice-versa" (van der Kroef in Kennedy p. 6). Ismoyo adds support offices: "panati, who helps Puang Matowa to care for the Arajang; and Jennang who manages the bola Arajang" (p. 284), and regalia rituals "Mattedduk Arajang, Mappedaung Arajang" (p. 282).

White blood. "The center is represented by the ruler and his family, the possessors of 'white blood', the supernatural 'bodily' fluid which determines power and prestige in the realm" (Andaya p. 71; Bugis toma'daratakku', "Takku' is a type of tree which has a milky sap", n.9). "Crucial in this role are the royal women, who are regarded as the prime repositories of the flow of this precious sacred fluid" (p. 72). "Preserving the progeny of these first rulers, those still possessing this sacred fluid, becomes then the primary concern of the bissu" (p. 72). "A vital component in the maintenance of white blood hierarchy is the bissu" (p. 72). "So respected and feared is the power of the bissu that they are also employed to repel all threats, whether human or supernatural, which may endanger the white-blooded rulers" (p. 81).

Royal weddings, births, installations. In the I La Galigo "bissu officiate at royal marriage and birth rituals to assure the perpetuation of the sacred white blood of the ruling house" (Andaya p. 73). "The royal wedding is the greatest manifestation of this fertility ritual and the prominence played by the bissu" (p. 81). Matthes: "Their true function lay in performing ceremonies dealing with rites of passage of the royal progeny" (p. 79). Kennedy: ceremonies "when a prince assumes his duties as regent" (p. 5). Still "summoned to perform at weddings of prominent officials or members from high nobility" (Andaya p. 82).

Rice and agrarian rites. "before work in the paddy fields" (Kennedy p. 5); "There are 40 Bissu tanre, and they are the highest level of Bissu who appeared only at the ceremony of going down to the rice fields (mappalili)" (Ismoyo p. 284); "to produce a new creation (i.e. ... during the growth of a new crop)" (Andaya p. 74); "to end droughts, ward off epidemics" (p. 82). A male bissu name, La Oddang Riwu', "is associated with the rice goddess, Sangiang Seri" (Andaya n.26, p. 79).

Trance and possession. Matthes: "the bissu called upon the spirits or deities (réwata) to descend to earth and enter their bodies as well as the special altar (lamming réwata). Only when they were possessed by the spirits did they become true bissu. It was during these occasions that the bissu danced, sang, and performed their cures" (Andaya p. 79). The Arung Palakka text: "By falling into a state of trance, the bissu is able to see considerable activity in the Upperworld ... Anurungeng Telinoé's soul then ascends via the rainbow to the Upperworld" (p. 77). Titles: "'Anurungeng' is a special title held by bissu who are able to intervene on behalf of humankind, and a 'bissu terru' is a bissu able to foresee the future" (n.21, p. 77).

Blood, war and the ma'giri'. None of the five sources names the ma'giri' self-stabbing; what they give is its mythic and historic frame. I La Galigo: "the bissu are summoned to do battle with one another so that their spilt blood will release the souls (bannapati) from the Upperworld and enable Datu Sénngeng to give birth ... Those bissu who were sacrificed to ease Datu Sénngeng's suffering regain their lives after the birth" (Andaya p. 75). "Throughout the I La Galigo, reference is made to the sacrifice of bissu and other 'monstrous' beings" (p. 75). War function: "before taking the field against an enemy in war" (Kennedy p. 5); 1660s Lamatti, "the bissu then were also employed in one case as the first line of defense ... As they approached, the Bone troops fired on them but no one was injured" (Andaya p. 78). Buffalo sacrifice closes ceremonies, "regarded as a symbol of the moon, the creator deity of the bissu" (p. 80). Healing: "when healing is needed during an epidemic" (Kennedy p. 5); "to remake an imperfect creation (i.e., restore the health or soul of a sick person or crops)" (Andaya p. 74).

Text and verbal magic. "They use a separate language, a 'language of the gods', while reciting or writing down their sacred texts" (Andaya p. 74). "Pelras believes that the epic was the work of bissu. To this day they continue to be the main preservers and scholars of episodes from the I La Galigo" (n.12, p. 72). The bissu produce "verbal/written magic to enhance the spiritual powers of the white-bloods" (p. 81); the 17th-c. Arung Palakka tale "is not simply an entertaining legend but a form of oral magic which reactivates the beginning of time in order to endow the favored ruler with powers of that primeval period" (p. 77).

Nursemaids and domestic service. "Some of the bissu attached to the courts are mentioned as nursemaids to the princes" (Andaya p. 74); "a highly honored post because milk relations were valued as much as ties of blood" (n.16). Matthes: "the women bissu performed all types of domestic (hence female) chores in the courts" (p. 79). Male bissu "were considered impotent and therefore had access to the princesses of the court. Because of this belief any sexual contact between the bissu and the princesses was punishable by drowning, a fate usually reserved for those convicted of incest" (p. 79).

### 2.7 Celibacy or marriage

- Paiva 1545 (Siang): "They marry and are received, according to the custom of the land, with other common men, and they live indoors, uniting carnally in their secret places with the men whom they have for husbands. This is public [knowledge] ... These priests, if they touch a woman in thought or deed, are boiled in tar because they hold that all their religion would be lost if they did it" (Ismoyo p. 284; Andaya pp. 76-77 summarises: "sentenced to death by fire").
- Modern typology (male bissu only): "Some marry and have families and assume a male role; others assume a female role by marrying males or having sexual relations with males; while a third group continues to eschew any sexual contact with others, living in the regalia house as guardians of the arajang" (Andaya pp. 82-83). Acceptance ranking: the first "is acceptable"; the second "less acceptance"; the third "greater acceptance of the role of the bissu in ritual" (p. 83).
- Ascetic ideal (Ismoyo via Davies): "Bissu may not have sexual relations and be able to control their sexual desires. Bissu must also show the quality of other asceticism, such as not drinking tea or coffee; or not eating sweets" (p. 282); etymology "Bissu comes from the word 'bessi', which means clean; in a sense, not bleeding, holy, not menstruating, and not breastfeeding" (p. 282).
- Kennedy: "many or all of the bissu are known to be practicing homosexual men (Hamonic, 1975; Holt, 1980; van der Kroef, 1956)" (p. 9); "Perhaps homosexual behavior heightens, rather than detracts from, this rarefied ambiguous state" (p. 9).

### 2.8 Relation to Islam and to the La Galigo

- Islam: Gowa and Tallo conversions 1605 (Ismoyo p. 285); "widespread conversion to Islam in the seventeenth century" (Kennedy p. 4). Bissu survived it: "The legitimatized transvestite is historically continuous, surviving widespread conversion to Islam, Dutch colonialism, and modern nationalism" (Kennedy p. 2). Modern hostility: "Despite hostility from many Muslims, among the Bugis population there is still a residue of reverence" (Andaya p. 83); "Islamic scholars consider the practice carried out by Bissu is 'disgraceful' and 'damned'" (Ismoyo p. 286, citing Davies). Syncretic adaptation: Sanro Barlian "as Puang Matowa Bissu Segeri as the last to carry out the Hajj as an adaptation effort so that his charisma and position as a traditional leader is still 'respected'" (Ismoyo p. 286); Islam in a bissu ritual "is considered more as a stream of occult rather than dogma" (p. 286).
- La Galigo: "the bissu tend to regard this epic work as a ritual manual" (Andaya p. 75). Three categories of bissu in it: "bissu born of the gods who belong to the purest nobility"; "bissu who possess land and palaces" (titles Datu Bissu and Bissu Lolo, with no apparent religious function); and "Puang (Lord) or Puang Matoa (Elder Lord)" who "only congregate to perform important religious ceremonies" (p. 74). Of "some eighty bissu names from the I La Galigo. Only three were male" (n.25, p. 79). First bissu: "Lae Lae is the first Bissu descended from the sky along with Raja Luwu, Batara Guru" (Ismoyo p. 281).

### 2.9 Succession and property

- Succession is by apprenticeship and appointment, not descent. "Puang Lolo (young master) is considered as a representative or confidant of Puang Matowa. Puang Lolo inherits all knowledge from Puang Matowa" (Ismoyo p. 284). "A Puang Matowa must come from Puang Lolo. He is the head of the Bissu, who are appointed by the king" (p. 283). Rank ladder: Bissu Mama (uninitiated) → Ana'Bissu (bissu tanre / bissu poncok) → Puang Lolo → Puang Matowa (pp. 283-284); Andaya's: bissu mamata → bissu tanré → bissu nasolo' ridéwata (p. 80).
- Numbers: "There are 40 Bissu in Bone. It is believed that 40 is considered a perfect number ... so it is also called the Bissu patappuloe" (Ismoyo p. 283); "It is no coincidence that forty bissu are required in the most significant ceremonies" (Andaya n.13, p. 73).
- Property: royal land grant to the office. "In the past, anyone who became the Puang Matowa received one hectare of land, given by the King. Now, the government is establishing a cultural office and school on the land that is given to Bissu" (Bissu Mariani in Ismoyo p. 286). "Since the last Puang Matowa died, customary land ownership belongs to the government" (p. 286); "the Puang Longi heritage fields in Segeri partially belonged to the local government" (p. 286). Housing: bissu "lived in Bola Arajang" (p. 286). In the epic, one class of bissu "possess land and palaces" (Andaya p. 74). Crisis: "the absence of Bissu's regeneration also caused a leadership crisis, especially since the death of Puang Matowa Saidi in 2011" (Ismoyo p. 285).
- No political rule: "there is no historical evidence of a bissu ever exercising political leadership in any of the South Sulawesi kingdoms ... their influence was usually indirect" (Andaya pp. 74-75). But "princesses in the Makassar kingdom of Gowa ... incorporated the word 'bissu' into their titles" (p. 74).

### 2.10 Colonial and modern suppression

- Portuguese disgust (1545): Paiva "amazed [that] Our Lord would destroy those three cities of Sodom for the same sin and considering how a destruction had not come over such a wanton people" (Ismoyo p. 284). Flag: Ismoyo says Paiva "had an Orthodox Christian background" (p. 284); he was a Portuguese Catholic.
- Dutch: "During the Dutch period the ceremony [ma'rebba] was banned" (Andaya p. 80). Kennedy: "Dutch colonial accounts include mention of unusual people (dwarfs, hermaphrodites, etc.) populating royal courts" (p. 9).
- Republic: "Soon after the creation of the independent Republic of Indonesia in 1950, a decision was made to eliminate the remaining sovereign kingdoms ... This act deprived the bissu of their royal patrons and their principal raison d'être" (Andaya p. 82).
- DI/TII and Operasi Tobat: "Operation Toba (Operation Taubat) was initiated by Kahar Muzakar (DI/TII) in 1966, targeting the Bissu community. They were considered inappropriate with Islamic teachings because of homosexuality and rituals that are considered 'heretical'. This movement forced the Sanros and Bissu to become 'men'; those who refused were killed in Operation Toba. All ceremonial equipment was burned and sunk to the sea ... during the New Order, in 1965, the Bissu and sanros would be associated with PKI ... Since Operation Toba, the mappalili ceremony was no longer held on a large scale; the Bissu hid from the threat of death" (Ismoyo p. 285, citing Lathief's 1999 interview with Angkong Petta Rala). Flag: Ismoyo's dating is internally muddled (1966 for Kahar Muzakkar, who was killed in 1965; "New Order, in 1965"); treat the sequence (DI/TII Islamist campaign, then New Order anti-PKI pressure) as sound and the years as approximate. Andaya does not mention Operasi Tobat.
- Economic collapse and stereotype: "Bissu only relies on income from working in salons, bridal makeup, and invitations to perform custom events. Bissu no longer resides in Arajang" (Ismoyo p. 285); "That is why they are working in Salon, and thus the stereotypes begin to embedded in them as 'Banci Salon'" (p. 286). Numbers: "approximately 3,000 bissu now live in Sulawesi, though only 200 know the bissu vocabulary (personal communication, Saudara Gandaria, August 27, 1991)" (Kennedy p. 8); Ismoyo's 40 in Bone (p. 283). Sacred/secular split: "As long as the bissu perform their sacred role, they are accepted and revered. But when they revert to a mundane existence, they are expected to behave according to society's ideas of male and female roles" (Andaya p. 82).

## 3. Baylan / asog dossier (Philippines)

### 3.1 Baylan / babaylan as (mostly) female religious specialists

- Social structure at contact: "Beneath the datu was a relatively unelaborated social structure comprising the bayani warrior class, the baylan priesthood, freemen, and several classes of slaves. While the datu and bayani warriors were male, it was the common practice to have women serving as baylan" (McCoy p. 154).
- Loarca, Arevalo 1582: "These sacrifices are called baylanes, and priestesses, or the men who perform this office, are also called baylanes. ... The priestess chants her songs and invokes the demon, who appears to her all glistening in gold. Then he enters her body and hurls her to the ground, foaming at the mouth as one possessed. In this state she declares whether the sick person is to recover or not. ... Then she rises and taking a spear, she pierces the heart of the hog" (McCoy p. 158).
- Pigafetta, Cebu 1521: "Two 'old women' were the ritual facilitators: dressing in specific clothes; chanting prayers and dancing; offering food thanksgiving to the gods; killing a pig; anointing the foreheads of the people present with fresh pig's blood ... Pigafetta explained that only women were able to perform this ceremony and that the pig meat was never eaten unless it was consecrated and killed in this way by women" (Brewer ¶28).
- Alcina 1668: "what is generally accepted and least doubtful is that they were commonly women not men" (Brewer n.81). Alcina on recruitment of women: "although their priestesses were ordinarily women selected ... by the Devil, he also chose some effeminate man [sic] that they called asog in ancient times" (Brewer ¶37).
- Count, Bolinao 1679-85: "Named were 145 female shamans and three males who dressed as women to perform the rituals - a ratio of almost 50:1" (Brewer ¶29; n.82 says instrumentos were confiscated "from around 300 named women").
- Brewer's social frame for pre-contact Philippines: "there was a bilateral kinship system, women actively participated in the economic realm and maintained control over their earnings, virginity was not valued, 'adultery' was not noteworthy, both women and men were 'chieftains,' and women predominated in the spiritual domain" (¶4).
- The 1970s Western Visayas practitioners McCoy interviewed were mostly men (ten of eleven named babaylan, p. 164 n.10), and "In contrast to the traditional religious babaylan who were predominantly women, all of the babaylan revolt leaders were male" (p. 166). Brewer concurs for Negros: "by the late nineteenth early twentieth centuries, as Evelyn Tan Cullamar suggests, all the babaylan on Negros were male" (¶33). Both treat the masculinisation as a colonial-era shift.

### 3.2 The asog / bayog / bayoguin: male-bodied, woman-dressed priests "like women"

Terminology (Brewer ¶9): "bayog or bayoguin by both Juan de Plasencia and the anonymous author of the 'Manila Manuscript,' bayoc by Salazar, and as asog in Alcina's account from the Visayas ... In labelling the male shamans I use bayog for examples from the Luzon area and asog for the Visayas. The most common indigenous referents for the women who performed the role of spirit medium and healer were baylan (Visayan), catalonan (Tagalog) and the more general anitera."

Appearance and manner:
- Manila MS c. 1590: "old women or Indians [indigenous men] dressed as women" perform the maganitos; many of these men "ordinarily act like prudes, and are so effeminate that one who does not know them would believe they are women" (Brewer ¶13).
- Chirino 1604: the "pagan priest ... braided his hair, which for his particular calling, he wore long like that of a woman" (¶13; epigraph adds "was possessed by the Devil who caused him to make most ugly grimaces"). Rodriguez 1602: hair "filled ... with a kind of resin or turpentine" (¶13).
- Alcina 1668 on the "ancient" asog: "The fact is the Asog considered themselves more like women than like men in their manner of living, or going about, or even in their occupations. Some of them applied themselves to women's tasks, like weaving and cultivating, etc. In dress, although they did not wear petticoats (these were not worn by women in ancient times either) they did wear some Lambon, as they are called here. This is a kind of long skirt down to the feet, so that they were recognized even by their dress" (¶14).
- Alcina's living informant, the unnamed "mute indian" of Sulat, Ibabao: "was so effeminate that in every way he seemed more like a woman than a man.... His dress was even over the legs with a wide Bahaque which resembled, under the Lambon, the old time petticoats. All the things that the women did, he performed, such as weaving blankets, embroidering and sewing clothes, making pots, which is the work of [women]. He danced also like they did, never like a man whose dance is different. In all he appeared more a woman than a man" (¶15). Brewer cautions "no evidence is provided to prove that the 'mute indian' did practice as a shaman" (¶16).
- Bolinao 1679-85: "the three identified male shamans dressed in women's clothes only when they performed the ceremonies for the Anitos. These included the seventy year old, Calimlim, ... Calinog, and Mamacuit" (¶17). Brewer: "the minimum prerequisite for male entry into the ranks of shamanism was that he dress in feminine clothes for ritual purposes" (¶24).
- Blumentritt 1895 (Bikol): asog = "name of a type of priest of the ancient Bikols. The asog dress as women and they imitate their flirtatiousness totally"; also "name of the idolatrous priests of the Bisayas" (Brewer n.73). English 1986 Tagalog dictionary keeps only "sterile" and "asexual" (n.73).

Took husbands:
- Manila MS: bayog "marry other males and sleep with them as man and wife and have carnal knowledge" (Brewer ¶22), noted "in a non-condemnatory tone".
- But also married women: "there were also examples from Bolinao, of male shamans marrying women ... In the two instances in the Bolinao Manuscript where bayog were married to women who were also shamans, the bayog readily abandoned the religious tradition of their ancestors for the privilege of heterosexual Hispano/Catholic masculinity ... giving evidence against their own wives" (¶22).
- Alcina's informant: "would never allow himself to be touched, nor would he ever bathe in front of others" (¶18); "maintained his distance from men"; "it would appear that the 'mute indian' was, like Alcina himself, celibate" (¶19).

### 3.3 Functions: healing, sacrifice, funerals, harvest, war, curse

- Occasions: "It is only in case of sickness, and in times of seed-sowing or of war, that sacrifices are offered" (Loarca, McCoy p. 158).
- Healing by trance and familiar spirit: "the babaylan goes into trance, in which his guardian spirit whispers the name of the offended spirit into his ear. The babaylan can then call upon the malign force to accept an appropriate offering. If the spirit is not defined and appeased, it is assumed that the disease will turn fatal" (McCoy p. 162). "most mediums with any real influence in the spirit world have a divine calling, often an hereditary one, and are frequently guided in their work by a familiar spirit" (p. 144).
- Blood sacrifice as payment: pigs and chickens scaled to the house (p. 164); "The use of human sacrifice to restore major disruptions in the balance between the cosmic, spiritual and terrestrial is perhaps the most important of the other rituals commonly practised in Southeast Asia" (p. 152); Ngaju: "the balian smeared everyone with the victim's blood at four spots indicating the four cardinal points" (p. 152); 1976 Candado testimony of a sugar-mill daga (p. 153).
- Great assemblies and harvest: Alcina's Pagtigman, "a gathering of many ... They ask for its help and that they may have plentiful and good crops ... and danced and whirled about until they could do so no longer" (McCoy pp. 158-159); the Mt. Balabago rain ritual "every seven years during Christian Holy Week", "the sacrifice of seven pigs by seven babaylan", readings by a "Mayor-mayor babaylan dressed in a black tunic with red bands and headdress" (pp. 159-160); still held 1976 (p. 162).
- Funerals: the five sources give no direct funerary liturgy. Plasencia's 1589 title is "Relation of the Worship of the Tagalogs, their Gods, and their Burials and Superstitions" (Brewer n.23) and McCoy's appendix lists *amalanhig*, *murto*, *mamanhig* (ghosts/haunting, pp. 186-187), so the role in death-ritual is implied but not described here. Flag as a gap.
- Curse: "the baylan's use of their powers to curse, sicken, or murder on behalf of their clients ... specialists in the curse (hiwit), generally condemned by Western Visayan baylan as a mercenary corruption, generally use beetles (barang)" (McCoy p. 153).
- War leadership and invulnerability: the babaylan's "magical powers enabled him to grant his followers immunity from enemy weapons and, simultaneously, to threaten all who failed to follow him with spiritual retribution" (p. 166); amulets (anting-anting), oracion in garbled Latin (pp. 162-163).
- Hierarchy: "A low ranking healer, a siruhano, merely offers a physical cure ... an intermediary level practitioner, the lower ranking type of babaylan, usually has a spiritual cure ... At the peak of the hierarchy in times past was a small group of mediums with exceptional powers who were known as dalagangan (root word: dagan, fire-red or prominent) and had the power to command the elements" (McCoy p. 162). Alcina: "the baliana priest passed through a period of madness and attained strong spiritual powers, the tambalan remained a pharmacopoeia specialist" (p. 158).

### 3.4 The Spanish accounts and their sodomy framing

- Plasencia 1589: introduced a hierarchy "suggesting that the male sonat is 'a sort of bishop who ordains the priestesses, for they knelt before him.' Plasencia, however, admitted that women were not represented in the sample from whom he obtained information. As he stated he interviewed only 'old men'" (Brewer ¶31).
- Manila MS / Boxer Codex c. 1590 (Brewer n.20: a lay compilation, probably for Governor Dasmariñas): "The function of the priests ... is to help [the priestesses] on all occasions; in general, to help the priestesses invoke the spirits" yet "the men who dressed as women 'act[ed] with more pomp, ceremony and authority'" (¶31). Also "almost all asog are impotent for the reproductive act" (¶18).
- Chirino 1604: "possessed by the Devil" (epigraph).
- Aduarte 1640: male shamans "a wretched class of people, and with some reason despised on account of their foul manner of life" (¶22).
- Alcina 1668: asog "impotent men and deficient for the practice of matrimony" (epigraph; ¶18); searched for hermaphrodites, found community denial, "was reluctant to believe them" (¶19); "it was the effeminacy of the asog men that attracted the satanic spirits who 'chose them [asog] for this ministry'" (¶37).
- The sodomy discourse: Pretty 1588 ("fowle sinne of Sodomie"), Archbishop Santibáñez 1598 blaming the Chinese, Talavera 1605 and Morga 1609 agreeing (¶20-21; n.69-70). Brewer: "there was a persistent reference to sodomy amongst male shamans" (¶22) but "the 'sin against nature,' in the source documents, refers equally to anal intercourse between two men, or a man and a woman" (¶6); she deliberately avoids "homosexuality".
- Inquisitorial method: the Bolinao Manuscript "was collated on the orders of Archbishop Felipe Pardo with the express purpose of eliminating Animist practice" (¶7); confiscation lists (n.95: "67 items were confiscated from Da Ana Masanti, 61 from Michaela Samari, while only 26 were taken from Calinog").
- Witchcraft lens: the Spaniards "believed that the Devil was specifically attracted to women" (¶36, with the Malleus Maleficarum); "the non-conformist feminised gender identification of the male shamans of the Philippines confirmed that the Devil was attracted, not only to the female body, but also to the feminine" (¶37).

### 3.5 Baylan in peasant revolts (McCoy)

- Structural point: "There was only the vaguest distinction in many societies between the religious role of the spirit medium and the political-military role of the secular leadership, the sultan and lesser datu ... a spirit medium could assume temporary political leadership, particularly during times of insurgency" (p. 145). After the Spanish removed the datu, "The babaylan thus remained the peasantry's only potential focus for mobilisation" (p. 165).
- Named leaders: Buhawi/Ponciano Elofre (1887), "A reputed homosexual and traditional 'miraculous curer,' two often complementary attributes" with "command of fire, flood and rain; invulnerability to Spanish bullets; and the power to change shape and fly with a magic handkerchief" (p. 167); Gregorio "Dios" (1888-91, p. 168); Gregorio Lampino, "a homosexual peasant from the town of Janiuay, Iloilo ... Believed to have dalagangan powers, most particularly the ability to fly and disappear" (p. 169); Papa Isio/Dionisio Sigobela (1896-1907), "sacred bolo", believed "immortal, and cannot be hit or wounded by bullets" (pp. 171-172), later "a Utopian community based on independent pagan villages" (p. 175); Oto the bandit with a tamawo-given libreto (pp. 170-171); Emperor Florencio Yntrencherado (1926-27), "attired in a jewelled crown and elaborate brocade robes" (p. 177); the 1874 Balabago killing of Fr. Badrena by the babaylan Ramos, "the guardian of the two PRINTED books, the babaylan Vedas" (p. 160).
- Pattern: "all of the babaylan revolt leaders were male, indicating that these movements may have recreated the magical datu war leader" (p. 166); "the fear/charisma component which gives the movements their initial solidarity is bound up with the person of the magical leader, making it virtually impossible to sustain an organized insurgency once the leader is removed" (p. 180).

### 3.6 How the role passed on

- Calling (often hereditary) plus madness: "most mediums with any real influence in the spirit world have a divine calling, often an hereditary one ... many baylan suffer a brief period of madness before accepting their calling" (McCoy pp. 144-145).
- Apprenticeship: "low-level practitioners can achieve a modicum of skill through a simple apprenticeship" (p. 144).
- Women to women, after marriage: "the older female shamans passed on 'the art of doing sacrifices to the anito or anitos' to the younger women-only after they were married" (Brewer ¶29, Bolinao f. 9).
- Books down the male line (modern): "Most ranking babaylan still possess small books of ritual Latin prayers (oracion), inherited from their fathers and grandfathers" (McCoy p. 162); Conrado Santiago's libreto "he had inherited from his father in 1933. The elder Santiago had in turn inherited it from his father-in-law, a dalagangan practicing in neighbouring Antique Province" (p. 163). Note the affinal link: inheritance through a son-in-law.
- Vision-gift: Oto "found it under his pillow ... the tamawo vision is a common means for mediums to acquire powers and the libreto is a basic tool for all babaylan" (pp. 170-171).
- Sibling pairing: Bolinao's deceased "principal priest" had a sister who "was in charge of the most powerful Anito" (Brewer ¶32).

### 3.7 Were asog regarded as women?

Evidence for "yes, functionally":
- "considered themselves more like women than like men" (Alcina, Brewer ¶14); "so effeminate that one who does not know them would believe they are women" (Manila MS, ¶13); "marry other males and sleep with them as man and wife" (¶22); "so that they were recognized even by their dress" (¶14).
- Brewer's thesis: "it was the male shaman's identification with the feminine, either as temporary transvestism or as a more permanent lifestyle choice, that reinforced the normative situation of female as shaman, and femininity as the vehicle to the spirit world" (¶38). "In this instance biology did not represent destiny, and it was sufficient for a male to outwardly replicate the look and behaviour of the woman shaman by dressing up and performing the feminine" (n.99).
Evidence for "not a separate class, and the Spanish made them a deficiency":
- Spanish explanation was "either a supposed anatomical deficiency or ... sodomy" (¶6). Community denied hermaphrodites (¶19).
- Bolinao men cross-dressed only for ritual and some had wives (¶17, 22); they were subordinate helpers to the priestesses (¶31).
- Brewer's own limit: "it is neither possible to discern how these men understood their own gendered subjectivity, nor to understand how other Filipinos perceived them" (¶8).
Verdict for the mod: the Philippine material supports a priesthood in which womanhood is the qualifying condition and male-bodied entrants qualify by *becoming* women in dress, labour, dance and marriage, with no bodily operation. It does not support a formally recognised third class; Brewer argues explicitly against that (¶34).

## 5. Belief-plausibility matrix for a Southeast Asian setting

Each row: what the on-map evidence says, entries that STRENGTHEN or WEAKEN plausibility for a Southeast Asian branch/neighbour of the Adelphai Dorleyaiou, and a verdict. "Plausible" means a 9th-15th-century Southeast Asian population could credibly hold or recognise the belief, given these sources; it does not mean the sources prove it existed then.

| Item | Evidence | STRENGTHEN | WEAKEN | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Distinct sex class (castrates / bissu counted as a class of their own) | Andaya: bissu "classified not as mortal Bugis but as a unique third category of being" (p. 84); Ngaju basir/balian belong "to neither moiety" (P32); Mon neuter sex (P37); hijra "neither man nor woman" (P36) | P1, P32, P36, P37, P38: a sacred class outside male/female is deeply attested across the archipelago | Andaya insists ordinary society had only two categories (p. 84); Brewer rejects a third class for the Philippines (¶34); Ismoyo's "five genders" overstates (flag) | PLAUSIBLE for Sulawesi/Borneo as a *sacred* class, not a civil one. For the Philippines, weak: there the class is "women, including those who became women". A branch faith could present castrates as "a sacred kind, as the bissu are", but should not expect laity to see a third civil sex. |
| (b) Womanhood/priesthood conferred by rite | ma'rebba creates "a new bissu" (P5); the inert child animated and initiated (P11); Bolinao women taught after marriage (P25); Brewer: "it was sufficient for a male to outwardly replicate the look and behaviour of the woman shaman" (n.99) | P5, P11, P23, P25: ordination is a real transformation ("bissu nasolo' ridéwata") and femininity is performed into being | Rite confers *priesthood*; nowhere do the sources say it confers *womanhood* in law or kinship. Paiva (P16) and Alcina (P23) describe lived womanhood but not a conferring rite | PLAUSIBLE for priesthood-by-rite; womanhood-by-rite is an extension the sources do not make but do not contradict. Good basis for a branch tenet "the trance-death makes the priestess". |
| (c) Bodily rite as sacred rebirth (no castration here) | ma'rebba = "to fall, die" and return (P5); shrouding and water-jar breaking (P5); matelo/mattelo "to die"/"to make love" (Andaya p. 81); bissu blood-battle that revives (P10); Iban manang bali must be "sexually disabled" (P33); hijra "some even castrated" (P36, outside the region) | P5, P10, P33, P36: death-and-return and (in Borneo) a bodily prerequisite are present | No Southeast Asian source here records ritual castration. Brewer: community "disavowal of the existence of hermaphrodites" (¶19); Andaya: Roth "does not explain how" the manang bali was disabled (n.4) | PLAUSIBLE as *symbolic* death-rebirth; castration is NOT attested on-map in this corpus. A Southeast Asian branch should substitute the shrouded trance-death for the knife, or explain the knife as an Anatolian import that locals recognise through the manang bali precedent. Note this explicitly in any in-game text. |
| (d) Male-bodied ritualists regarded as women | Alcina: asog "considered themselves more like women than like men" (P23); Manila MS: "would believe they are women" (P21); Paiva: bissu "dress in a womanly fashion ... imitate [women's] speech" (P16); Iban manang bali "treated like a woman" (P33); saladéwi "false women" (Andaya p. 80) | P16, P21, P23, P33 all independent, 16th-19th c. | Bugis term is "false women", i.e. not simply women; Bolinao men cross-dressed only for ritual (P24); Andaya stresses androgyny not womanhood | PLAUSIBLE, strongest in the Philippines (Brewer's whole thesis). For Sulawesi, "woman-like sacred being" rather than "woman". |
| (e) Women-plus-transfeminine priesthood | I La Galigo bissu ~80 names, 3 male (P13); Lamatti female Puang Matoa with 100 female bissu (P12); Pigafetta women-only rite (P19); Loarca "priestesses, or the men who perform this office" (P20); Bolinao 145:3 (P24); Ngaju balian women + basir men (P32); bedhaya women + boys (P30) | P12, P13, P19, P20, P21, P24, P30, P32: the mixed female/transfeminine order is the dominant pattern in every region sampled | Male-bodied bissu dominate 16th-c. and 19th-20th-c. descriptions (Paiva, Brooke, Hamonic); Negros babaylan all male by 1900 (Brewer ¶33) | STRONGLY PLAUSIBLE. This is the single best-supported item. Brewer's formula, "femininity as the vehicle to the spirit world" (¶38), is ready-made doctrine. |
| (f) Separatism (a community apart from men) | Bissu living in the bola arajang (P1, P18); Paiva: "they live indoors" (P16); taboo on touching women enforced by death (P16); Papa Isio's "independent pagan villages" (McCoy p. 175); Buhawi's "independent mountain community" (p. 167) | P1, P16, P18 (a priestly house); McCoy's upland withdrawals as a model for a separatist settlement | Bissu separatism is from *women* (Paiva) and from laity, not from men; Philippine priestesses lived in ordinary households and married (P25); McCoy's communes are peasant, not gender-based | WEAK-TO-MODERATE. A cloistered regalia-house is well attested; a women-and-castrates settlement apart from men is not. Frame separatism as "the house of the regalia" rather than a sex-segregated commune. |

A branch can lean on milk-kinship (nursemaid bissu) as the local idiom for adoptive motherhood. |
| (h) Communal property | Puang Matowa's hectare "given by the King" (P18); bissu "lived in Bola Arajang" (Ismoyo p. 286); epic bissu "who possess land and palaces" (Andaya p. 74); Iban manang bali's "willingness to place his wealth at the disposal of his followers" (Andaya p. 68) | P18 and the regalia-house: property attached to the office/house, not the person | Land was a royal grant to the office-holder, not a commune; Philippine babaylan were paid per ritual (McCoy p. 153: fees P0.50-P10) | MODERATE. Endowed priestly house with office-held land is credible; full communal ownership is not shown. |
| (i) Non-marital kinship / marriage of asog to men | Paiva: bissu "marry ... with other common men ... the men whom they have for husbands" (P16); Manila MS: bayog "marry other males ... as man and wife" (P21); Andaya: some bissu "assume a female role by marrying males" (p. 83); spirit spouses male and female (p. 82); Ngaju sacred sexual service (P32) | P16, P21, P32 plus the spirit-spouse idea: marriage of the transfeminine priest to a man is on-map and public ("This is public [knowledge]", Paiva) | Spanish framed it as sodomy (Brewer ¶6, ¶22); Bugis today accept the married-to-males group least (Andaya p. 83); some bayog married women (P24) | STRONGLY PLAUSIBLE. Two independent 16th-c. witnesses in two regions. Also offers "spirit spouse" as a non-marital kinship form. |
| (k) Intra-group hostility (factions, apostasy, rival orders) | Bolinao bayog testifying against priestess wives (P42); Buhawi's relatives vs. Spanish; Ramos "murdered in a quarrel with his nephew" (McCoy p. 160); Plasencia's male sonat "bishop" vs. priestesses (P22); Andaya's three bissu classes who "move in completely different circles" (p. 74); hiwit sorcery "condemned by Western Visayan baylan as a mercenary corruption" (McCoy p. 153) | P22, P42 and the sorcery condemnation show internal rank and doctrinal disputes | Evidence of hostility is mostly colonial-induced (P42) or personal; no attested schism among bissu | PLAUSIBLE as colonial-pressure apostasy and as rank/purity disputes (ritual-only cross-dressers vs. lifelong; curse-workers vs. healers). |
| (l) Outlaw framing (Islamic and Spanish suppression) | Paiva's Sodom comparison (P16); Dutch ban on ma'rebba (P39); Operasi Tobat killings and forced manhood (P40); Spanish inquisitorial confiscations and Aduarte's "wretched class" (P42, Brewer ¶22); Islamic scholars: "disgraceful" and "damned" (Ismoyo p. 286); bissu "hid from the threat of death" (p. 285); 1874 friar killing (P41) | P16, P39, P40, P41, P42: every monotheist power that met these orders tried to end them | Islamisation of Gowa/Tallo is 1605, Spanish arrival 1565, both after game end; within 867-1453 there is no attested suppressor on these islands (Islam reaches Sulawesi courts only post-1453) | PLAUSIBLE as *latent* hostility and as a post-1400 Islamic-court dynamic (Malay/Sumatran sultanates, Brunei); the Spanish cannot appear. Use Islam as the in-game outlaw-framer in the late period, with the sida-sida (P35) as the ambiguous courtly survival. |
| (m) Female-dominated doctrine (bilateral kinship, female rulers) | Brewer: bilateral kinship, women chieftains, women predominated spiritually (¶4); Pigafetta women-only rite (P19); royal women as white-blood repositories (P2); Lamatti female Puang Matoa (P12); epic bissu as royal women (P13); Gowa princesses with "bissu" in titles (Andaya p. 74); Minangkabau matriliny (Kennedy p. 11); moon-goddess as creator of the bissu (P38) | P2, P12, P13, P19, P38: feminine sacrality is central in both regions | Andaya: "no historical evidence of a bissu ever exercising political leadership" (pp. 74-75); kings appoint the Puang Matowa; McCoy's revolt leaders all male | STRONGLY PLAUSIBLE for doctrine and ritual authority; WEAK for priestly *political* rule. Female rulers are attested in Southeast Asia generally but not by these five sources beyond Gowa princess-titles. |
| (o) Apprenticeship / sponsor | "raised among women but tutored by a bissu" (Andaya p. 80); "A true bissu is one who has been tutored by a bissu" (p. 82); Puang Lolo as confidant and heir (Ismoyo p. 284); Brooke's boys presented to rajahs (P17); Bolinao older-to-younger women (P25); McCoy "simple apprenticeship" vs. "divine calling, often an hereditary one" (p. 144); father/father-in-law libretos (P26) | P17, P25, P26 and Andaya p. 80: every case has a named teacher or sponsor | None significant | STRONGLY PLAUSIBLE. Sponsor + apprenticeship + a royal or spirit "call" is the regional norm. |

Summary of verdicts: strongest on-map support for (e) women-plus-transfeminine priesthood, (i) marriage of transfeminine priests to men, (m) feminine sacrality, (o) sponsorship; moderate for (a), (b), (d), (g), (h), (k), (l); the castrating body-rite (c) has no Southeast Asian attestation here and must be presented as foreign or substituted by the trance-death.

Design notes:
- The branch's castration (locked in the parent faith) should be marked in Southeast Asian text as an Anatolian inheritance that locals read through their own idiom of the trance-death and the "sexually disabled" manang bali; no Southeast Asian source here attests it (see §5 row c).
- Prefer Brewer's framing ("femininity as the vehicle") for Philippine content and Andaya's ("outside male and female, older than both") for Bugis content; they are different theologies and should not be merged into one tenet.
- Ismoyo-derived details (six initiation steps, title ladder, land grant, 1966 purge) are usable as flavour but rest on Lathief/Suliyati/Davies; do not cite Ismoyo's dating of the La Galigo or his "five genders" as historical.
- Chronology guardrail: nothing here is dated before 1521/1545. Any in-game claim of antiquity (Kennedy's "600 years", Ismoyo's "13th-15th century") should appear only as in-faith legend.
