# Research notes: *The Transgender Studies Reader Remix* (eds. Susan Stryker & Dylan McCarthy Blackston, Routledge 2022/2023)

Source file: `scratchpad/books/tsr_remix.txt` (29,459 lines). Line numbers below (`L####`) refer to that file. All quotations are verbatim from the file (hyphenation at line breaks silently rejoined; editorial ellipses in the source are kept as `[. . .]`).

Purpose: ground the fictional CK3 culture/faith "Transbian Maximalism" in (1) baedelism, (2) "third wave transfeminism", (3) transgender Marxism, and (4) real historical precedents for a veneer of non-anachronism.

**Headline findings**
- *Baeddel/baedelism*: **absent** from the book. *Tumblr*: 2 incidental hits (a URL; a list of companies). *Talia Bhatt*: absent. *Talia Mae Bettcher*: present (Keegan ch. 20). *"Third wave"*: present, but it refers to **academic/activist third-wave feminism** (Espineira & Bourcier ch. 4 explicitly derive transfeminism from third-wave feminism; Keegan ch. 20), not the Bluesky/Substack "third wave transfeminism". *t4t*: exactly one hit, in the editors' note to Hirschfeld's Case 13 (1905 letter proposing a registry for "manly women" and "womanly men", glossed as "something like a T4T dating service"). *"Transgender Marxism"* by name: **absent**; Marx/Marxism appear in Haraway (socialist-feminism), Combahee River Collective (socialist Black feminism; "in essential agreement with Marx's theory ... must be extended"), Espineira & Bourcier ("resurgence of Marxism" in Spanish transfeminism), Preciado ("pharmaco-pornopolitical factories" vs. "economical classes in the Marxist sense"), Barad ("a genealogy going back at least to Marx"), Steinbock (commodity fetish), Gramsci via Muñoz.
- Medieval European material is thin: DeVun & Tortorici's note describes their own research "about trans, hermaphroditism, sodomy, and the 'sins against nature' in a medieval (European), early modern (Iberian Atlantic), and colonial (Latin American) contexts" and their bibliography cites Karras & Linkinen "John/Eleanor Rykener Revisited", Mills *Seeing Sodomy in the Middle Ages*, Dinshaw *Getting Medieval*, Strassfeld "Eunuchs and Androgynes in Rabbinic Literature", *TransAntiquity*, and Feinberg's *Transgender Warriors: Making History from Joan of Arc to ...*. The richest premodern material is instead: Iron Age Celtic (Vix), Neolithic Anatolia, Olmec/Maya/Aztec/Chavín (Weismantel ch. 32); Spanish-colonial California joyas/'aqi (Miranda ch. 13); pre-colonial Yoruba and Native American gynecracies (Lugones ch. 12); hijra (Dutta & Roy ch. 15); Assyrian/Roman/18th-c. examples (Stone ch. 1); eunuchs in Islamic harems and Dionysus myth (Raymond ch. 2); Persian "housewifery" and the Khomeini sanction (Najmabadi ch. 35).

## 2. Key theses of the most relevant chapters

### Editors' framing (Foreword, Stryker; Introduction, Blackston)
Stryker frames "trans-ing" as "an exploratory and experimental practice for surviving within, and pointing beyond, the biocentric world order constructed in the wake of European colonization and racial chattel slavery" (L408–410), drawing strength from "the different onto-epistemologies of the premodern past, the reality of Indigenous cosmologies" (L418–419). Blackston proposes trans* as a method rather than an identity ("crossing socially constructed gender boundaries is not the unique property of a 'transgender person'", L683–684) and describes "remixing" as "a survival practice" (L669). Quote: "Transness is a practice of worming one's way through the belly of the beast." (L416–417). Note the explicit attack on "transphobic 'gender-critical' feminist ideology" and the identification of Janice Raymond as "one of the first TERFs" (L534; L749).

### Ch. 1 — Sandy Stone, "The Empire Strikes Back: A Posttranssexual Manifesto" (1991)
Founding text of the field. Stone (a trans woman in the Olivia Records lesbian collective, boycotted by Raymond) argues the medical "transsexual" category forces trans people into "passing" and "constructing a plausible history—learning to lie effectively about one's past" (L1526–1527). She proposes a "posttranssexual" counter-discourse in which trans people refuse erasure, "read oneself aloud", and become "a genre—a set of embodied texts" rather than a class or third gender. Key for transfeminism: she turns Raymond's charge that "transsexuals divide women" into a tool "to multiplicatively divide the old binary discourses of gender" (L1569–1571). Quotes: "I could not ask a transsexual for anything more inconceivable than to forgo passing, to be consciously 'read,' to read oneself aloud—and by this troubling and productive reading, to begin to write oneself into the discourses by which one has been written—in effect, then, to become a (look out—dare I say it again?) posttranssexual." (L1640–1644). "Passing means the denial of mixture." (L1579). "The clinic is a technology of inscription." (L1521).

### Ch. 2 — Janice G. Raymond, "Sappho by Surgery" (1979) [included as the transphobic foil]
Raymond's claim that trans lesbian-feminists are men invading women's space supplies the exact enemy ideology the mod's faith opposes. Useful for in-game "hostile faith" text and, inverted, for precedents: she concedes that eunuchs "has historical precedent" (harem keepers) and that transsexuals "are living and acting out a very ancient myth, that of single parenthood by the father" (Eve from Adam; Dionysus and Athena from Zeus). Quotes: "All transsexuals rape women's bodies by reducing the female form to an artifact, appropriating this body for themselves." (quoted in Stone, L1165–1166). "the transsexually constructed lesbian-feminist is a boundary violator. This violation is also profoundly mythic, for as Norman O. Brown writes of Dionysus, he as the 'mad god who breaks down boundaries.'" (L2086–2088).

### Ch. 3 — Lou Sullivan, "A Transvestite Answers a Feminist" (c. 1973)
Pre-transition gay trans man's rebuttal to second-wave transphobia; editors note he "called himself a 'heterosexual female transvestite' and identified strongly with male drag queens" (L2475–2476) and later convinced gatekeepers that trans people could be gay in their self-perceived gender. Flavor for a transmasculine/gay-trans current inside the faith.

### Ch. 4 — Karine Espineira & Sam Bourcier, "Transfeminism: Something Else, Somewhere Else" (TSQ 2014)
The book's closest thing to a programme for transfeminism as a movement. Traces French (Outrans, Le Zoo) and Spanish (Jornadas Feministas 2000/2009) transfeminismo; explicitly rooted in "third-wave feminism" (Mensah) and Koyama/Serano; coalitional and anti-capitalist; postporn "artivism" against "sexual dualism"; critical of white Anglo queer theory. Quotes: "transfeminism is a major opportunity to build a politics of resistance and alliance, because we consider domination to be a multilayered system that produces cross-oppressions, including transphobia. Our analysis is a feminist one, drawing from third-wave feminism, queer feminism, and postidentity feminism" (Outrans 2012, L2821–2825). Koyama: transfeminism "is 'primarily a movement by and for trans women who view their liberation to be intrinsically linked to the liberation of all women and beyond'" (L2859–2862). Serano: "by necessity, trans activism must be at its core a feminist movement" (L2866–2867). Califia: "'trash the clinic and burn down the beauty parlor'" (L2841). Coalition list: "okupas, lesbians, anticapitalists, maricas, transgender people, and sex workers" (L2919–2920). Marxism: "a blend of Foucaldian biopolitics and feminist materialism rooted in the resurgence of Marxism after the recent violence of the economic crisis" (L2981–2983). Bodies: "reclaiming bodies that matter precisely because they are 'unproductive' bodies capable of becoming bodies of pleasure dedicated to nonreproductive forms of sex" (L3017–3019). Le Zoo trans lesbians: "'We identify as trans because we are doing politics, not because of our transsexualism'" (L2922–2924).

### Ch. 5 — Daniel B. Coleman, "Transmasculine Insurgency" (TSQ 2016)
Asks "Can masculinity be decolonized?"; a transmasculine two-spirit person in Chiapas finds trans healthcare easier in Mexico but is pushed out of feminist circles "inclusive of bio-women only" (L3206); links transphobia to anti-Blackness; draws on Audre Lorde's erotics.

### Ch. 6 — Susan Stryker, "My Words to Victor Frankenstein Above the Village of Chamounix" (1994)
Performance-essay reclaiming "monster" and "creature" and converting exclusion into "transgender rage", a generative force; sacramental language throughout (benediction, rebirth, "anarchic Womb", "enlivening darkness"). Written as a lesbian trans woman in a polyamorous queer family (birth of her partner's daughter). Quotes: "I am a transsexual, and therefore I am a monster." (L3596). "'Monster' is derived from the Latin noun monstrum, 'divine portent,' itself formed on the root of the verb monere, 'to warn.'" (L3626–3627). "the Nature you bedevil me with is a lie. ... You are as constructed as me; the same anarchic Womb has birthed us both." (L3636–3640). "we ally ourselves instead with the chaos and blackness from which Nature itself spills forth." (L4001–4002). Benediction: "May you discover the enlivening power of darkness within yourself. May it nourish your rage. May your rage inform your actions, and your actions transform you as you struggle to transform your world." (L4004–4006). Family: "venturing into the heart of civilization itself to reclaim biological reproduction from heterosexism and free it for our own uses." (L3798–3800). Note 1 records the thesis that the 1970s FTM upsurge responded to lesbian "cultural feminism" suppressing butch-femme, and that this "raised the spectre of male-to-female transsexual lesbians as a particular threat" (L4027–4037).

### Ch. 7 — Marquis Bey, "The Trans*-Ness of Blackness, the Blackness of Trans*-Ness" (TSQ 2017)
Blackness and trans*-ness are "para-ontological" "nodes of one another", "differently inflected names for an anoriginal lawlessness that marks an escape from confinement" (L4290–4291); draws on Moten/Harney's "undercommons", Spillers, Snorton's "transfiguration", Puar's "there is no trans". Quotes: "in the beginning is 'trans'" (Colebrook epigraph, L4180). "To become black is to remain in instability, is to remain in solidarity together in instability." (L4172–4173). "The law can never grab blackness; blackness, in nursery rhyme fashion, is the Gingerbread Man" (L4405–4406). "Blackness, and the liberation of its corporeal bearers, is fueled by its trans* nucleus." (L4373–4374).

### Ch. 8 — Hortense J. Spillers, "Mama's Baby, Papa's Maybe" (1987) [abstract]
Enslavement "un-gendered" Black women, excluding them from the white patrilineal order; Spillers asks them to "gain the insurgent ground" of new personhood (L4801–4802) and (quoted in Bey) for Black men "the power of 'yes' to the 'female' within" (L4347–4348).

### Ch. 9 — Karen Barad, "TransMaterialities" (2015) [abstract + excerpt]
Quantum field theory shows "Nature ... is inherently queer and trans" (L5383–5384); the void is "a lively tension, a desiring orientation toward being/becoming" (Bey n.6, L4758). Barad calls to "build on an already existing radical tradition (a genealogy going back at least to Marx) that troubles nature and its naturalness 'all the way down'" (L5901–5902) and asks "What would it mean to reclaim our trans* natures as natural?" (L5908).

### Ch. 11 — Saylesh Wesley, "Twin-Spirited Woman" (TSQ 2014)
A Stó:lõ trans woman of "royal lineage" in a matriarchal society asks her grandmother (a hereditary "Big Woman", "the sovereign ruler and owner of the Fraser Valley, not unlike a queen" if 500 years earlier, L7158–7160) to dream a Halq'eméylem name for her kind: Sts'iyóye smestíyexw slhá:li, "Twin-Spirited Woman". Grandmothers (Sisele) are "the traditional makers of all laws on our matriarchal lands" (L6821–6822); Coast Salish "stand-up" naming feasts; proposes restoring matriarchal governance so grandmothers can legislate the reclaimed role. Quote: "I am certain the Ancestors have been wondering where we, as two-spirits, have been on 'This Side.'" (L7102–7103). Creator figure: "Xexa:ls (four children of Xa:ls, the Creator/Transformer) have had pity on me." (L7125).

### Ch. 12 — María Lugones, "The Coloniality of Gender" (2008)
Sex/gender binary, heterosexual patriarchy and "biological dimorphism" are the "light side" of a "modern/colonial gender system" imposed through Eurocentric capitalism; pre-colonial Yoruba had "no gender system", Native "gynecratic" tribes assigned gender by "proclivity, inclination, and temperament" and by dreams. Quotes: "'gender was not an organizing principle in Yoruba society prior to colonization by the West.'" (L7458–7459). "Replacing this gynecratic spiritual plurality with one supreme male being as Christianity did, was crucial in subduing the tribes." (L7510–7511). "Race is no more mythical and fictional than gender, both powerful fictions." (L7595).

### Ch. 13 — Deborah A. Miranda, "Extermination of the Joyas" (GLQ 2010)
Spanish "gendercide" of California third-gender joyas ('aqi, "undertaker") by mastiffs, renaming, flogging/"regendering", and replacement of their funerary office with Catholic burial. Argues Two-Spirits are joyas' rightful descendants and sets a six-point programme of reclamation. Quotes: "They are called joyas, and are held in great esteem." (Fages 1775, L7976). "The threshold of death was the realm of the 'aqi, and no California Indian community was safe or complete without that mediator." (L8208–8209). "A Two-Spirit person is born regardless of biological genealogy. Thus we will always be with you. We are you." (L8303–8304).

### Ch. 14 — Gloria Anzaldúa, Borderlands/La Frontera (1987)
Nepantla / borderlands as "a vague and undetermined place created by the emotional residue of an unnatural boundary" inhabited by "Los atravesados ... those who cross over, pass over, or go through the confines of the 'normal.'" (L8611–8616). Poem: "forerunner of a new race, / half and half—both woman and man, neither— / a new gender" (L8650–8652); "To survive the Borderlands / you must live sin fronteras / be a crossroads." (L8686–8688).

### Ch. 15 — Aniruddha Dutta & Raina Roy, "Decolonizing Transgender in India" (TSQ 2014)
"Transgender" works as a colonizing umbrella in NGO/state funding that subsumes hijra/kothi/dhurani as "local" variants and imposes cis/trans, homo/trans binaries and "singular, consistent" identity; hijras deliberately mix identifications (female voter card plus the thikri clap). Calls to "deontologize" transgender and dismantle the tiered political economy of movements. Quotes: hijras "pursue distinct professions such as ritualized blessing during weddings and childbirth" (L8800–8801). "'I went to a house where they mistook me for a woman: I just gave three claps!'" (L9195–9196). "The decolonization of transgender is not likely to be achieved in isolation from the transformation of the political economy of social movements" (L9270–9271).

### Ch. 20 — Cáel M. Keegan, "Getting Disciplined" (2018)
Trans* studies is a "special guest" in women's studies; "subordination" feminisms need "T to stay quiet"; cites Stryker & Bettcher's TSQ "Trans/Feminisms" issue as "a retort to Jeffreys's (2014) ... Gender Hurts", and Bettcher's "trapped in the wrong theory". Quote (Bettcher, n.6): "If all the world's a stage on which we all play a part, trans individuals play actors." (L12272–12273). Quote: "trans* studies might turn to women's studies and say, but—you invited me." (L12023–12024).

### Ch. 25 — Jules Gill-Peterson, "Trans of Color Critique Before Transsexuality" (TSQ 2018)
Johns Hopkins 1930s archive: "Billie", a Black transmasculine patient, refuses vaginectomy ("that's my meal ticket") and thereby interrogates the surgical/heterosexual logic of medicine; transsexuality's mid-century whiteness is built on forgetting Black trans "fugitivity"; Black trans "opacity" and Quashie's "sovereignty of quiet" are counter-histories. Quotes: "'Would you have to remove the vagina? I don't know about that because that's my meal ticket.'" (L14478–14479). "Billie is a fugitive who escapes toward a freedom beyond the boundaries of transsexuality" (L14192–14193). "'silence becomes countermythological'" (Snorton, L14605).

### Ch. 26 — C. Riley Snorton & Jin Haritaworn, "Trans Necropolitics" (2013)
Trans-of-color death (Tyra Hunter 1995) is "a resource" vitalizing white transnormative politics, TDOR, hate-crime law and gentrification; calls for a politics refusing "murderous inclusion". Quotes: "trans death—and most frequently the deaths of trans women or trans-feminine people of color—act as a resource for the development and dissemination of many different agendas." (L14780–14782). "it is in their death that poor and sex working trans people of color are invited back in; it is in death that they suddenly come to matter." (L15177–15178).

### Ch. 27 — Dean Spade, "Trans Law and Politics on a Neoliberal Landscape" (2015)
Rights-based LGBT nonprofitism serves the already-privileged; a "critical trans politics" demands redistribution and abolition. Quote: "A critical trans politics imagines and demands an end to prisons, homelessness, landlords, bosses, immigration enforcement, poverty, and wealth. It imagines a world in which people have what they need and govern themselves in ways that value collectivity, interdependence, and difference." (L15758–15761). Also the chart contrasting "Official Lesbian & Gay Solutions" with "Critical Queer and Trans Political Approaches" (L15640–15700), incl. "abolish inheritance and demand radical redistribution of wealth".

### Ch. 29 — Clare Sears, "Electric Brilliancy" (2008) [abstract/opening]
San Francisco's 1863 ordinance against appearing "in a dress not belonging to his or her sex"; "between 1848 and 1900, thirty-four cities in twenty-one states passed laws against cross-dressing" (L16555–16556), part of policing "problem bodies" out of public space while exhibiting them in freak shows.

### Ch. 30 — Paisley Currah, "Incarceration, Identity Politics, and the Trans-Cis Divide" (2022) [abstract]
Prison "freeze-frame" healthcare policies reflect "the more general relationship between prisons and civil society"; incarceration is "central to the constructions of liberal freedom and the principal of state noninterference in free markets" (L17128–17129).

### Ch. 31 — Leah DeVun & Zeb Tortorici, "Trans, Time, and History" (TSQ 2018)
Proposes "trans*historicities": neither "ancestral essentialism" (Bornstein, Feinberg) nor "radical altericism" (Foucauldian constructionism) but asynchronous, plural, power-aware histories; warns against the "transgender native" trope. Quotes: Bornstein: "'My ancestors were performers. In life. The earliest shamanic rituals involved women and men exchanging genders. Old, old rituals.'" (L17905–17907). Stryker & Aizura: "why we think 'man' and 'woman' are any more transhistorical, or less contingent, than any other category of identity" (L18013–18014). "trans helps us think about time, historical analysis helps us think about trans" (L18025).

### Ch. 32 — Mary Weismantel, "Towards a Transgender Archaeology" (2013)
The archaeological record (Vix, rock art, Çatalhöyük, Olmec/Maya, Chavín, Aztec goddesses, composite burials) does not support a universal two-sex/nuclear-family past; "ungendering" the past undermines the "natural" body; premodern ontologies treat bodies as assembled after birth ("dividuals"). Quotes: "It is as if the premodern past had to wait for transgender scholarship to arrive" (L18519). "the idea of constructing one's body through appropriating and suturing together disparate body parts from diverse origins is not modern at all; many ancient societies would find this way of thinking about oneself completely familiar." (L18711–18713). "To show femininity in this context makes a man more powerful, not less." (L18757–18758).

### Ch. 34 — Paul B. Preciado, "Pharmaco-Pornographic Regime" (2008)
Post-WWII capitalism is "hot, psychotropic and punk"; power now operates by molecules incorporated in the body; "gender" is a 1950s biotech invention; the trans body is paradigmatic; resistance is DIY "gender-copyleft". Quotes: "Techno-bodies are either not-yet-alive or already-dead: we are half foetuses, half zombies. Thus, every politics of resistance is a monster politics." (L19929–19931). "Bio-men and the bio-women ... but also those transsexuals who have access to chirurgical, endocrinological or legal techniques to produce their identity, are not simple economical classes in the Marxist sense of the term, but authentic pharmaco-pornopolitical factories." (L20046–20050). "Your memory, your desire, your sensibility, your skin, your dick, your dildo, your blood, your sperm, your vulva, your gonads, etc. are the tools of a possible gender-copyleft revolution." (L20060–20062).

### Ch. 35 — Afsaneh Najmabadi, "Reading Transsexuality in 'Gay' Tehran (Around 1979)" (2013)
1970s Tehran held a continuum of non-heteronormative maleness (zan-push dancers, khanum parties, "iva-khvahar [o'sister]"); the 1976 Medical Council ban, then the Islamic Republic's gender codes, bifurcated gay and trans; Khomeini's religious sanction made "transsexual, rather than gay ... the more socially acceptable way of being a non-heteronormative male" (L20582–20583). Quotes: "In earlier eras, a male dressing as a woman and opting for a womanly career constituted 'housewifery,' that is, becoming a male kept by a man." (L20268–20269). "She visited Ayatollah Bihbihani, who consulted the Qur'an; it opened on the Maryam chapter." (L20501–20502).

### Ch. 36 — Donna J. Haraway, "A Cyborg Manifesto" (1983)
"an ironic political myth faithful to feminism, socialism, and materialism" built on "blasphemy" (L20786–20787); critiques Marxist/socialist-feminism's essentializing of "labor" while keeping its emphasis on building unities. Quote: "I would rather be a cyborg than a goddess." (L21135). "Marxist/socialist-feminism does not 'naturalize' unity; it is a possible achievement based on a possible standpoint rooted in social relations." (L20999–21000).

### Ch. 37 — Hil Malatino, "Biohacking Gender" (2017)
Two biohackings: posthuman, collective, illicit trans hormone networks vs. transhumanist bourgeois "Bulletproof" self-optimization; cyborgs are "the illegitimate offspring of militarism and patriarchal capitalism"; hormone science is rooted in colonial/eugenic experiments (Rio Piedras pill trials, sterilization of Indigenous women). Quote: "the sexual prostheses, the biomolecular negotiations we go through as we create alternative ways of being gendered, the communities of emotional and financial support we form to aid each other through transition" (L21675–21677). "a faith in prefigurative politics: the idea that a new world can be built in the shell of the old" (L21809–21810).

### Ch. 40 — Abram J. Lewis, "Trans Animisms" (2017)

**Tension notes for the designer**
- Rows 6/7/22 (adoptive matriline, houses) sit against row 20 (abolish inheritance); treat #20 as a heresy/alternate doctrine.
- Row 1 ("born among us") versus row 3 ("severed are women"): the sources support *both* a spontaneously arising third gender (joyas, two-spirit) and a *made* category by castration (hijra chhibrano; Elbe). ## 5. Historical, premodern and non-Western precedents (exhaustive for this book)

Ordered roughly by period/region. Each item: precedent, verbatim quote, chapter, line numbers. A verdict per mechanic follows in §5.8. Reminder: the book itself warns against "ancestral essentialism" and the "transgender native" trope (DeVun & Tortorici, L17938–17940; L17915–17917); the designer should present these as *resonances* for flavor text, not lineal ancestry.

### 5.1 Prehistory and deep antiquity (Weismantel ch. 32; Stone ch. 1; Raymond ch. 2)
1. **Palaeolithic/Indigenous rock art, non-binary genitals.** "Carved and painted images on caves and cliffs from Australia to Colorado, some of them tens of thousands of years old ... 'With surprising frequency, one encounters figures with something fancy between the legs that can't readily be assigned to one of two categories, neither penis nor vagina' (2004: 15–16)." (Weismantel, L18511–18516)
2. **Çatalhöyük (Anatolia, 7500–5700 BCE): unsexed human form; egalitarian non-differentiation; composite burials.** Meskell: "'[W]hat seems to have been most salient at Čatalhöyük was . . . not a specifically gendered person with discrete sexual markers, but an [unsexed] . . . human form.'" (L18617–18619). "its egalitarian residents refused to differentiate by gender, in life or in death" (L18690–18691). Burials: "the grave of a woman at Čatalhöyük who cradles a man's head in her lap, or another young female whose own head has been replaced with that of a man who died long after her." (L18819–18821)
3. **Vix, Iron Age Celtic France, c. 450 BCE: possibly intersex ruler/priest.** Knüdson's list of readings: "'nomad princess' . . . a 'lady' . . . a 'man who did not mind wearing women's clothing' . . . a 'transvestite priest' . . . a 'rich woman and possibly a chief or tribal ruler' . . . [or] an 'honorary male.'" (L18417–18420). Weismantel: "Was the body buried at Vix, for example, a woman who attained a lordly political status usually reserved for men? Or an intersex individual who attained the status of a religious leader or shaman, partly because of her unusual body, which was small, asymmetrical, and mostly but not entirely female?" (L18442–18445). Grave goods: "the bronze-studded chariot that rolled the bier into the grave at Vix, and the accompanying krater (a bronze vessel imported from Greece), the largest and fanciest ever found in the Celtic world—four feet in diameter and five feet tall, and entirely filled with mead" (L18449–18452); "the enormous gold torc ... adorned with winged horses, lion's paws, and poppies" (L18453–18455). **Directly usable: a Celtic-area ruler of ambiguous sex with a mead-filled krater, 867 start adjacent in geography if not date.**
4. **Classical Greece: Hermaphroditus cult vs. execution of intersex women.** "the same society that produced those statues also put women to death by public drowning or burning alive if their bodies were determined by the authorities to be partly male (Ajootian 1995: 102–103)." (L18471–18475)
5. **Ancient European figurines with mixed traits.** "a woman/centaur, a man seated on a birthing stool, bodies with a penis and breasts (Hamilton 2000)." (L18741–18742)
6. **Assyria: Sardanapalus.** Stone: "The earliest mention of something which we can recognize ex post facto as transsexualism ... was of the Assyrian king Sardanapalus, who was reported to have dressed in women's clothing and spun with his wives." (L1118–1120)
7. **Roman Empire: Philo of Judea.** "Later instances of something very like transsexualism were reported by Philo of Judea, during the Roman Empire." (L1120–1122)
8. **Greek myth of male self-birth (Dionysus from Zeus's thigh).** Raymond (foil): "Transsexuals are living and acting out a very ancient myth, that of single parenthood by the father ... Eve was born of Adam; Dionysus and Athena were born of Zeus" (L2023–2025); "Hermes saves the six-month fetal Dionysus, sews him upon Zeus's thigh, and after three more months, Zeus 'births' him." (L2050–2051); Dionysus "the 'mad god who breaks down boundaries'" (L2087–2088). Invertible as a faith myth of self-rebirth.
9. **Monstrum as divine portent (Roman).** Stryker: "the ancients considered the appearance of such beings to be a sign of some impending supernatural event. Monsters, like angels, functioned as messengers and heralds of the extraordinary." (L3629–3630)
10. **Priests of the Goddess (galli) — bibliographic only.** Stryker's works-cited lists Will Roscoe, "Priests of the Goddess: Gender Transgression in the Ancient World" (AHA paper, 1994) (L4130–4131). The galli/Cybele themselves are never discussed (§3.9). Also bibliographic: *TransAntiquity: Cross-Dressing and Transgender Dynamics in the Ancient World* (L18267–18268); Strassfeld, "Classically Queer: Eunuchs and Androgynes in Rabbinic Literature" (L18366–18367).

11. **Olmec Las Limas figure (gender-ambiguous "Virgin"/"Lord").** "The children and their parents initially worshipped this image as the miraculous 'Virgin of Las Limas' ... The experts re-named the statue the 'Lord' of Las Limas" (L18653–18657).

### 5.3 Native North America, colonial contact (Miranda ch. 13; Lugones ch. 12; Wesley ch. 11; Stone)
19. **Chumash/Californian joyas / 'aqi: transfeminine undertakers, "held in great esteem", two or three per village, raised as girls, married to men.** Fages 1775: "those Indian men who ... are observed in the dress, clothing, and character of women—there being two or three such in each village—pass as sodomites by profession ... Mission San Antonio 1776/7: "The pagan replied that that joya was his wife" (L8113). Fages: joyas accompany women gathering seeds with the little girls (L8099–8103).
20. **Joya mortuary monopoly and the afterlife mediated by male and female powers.** "In California, death, burial, and mourning rituals were the exclusive province of the joyas; they were the undertakers of their communities. As the only members of California Indian communities who possessed the necessary training to touch the dead or handle burials without endangering themselves or the community" (L8188–8191); "The journey to the afterlife was known to be a prescribed series of experiences with both male and female supernatural entities, and the 'aqi, with their male-female liminality, were the only people who could mediate these experiences." (L8200–8203); "Baskets used to scoop up the earth of a grave, for example, were given to the 'aqi by the deceased person's relatives as partial payment for burial services" (L8207–8209); "The threshold of death was the realm of the 'aqi, and no California Indian community was safe or complete without that mediator." (L8211–8212)
21. **'Aqi status shared by post-menopausal women; the role passed mother to daughter; adoptive "daughters".** "Asserting that undertakers were exclusively 'aqi or postmenopausal women (also called 'aqi), Hollimon speculates that perhaps 'the mediation between death and the afterlife ... was entrusted by the Chumash to individuals who could not be harmed by symbolic pollution of the corpse, and who were no longer (or never had been) capable of giving birth.'" (L8212–8216). Solares (1930s): "all undertakers ('aqi') were women, strong enough to carry bodies and dig deep graves, and that the role was passed from mother to daughter." (L8232–8234). N.37: "Hollimon suggests that 'daughters' of male-bodied 'aqi were probably fictive kinships (such as adoption) formed with other members of the same guild or role or premenopausal children of women who took up the 'aqi role late in life" (L8471–8474).
22. **Joya sexual rule: receptive with "normative" men; never with another joya.** "joyas were sexually active with 'normative' men as the recipients of anal sex. In fact, a joya would never consider having sex with another joya—this was not forbidden, simply unthinkable" (L8149–8152).
23. **Spanish gendercide by mastiffs; Balboa 1513.** "Shouting the command 'Tómalos!' (take them, or sic'em), the Spanish soldiers ordered execution of joyas by specially bred mastiffs and greyhounds." (L7998–8000); Balboa "came on about forty indigenous men, all dressed as women, engaged in what he called 'preposterous Venus.' He commanded his men to give the men as 'a prey to his dogges,' and the men were torn apart alive." (L8010–8012). Punishments: "flogging with a leather whip ... time in the stocks, and corma" (L8168–8169); "In a kind of involuntary gender-reassignment, joyas were made to dress as men" (L8174). Catholic burial "usurped the traditional responsibilities of the 'aqi" (L8195–8197). **Usable as the hostile-faith (Catholic) interaction set.**
24. **Spanish lexicon for the "sin against nature".** "'el acto pecado nefando' ('the silent/unspoken sin') and its participants (hermafrodita, sodomía, bujarrón, nefandario, maricón, amujerado)" (L8094–8096).
25. **Two-Spirit definition and survival.** "Two-Spirit people are 'Aboriginal people who possess the sacred gifts of the female-male spirit, which exists in harmony with those of the female and the male.'" (L8367–8370). Gilley: "'The institution of the third gender [in Native American precontact societies] was less about an individual's sexuality and more about the ways their special qualities were incorporated into the social and religious life of their community'" (L8386–8388). Adult joya baptisms as late as 1832 (L8255–8257). Mentorship: "In traditional times, there would have been older joyas to guide inexperienced ones; there would have been ceremony, role modeling, community support" (L8333–8335).
26. **Gynecratic tribes; female primacy; dual chiefs; gender by dream.** Lugones: "Many American Indian tribes 'thought that the primary potency in the universe was female' ... Old Spider Woman, Corn Woman, Serpent Woman, Thought Woman are some of the names of powerful creators. For the gynecratic tribes, Woman is at the center and 'no thing is sacred without her blessing, her thinking.'" (L7505–7509); "Replacing this gynecratic spiritual plurality with one supreme male being as Christianity did, was crucial in subduing the tribes." (L7510–7511); internal female chief/external male chief (L7514–7518); "The Yuma had a tradition of gender designation based on dreams; a female who dreamed of weapons became a male for all practical purposes." (L7519–7521); gynecratic list: "Susquehanna, Hurons, Iroquois, Cherokee, Pueblo, Navajo, Narragansett, Coastal Algonkians, Montagnais" (L7550–7552); "among the eighty-eight tribes that recognized homosexuality" a long list incl. Aztec and Maya (L7552–7557).
27. **Cherokee and Iroquois matriarchies dismantled under colonial pressure.** "Cherokee women had had the power to wage war, to decide the fate of captives, to speak to the men's council ... The Women's Council was politically and spiritually powerful." (L7540–7543); "The Iroquois shifted from a Mother-centered, Mother-right people organized politically under the authority of the Matrons, to a patriarchal society when the Iroquois became a subject people." (L7544–7546); 1800s Cherokee constitution "relegated women to the position of chattel" (L7535–7536).
28. **Colonizers imagined Natives as hermaphrodites; intersex recognised without correction.** "Sexual fears of colonizers led them to imagine the indigenous people of the Americas as hermaphrodites or intersexed, with large penises and breasts with flowing milk. But as Gunn Allen and others make clear, intersexed individuals were recognized in many tribal societies prior to colonization without assimilation to the sexual binary." (L7436–7440)
29. **Stó:lõ/Coast Salish matrilineal law, naming feasts, hereditary "Big Woman".** "the Sisele, that as the traditional makers of all laws on our matriarchal lands" (L6833); "As both the Stó:lõ and Tsimshian are matrilineal, it goes without saying that I am to identify as Stó:lõ even though my blood is a blend of the two, and to this day, she maintains political jurisdiction over me." (L6899–6901); "Coast Salish nations traditionally hold ceremonial gatherings to 'stand-up' ones who are receiving such names or honors, and as our systems of passing knowledge and title down are matrilineal, only the eldest woman can legitimize this sort of work." (L7055–7057); "if we went back in time five hundred years, she would be the sovereign ruler and owner of the Fraser Valley, not unlike a queen." (L7156–7157). ### 5.5 South Asia: hijra and kothi (Dutta & Roy ch. 15)
33. **Hijra: structured clans, ritual blessing profession, optional castration-penectomy, self-designation as neither man nor woman.** "Hijra is a well-known term connoting a structured community of feminine-identified persons who pursue distinct professions such as ritualized blessing during weddings and childbirth; hijras typically dress in women's clothes and may undergo penectomy and castration (orchiectomy) but also commonly designate themselves as distinct from men and women (Reddy 2005: 134; Nanda 1990)." ### 5.8 Verdicts: which precedents STRENGTHEN or WEAKEN each planned mechanic

**(a) Castrated men regarded as women**
- STRENGTHEN: Lili Elbe's castration admits her to a women-only clinic (Stone, L1351–1352). Raymond's hostile account concedes self-castration = "given up the supposed ultimate possession of manhood" (L2171–2172). Chumash 'aqi grouped male-bodied joyas with post-menopausal women as a single category defined by non-reproduction, not anatomy (L8212–8216); joyas raised "in all the duties of the women" and taken as "wife" (L8122–8127; L8113). Persian "housewifery" (L20268–20269). Khomeini's ruling: after surgery "she must live her life as a woman" (L20506). Weismantel: femininity increases male power in Pre-Columbian ontology (L18758–18759).
- WEAKEN: Hijras *elect* castration yet "designate themselves as distinct from men and women" and do not wish to pass (L8801–8802; L9110–9114) — the strongest real castration-culture in the book is a *third* gender, not womanhood. Raymond's harem eunuchs are "men that other more powerful men used to keep their women in place" (L2017), i.e. remained men socially. Stone notes castration has no effect on voice (L1275–1277), so "passing by castration" is a fiction. Joyas were not described as castrated at all. Design implication: make "counted among women" an explicit *legal/doctrinal* choice of the faith (a doctrine), not a claim that castration naturally feminises.

**(b) Adoptive / matrilineal heirship**
- STRENGTHEN: Hollimon's 'aqi "daughters" as fictive kin by adoption within the guild (L8471–8474) and the office "passed from mother to daughter" (L8234) — a near-exact model of adoptive matrilineal succession to a transfem office. Stó:lõ/Tsimshian matrilineal title and law, grandmother's "political jurisdiction" (L6899–6901; L7056–7057; L7155–7157). Iroquois "Mother-right ... authority of the Matrons" and Cherokee Women's Council (L7540–7546). Ballroom houses led by "mothers" with non-blood children (L24516–24522). Miranda: joyas "did not cease to be born" regardless of genealogy (L8289–8304), so an *adoptive* line is the only way to keep a transfem succession.
- WEAKEN: Spillers shows "Mother Right" could be denied and "matriarchy" misnamed under slavery (L5275–5281), a caution against treating matriliny as automatically liberatory. Spade's critical trans politics would "abolish inheritance" altogether (L15689). Lugones/Oyěwùmí: the Yoruba case had *no* gender principle, so neither matriliny nor patriliny (L7458–7460). DeVun & Tortorici warn against "ancestral essentialism" (L17938–17940). No European medieval adoptive-matrilineal precedent appears in the book.

**(c) Women-only / transfeminine priesthood**
- STRENGTHEN: 'aqi mortuary priesthood restricted to transfem joyas and post-menopausal women — "no California Indian community was safe or complete without that mediator" (L8211–8212; L8188–8191). Gynecratic principle "no thing is sacred without her blessing" (L7508–7509) and the "internal female chief" (L7514–7517). Vix as possible intersex "religious leader or shaman" / "transvestite priest" (L18444; L18419). Hijra ritual blessing profession (L8800–8801). Koyama: transfeminism "primarily a movement by and for trans women" (L2859–2862). Wesley: grandmothers legislate and legitimate names (L6833; L7056–7057).
- WEAKEN: Many of these are *third-gender* or *mixed* offices (joyas plus elder women; hijra as neither/nor), not "women-only" in a binary sense; the book repeatedly rejects the "third gender" label as a universal (Towle & Morgan, L18373–18374; Dutta & Roy, L8763–8766). Raymond's eunuchs served male rulers, not a female cult. Combahee rejects "queenhood" and pedestals (L27266), and Haraway prefers cyborg to goddess (L21135) — i.e. the transfeminist current itself is wary of goddess-priestess essentialism. Galli/Cybele absent.

**(d) Castration as a sacred rite**
- STRENGTHEN (weakly): Hijra castration-penectomy (chhibrano) as community-embedded body practice tied to a sacred blessing profession (L8801; L9130–9131). Stone's baptismal gloss of transition as "putting on" and Sahagún's priest in a woman's flayed skin (L1760–1766). Hayward: the cut as "generative enactment of 'grow[ing] back'" (L22779–22781). Weismantel: bodies "assembled rather than birthed" in premodern ontology (L18712–18714; L18098–18100). Raymond's Dionysus sewn into Zeus's thigh as self-rebirth (L2050–2054). Khomeini's religious *permission* (L20505–20506).
- WEAKEN: The book contains **no** account of castration as a *religious* initiation (no galli, no Cybele, no Attis, no Skoptsy, no Byzantine or Chinese court eunuchs). Raymond's eunuchs are administrative, Dutta & Roy's hijra castration is described in secular/subcultural terms (NGO, surgery hierarchy), Stone treats Elbe's castration as medical. Preciado/Malatino frame body modification as pharmacological and DIY, not liturgical. Expect to import the galli model from outside this source (Roscoe's "Priests of the Goddess" is the pointer the book gives, L4130–4131).

**Most defensible "veneer" package from this book alone:** a transfem + post-reproductive-women mortuary/blessing priesthood (joya + hijra), adoptive mother-to-daughter transmission of that office (Hollimon), grandmother-legislators and a female "Big Woman" sovereign (Wesley), gender assigned by dream/temperament (Lugones), and a Celtic-adjacent intersex ruler-priest burial with a mead krater (Vix) for 867-era Frankish/Celtic flavor; plus Catholic-style persecution scripts (mastiffs, flogging, regendering, renaming, burial-rite replacement) for the hostile faith.
