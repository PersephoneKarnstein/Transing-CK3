**Scope and bias.** A history of Sogdian long-distance trade from its origins to its end around 930–1000. It is strongest on the Chinese documents (Turfan, Dunhuang, epitaphs) and on the Türk and Uighur connections. It rests heavily on Pulleyblank for An Lushan and on Rong Xinjiang and Trombert for the communities in China. For the period after 867 the evidence is thin, and the author says so (pp. 309, 321, 335).

**Relation to other notes.** Read with `cn_rong_eighteen_lectures.md` (Shazhou 786–1036, the *she*, the Zoroastrian cult as a Guiyijun folk festival) and `cn_hao_she_associations.md` (the *she* bylaws, dues and circulars). This book adds that **Sogdian families sat inside the Dunhuang *she*** (a circular of 973, p. 324), that **Sogdian functionaries clustered at Changle/Guazhou** under the Guiyijun (p. 324), and the long Chinese model of a **foreign community under a head of its own whom the state ranks as an official** (*sabao*, pp. 147–157).

**Relevance:** high for Yutai Dao (Dunhuang communities, the *sabao*, the community-to-office path, the 755 purge and name-hiding, the 9th–10th-c. aftermath), and for the general shape of a merchant diaspora. Moderate for women (the Ancient Letters). Low for the other five faiths.

## 1. The Ancient Letters (c. 313): a network, a family and a woman stranded at Dunhuang

- **The find.** A group of letters found "still sealed" in 1907 by Stein, in a rubbish heap in a ruined Han guard tower 90 km west of Dunhuang (p. 45). Dated to 313 from the sack of Luoyang (pp. 45–46).
- **A private post.** The letters came from different towns (Dunhuang, Wuwei, the Gansu corridor) but travelled together. Mail passed "from one Sogdian community to another, while excluding the other ethnic communities" (p. 47). Addresses were standardised. Letter II, for Samarkand, was doubly wrapped in linen and silk, and was probably to be passed on at Loulan (pp. 47–48). The post was faster than the goods (p. 48).
- **Three tiers in Letter II.** Varzakk at Samarkand; Nanai-vandak, manager of the network in Gansu and China; local agents settled in Jiuquan, Wuwei and elsewhere. Nanai-vandak's branch was in practice autonomous, and the letter counts persons, not profits (pp. 48–50).
- **What Letter II says about the communities.** "A hundred freemen from Samarkand" in one town of China and forty men in another (p. 44; numbers discussed p. 56). The Indians and Sogdians at Luoyang "had all died of starvation" (p. 44). The writer and his people in the Dunhuang–Lanzhou region describe themselves: "we are without family, old and on the point of death" (p. 44). A deposit is to be kept sealed, its interest added, and a share kept for an orphan who has "no hope of anything other than this money"; the orphan is to be given a wife and kept close (pp. 44–45). Musk is split into five shares: three for the son, one for a partner, one for the guardian (p. 45).
- **Letter V** is from a small trader stranded at Wuwei to "the chief merchant Aspandhāt" (pp. 50–51). "Many Sogdians were ready to leave, (but) they could not leave" (p. 51). The address word for chief merchant is *s'rtp'w*, the *sartapao* (p. 151).
- **Women in the letters (Miunai).** The book spells her **Miunai** (the brief's "Miwnay" is Sims-Williams's later spelling; the book does not use it).
  - Letters I and III were "written by a woman, Miunai", the first to her mother Catisa, the second to her husband Nanai-dhat, with a postscript from her daughter (p. 47).
  - Her mother's name is attested in Loulan documents, so the daughter of a local (Indian or indianised) woman married a Sogdian (p. 76).
  - Vaissière's reading: she is under "a double guardianship", of her husband and of the family council, and has no resources of her own. "But she could possibly travel alone" (p. 56). Letter III may be in two hands, which "could argue in favor of female literacy" (p. 56).
  - A priest promised her a camel so that she could join a caravan to Loulan, and the caravans were large enough to protect a woman travelling alone (p. 188).
  - The worst fate for "a Sogdian woman abandoned at Dunhuang" was to have to "learn Chinese manners and enter into service with the Chinese" (p. 57). Henning's lines quoted in the note: Farnxunt has absconded and the Chinese have not found him; "through the guilt of Farnxunt (or 'through his debts') we have become the servants of the Chinamen, I as well as my mother" (p. 57 n. 37).
  - A further unedited fragment (LM II ii 09) is "written by a woman" and speaks of comings and goings and of selling (p. 45 n. 3; p. 56 n. 32: women "are not excluded from economic activity").
- **Community organs at Dunhuang c. 313.** A priest (Letter I), "authorities" (*'yps'r*) and a "tax collector" (*b'zkr'm*), read as the community's own officers (pp. 56, 147). A community "with women, children and a community structure" in many towns (p. 60).
- **Hostility.** Chinese frontier companies were named "who devour the hu", "who crush the hu" and so on (p. 57). The Sogdians heard the news of China only through their own channels (p. 57).

## 2. Community heads: from *sartapao* to *sabao*, and from *sabao* to township

- **The earliest community heads.** In 227 the "hu lords" of the Yuezhi and the Sogdians at Liangzhou "fought to be the first" to welcome an invading Chinese army (pp. 60–61, 147). The community's head negotiated with the conqueror.
- **The word.** *Sabao* transcribes the Sogdian *sartapao*, from Sanskrit *sārthavāha*, "head of the caravan", and by extension "head of the guild of merchants" (p. 151). Vaissière glosses it "Master of the Caravan" (p. 151). Buddhist texts used *sabo* for bodhisattvas as guides on the way (p. 151).
- **The Chinese office.**
  - Under the Northern Qi (550–577) two *safu* sat at the capital and one in every region (p. 149).
  - Under the Sui there was a *sabao* for every Hu community "including more than 200 households", in 30 grades (p. 149).
  - The Tang bureau (Tongdian, 800) was headed by the *sabao* and the head of the *xian* (Zoroastrian) cult, with an invoker, a duty officer and a scribe-archivist (pp. 148–149).
  - Mandarin rank for the head of a village-sized community was "quite exceptional" and shows the communities' economic weight (p. 152).
- **Recruited from the community.** "Each *nāf* was under the jurisdiction of a *sartapao*, who also presided over the cult", with a hierarchy below him (p. 151). *Nāf* means "community", from the family group up to a city's citizens (p. 148). Families of the *sabao* of Wuwei held the post for three generations in the 6th c. (p. 150). A Sogdian tomb at Xi'an gives the title "sabao of Wuwei" in Sogdian and Chinese (p. 150). Some families may have turned "modest caravaneer ancestors into functionaries" retrospectively (p. 150).
- **The parallel at Canton.** In the 9th c. the Chinese ruler gave a Muslim "the administration of justice" among his co-religionists at Canton (p. 150).
- **The end of the *sabao* system.** The last known *sabao* held office before 646 (p. 152). At Dunhuang and Turfan in the 8th c., Sogdians lived instead in **"rallied" townships**, Conghua and Chonghua (both mean "rallied" to China; Vaissière compares naturalisation). Their people were full subjects with the same rights and duties as the Chinese (pp. 152–153): "from an administration by individuals of the community to direct administration by the state" (p. 153).
  - **Conghua at Dunhuang** had about 300 households (some 1,400 people), and the majority were Sogdian farmers on the corvée register of 751 (p. 130 and n. 51).
  - A Sogdian temple stood in the village of Ancheng in Conghua township (p. 129).
- **Assimilation in a generation.** In the 751 register, 10 of 13 people over sixty had Sogdian given names, and none aged 17–20 had one. Fathers with Sogdian names mostly gave Chinese ones to their sons (pp. 153–154). Yet 19 of 21 known marriages of Sogdians in China, 580–650, were within the *hu* communities (p. 154).
- **Temples.**
  - The Dunhuang *xian* temple stood one *li* east of the town, with a painting, twenty niches and a courtyard of a hundred paces (P. 2005, mid-8th c., p. 128). Offerings there are described in 9th–10th-c. texts "perhaps up to the year 964" (pp. 128–129).
  - No Sogdian temple has been excavated at Dunhuang; the site was probably washed away (p. 127).
  - At Hami a *xian* temple had "countless images", and its leader had visited the Chinese court (p. 129).
  - Three of the five Sogdian temples of Chang'an stood by the western market (pp. 139–140).
  - Under the Guiyijun the Chinese administration **supplied the Sogdian temple at Dunhuang** with alcohol and drawing paper, probably for images carried in procession, as it did for Chinese cults (p. 325).
- **Associations.** In 689 the perfumers' association (*Xianghang she*) of Luoyang dedicated Longmen cave 1410. Its president, secretary and three other members bore Sogdian names, out of nineteen (p. 141 and n. 91).

## 3. Merchants, law and the shape of a diaspora company

- **A family firm.** The basic unit was the extended family, with "a head of the clan and a family council" (p. 56). Upper Indus graffiti show Sogdians travelling in family groups across three generations (pp. 166–167). The "possible Sogdian commercial companies" of the golden age are unknown; no document shows how great and small merchants related (p. 167; again p. 335).
- **Status.** Foreign observers agree that the Sogdians were merchants. Asked whether the Sogdians were braver than his own people, the poet Bashshar told the caliph: "No, the Sogdians are merchants" (p. 161). Sogdian art shows an aristocratic self-image; at Panjikent the merchants' banquet has a purse where the nobles wear swords (p. 162). The Kashkathān of Bukhara were honoured and rich traders "of foreign origin", not *dihqāns* (p. 163).
- **The city as a legal person.** The *nāf* of Panjikent leased the toll of a bridge to two men for 150 drachms a year, paid in advance (p. 168). Coins were struck in the name of the *nāf* (p. 168). The king contracted "as a simple individual" (p. 168). Paykent, "the city of merchants", names no sovereign (p. 169).
- **Status in China.** Resident Sogdians were on the registers and owed corvée, and merchants among them owed the "merchant corvée". "Authorised hu" (*xing hu*) and "merchant hu" (*shang hu*) were travelling foreigners who needed permits and paid trade taxes but no corvée. Residents witnessed and guaranteed their contracts (pp. 131–132).
- **Dominance.** In a Turfan customs register of c. 610–620, 29 of 35 transactions involve at least one Sogdian, in gold, silver, perfume, saffron, brass, medicines, ammonia and silk (pp. 133–134).
- **Not only merchants.** Sogdians at Turfan were farmers, leather workers, innkeepers, banner painters, vintners, bronze-smiths, tanners, functionaries and "nailers of camels' feet" (p. 130). The 8th-c. Dunhuang brewer An Hudaofen supplied the local administration (p. 131).
- **Caravans.** Business documents show small caravans of "at the most some forty people". Kuchean travel permits of 641–644 list caravans such as "6 men, 10 women and 4 donkeys" (p. 187). Large caravans formed only to cross deserts or war zones (p. 188).
- **Hospitality as the inn.** The Sogdians had no caravanserai of their own. Their word for "inn" was borrowed from Chinese (p. 191). Ibn Ḥawqal (10th c.) says landowners in Transoxiana competed to lodge travellers, and spent their fortunes on hospices, roads, bridges and endowments (pp. 191–192). Vaissière reads the great courtyards of the castles as the lodging places (pp. 192–193).
- **Moneylending.** At Merv in 699 Sogdian merchants lent to the Arab army and had a lieutenant who broke his contract imprisoned (p. 274). In Chang'an in the late 8th and early 9th c. Sogdian moneylenders "became the dominant moneylenders" and the government limited debts owed to them (p. 139). *This is Sogdian, not Jewish; see §9 on guardrails.*

## 4. Soldiers, colonists and the path from community to office

- **Noble colonists.** In 627–649 "a great leader" of Samarkand, Kang Yandian, settled at Shanshan (Lop Nor) with other *hu*. He built the "New Town", the "Town of the Grape" (he planted vines inside it) and the town of Sapi near the Tibetan and Tuyuhun routes. In 675 the place became the fortified Town of Stone, joined to Shazhou. At the end of the 7th c. its governor still bore a Sogdian name (pp. 123–124, from a Dunhuang text of 885). The colonies of Semirech'e and Lop Nor were "of noble origin" and largely agricultural (pp. 124, 164).
- **Settled by the state.** The Tang settled merchant *hu* (*shang hu*) at Yingzhou, its northeastern frontier stronghold, when it was re-founded in 717 (pp. 143, 217). Vaissière sees "a deliberate Tang policy" of placing foreign merchants in frontier military zones (p. 143).
- **Army auxiliaries.** Sogdians served the army "as auxiliaries" in the northwest and northeast (p. 187). Caravaneers who had lost their trade to the state's silk transports were later used by the army for transport (p. 155).
- **From *sabao* to office: dated family paths.**
  - "The milieu of the Central Asian *sabao* was a recruiting area for the administration and above all for the Tang army" (p. 155; again p. 213).
  - **Kang Po (573–647).** His father was *sabao* of Dingzhou under the Sui, then head of a department of the imperial household. Kang Po inherited his titles, grew rich, and under the Tang directed farm works in a prince's fief (pp. 155–156).
  - **Kang Yuanjing (stele of 673).** His father was "Grand Sabao of All China", then a general. Yuanjing followed the Tang to power and was made a duke (p. 156).
  - **An Jia (518–579).** He was *sabao* of Tongzhou in the capital area under the Zhou, then governor general (pp. 156–157).
  - **The Shi of Guyuan (seven tombs).** The great-grandfather and grandfather were *sabao*. Shi Shewu made a career in the Sui army (d. 610). His son Shi Hedan was the first known Tang court interpreter (d. 669). A nephew and a cousin ran imperial stud farms (d. 666, 678). They still married Sogdians after five generations (pp. 211–213).
- **The six *hu* counties (679).** The Ordos "hu park" was organised in 679 as six counties of Sogdo-Türk families who bred horses for the Tang army. Kang Sumi surrendered in 630 and was made a governor in the Ordos. An Tuhan "surrendered with 5,000 men and was made prefect" (p. 214). They revolted in 721 under leaders with Sogdian names, and Sogdians in Tang service put the revolt down (p. 214). The Türks called the area "Sogdians of the six districts" (p. 214).

## 5. The An Lushan rebellion (755–763) and after

- **An Lushan.** Born 703 to a Sogdian officer in the Türk armies and a Türk mother. He worked in the frontier markets of the northeast, said to be a commercial interpreter. He became governor of Pinglu in 742, revolted in December 755, took Chang'an in July 756, and was killed by his son in January 757. The revolt ended in January 763 (pp. 215–216).
- **The merchant network behind the revolt.** The History of An Lushan says western merchants set up markets in secret in the districts. When they came, An Lushan sat in *hu* dress among burning incense while the *hu* prostrated themselves; animals were sacrificed, and "The sorceresses beat the drums, danced and sang." He then sent the *hu* to sell silks and insignia in secret to build a war chest, "for eight or nine years" (p. 218). His appeal to the Türk-Khotanese general Geshu Han rested on shared *hu* birth (p. 218). Sogdian cavalry called *chākar* (Chinese *zhijie*) rode a thousand strong (p. 219). His successor Shi Siming styled himself both Chinese emperor and Sogdian "Emperor Jamūk" ("Jewel") (p. 219).
- **Retaliation.** As the Tang retook the cities, they ordered massacres of the *hu*; at Beijing people were killed for having large noses "in error" (p. 220). Elite attitudes turned "largely xenophobic" (p. 220). *Not usable as event content (§9).*
- **Hiding the name.** "The hu began to hide their origins" (p. 220).
  - In 756 the Li Baoyu family changed its surname from An to Li, retroactively over four generations (p. 221).
  - Others claimed to come from Guiji, a famous southern city whose name was also the old name of Changle near Dunhuang, thought to be An Lushan's ancestral home (p. 221).
  - He Hongjing (805–865), military governor of Hebei, had a Kang mother and an An wife but claimed to come from Lujiang near Nanjing (p. 222).
  - Families whose loyalty was proven kept their Sogdian descent openly, like He Wenzhe (764–830), whose father helped the Tang against "the traitorous bandit Lushan" (p. 222).
  - Result: "very rapidly, the Sogdians disappear from the sources" (p. 222).
- **Uighur protection.** The Uighur qaghan's conversion to Manichaeism at Luoyang in 762–763, through Sogdians, made the Uighurs "protectors of the hu in China". When their power fell in 840, "the persecutions resumed" (p. 223). Inside the Uighur state a hostile faction killed some 2,000 people in 779–789, including Sogdians (pp. 224–225).
- **Uighur-era trade.** About a thousand Sogdians who came with Uighur missions stayed at Chang'an and grew rich (p. 308). An Uighur caravan of 780 carried Uighurs, Sogdians and "their Chinese concubines", with over 100,000 rolls of silk (p. 308). The Tang bought horses at 38–40 rolls each (p. 308), about 7,500 a year for 300,000 rolls (pp. 308–309).

## 6. The communities by 867 and in the 10th century

- **Dunhuang went back to barter.** After the 760s the Tibetans took the garrisons one after another, and coins vanished from Dunhuang contracts (p. 307). Contracts were in grain from the late 8th to the early 10th c., then in cloth, wool and silk (p. 320).
- **The communities persisted without immigration.** At Dunhuang "what is striking here is not the disintegration of the Sogdian communities, but to the contrary, their continuation right into the 10th century" (p. 323).
  - Sogdians of Conghua appear among the semi-serf households of the Buddhist monasteries in a corvée register of 818–823. They "continued to marry among each other" (pp. 323–324).
  - **A *she* circular of 973** calls members to the year-end banquet "at the inn of the Cao family". A third of its fifteen members, including the head and the assessor, were An or Cao (p. 324; S. 2894v).
  - Under Tibet (787–848), Sogdian names appear among functionaries and clergy (p. 324).
  - **c. 874/879 (inside the game's opening years):** a Guiyijun mission to China had Chinese ambassadors but four Sogdians among its managerial officers: Kang Wensheng, Kang Shuda, Cao Guangjin and An Zaisheng (p. 324 and n. 121; P. 3547).
  - Sogdian functionaries of every rank were concentrated at **Changle**, the very place Sogdians in inner China tried to distance themselves from, suggesting "a settlement which was still structured by connections of solidarity" (p. 324). A Sogdian wool-cloth account shows a Sogdian merchant trading at Changle (p. 324).
  - A Sogdian letter of c. 884 from a sovereign to a Christian cleric reports diplomatic news from Ganzhou (p. 324).
- **Diplomats to the end.** Of 53 Uighur ambassadors to China in 907–960, 14 bore Sogdian names (p. 326). A 10th-c. Uighur merchant's letter at Dunhuang asks a Sogdian kinsman for instructions (p. 325). Sogdian and Türk were blended in "Turco-Sogdian" business documents (p. 326).
- **The end of the long-distance trade.** There was a short revival under the Uighurs before 840 (p. 321). The last contacts with China are attested "around 930" (pp. 319, 334). In the 10th c. a man is seen walking from Samarkand to Canton with musk on his back (pp. 316–317). Mas'ūdī met a "fine old man" at Balkh who had often gone to China by land (p. 317). Only musk and slaves remained of the old trade (pp. 330–331).
- **The language's end.** Sogdian was still spoken in Semirech'e in the 11th c. (p. 328). The last dated Sogdian graffito in the Talas valley is of February–March 1026 (p. 329). Rubruck in 1253 heard that the Nestorians of Cailac used the old language for their services (pp. 329–330).
- **Kaifeng.** A *xian* cult still existed there in 1093, being absorbed into Buddhism (p. 323).

## 7. Merchants and rulers

- **Courts used merchant embassies.** Sogdian city embassies to the Tang brought dwarfs, dancers, lions and golden peaches. Vaissière thinks the quantities were small and the aim was to keep the courts in touch (pp. 137–138).
- **The state settled and registered them** (p. 143), and paid them in silk (pp. 65–66, 155).
- **Conquerors spared merchants.** At Khujand in 722 the Arabs separated nobles from merchants and killed only the nobles (pp. 161, 278). Vaissière reads this as at most "a pro-merchant Arab party", not a pro-Arab merchant party (p. 278).
- **Merchants led resistance too.** When Qutayba took Bukhara, the Kashkathān gave up their houses and built "seven hundred villas" outside the town, which long remained the centre of the old religion (p. 278).
- **A ruler as protector of a faith's merchants.** In the early 10th c. the Uighur ruler threatened the Samanids: if they killed one Manichaean of the 500 gathered at Samarkand, he would kill the Muslims in his own land. The Manichaeans were left alone "except for exacting tribute" (p. 314).

## 8. Women, eunuchs, gender crossing, disguise: findings and absences

- **Women in the diaspora:**
  - Miunai, her mother Catisa (a Loulan name) and her daughter, writing letters; possibly literate; perhaps able to travel alone; under husband and family council; abandoned at Dunhuang and in service to the Chinese (pp. 47, 56–57, 76, 188);
  - the woman's fragment LM II ii 09, on selling (p. 45 n. 3);
  - a caravan of 6 men and 10 women (p. 187);
  - the Türk wife of a Sogdian officer (An Lushan's mother, p. 215), a noble Sogdian woman married to a Türk (p. 202), and Kang and An wives of the Shi family (p. 212);
  - "sorceresses" (book's translation) who drummed, danced and sang at An Lushan's rite for the merchants (p. 218);
  - Chinese concubines travelling with Uighur-Sogdian missions (p. 308);
  - *hu ji*, "Sogdian barmaids", in Tang poetry (p. 140). The author doubts that a Turfan slave-sale contract sheds light on them (p. 140 n. 85).
- **Women as property.** The 639 contract for the sale of the girl Upach (pp. 169–170), slave girls on a trading permit (p. 165), the trade in Türk slaves (pp. 330–331). **Not usable as event content.**
- **Eunuchs:** none in the pages read.
- **Gender crossing:** none.
- **Disguise:** the only "disguise" is the concealment of descent after 755: new surnames, invented southern origins, Chinese ancestors (pp. 220–222). Tang tales also have princes disguised as ravioli sellers (p. 137).
- **Design consequence.** The book supports women in a merchant diaspora as letter-writers, travellers in caravans and dependants of a family council, never as heads of a firm or a community. It gives no woman *sabao*. A Yutai woman heading a community or a caravan is the mod's invention.

## 9. Guardrails and risks

- **Ha-Nehpakhot.** pp. 184–186 discuss the Rādhānite Jewish merchants (Ibn Khurdādhbih), a 9th-c. Hebrew text at Dunhuang used as an amulet, and an 8th-c. Judeo-Persian letter from near Khotan "about the trade of livestock and perhaps also of clothing and slaves" (p. 184). **Propose nothing from these pages.** The slave reference is exactly the motif the README excludes (Phelan 2023). The mod's Jewish faith is set in Provence and the Maghreb, not on the Silk Road.
- **Moneylending.** The book's moneylenders are Sogdian (pp. 139, 274). The Yutai righteous store already lends at interest. Do not add a Sogdian "usurer" figure; it would encode a people as moneylenders by nature.
- **No people as criminal or traitorous by nature.** The Tang reading of the *hu* as rebels after 755 is the enemy's voice. The massacre at Beijing (p. 220) and the killing of 2,000 under the Uighurs (pp. 224–225) are not event content.
- **Living communities.** Sogdians are a historical people with no living community claiming the name. Do not present the *xian* cult as a faith of the six; the Yutai sisters may only meet it as neighbours.
- **The narrator never claims Miunai** or any Sogdian woman for a faith.
- **Sexualised women.** The "barmaids" and "concubines" are not usable for flavour.

## 11. Design proposals

Vanilla *Roads to Power* pieces as checked in `byz_sophoulis_banditry.md` §11: `negotiate_settlement_interaction`, `purchase_land_interaction`, `hire_laamp_mercenaries_interaction`, the `laamp_*` contracts, and the camp purposes. Their internals were not re-checked for this note.
