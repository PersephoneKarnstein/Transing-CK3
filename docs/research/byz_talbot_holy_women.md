# Talbot (ed.), *Holy Women of Byzantium* (1996), design reading

**Citation (verified against the downloaded PDF).** Alice-Mary Talbot (ed.), *Holy Women of Byzantium: Ten Saints' Lives in English Translation*. Byzantine Saints' Lives in Translation 1. Washington, DC: Dumbarton Oaks Research Library and Collection, 1996. ISBN 0-88402-241-2. OA (CC BY-NC-ND) Dumbarton Oaks upload, archive.org. Reading-list item 13.
**Read in full:** General Introduction (pp. vii–xvi); Life of St. Mary/Marinos, trans. Nicholas Constas (pp. 1–12, intro and text); Life of St. Matrona of Perge, trans. Jeffrey Featherstone, intro and notes Cyril Mango (pp. 13–64). **Skimmed:** the introduction to Theodora of Thessalonike (pp. 159–61) and index entries for eunuchs.

**Relevance, candidly.** Both Lives read in full are of people assigned female who live among monks as eunuchs. Under the mod's constraint they are not Sisters and not precedents for trans women. Neither Life, nor any other Life in the volume, shows a person assigned male living as a woman or nun. What they give the design is (a) how a Byzantine reader placed "eunuch" beside "girl", (b) a detailed 5th-century template for a woman-founded house with its own investiture rite, succession and property, and (c) a canon on runaway slaves that cuts across Rescue the Daughters.

## 1. What the texts say

**General Introduction (Talbot).**
- The type of female saint shifts: martyrs, then "transvestite nuns and female hermits in the fifth and sixth centuries", then "cenobitic nuns and married laywomen in the eighth through tenth centuries" (p. xii). In the game window the living model is the convent, not the disguise; the disguised saints survive as old, popular cults (Theodora and Euphrosyne of Alexandria among the most depicted, p. xiv), and one new case is known from the 9th century, Anna/Euphemianos (p. xiii n. 19).
- Female sanctity is rare: the Synaxarion has "none from the seventh" century and eight women for iconoclasm against sixty-four men (pp. x–xi); offices of widow and deaconess "virtually disappeared" after the 6th century (p. xi n. 12).
- Almost all hagiographers were male; the only two known medieval Greek women hagiographers are Sergia and Theodora Raoulaina (p. xiv n. 26).

**Mary/Marinos (Constas).**
- Written c. 525–650, probably Syria; three Athonite MSS, oldest 10th c.; in Latin, Syriac, Coptic, Ethiopic, Armenian, Arabic, German and French (pp. 1–2). Commemorated 12 February.
- The crossing is done by the father on the daughter's proposal: "I shall first cut off the hair of my head, and clothe myself like a man" (p. 7); he "changed her name to Marinos" (p. 7). Constas notes the father transformed her gender "although it was her idea" (p. 7 n. 21).
- Monks "considered her to be a eunuch, for she was beardless and of delicate voice" (p. 8).
- Justinian, Nov. 133.5, urges "aged or eunuch monks" for outside errands, to avoid liaisons (p. 8 n. 24).
- The false paternity charge; "Forgive me, father, for I have sinned as a man" (p. 9); three years at the gate nursing the boy, who later takes the habit (pp. 9–10). Discovered when washed for burial (p. 11).
- Constas reads the Life as "a mysterious rite of passage" of separation, liminality and reaggregation, and the saint as among "creatures of the margins" taken for eunuchs (pp. 4–5). He cites separate eunuch monasteries (Guilland) as evidence that eunuch marginality was real (p. 5 n. 16). Herrin: "Apparel, far more than physique, identified a person" (p. 5 n. 17).

**Matrona of Perge (Featherstone; Mango).**
- Historical: Theodore Lector (c. 525) names "the famous Matrona, who had abandoned her home, her wealth and her husband, and pretending to be a eunuch, entered a men's monastery" (p. 13). She later defied Anastasios I's anti-Chalcedonian pressure (c. 498/9) (p. 14). Vita prima in an 11th-c. MS; written after 543 (p. 15).
- The escape is from an abusive husband who "would prevent me, now insulting me, now quarreling and striking me" (p. 27). Friends Eugenia and Susannah plan the disguise; Susannah takes her daughter (pp. 21–22).
- "Cutting her hair and dressing herself as a eunuch", she becomes Babylas, "completely transformed into a man" (p. 22). The brethren admire "a eunuch, one frail by nature" (p. 23).
- **Pierced ears.** A former actor, Barnabas, asks why both lobes are pierced. Her cover: "The woman to whom I formerly belonged ... shrank not from putting gold about my ears, so that many of those who saw me said that I was a girl" (pp. 23–24). That is, a eunuch *owned by a woman* and dressed by her as a girl is a plausible thing to have been. Mango notes a single earring was evidently not abnormal for a man (p. 23 n. 40).
- Two abbots dream: "The eunuch you have in your monastery is not a eunuch, but a woman" (p. 25). Her lament on discovery: "no longer am I thought to be a eunuch, nor to be addressed as Babylas, but am soon once again to be a woman and to be called Matrona" (p. 26).
- Bassianos keeps her as kin: "even if she has proven to be a woman, nevertheless she was and is one of our members" (p. 30). Her friend tells the husband, "Who this woman is of whom you speak, I know not ... a eunuch by the name of Babylas" (p. 29).
- She flees the husband to Emesa, Jerusalem, Sinai and a pagan temple near Beirut, gathers converted pagan girls ("this holy one is both mother and father to me", p. 38), and returns to Constantinople.
- **The house.** Bassianos gives her three men's girdles and three cloaks and orders: "do not receive any servant against the wish of her master or mistress, on account of the holy canons" (p. 46; Chalcedon canon 4, n. 84). The patrician Antiochiane grants her an estate "to your full ownership" (p. 51); the estate gives her "lawful" residence in the capital (p. 52 n. 95). A widow, Athanasia, gives everything; Matrona at first refuses to administer it, then takes the stewardship (pp. 58–59). Athanasia's seven eunuchs are sent "to male monasteries" while her three ladies of the bedchamber stay as nuns (pp. 60–61).
- **The investiture rite.** Matrona would not "bestow the habit upon any woman ... unless she had spent time in proving her intention"; before the altar, after "those who had charge of them had testified to their way of life", the candidate is tonsured, girded and cloaked (p. 63). Bassianos "ordained her overseer of souls" and gave her not women's girdles and veils but "wide, dark leather men's girdles and white men's cloaks", kept "unto the present day in her monastery" (pp. 63–64).
- **Succession and memory.** She named as successor "the honorable and most God-loving deaconess Mosilia", not a kinswoman (p. 64). Her Life rests on notes by the nun Eulogia (p. 62).

## 2. What it changes in the design

| # | Current design | Pressure | Proposed change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Aristotelian main rite: the cut are women. | A eunuch "belonged" to a woman who dressed her so "many ... said that I was a girl" (pp. 23–24). The court's eunuchs were bought; this one was a lady's slave made to look like a girl. | No change, confirms. But write it into the **refusal of the court system**: Dorleyaian text should note that the only Byzantine eunuch "taken for a girl" in the Lives was someone's property. Pairs with "calls its eunuchs angels and buys them as boys". |
| 2 | Rite of the Knife: sponsor, new name. | Matrona's house has a full investiture: probation, sponsors who "testified to their way of life", then tonsure, girdle and cloak before the altar (p. 63). | Model the rite's event chain on it: (1) probation period (the existing novice stage); (2) the sponsor testifies (a sponsor-opinion or sponsor-trait check, not a test of the candidate, per the Serano revision); (3) the knife; (4) girding and veiling with **women's** girdle and veil, which is the exact inversion of Matrona's men's girdles. Loc: "the woollen girdle and the veil, such as women are accustomed to use." |
| 3 | Sisters' Houses (county-held, Elder Sister on a term); typikon inheritance. | Matrona's line passes to a chosen deaconess, Mosilia, not kin (p. 64); her land is a gift "to your full ownership" from a patrician woman, with a male deacon holding it as her representative (p. 51). Theodora of Thessalonike's convent is run by her kin (pp. 159–61). | Confirms Take as Daughter and non-kin succession. Event candidate: a noblewoman of the court grants a Sisters' House an estate (county modifier or gold), with a male clerical "representative" the House must keep sweet. Optional succession law flavour: "she named Mosilia" (heir chosen by the Elder Sister). |
| 4 | Rescue the Daughters CB; Spring the Cell; Grandmother's House takes "any captive". | Chalcedon canon 4, as Bassianos quotes it: a house may not "receive any servant against the wish of her master or mistress" (p. 46). Orthodox canon law forbids sheltering runaway slaves. | Make this the hostile Orthodox **reaction text** to Rescue the Daughters and to Sisters' Houses taking in runaways ("the holy canons forbid it"). It also gives the Gift Alone doctrine an argument against Grandmother's House: taking captives is what the canons and the slave-masters agree on, from opposite ends. |
| 5 | Court system of eunuchs as a third estate; Galenic splinter. | Athanasia's household eunuchs follow her into religion but are sent "to male monasteries" while her women stay (pp. 60–61); Guilland's separate eunuch monasteries (p. 5 n. 16). The church files eunuchs with men. | Event candidate: a widow joins a Sisters' House with her household; her eunuchs ask to come in with her. Grandmother's House sends them to the men; Aunt Bea's and the Gift Alone may receive them as sisters if they ask. Directly dramatises the Bhatt revision (eunuchs who seek womanhood are sisters). |
| 6 | Sister-Making (on brother-making). | Matrona's friends Eugenia and Susannah, met at all-night vigils (a confraternity, p. 20 n. 30), plan her escape and raise her child. Bassianos: "she was and is one of our members." | Use the vigil confraternity as the social setting where Sister-Making happens: a "night-vigil sisterhood" flavour for the interaction. No mechanical change. |
| 7 | Era decay after 1066; 867 start. | The disguised-saint genre belongs to the 5th–7th centuries; in 867 the living model is the cenobitic convent and the married laywoman saint (p. xii). Theodora of Thessalonike (d. 892) is alive at the start. | Write 867 flavour around convents, not disguise. Possible start-date event: news of Theodora's death and cult at Thessalonike (892) as an Orthodox women's cult the Sisters watch. |
| 8 | Trans men out of scope. | Mary/Marinos and Matrona are assigned-female crossings. Matrona's nuns wore men's habit openly (p. 13). | No change. Do not claim these saints. If used, they are other people's saints; the narrator never calls them trans women or Sisters. |
| 9 | "Do any Lives show an assigned-male person living as a woman?" | None in this volume. | Record the negative result. The nearest case found in this batch is Latin and Frankish (Gregory of Tours, *Hist.* 10.15, via Betancourt; see `hist_trans_historical.md` P68), not a saint's Life. |

## 3. In-world loc lines
- "She was taken for a girl, they wrote, because a lady owned her and put gold in her ears. They did not write what she was taken for when the lady sold her." (Dorleyaian preacher)
- "The holy canons forbid it: no house may receive a servant against the will of her master." (Orthodox bishop, reaction to Rescue the Daughters)
- "Not the leather girdle of the brothers, but the woollen girdle and the veil, such as women are accustomed to use." (investiture text)
- "Even if she has proven to be other than we thought, she was and is one of our members." (an elder sister receiving a eunuch, adapted from Bassianos)
