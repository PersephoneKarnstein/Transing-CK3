# Libby, "Wondrous Bodies" (2023): trans epistemology and nonbinary saints, design reading

**Citation (checked against the article text).** C. Libby, "Wondrous Bodies: Trans Epistemology and Nonbinary Saints," *WSQ: Women's Studies Quarterly* 51, nos. 3 & 4 (Fall/Winter 2023): 156–169. No DOI is printed in the text, so none is given here.
Read in full from a text extraction kept outside the repo, notes and works cited included. Page numbers are the journal's. Libby uses they/them (author note, p. 167).

**What kind of piece it is.** A short theory essay. It is mostly about 1970s feminism and a modern Argentine devotion. The medieval content is about two pages (pp. 158–159, 165–167). It reads no primary medieval text itself. For Wilgefortis it relies on the Bollandists, Wallace (2014) and Mills (2021).

## 1. The argument in brief

- **Wonder as a frame.** Libby wants to read nonbinary identity through wonder, the feeling that gathers around bodies that do not fit (pp. 156–158).
- **Two medieval wonders.** After Daston and Park: *mirabilia*, marvels of nature (an eclipse), and *miracula*, miracles beyond nature (p. 158). Wonder "registered the line between the known and the unknown" (Daston and Park, quoted p. 158).
- **The clerics' rule.** Saints' bodies do impossible things. Clergy warned that such marvels could be divine or demonic (p. 159). Bynum's formula: *non imitandum sed admirandum*, "not to be imitated but to be marveled at" (p. 159). Male clerics praised holy women's marvels and discouraged other women from copying them.
- **Libby's turn.** Hagiography made readers want to take part. Relics, pilgrimage and saints' lives led people to imitate, against the clerics' rule (p. 159).
- **The danger.** Zurn: trans people were "pigeonholed as curios" (p. 160). Wonder can turn a body into a specimen. Steinbock: curiosity must turn from the "curious object" to how difference is made (p. 160).
- **Androgyny, 1970s.** Feminists first praised androgyny as utopia. Daly and Raymond then turned on it. Raymond's *Transsexual Empire* (1979) calls the trans woman "a surgically constructed androgyne, and thus a synthetic hybrid" (p. 163). Libby says this draws on a medieval distaste for hybrids (p. 163).
- **Santa Librada.** Althaus-Reid reads the Argentine Librada, a Christ-like figure in Mary's dress, as a saint of the poor and of petty thieves. Hers is a theology "on the move" (pp. 163–164).
- **Wilgefortis.** Libby calls her legend "fourteenth-century" (p. 165). The story: she prays to be disfigured so she need not marry; she grows a beard; her father has her crucified. Her cult spread across Europe. She helped "women with their bad husbands" (pp. 165–166). The beard takes her out of marriage and childbearing.
- **Historians' doubt.** Mills advises "accepting the enduring provisionality" of any label for her (p. 166). Libby agrees in part.
- **Other points.** Hildegard of Bingen's *Cause et cure* describes men and women outside the norm. Charmaille reads it through intersex (p. 167). Libby closes on gender-crossing saints as "bodies on the move", escaping their bonds (p. 167).
- **Libby's own caution.** "intersex embodiments are not coterminous with nonbinary identity" (p. 157). Many ideas of androgyny "elide the material reality and violence faced by nonbinary subjects" (p. 157).

## 2. What is usable for the mod

1. **"Admire, do not imitate" as the vanilla Christian voice.** This is the best thing here. A Catholic or Orthodox cleric can praise the gender-crossing saints and still condemn a daughter. The saints are for wonder. Copying them is pride or demonic deceit. This explains a real gap: the Church venerated Marinos and Euphrosyne but punished living people who crossed.
   - *Period check.* Libby gives the phrase as a common clerical formula, after Bynum (2001, p. 51). The essay does not date it or give Greek. **Unverified** for the ninth century, and for the Greek Church. Use it as the Latin cleric's line. For a Greek priest, paraphrase it.
2. **The Adelphai answer: they imitate.** Libby's claim, that devotion merged admiring and imitating, is the Sisters' heresy put plainly. "What the Church admires, we do."
3. **Three readings of a daughter.** A Christian observer can read her as a marvel of nature (*mirabile*), a miracle (*miraculum*), or a demonic deception (p. 159). This gives hostile and neutral events three distinct options with one source.
4. **Wonder as a cage.** Zurn's "curios" (p. 160) is the same point as Bhatt's "reverence" (`book_bhatt_third_sex.md` row 1). A court that marvels at a daughter is not her friend.
5. **"Synthetic hybrid" as hostile text.** Raymond's line (p. 163) has a medieval root: the distaste for mixed things (see DeVun row 2.2). A hostile cleric or physician can call a daughter "a made thing, a mixture". The Aristotelian rite answers: not a mixture, a woman.
6. **Wilgefortis: late and Latin.** She is useful only as late-game Catholic flavour. Her cult belongs to the Latin West in the fourteenth century and after.

## 4. Cautions

- **Nonbinary framing.** Libby argues that saints like Wilgefortis "destabilize" binary gender (p. 156). The mod's rule follows Bhatt: daughters are women, not a third thing, and "third gender" romance is out (beliefs A3; `book_bhatt_third_sex.md`). Do not import Libby's frame into any faith's theology. Her own warning helps here: such ideas can "elide the material reality and violence" (p. 157).
- **Intersex is not trans.** Libby says this herself (p. 157). Hildegard's and Charmaille's bodies are intersex material. Keep them apart from the daughters.
- **Wilgefortis's gender.** The legend calls her a virgin and a bride of Christ. Libby uses "they" for her at times (pp. 165–166). Mills says to leave her label open (p. 166). The narrator should call her a saint and use the legend's "she". Only a faith speaking in its own voice may claim her. No faith in the mod should, since she is Latin and late.
- **Direction of crossing.** Wilgefortis gains a beard. She moves away from womanhood, as Marinos and Euphrosyne do. This matches the asymmetry the design already records (Wingard row 3): crossing toward manhood is praised; crossing toward womanhood is punished. A saint like this cannot be a precedent for trans women.
- **Librada is modern.** Althaus-Reid's Librada is twentieth-century Argentine popular devotion. Do not use it for any period content.
- **Thin evidence.** Libby reads no medieval text directly. Cite Bynum, Daston and Park, Wallace or Mills for any factual claim, not Libby.
