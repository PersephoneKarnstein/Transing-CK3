**Scope, and whether it transfers.** The book is about Jews of **Fatimid and Ayyubid Egypt and Syria, 969–1250**, mostly Fustat (Introduction). Krakowski counts Qayrawan, al-Mahdiyya and Tripoli (Libya) among the places Geniza writers came from. But she stresses that the corpus is uneven: 11th-c. documents come "occasionally" from the Maghrib, and after the mid-11th-c. Bedouin invasions of Ifriqiya communications collapsed, so that 12th- and 13th-c. documents are mostly from Egypt alone (Introduction, "Geniza Documents and 'Geniza Society'"). She does **not** claim that her Egyptian findings hold for the Maghrib. Where she cites Maghribi evidence (gaonic queries from Qayrawan and Qabis, Isaac Alfasi's responsa, Nissim ibn Shahin's tales), it usually marks a **difference**: child betrothal and premarital intimacy appear more accepted in the Maghrib. Her wider thesis is that Jewish courts kept rabbinic law in form while family life followed "Islamicate" norms shared with Muslims and Christians. That thesis suggests the same pattern under Maghribi rulers, but she does not test it there. Findings below are marked **[M]** (Maghribi evidence), **[E]** (Egypt/Syria) or **[M→E]**.

**Gender and the body: what is absent.** The book has no eunuchs (the only hit is a bibliography entry, Marmon's *Eunuchs and Sacred Boundaries*, cited for the female honorific *maṣūna*: ch. 5, n. 67). It has nothing on khuntha (intersex) law, cross-dressing, the mukhannathun, dress codes for Jews, or Purim. The body appears only as virginity, puberty, seclusion and veiling (§3.4).

## 2. The argument in brief

- **Kinship as a network of pairs.** Geniza families were "fluid social networks frequently disrupted and reconfigured by travel, divorce, remarriage, and death", held together by loyalties between pairs of relatives, not by clans. Women had almost no social bonds outside kinship (Introduction; ch. 1).
- **Remarriage was very common.** 37% of 297 datable marriage documents (933–1250) are for brides married before. Krakowski estimates that 41–69% of women remarried in the 11th c., and 44–78.5% in the 12th and 13th (ch. 1, "Remarriage"). 40% of first-time brides' fathers were already dead (ch. 1, "Mortality"). [E]
- **Courts that kept rabbinic law in form.** Rabbinic courts lacked any means of force. They had only oaths, fines and the ban, and the ban rarely worked: "Geniza evidence for people isolated by the ban is remarkably thin on the ground" (ch. 2, "Why Did Litigants Use Dhimmī Courts?"). Their power came from technical legal expertise, which Muslim rulers also respected. [E]
- **Islamicate social norms.** Families treated a daughter's first marriage "as a resource controlled by their relatives". Courts nonetheless upheld the rabbinic rule that a mature girl may refuse a match (ch. 6).

## 3. What matters for the design

### 3.1 Courts, the Islamic state and the ban

- **Women threatened the qadi.** A woman seeking divorce wrote: "Otherwise I will be forced to go to the nations of the world," meaning an Islamic court (ch. 2, opening). Jews used qadi and *maẓālim* (state petition) courts freely. Rabbanite jurists condemned this only when it undercut a Jewish court's ruling; they allowed it to enforce one (ch. 2). [E]
- **Muslim officials sent Jews back to Jewish courts.** Qadis and officials sometimes redirected Jewish litigants to a Jewish court (Alexandria, 1152; ch. 2, "The Nature of Rabbinic Power"). In the **Qayrawan query** (T-S Ar. 50.197, ed. Friedman), a father unhappy with a local judge went to a state official, who ordered a new Rabbanite judge to hear the case "in accordance with your legal doctrine (*madhhab*)". In the rehearing a contingent of "trustworthy" women questioned the bride to confirm that she wished to end the betrothal (ch. 6, "Negotiating Rabbinic Maturity Law", nn. 94–96). [M] This is the closest thing in either book to the faith's "lord's licence": the Muslim ruler does not license a ban, but he can order a Jewish rehearing under Jewish law. (Goitein, p. 212, tells the same case with "old women" sent to the girl.)
- **The ban was weak.** Excommunication was "nearly impossible to implement perfectly" and "best imposed infrequently, lest its overuse damage court officials' own credibility" (ch. 2). [E] This supports keeping the faith's `niddui` partial and contestable, as it is now.
- **Who led.** Jewish authority in Fatimid Egypt was "diffuse and multifocal" until the 1060s–1090s. Up to three sets of courts and synagogues coexisted (Palestinian, Iraqi and Karaite), and then the Head of the Jews (*ra'īs al-yahūd*) rose. That office was built by local Jewish elites, not created by the state (ch. 2, "Jewish Self-Government and Its Limits"). In the 10th c. the Iraqi ge'onim wrote polemics to win over the Jews of **Ifriqiya**, "at a time when it [gaonic authority] was already failing" (ch. 2, "Rabbinic Law and Gaonic Legal Writing"). [E; M]

### 3.2 Marriage, consent and alliance

- **The mature girl's veto.** Gaonic and Rabbanite authorities agree that "if she disavows her father's betrothal, it does not bind her". But they treat this as freedom from compulsion, not a right to choose. Most Sunni schools let a father compel a virgin daughter (Maliki law, the school of the Maghrib, included) (ch. 6, "Negotiating Rabbinic Maturity Law"; ch. 6 notes on *ijbār*). [general]
- **Marriage as alliance (*ittiṣāl*).** Labrāṭ b. Moshe b. Sughmār, merchant and Rabbanite judge of **al-Mahdiyya**, wrote to his brother Yehuda, newly married in Fustat (1056–57), praising the bride's family and urging him: "Make him [your father-in-law] a substitute for your late father and … for me" (ch. 6, "Marriage Alliance in Geniza Letters"). In 1058 he wrote: "You are dearer to me than the world" (ch. 1, "Rethinking Kinship"). [M] Goitein quotes the same letters (his p. 171).
- **Marrying off orphans.** Yūsuf b. Mūsā al-Tāhirtī of al-Mahdiyya, mid-11th c.: "I spent money to adorn her, and married her to my sister's son." Krakowski reads "adorn" as providing a dowry, and calls a dowry for an orphan "an important act of charity" (ch. 4, "Charity from Nonkin"). [M]
- **Stipulations.** Personal behaviour clauses appear in Jewish marriage documents only from 1047 (one case) and regularly from c. 1100. They cover monogamy, no female slave without the wife's permission, residence, travel, the wife's labour and her movement. Krakowski argues that scribes took them from **Islamic** contracts, attested from 9th-c. Egypt (ch. 7). [E]

### 3.3 Maghribi differences (do not build)

- **Child betrothal.** Gaonic and local responsa describe child betrothal and child marriage "as more common in Andalusia and the Maghrib (especially Ifrīqiyya)" than in Egypt. A query from 10th-c. Qabis (Gabes) treats a minor's betrothal as routine. Krakowski also quotes Simon b. Ṣemaḥ Duran (14th–15th c.), who calls it the norm (ch. 3, "Minor and Mature Brides"). [M]
- **Intimacy before the wedding.** An index of lost gaonic responsa calls it "the custom in many cities of the Maghrib" for a betrothed man to have sex with his fiancée in her father's house (ch. 5, "Social Approaches to Virginity", n. 28). [M]
- **Nissim ibn Shahin's tale.** In *al-Faraj ba'd al-shidda* (Qayrawan, 11th c.), a butcher ransoms a captive girl to marry his son. A stranger proves an earlier betrothal by a hidden birthmark, and the butcher marries her to him instead. Krakowski reads it as evidence that a girl in **Zirid Ifriqiya**, "unlike most in Fatimid or Ayyubid Fustat", might be married off at puberty (ch. 5, opening). [M]

All three are real Maghribi evidence, but they concern minors and sex before marriage. They are out of bounds for events. The ibn Shahin tale is safe only as a title or a story told (the ransom and the wedding), not its ending.

### 3.4 Seclusion, mobility and the body

- **Seclusion marked class, not virginity.** Krakowski's main finding: unmarried girls were not watched more closely for being virgins. Seclusion was "a class category" that set elite women of any age apart. Poor women and girls queued for bread with men; elite women stayed in (ch. 5, "Modesty as a Class Category"). A Fustat court gave a woman alimony for her slave too, because she was among "the elite of the city who remain secluded in their homes" (ch. 5, n. 67). Secluded women depended on non-secluded women, often slaves, to go out for them (ch. 5, n. 67). [E]
- **Mothers, not fathers, kept daughters in.** Maimonides: "It is not customary for a father to sit and guard his daughter", so a girl's modesty depends on her mother (ch. 5, "Seclusion before Marriage"). [E]
- **The outings allowed a "proper" wife:** synagogue, bathhouse, celebrations, mournings, family, and the flax market, "permitted to a proper Jewish woman of her type" (Ẓāfira's contract; ch. 5). The harshest contract (Fustat, 1127) lets the husband "lock her in" (ch. 5, n. 57). [E]
- **Veils.** Maimonides says that where women go out only "covered by a *radid* that covers her whole body like a prayer shawl", a husband must give his wife at least the meanest kind. The *radda* and many face veils appear in Geniza trousseau lists (ch. 5). [E] (General knowledge, *unverified* here: Maimonides came from the Maghrib, so the custom he records might be Maghribi. Krakowski does not say so.)
- **The bathhouse.** A woman of "mid-eleventh-century Tripoli" vowed that neither she nor her daughter, about to marry, would go to the bathhouse until her brother recovered from illness (ch. 5, n. 74, T-S 10 J 14.20). By Krakowski's stated convention ("Tripoli al-Shām" for Lebanon), plain "Tripoli" means **Tripoli in Libya**; *unverified*. [M?]
- **The voice.** The Jerusalem woman Ester would not let her voice be heard by men in the next room (cf. bBerakhot 24a, "a woman's voice is a form of nudity") (ch. 5). [E]
- **Virginity.** Virginity suits were rare. Only three cases are known, and a midwife's examination appears once, as an act of malice (ch. 5, "Virginity Claims"). Before betrothal, a girl could be found "mature (*bāligh*)" before two witnesses; Krakowski notes that Geniza documents routinely use this masculine form of the word for girls (ch. 3, "Minor and Mature Brides", n. 49). [E] Not material for events.
- **State bans on women going out.** The Fatimid caliph al-Ḥākim (996–1021) banned women from cemeteries and bathhouses, then from leaving home, and banned the making of women's shoes. These bans leave "no apparent traces" in the Geniza (ch. 5, "Modesty as a Class Category"). [E]

### 3.5 Women's learning and work

- **Prayer.** The opening letter (Fustat, early 12th c.) asks for a teacher for two orphan sisters, to "teach them prayer, so they will not grow up like animals, not knowing *shema' yisra'el*". A childless woman would teach them embroidery (Introduction; ch. 4). [E]
- **Women's trades:** midwife, hairdresser, corpse-dresser, broker, peddler, elementary religious teacher, and above all textile work (spinning, embroidery) done at home (ch. 4, "Learning to Work"). [E]
- **The *mu'allima*.** Krakowski finds female teachers only in boys' religious schools. She finds no evidence for Goitein's claim that girls attended synagogue or school with boys (ch. 4, n. 81; ch. 5, n. 81). The schoolmistress of Maimonides' responsa learned the Bible after marriage and taught from need (ch. 4). [E]
- **Mothers teach daughters to weave.** Yosef ibn Migash (Andalusia, 12th c.) holds that a mother "teaches her and trains her in that which girls need … such as weaving and supervising the needs of the household" (ch. 4). [Andalusian]

### 3.6 Slavery

- Minor slave girls did housework from age five or six. Only an elite minority owned slaves (ch. 4, "Unmarried Girls' Labor"). [E]
- Converts to Judaism were "usually freed slaves who were formerly owned by Jews" (Introduction). [E]
- The marriage clause barring a female slave without the wife's consent: see §3.2. [E]

## 5. Guardrails

- Leave out §3.3 entirely: minors, premarital sex and the birthmark ending.
- One quoted letter (al-Jawziyya, ch. 6) contains a threat of self-harm. Do not quote or adapt it (no suicide or self-harm content).
- The orphan's "adornment" (dowry) is family charity, not finance. Keep any cost small and framed as a gift.
- Slavery: context only. Never model the buying or keeping of people.
