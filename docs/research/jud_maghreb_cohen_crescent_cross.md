**What the book is.** It is a comparative argument: the Jews of Islam fared better than those of Latin Christendom because Islamic law was predictable, the Jews were one dhimmi group among several, and the social order was a hierarchy with a low but recognised place for them. It is not a regional history of the Maghreb. Maghrebi material is thin, but its account of how the dhimma *worked* is the clearest of the three books.

## 1. The dhimma as a contract of protection

- **Subjects, not chattel.** Islam held dhimmis "subject to the jurisdiction of Islamic law" while they kept "internal autonomy in religious and most civil matters". Islamic law "never asserted the ruler's possessory rights over the non-Muslims. They were his subjects, albeit second-class ones, not his chattel" (p. 52). This is the contrast with Provence, where Jews belonged to a lord.
- **The Pact as a petition.** The Pact is "a kind of bilateral contract in which the non-Muslims agree to a host of discriminatory regulations in return for protection". No text of it is earlier than the 10th or 11th century (c. p. 54). Cohen reads its odd form, a letter from the conquered, as "a kind of petition from the losers promising submission in return for a decree of protection (amān, dhimma)" (c. p. 57, with index "Noth, 57").
- **Predictable, not renewed.** Unlike a Latin charter, which each new king had to renew and could change, the Pact was held not to need renewing. Ibn Taymiyya: the original Pact sufficed for all later generations, and "a ruler could not grant relief to the dhimmīs from any of the Pact's restrictions". Fear of *bid'a* (innovation) meant rulers would "neither arbitrarily add to nor detract from" the canon (c. pp. 73–74). Cohen's conclusion: "the relative stability over time of the basic law ... assured them a considerable degree of continuity" (p. 74).
- **Hands off personal status.** "Islamic judges remained faithful to the principle of noninterference in the adjudication of intra-Jewish issues unless brought before them voluntarily by the parties". In marital law the formula is "we do not interfere in this matter ... on account of the dhimma pact" (p. 74).
- **No collective punishment (Maliki view).** The Maliki school holds that Islam "should not impose collective retribution for one person's violation of the Pact if the other non-Muslims repudiate the act" (p. 74). The Maghreb was Maliki (general knowledge), so this is the relevant school.
- **The jizya as protection.** It was humiliating in form and a real burden on the poor, but "Conceived as 'protection money', however, jizya played a constructive role ... The very regularity of the jizya assured dhimmīs of regular and continuous security" (c. pp. 70–71; index: Neṭira 71). Women, children, slaves and the infirm were exempt (c. p. 70). *Guardrail: money carries this. Do not build content on the jizya.*

## 2. Ghiyar: the marks and what they meant

- **Origins.** The dress rule probably began as the conquerors insisting that Christians keep their own dress, especially the *zunnar* belt, an old Christian mark of status, so the Arabs could tell ruler from ruled (pp. 62–63, after Noth). "Only secondarily, and later ... did the Islamic dress code serve to reinforce the humiliating laws of the dhimma, and even then …" (p. 63). The first extant edict is al-Mutawakkil's of 850 (p. 63).
- **Not enforced in the Fatimid Geniza world.** Geniza evidence shows "no differences between the attire of Jews and Muslims during Fatimid and early Ayyubid times (mid-tenth to the late twelfth century). To …" (p. 64). Fatimid Ifriqiya (909–69) and Zirid Ifriqiya fall inside this span. Stillman's story of the nagid's unmarked turban (Stillman p. 63) fits it: the courtier did not wear the mark.
- **Hierarchy, not exclusion.** The purpose was "to demonstrate unambiguously who were the rulers and who the ruled ... in an inherently hierarchical society" (p. 64). Unlike the Lateran badge of 1215, it was not designed to prevent sexual contact or contamination (pp. 62–64).
- **Two words.** *Saghar* is lowliness, from the jizya verse. Ibn Hazm, the 11th-c. Andalusi jurist, defined it as being subject to Islam's authority and not displaying unbelief in public (p. 113). *Ghiyar*, or *tamyiz*, began as "the distinctive dress required of non-Muslims" and came to mean the whole system of differentiation. Being "less imperious than ṣaghār", it "suggests the possibility of 'horizontal' relationships between differentiated ethnoreligious groups" (p. 113).
- **The dhimmi's place.** "The dhimmī, however, occupied a definite slot in Islamic society—a low rank but a rank, nevertheless" (c. p. 112). Dhimmis worked as merchants, artisans, physicians and clerks, and "ghiyār let everyone know who was a non-Muslim" (p. 114).
- **Two *ta'ifas*.** Arabic sources call the Jews *ta'ifat al-yahud*, a non-pejorative word for a social group or faction. Among Jews, "the two main factions, the Rabbanites and the Karaites, are regularly referred to as 'the two ṭā'ifas'" (p. 114). This is the term for a dissenting current living inside the Jewish community, not outside it.

## 3. Courts, the ban and the limits of Jewish jurisdiction

- **Muslim courts.** Jews often drew up business deeds and settled disputes in qadi courts, and Jewish courts admitted those deeds. A gaonic responsum: "we also adjudicate according to that legal document and consider it valid ... it happens daily". Hay Gaon (d. 1038), whom Cohen calls the "chief justice" of the Jews of Islam: "These Muslims treat us with great solicitude and protection" (pp. 94–95).
- **Family law stayed Jewish.** "In matters of personal status, especially family law ... qāḍīs often referred litigants back to the Jewish judiciary" (p. 95).
- **The ban.** Cohen does not discuss *herem* or *niddui*. In theory geonim "frowned on Jews applying to Muslim courts, and they regularly repeated the talmudic ban" (p. 95), but they were flexible in practice. For who could ban and on whose licence, see Rustow (her note §§3–4).

## 4. Courtiers

- **Common and useful, despite the law.** Exclusion from office was decreed again and again (by 'Umar II and al-Mutawakkil in 850) and "only sporadically enforced" (pp. 65–66). Dhimmi office-holders gave their communities "a feeling of embeddedness in the larger society" (p. 66).
- **The limits.** "More than one Jewish courtier lost his life when he was perceived as lording it over Muslims" (pp. 66–67). The 1066 Granada pogrom followed charges that the Jews had changed the order of things "in violation of their pact" (p. 165). Cohen calls it "an exception to the rule in the classical period" (p. 74).
- **A Moroccan zealot in Cairo (early 14th c.).** A Moroccan dignitary came to Egypt from a country where the dhimma's disabilities "were rigorously enforced", and persuaded the Mamluks to enforce the Pact (c. p. 67). This is evidence that by c. 1300 Morocco was known for strict enforcement.
- **Suspect converts.** In the post-classical period, converted courtiers suspected of keeping their office by insincere conversion were called *musalima* and were investigated by qadi courts (p. 68).

## 5. The Maghreb: persecution and response

- **Fez, 1032.** "the Jewish quarter of Fez came under attack along with the rest of the city, when it was taken over by a …" (p. 166). This dates the Zanata wars in Fez (general knowledge: the Maghrawa–Banu Ifran struggle). *Guardrail: massacre, not to be modelled.*
- **The Almohads, from the mid-1140s.** They offered Jews and Christians "the choice of death or conversion; most chose conversion" (p. 166). Cohen knows "of no evidence suggesting that Almohads included expulsion among the choices" (p. 168).
- **An eyewitness letter (Fustat, 1148)** by Solomon ha-Kohen al-Sijilmasi, whose family came from Sijilmasa (Goitein's translation, p. 181). At Sijilmasa the Jews negotiated with 'Abd al-Mu'min for seven months, "during all of which they fasted and prayed". Then 150 were killed; "the first of the apostates was Joseph b. 'Imrān, the judge of Sijilmāsa"; about 200 who had taken refuge in the fortress are now in Dar'a; "of all the countries of the Almoravids there remained ... only Der'ā and Miknāsa" (pp. 181–82). The letter's figures for Fez (100,000 killed) and Marrakesh (120,000) are its own (p. 182).
- **Why conversion and not martyrdom.** Rabbis in Islamic lands did not count Islam as idolatry, so the duty to die did not apply. Maimonides "put the stamp of approval on this theological judgment" (p. 176). Muslims under persecution practised *taqiyya*, outward conformity (p. 176). After the persecutions of al-Hakim, the Almohads and Yemen, forced converts "were allowed to revert to their original faith". Al-Zahir's decree after al-Hakim restated Islam's "opposition ... to forced conversion" (p. 176). A 10th-c. formulary from al-Andalus lets a forced convert "return" (c. p. 176).
- **The epistles.** Maimon b. Joseph "the Dayyan" (Maimonides' father), *Epistle of Consolation*: forced converts remain Jews "if they clandestinely practice basic Jewish rituals, especially prayer, even in abbreviated form and even in Arabic" (Cohen's paraphrase, p. 182). Maimonides, *Epistle on Apostasy*, condoned outward conversion with covert practice and advised leaving for a land of freedom, "the course the Maimon family eventually took" (pp. 182–83).
- **Ibn 'Aqnin of Fez.** Joseph b. Judah ibn 'Aqnin was "a crypto-Jew of the early Almohad period who lived in Fez, Morocco, where he met Maimonides" (p. 186 and note 131). His *Therapy of the Soul* deals with the second wave under al-Mansur (1184–99), aimed at the children of the first converts. He names the martyred communities "of Fez, of Sijilmasa, of Dar'a" (note 134).
- **Memory: the scroll, not the lament.** The Jews of Islam wrote few elegies on persecution. When a persecution was thwarted they wrote a *megilla*, a scroll of deliverance on the model of Esther. Examples are the "Egyptian Scroll" of 1012, with an annual fast (pp. 184–85), and scrolls for victories in *internal* Jewish disputes, Evyatar's and the "Zuta" scroll, which call the opponent a "Haman" (p. 187). Geniza letters call local Jew-baiters "Haman" and a victor "Mordecai of Our Time" (c. pp. 186–87). Ibn Ezra's lament for the Almohad ruin, "Aha yarad 'al sefarad", is the only clear Hebrew elegy of its kind (pp. 183–84).

## 6. Gender and the body

- **Baths.** Jews and Muslims "met in the public baths". Rules sometimes made dhimmis wait until after Friday prayer or "wear something distinctive around their necks while sitting unclothed". Mixed bathing itself "was not banned" (pp. 130–31).
- **Intermarriage.** A Muslim man may marry a dhimmi woman. The husband must let a Jewish wife keep the Sabbath and food laws and read her Scriptures quietly, but she might not go to her house of worship. Jurists debated whether he could require Islamic purity washing after menstruation (pp. 132–33). *Guardrail: the last point touches ritual immersion. Do not use (no mikveh content).*
- **Absent.** No khuntha, mukhannathun, eunuch, cross-dressing or Purim-dress material for the Maghreb. Purim appears only as the Roman law forbidding the hanging of Haman in effigy (p. 60) and as the megilla model (pp. 184–87).
