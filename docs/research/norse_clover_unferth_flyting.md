## 1. The argument in brief

- **One genre, not three.** The native terms *senna* (quarrel) and *mannjafnaðr* (man-comparison) were turned by scholars into separate genres, but "neither category has a pure representative" (p. 445). Descriptively there is one form, the **flyting**. *Níð* (sexual defamation) attached itself to it very early; it was "supposed to have been proscribed in real life, but it clearly flourished in the literary underbrush" (p. 445). Clover's survey covers nearly forty examples from the Edda, Saxo, the sagas, *þættir* and skaldic verse (n. 9).
- **Setting.** It happens either over "the sundering flood" (water between the two speakers) or indoors in the hall, at drinking, often at court or at the Alþing (p. 447). It starts calm and grows angrier. Drinking can be part of the contest: in *Örvar-Odds saga* each man drains a horn and then speaks an insult stanza, 24 stanzas in all (p. 449). The Old English *Vainglory* shows the same hall custom in England, with its "indoor battlefield" (pp. 448-49).
- **Contenders.** Usually male/male or male/female, and in the second case "the woman always loses" (n. 15). Female/female flyting belongs to the queens' quarrel of the Brynhildr tradition (p. 450). One on one is the rule, with serial forms (one against several in turn). There is also an old **delegate** pattern: a younger figure speaks for a lord (Skírnir for Freyr, Sinfjötli for Helgi), and Unferþ speaks for Hroðgar (pp. 450, 460).
- **The threshold test.** A newcomer at a shore, gate or hall door is put through a hostile **identification**: name, paternity, credentials. It is usually baited ("Fool, who are you? ... Who your father? What your lineage?", Saxo, p. 450). The custom is so common it "may ... reflect actual Germanic etiquette" (p. 451). Newcomers may also have to name and show their *íþróttir* (accomplishments, n. 23).
- **Eloquence is the only shared trait** (p. 451). The flyting is "not just a prelude to violence but itself the oral equivalent of war" (p. 452). Its terms are *orðom bregðask* (to battle with words), *sakask sáryrðom* (to fight with wound-words) and Old English *onband beadurune* (unbound war-words, p. 460). In Saxo, of Gotwar, "words were her weapons" (p. 452).
- **Structure: Claim, Defense, Counterclaim** (p. 452). Claims and Counterclaims carry insults and boasts (about the past) and threats, vows and curses (about the future). The Defense is concessive: "that may be, but". The speech is highly parallel: the opponent's name in direct address, an emphatic I/you contrast, and refrains such as *þegi þú* ("be quiet") and "you weren't there while ..." (p. 453).
- **Insult repertory** (p. 453): appearance, cowardice, defeat, trivial or domestic softness, failures of honour (unavenged kin, strife with kin), food taboos, and sexual irregularity: "castration, bestiality, and passive homosexuality for men". Crimes against kin are the central theme. One stock taunt for a man is that he "sat at home like your father's daughter" (p. 456). *Skauð hernumin* ("a captive gelding", or a cruder female reading) implies castration (n. 39).
- **Accuracy wins.** "It is not ... the most flamboyant provocations that win the flyting, but the most accurate ones" (p. 454). Weak flyters (Þórr, Grep, Sinfjötli) are "random and excessive"; masters work a few oppositions: action vs talk, hard life vs soft, adventurer vs stay-at-home (p. 454).
- **They argue meanings, not facts.** The incident raised is never denied: "they argue interpretations, not facts" (p. 458). The art of the boast is the best version of the event "within the limitations of the facts", and the insult is the worst version (p. 459). The fantastic sexual insults are the exception: their oblique diction is "a circumvention of legal injunctions against *nid*" (p. 458).
- **Reversal is the master stroke.** King Eysteinn answers Sigurðr's crusade-boast ("you sat at home like your father's daughter") by conceding it and turning it: he stayed and built churches, harbours and hospices, governed Norway and took Jämtland (pp. 456-57). His success lies in "having upended the traditional terms" (p. 457).
- **Outcome: silence, not blood.** Violence is "not a requisite ending" (p. 464). The flyting is "a verbal combat complete in itself, with its own logic, rules, winners and losers" (p. 459). Any revenge is a new phase. The loser goes quiet, and "having the last word is equivalent to winning" (p. 465). Unferþ "was the more silent" afterwards. Strained peace can last for life, as between Eysteinn and Sigurðr (p. 465).
- **A woman closes it.** After both *Lokasenna* and Unferþ, a lady (Sif, Wealhþeow) steps forth with mead and conciliating words, and the *Morkinskinna* brothers' flyting has the same queen's intervention (n. 72).
- **For Beowulf:** Unferþ is Hroðgar's licensed interrogator of the stranger, which is why he is never rebuked. The job was "part-time, ad hoc, and thankless" (p. 467). Beowulf wins by conceding the Breca facts and reinterpreting them, then countering with fratricide and a Hel-curse (pp. 461-64).
