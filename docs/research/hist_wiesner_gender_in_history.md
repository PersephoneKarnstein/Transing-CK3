# Wiesner-Hanks, *Gender in History: Global Perspectives* (2nd ed., 2011) — Research Notes for "Transbian Maximalism" (CK3 mod)

Source: Merry E. Wiesner-Hanks, *Gender in History: Global Perspectives*, 2nd edn (Wiley-Blackwell, 2011). Plain-text copy at `scratchpad/books/wiesner_gender_in_history.txt`. Page numbers below are the printed book's pages (visible in the text as running heads); chapter numbers are given for every quote. All quotes are verbatim from the text file (hyphenation across line breaks silently rejoined).

Purpose: mine the book for historically attested practices, institutions and ideas (prioritising 800–1450 CE and the CK3 map) that can lend a "veneer of non-anachronism" to a fictional faith/culture whose tenets encode modern trans political thought: randomly assigned "transgender" trait; ritual castration of male-assigned characters in the manner of the galli; castrated men regarded as women; inheritance through adoptive matrilines; a women-/trans-woman-centred priesthood.

Caveat on the source: this is a short synthetic survey (c. 230 pages) written for students. It gives breadth, not depth: many practices get one or two sentences and no primary citation. Entries below say exactly how much the book says. Where the book is thin, that is flagged so the mod team knows to look elsewhere for detail.

## 1. Book map

**Ch. 1 Introduction (pp. 1–24).** Lays out the sex/gender distinction and the four sources of doubt about it (intersex bodies, ethnographic third/fourth genders, trans people, historians' differences-among-women), then surveys theory (linguistic turn, queer theory, postcolonial/critical race theory, Gramscian hegemony, Connell's hegemonic masculinity). The long final section, "The Origins of Patriarchy," reviews sociobiology, primate studies, the Bachofen/Engels/Gimbutas primitive-matriarchy thesis and its critics (Diop and Amadiume on African matriarchy; PRC historians), Gerda Lerner's "women were the first property" thesis, and the consensus that plow agriculture, property, the bureaucratic state, writing, hereditary aristocracy and organised religion together produced male dominance. Very useful for the mod's ideological framing (Engels-style matriarchy-to-patriarchy story is itself a historical belief system the faith could hold).

**Ch. 2 The Family (pp. 25–54).** A chronological tour of household/kinship forms: Hammurabi and New Kingdom Egypt; classical China (betrothal gifts, concubines, adoption of a son by a sonless wife), India, Sparta, Athens, Rome; the premodern Africa/Americas/SE Asia section (matrilineal and matrilocal systems, bridewealth and brideservice, resource polygyny, bilateral inheritance, easy divorce, temporary marriage in SE Asia); medieval Europe (consent-based sacramental marriage, canon law, Orthodox divorce, the NW European late-marriage pattern); colonial "blood", casta and race; the industrial and 20th-century family. Rich on matriliny, adoption-as-kinship, and alternative marriage forms, which is where the mod's inheritance ideas live.

**Ch. 3 Economic Life (pp. 55–82).** Gender and work from foragers to the service economy: foraging and horticulture (women as first planters, women's control of crops and matrilineal land in hoe cultures), plow agriculture ("men plow and women weave"; plow = patrilineal inheritance), women's land inheritance by default when sons were lacking, slavery (Byzantine/Ottoman male slaves in high office; concubines' children free under Islamic law), medieval guilds and journeymen's all-male honour, capitalism, industrialism, the family wage, the "second shift" and "wages for housework". Key for the class-struggle framing; also notes that men doing women's work could be re-gendered as a third gender.

**Ch. 4 Ideas, Ideals, Norms, and Laws (pp. 83–108).** Thematic rather than chronological: the Other/One asymmetry (men differentiated by estate, women by virgin/wife/widow); the Aristotelian one-sex model (woman as imperfect male, women can become male but never the reverse) vs the Galenic two-sex model, and the Vedic idea that all foetuses begin male; binaries (inner/outer, nature/culture, order/disorder, purity/impurity, honour/shame); postmenopausal women gendered "male"; menstrual huts as women's communities; motherhood and fatherhood as power (abu-names, couvade); inequity-prescribing practices (footbinding as a mother-administered female rite of passage; veiling, seclusion, the veiled Tuareg men, coverture); and ideologies of egalitarianism (Christine de Pizan, feminist waves, CEDAW). The one-sex material and the Vedic male-first doctrine are the best "period medicine" for castration-as-sex-change.

**Ch. 5 Religion (pp. 109–136).** Animism/shamanism (mixed-gender ritualists: two-spirit, hijra, bissu; women shamans; the 18th-c. zar cult as a women-only healing religion; Yoruba Àjé; Anlo women's orders; Alice Lakwena and Joan of Arc charged with witchcraft), Near Eastern written religion (mother-goddess displaced by Marduk/Yahweh), Confucianism/Taoism (yin-yang; Taoist women leaders), Hinduism/Buddhism (sacred thread; Laws of Manu; sati; bhakti from the 8th c.; nuns' order under male control; "women must be reborn as men"), Christianity (brother/sister fictive kin; virgins; abbesses; 12th-c. clerical celibacy; witch hunts; Shakers, Moravian lottery marriage, Mormon polygamy), Islam (Qur'anic equality debate; Khadijah; women's separate inheritance and endowments; Sofreh; Qing-China women's mosques with female leaders; Sufism). Supplies the priesthood, separatism and "women's religion" precedents, and the model of a new faith that attacks the family and calls members brother and sister.

**Ch. 6 Political Life (pp. 137–169).** Kin groups and villages (women's councils among Cherokee and Igbo; Iroquois women's decisions subject to male review), hereditary aristocracies (female pharaohs with ceremonial beards; Enheduanna; Japanese shaman-empresses; Maya queens and rulers in blended-gender costume; empress dowagers; the Hausa magajiya chosen by the iya; Ottoman valide sultans; Aceh's sultanahs 1641–99; Bugis "white blood" queens; Begums of Bhopal passing rule mother to daughter; Salic Law; the Knox/Bodin/Aylmer debate and the queen's "two bodies"), warfare (rape as weapon; Mulan; Dahomey women soldiers; camp women; male comradeship as the highest bond; **eunuch generals and officials in China and the Ottoman Empire castrated by parental order, trusted because childless, paralleled with cross-dressing warriors and unmarried queens**), citizenship (Athenian naming ceremony; medieval urban citizenship for women; Chaumette "since when is it permitted to give up one's sex?"), women's rights movements, colonialism/anticolonialism, communism.

**Ch. 7 Education and Culture (pp. 170–189).** Gendered access to literacy, schooling and artistic training. Opening section: oral cultures with men's and women's dances and stories "for women's ears only"; writing as elite/professional male skill; nuns reading to copy scripture and secluded women studying medicine for female patients; the Inuit/Navaho counter-example and the warning that reading equality back into prehistory is "akin to the search for a primitive matriarchy." Classical and postclassical (600 BCE–1450): men-only academies, Han university, Tang exams, Timbuktu's Quranic schools; three routes to female learning (enlightened fathers: Ban Zhao; nuns: Therigatha, women hadith scholars of the 13th–15th c., Hildegard of Bingen; courtesans: kisang, hetaerae, geisha); "an eloquent woman is never chaste." Renaissance: artist-as-genius, art/craft split demoting embroidery (formerly a mixed-sex, guild-organised, painter-paid craft), exceptional women "judged a hermaphrodite," reading taught without writing. Modern: mass schooling, feminisation of teaching, 1970s "women's language" debate and the retreat from essentialism. Thin for the mod but gives the priestess-scholar (Hildegard), the women's-script idea (Heian kana), and the courtesan route to learning.

**Ch. 8 Sexuality (pp. 195–218).** Theory (acts vs identities, Foucault, Sedgwick's "Inheritance, Marriage, Dynasty" line); classical Eurasia (Athens: active/passive, perfection only in the male; Rome: wives/concubines/prostitutes, male prostitutes "not viewed as women, but... also not fully men"; Song China: eunuch-guarded palace, tolerated male homosexual subculture; India: sexual pleasure linked to spirituality; Japan/SE Asia: monks and acolytes, semen-ingestion initiations); the Americas (trial marriage, easy divorce, lifelong chastity "bizarre," Aztec priests in a woman's flayed skin, Inca acllas, boys' ear-piercing to bleed like girls); **the "Third Genders" section (pp. 204–6)**: two-spirit, the general rule that intersex-by-castration or culturally androgynous people "in almost all cultures... had special ceremonial or religious roles," Philippine baylans/catalonans, Bugis bissu guarding royal "white blood," **hijra as "impotent or castrated men dedicated to the goddess Bahuchara Mata" who bless marriages and births**, Omani xanith, Polynesian mahu; colonial horror at all of these; modern sexology, identity, trans movement, MTF/FTM, non-binarised gender. The single most important chapter for the castration-as-sacred and third-sex-priesthood mechanics.

**Afterword (pp. 224–227).** Personal reflection: objectivity vs agenda; Joan Scott's paradox that "in order to eliminate sexual difference, feminists first had to proclaim that difference"; Peter Stearns's caveat that sex equality is "a modern and not uncontested value"; "Investigating gender in the past is a political act." Useful only as a reminder of the (k) tension between abolishing and asserting the category.

### 2A. Gender systems, third/fourth genders, eunuchs (general and ethnographic)

**1. Third and fourth genders; age-graded genders** | worldwide (ethnographic, many extinct by 1900) | The book's intro states that most cultures are dichotomous but some develop a third or fourth gender; in some, gender is tied to reproductive status so children and the elderly are separate genders. It lists regions where people "assume the gender identity of the other sex" and notes that some are intersex or eunuchs. | Ch. 1, p. 4: "Though most of the world's cultures have a dichotomous view of gender, occasionally cultures develop a third or even a fourth gender. In some cultures, gender is determined by one's relationship to reproduction, so that adults are gendered male and female, but children and old people are regarded as different genders; in such cultures there are thus four genders, with linguistic, clothing, and behavioral distinctions for each one. In a number of areas throughout the world, including Alaska, the Amazon region, North America, Australia, Siberia, Central and South Asia, Oceania, and the Sudan, individuals who were originally viewed as male or female assume (or assumed, for in many areas such practices have ended) the gender identity of the other sex or combine the tasks, behavior, and clothing of men and women. Some of these individuals are intersexed and occasionally they are eunuchs (castrated males), but more commonly they are morphologically male or female." | Directly supports (a) a distinct sex class and (d) castrated men gendered otherwise: the book explicitly bundles eunuchs into third-gender categories. Siberia and Central/South Asia are on the CK3 map. The "gender determined by relationship to reproduction" model is a ready-made premodern rationale for the mod: a castrated person is outside the reproductive male role and is therefore re-gendered.

**2. Two-spirit people ("berdache")** | North America, pre-contact to 19th c. [off-map] | Europeans called them "berdaches" (from an Arabic word for male prostitute); scholars now say "two-spirit"; they are distinguished by work and religious roles, not sexual activity, and treated as a third gender. | Ch. 1, p. 4: "The best known of these are found among several Native American peoples, and the Europeans who first encountered them regarded them as homosexuals and called them 'berdaches,' from an Arabic word for male prostitute. Now most scholars choose to use the term 'two-spirit people,' and note that they are distinguished from other men more by their work or religious roles than by their sexual activities; they are usually thought of as a third gender rather than effeminate males or masculine women." | Model for the priesthood: a gender category constituted by *work and religious role*, not by sexual behaviour. Note the Arabic etymology ("bardaj") links the vocabulary to the Islamic world on the CK3 map. See also the Ch. 8 third-gender entries (#105–110).

**3. Gender attribution is cultural; intersex assignment; "gender determined sex"** | general; 19th-c. surgery | Historically intersex people were simply assigned the sex they most resembled; since the 19th c. surgery enforced the assignment: "gender determined sex." | Ch. 1, p. 3: "In earlier times most intersex people were simply assigned to the sex they most closely resembled, with their condition only becoming a matter of historical record if they came to the attention of religious, medical, or legal authorities. Since the nineteenth century this gender assignment was sometimes reinforced by surgical procedures modifying or removing the body parts that did not fit with the chosen gender. Thus in these cases 'gender' determined 'sex' rather than the other way around." Also p. 4: "day-to-day gender attribution is based everywhere on cultural norms rather than biology; not only are chromosomes and hormones not visible, but in most of the world's cultures clothing hides external genitalia." | Gives the mod a historically grounded principle: a society may let a social decision (rite, dress, role) determine sex. Supports (b) womanhood by rite and (j) bodily modification aligning body to assigned gender.

**4. Trans history as a movement project; the "women-only spaces" question** | 1950s–1990s (modern) | Sex-reassignment surgery from the 1950s; "transgendered" as neither/both from the 1990s; debates about women-only spaces; trans activists began doing their own history. | Ch. 1, pp. 4–5: "At what point in this process does a 'man' become a 'woman,' or vice versa? With the loss or acquisition of a penis? Breasts? From the beginning?" and "Should such individuals be allowed in spaces designated 'women only' or 'men only'? Should they have to choose between them, or should there be more than two choices?" Also p. 8: "A similar debate can be found within the contemporary trans movement, with some people arguing that gender and sexual orientation are fundamental aspects of identity and others that they are not or should not be." | Not a premodern precedent but a useful statement of the very questions the faith's doctrines answer (the faith answers: the rite is the moment). The intra-movement essentialism debate is a basis for (k) intra-queer hostility as a doctrinal schism.

### 2B. Matriarchy theory, matriliny, matrilocality, female inheritance, adoption

**5. The primitive-matriarchy thesis (Bachofen, Engels, Gimbutas, Diop, Amadiume)** | 19th–20th c. theory about prehistory and ancient Africa/Egypt | The book lays out the Bachofen/Engels two-stage story (matriarchy with common property -> private property -> patrilineal inheritance -> control of women's sexuality -> nuclear family -> state), Gimbutas's goddess-worshipping "Old Europe" overrun by Indo-European patriarchal steppe peoples after 4000 BCE, Diop on Egyptian queenship and Amadiume on matricentric households, and PRC Marxist historians. It notes most archaeologists reject Gimbutas but that Goddess movements have adopted her. | Ch. 1, p. 16: "Bachofen asserted that human society had originally been a matriarchy in which mothers were all-powerful; the mother–child bond was the original source of culture, religion, and community... In matriarchal cultures, goods were owned in common, but with the expansion of agriculture and animal husbandry men began to claim ownership of crops, animals, and land, thus developing the notion of private property. Once men had private property, in most cultures they established patrilineal inheritance systems in which property was passed down through the male line. They became very concerned about passing it on to their own heirs and attempted to control women's sexual lives to assure that offspring were legitimate." p. 17: "a process Engels describes as the 'world historical defeat of the female sex.'" p. 17: "Gimbutus argues that during the Paleolithic and Neolithic period, people living in Europe and the Mediterranean area were egalitarian, peaceful, and woman-centered, honoring the earth as a mother goddess. This 'Old Europe' was gradually overtaken through conquest and migration after 4000 bce by Indo-European speaking people who originated in the steppes of Russia. These new people were militaristic, seminomadic, and patriarchal, and they worshipped a single male god." | This is the single most useful ideological template in the book. A faith can hold as *myth* that there was an original mother-centred commons destroyed by property, patriliny and the male god, and that restoring adoptive matriliny and communal property undoes the "defeat". It fuses (g), (h), (l) and (m) in one origin story, and because the story is itself 19th-century it carries the Marxist content the user wants while sounding like a creation myth. Flag: Wiesner-Hanks presents it as contested/rejected scholarship.

**6. Lerner: women as the first property; patriarchy before class** | theory about ancient Mesopotamia | Lerner inverts Engels: women were the first property, exchanged for procreative power via marriage, prostitution and slavery, so patriarchy preceded class. Women excluded from direct links to the divine in Mesopotamian religion and Judaism. | Ch. 1, p. 18: "women, she argues, were the first property, exchanged for their procreative power by men with other men through marriage, prostitution, and slavery. Thus patriarchy preceded other forms of hierarchy and domination such as social classes, and women became primarily defined by their relation to men." and "Women were excluded from direct links to the divine in Mesopotamian religion and Judaism." | Supports (l) class-struggle framing in a sex-class register (sex as the first class). A tenet could hold that "the first theft was of the womb".

**7. Plow agriculture, patrilineal inheritance and sexual surveillance; matrilineal exception** | Neolithic onward, general | Patrilineal inheritance motivates control of women's sexuality; in matrilineal systems a man's heirs are his sister's children so surveillance is about honour not biology. | Ch. 1, pp. 18–19: "In the less common matrilineal systems a man's heirs are his sister's children, not his wife's, so such surveillance was clearly based more on cultural norms about family honor than on a 'biological' imperative to reproduce." | Key for (g): the book itself argues that non-patrilineal heirship removes the rationale for policing women's sexuality, which lets the mod's matrilineal faith plausibly also relax chastity/bastardy doctrines (CK3 bastardry doctrine = "no bastards"/"legitimization allowed").

**8. Egalitarian gatherer-hunter peoples** | !Kung, Mbuti, Innu (Montagnais-Naskapi); ethnographic | Cultures lacking plow agriculture, state, writing etc. appear egalitarian with tasks differentiated but equally valued. | Ch. 1, p. 20: "cultures in which most of these did not develop, such as the !Kung (Jul'hoansi) of South Africa, Mbuti of Zaire, or Innu (Montagnais-Naskapi) of Labrador, appear to be (or have been) quite egalitarian, with the tasks of men and women differentiated, but equally valued." | Weak direct precedent; useful as the faith's claim about the "pre-fall" condition.

**9. Khasi (NE India) and Mosuo/"Musuo" (SW China) matrifocal peoples** | India/China, continuing to today [Khasi: eastern edge of CK3 India map] | Women head households, own businesses, pass property and family name to daughters. | Ch. 2, p. 33: "Among the Khasi people in northwestern India and the Musuo in southwestern China, for example, women headed the households, owned businesses, and handed down property and the family name to their daughters. These matrifocal practices have continued to today." (Book says "northwestern"; the Khasi are in fact in Meghalaya, NE India.) Quote of Lakyntieth Lyndoh (1996): "Why should we be in such a hurry to give up our long-fought-for rights as independent women when most other women in the world are clamoring to increase theirs?" | Living matrilineal inheritance *and matrilineal surname transmission* on the map's edge. Supports (g) and (m). CK3 mapping: female-preference/female-only succession law plus a cultural tradition like "Matrilineal Houses" (dynasty name through mother).

**10. Matrilineal/matrilocal systems in Africa, the Americas and the Pacific (sister's children inherit; mother's brother; easy in-marriage of outsider men)** | 600 BCE–1600 CE, Africa/Americas/Pacific [West African coast is on the CK3 map] | Kin membership through the female line; a man's heirs are his sister's children; women depend on brothers rather than husbands; bridewealth and brideservice; matrilocal groups let outsider men marry in because they cannot claim wives' property; divorce easy, children stay with the mother. | Ch. 2, p. 34: "Many cultures in Africa, the Americas, and the Pacific were matrilineal, in which membership in a kin group is traced through the female line and a man's heirs were his sister's children. This did not necessarily mean that women were economically or legally autonomous, but that they depended on their brothers rather than their husbands." p. 35: "Matrilineal inheritance systems encouraged close lifelong relations among siblings, with women relying on their birth families for support if they came into conflict with their husbands. This was particularly true in groups that were also matrilocal, such as those in eastern North America, in which husbands came to live with their wives' clans and related women lived together. Relations with one's mother's kin were thus more important than those with one's father's kin or even one's spouse, and children often regarded their mother's brothers with particular respect. Matrilineal, matrilocal systems allowed for the relatively easy in-marriage of men from other groups, because they could not claim immediate control over their wives' property." p. 35 (divorce): "a man who wished to divorce simply left his wife's house, while a woman put her husband's belongings outside her family's house, indicating she wished him to leave; the children in both cases stayed with the mother and her family." | Core support for (g) matrilineal heirship and (i) non-marital kinship (husbands as peripheral). The "outsider men marry in without property claims" point gives a game rationale for matrilineal marriages being the faith's default and for low consanguinity anxiety. The "put his belongings outside" divorce is a flavourful event.

**11. Bilateral inheritance (Andes; Yako of Nigeria)** | premodern Andes; Nigeria [Nigeria is at the map's southern edge] | Andean descent reckoned through both sexes: girls inherit land/water/animal rights from mothers, boys from fathers; among the Yako only men inherit but from both fathers and mothers' brothers. | Ch. 2, p. 35: "Among many peoples living in the Andean region, for example, lines of descent were reckoned through both sexes, with girls inheriting access to resources such as land, water, and animals through their mothers, and boys through their fathers." | Precedent for parallel, sex-segregated inheritance lines: in the mod, a "daughters (including rite-made daughters) inherit from the mother-line" rule has an ethnographic analogue.

**12. Chinese adoption of a son by a sonless wife; women entered on the ancestral list only through a son** | Han China onward [CK3-adjacent, East Asia edge] | Women without sons "disappeared from family memory, unless they could arrange to adopt one, perhaps from a concubine or slave of their husband." | Ch. 2, p. 30: "Their names were inscribed on the official family list, and women's on the list of their marital families once they had a son; women who had no sons disappeared from family memory, unless they could arrange to adopt one, perhaps from a concubine or slave of their husband." | Precedent for adoption as a woman's instrument for securing a place in the lineage. For (g): the mod can invert it so that a woman's adopted *daughter* secures her memory.

**13. Fictive/spiritual kinship: adoption and godparentage** | general; medieval/early-modern Europe implied | The chapter's opening survey lists adoption and godparentage as creating "significant kinship-like ties (termed fictive or spiritual kinship)". | Ch. 2, p. 25: "Adoption or godparentage created significant kinship-like ties (termed fictive or spiritual kinship) in some places while in others only blood and marriage mattered." | Sanctions the mod's "adoptive matriline" as a known category (spiritual kinship). CK3 mapping: a doctrine allowing adoption/designation of heirs; flavour text can call it "kinship of the spirit, not of the blood".

**14. Levirate marriage; sons attributed to the dead husband** | classical cultures, general | A widow marries her husband's brother and her sons by him are legally the dead man's. | Ch. 2, p. 29: "In some cultures she was expected to marry the brother of her dead husband – a practice called levirate marriage – particularly if her husband had not had a son; her sons by her new husband were legally regarded as belonging to her deceased first husband." | Shows premodern law readily assigned legal parentage against biology. Supports the general principle behind (g): heirship is a legal fiction societies freely manipulate.

### 2C. Marriage forms: polygyny, concubinage, temporary marriage, divorce

**15. Resource polygyny; rulers' many wives as status; Islamic law makes concubines' children free** | Africa/SE Asia, 600 BCE–1600 CE; expansion of Islam [CK3-core for Sahel/Swahili/SE Asia edge] | Wealthy men had several wives or concubines; Islamic expansion supported it; children's status follows the father so all a free man's children are free. | Ch. 2, p. 34: "wealthy and powerful men married more than one wife or had several secondary wives or concubines along with a principal wife, a pattern often termed 'resource polygyny.' Rulers of states and villages had the most wives, concubines, slaves, and other types of female dependents as a sign of status. The expansion of Islam in Africa and Southeast Asia supported this pattern. Under Islamic law, the legal status of children followed that of their father, not their mother, so all the children born in the household of a free man would be free... Those born to free or slave concubines had a lesser status than those born to wives, but they were free." | Baseline for CK3's concubinage doctrine. The mod could flip the vector: a mother-centred house where a woman heads a household of several spouses/consorts ("resource polyandry" is not attested here, so flag as invention).

**16. African co-wives each own their own house, cattle and fields** | precolonial Africa [CK3-adjacent] | Each wife had her own house in the compound and her own property; the European idea that a wife's property is her husband's was not accepted. | Ch. 2, p. 34: "In much of Africa, families lived in house-compounds in which each wife had her own house; each wife also had her own cattle, fields, and property, for the notion that a wife's property actually belonged to her husband that became standard in Europe was not accepted in most of Africa." | Precedent for women's separate property within plural households; supports (h)/(i) partially: a plural household as a federation of women's holdings.

**17. Temporary marriage and debt-based alliance (SE Asia/Pacific)** | premodern SE Asia (Vietnam, Philippines) [SE Asia edge of map] | Cultures built on debt/obligation (òn, hiya) used gift-exchange including women to turn strangers into kin; marriages with ceremony and fidelity expectations but understood as temporary and ended on departure or a year's absence. | Ch. 2, p. 36: "These unions were often accompanied by a marriage ceremony and the expectation of spousal fidelity, but they were also understood to be temporary. If the spouses disagreed with one another or the man was from elsewhere and returned to his home country, the marriage ended, just as marriages between local spouses ended if there was conflict or one spouse disappeared for a year or more." | Precedent for (i) non-permanent kinship bonds; CK3 mapping: divorce doctrine "allowed" plus a cultural tradition of temporary/renewable unions. Also shows marriage as an alliance tool detached from permanence.

**18. Sparta: trial marriage, divorce and remarriage for fertility, women own property, male same-sex bonds militarily expedient** | classical Sparta [CK3-adjacent, Greece] | Trial marriage to test fertility; women free, athletic, property-owning; male homosexuality accepted as fostering fierce fighting. | Ch. 2, p. 31: "Marriage often began with a trial marriage period to make sure the couple could have children, with divorce and remarriage the normal course if they were unsuccessful. In contrast to India, despite the emphasis on procreation, homosexuality was widely accepted, with male same-sex relationships in particular viewed as militarily expedient, leading men to fight more fiercely in defense of their lovers and comrades." | An ancient Greek precedent (in Byzantine territory) for a culture that combines women's economic autonomy with accepted same-sex bonds; useful for flavour on Byzantine/Greek regions.

**19. Roman marriage: divorce at either spouse's instigation; mothers as educators** | late Republic Rome | Divorce possible at the instigation of either husband or wife; mothers idealised as educators and so educated. | Ch. 2, p. 32: "by the late Republic (first century bce), divorce was possible at the instigation of either the husband or wife. Romans also idealized the role of the mother, viewing women as important in their children's education and thus worthy of an education themselves." | Supports a divorce-allowed doctrine with Roman pedigree.

**20. Orthodox Christian divorce; Jewish divorce; canon-law annulment** | medieval eastern and western Europe [CK3-core] | Orthodox churches gradually allowed divorce for adultery, abuse, abandonment, impotence, barrenness; Judaism allowed divorce; western elites used annulments. | Ch. 2, p. 37: "In eastern Europe, the Orthodox Christian churches gradually came to allow divorce for adultery, abuse, abandonment, impotence, and barrenness, with both spouses allowed (somewhat grudgingly) to remarry." p. 38: "Judaism did allow divorce, which was then sometimes justified on the grounds that the spouses had obviously not been predestined for each other." | CK3-period precedent for the divorce doctrine inside Christendom; "impotence" as grounds is relevant to a castration-practising faith (it must explicitly reject impotence as grounds).

**21. Single-sex religious communities as an honourable alternative to the family; widows admitted** | medieval Europe, 500–1600 [CK3-core] | Early Christian thinkers hostile to the family; single-sex communities took in widows and the never-married, providing "a safe and honorable place for women who chose not to marry." | Ch. 2, p. 36: "Early Christian thinkers were often hostile to the family, viewing virginity as the preferred form of existence; alternatives to family life, in which men and women lived in single-sex communities dedicated to service to God, developed in most parts of Europe. In some areas, these communities took in widows as well as never-married people, providing a safe and honorable place for women who chose not to marry or not to remarry, or whose families made this decision for them." | Precedent for (f) separatism in a women-only community form inside the CK3 period. Convents = women-only space with its own property. More on this in Ch. 5.

**22. NW European late-marriage pattern; 10–25% never married; laws against the unmarried living alone** | 1400–1800 NW Europe [CK3-adjacent, late period] | Unique late marriage; many never married; religious reformers urged everyone to marry and laws forbade unmarried people to live on their own. | Ch. 2, p. 38: "Demographers estimate that between 10 and 15 percent of the northwestern European population never married in the fifteenth through the eighteenth centuries, and that in some places this figure may have been as high as 25 percent." and "political leaders passed laws forbidding unmarried people to live on their own." | Shows the unmarried as a demographic bloc that states tried to suppress: a mod faith that honours the unmarried (and castrated) would be in friction with that policy, good for hostility mechanics.

### Mapping onto the CK3 doctrine and tradition menus

- **Gender doctrine**: *Female-dominated* is defensible from #71–77 (magajiya, Aminatu, sultanahs, Begums) and carries (m); *Equal* is the conservative fit (#70 Iroquois ratification caveat). Recommended default: Female-dominated for the "Proclaimer" variant, Equal for the "Abolisher" variant (T11).
- **Clerical gender**: *Only women* (abbesses #63, zar healers #53, women's mosques #66, iya #71), with scripted exception that twice-born count as women (T1). CK3 reads sex from the character record, so T1 must actually flip `sex`/use a flag the clergy-eligibility trigger checks.
- **Clerical marriage / function**: clergy *may* marry (Taoist, Islamic, bhakti precedent #59, #65, #60) but the Rite makes them childless (#69); clerical function "Alms and Pacification" or "Recruitment" to model fertility/legitimacy blessing (T5).
- **Consanguinity**: *Unrestricted* or *Cousin marriage* is the historically neutral choice; because heirs are adoptive (T3) the biological rationale for consanguinity rules collapses (#7), so "Unrestricted" with an in-world justification is coherent; #73 (pharaonic sibling marriage "to increase the amount of divinity") shows a sacral rationale existed.
- **Bastardry**: *No bastards* (all a mother's children, natal or adopted, are legitimate), from #7 (matriliny removes legitimacy anxiety), #10 (children stay with the mother), #15 (Islamic law: all children of a free man are free, inverted to the mother).
- **Divorce**: *Allowed* (#10, #19, #20, #103); must explicitly reject "impotence" as grounds (#20).
- **Polygamy / concubines**: Vanilla offers male-centred *Polygamy* or *Concubines* (#15 resource polygyny). A woman-headed plural household has no precedent in the book; if used, flag as invention (see (i) verdict) or restrict to *Monogamy* + Allowed divorce + T6 for plausibility.
- **Adoption**: CK3 has no vanilla adoption doctrine; T3 needs a scripted "Adopt Daughter" interaction (precedent #12, #13). Succession laws: use *Female-preference* or *Female-only* succession (unlocked by the Female-dominated/Equal gender doctrine) and consider the *Equal Inheritance* cultural tradition; dynasty-name transmission through the mother (#9 Khasi) needs a custom tradition ("Matrilineal Houses").
- **Castration parameter**: vanilla gates the prison "Castrate" interaction behind a faith/culture parameter; T1 should grant that parameter *and* add a voluntary, consensual rite interaction distinct from the punitive one, since the book's only non-sacred castrations are punitive (#25) or parental-career (#69). Keep the "Eunuch" trait but add a faith-specific trait/flag ("Twice-Born") so hostile faiths treat the character as eunuch while the faith treats her as a woman (#96 vs #107 logic).
- **Equal/female-only inheritance traditions**: pair *Equal Inheritance* with T3; for Hausa/Sahel and SE Asian cultures, the female-rule precedents (#71–72, #77) justify *Female-preference* succession as a *cultural* default even for other faiths in those regions.
- **Hostility settings**: make neighbouring Christian/Islamic/Aristotelian faiths *Hostile* or *Evil* with opinion text drawn from Section 4E (#83, #79, #96, #111); the two internal variants (T11) *Astray* toward each other.
