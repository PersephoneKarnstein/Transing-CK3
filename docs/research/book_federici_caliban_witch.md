# Federici, *Caliban and the Witch* (2004): design reading

**Citation.** Silvia Federici, *Caliban and the Witch: Women, the Body and Primitive Accumulation*. Brooklyn, NY: Autonomedia, 2004 (first edition; this copy is the third printing, 2009). ISBN 1-57027-059-7. The copyright page declares the text anti-copyright and says it "may be freely reproduced for non-commercial purposes" (l.62-63). The subtitle is not on the scanned title page; it is the book's published subtitle. The book grew out of *Il Grande Calibano* (Milan: Franco Angeli, 1984), written with Leopoldina Fortunati (Preface, l.106-110).

**What was read.** From a plain-text OCR of the PDF, kept outside the repo (`scratchpad/books/tr/16.txt`, 12,167 lines). Cited as "l." (line of that file) and "p." (printed page, from the page numbers in the OCR; a page number marks the end of its page).

- *In full:* ch. 1, "All the World Needs a Jolt: Social Movements and Political Crisis in Medieval Europe" (pp. 21-59, l.698-2818), with its 33 endnotes; ch. 4, "The Great Witch-Hunt in Europe" (pp. 163-217, l.8239-11078), with its 41 endnotes.
- *Triaged by headings and keyword search:* front matter and table of contents (l.1-104); Preface and Introduction (pp. 7-20); ch. 2, "The Accumulation of Labor and the Degradation of Women" (pp. 61-132: enclosures, Price Revolution, population decline, "devaluation of women's labor", "patriarchy of the wage", colonies); ch. 3, "The Great Caliban" (pp. 133-162: mechanical philosophy and the body); ch. 5, "Colonization and Christianization" (pp. 219-243). **Quotes.** Verbatim, 25 words or fewer. The rest is paraphrase. No book text beyond these short quotations is kept in the repo.

## 1. The argument in brief

**Thesis.** Capitalism was not a natural growth out of feudalism. It was a counter-revolution by lords, merchants and clergy against two centuries of peasant and artisan revolt (ch. 1, l.733-736, p. 21). The witch-hunt of c. 1550-1650 was one of its main tools. It broke women's control over reproduction and their healing, sexual and communal power, and it put the female body to work as a machine for producing labour (ch. 4, l.9378-9382, p. 184). The Marxist account of "primitive accumulation" (enclosures, colonies, the slave trade) is incomplete without it.

**Ch. 1 (medieval Europe, c. 1100-1450).**
- Serfdom (from the 5th-7th c.) bound peasants but gave them land and the commons, so women in the servile household had a share in the land and their work was not yet split off as unpaid "housework" (l.808-949, pp. 23-25).
- Money rents from the 13th c. split the peasantry and pushed poor women, widows and single women into the towns, where many found work and some autonomy (l.1183-1290, pp. 29-31).
- Popular heresy (Cathars, Waldenses, Free Spirit, Apostolics, later Lollards and Taborites) was the poor's protest movement: against Church property, tithes, paid sacraments and hierarchy. In Federici's phrase it was "the equivalent of 'liberation theology' for the medieval proletariat" (l.1388-1389, p. 33).
- Women had a high place in the sects, could preach and administer sacraments, and lived alongside men as "brothers and sisters" or in their own communities, such as the Beguines (l.1643-1683, pp. 38-39).
- The heretics' refusal of marriage and procreation may have been, she suggests, a medieval form of birth control. After the Black Death (1347-52) and the labour crisis, heresy came to be charged with "sodomy", infanticide and abortion (l.1534-1556, p. 36).
- After the Black Death wages rose and serfdom ended. The authorities answered with a "sexual politics" that divided the poor: tolerated rape of lower-class women in French towns and municipal brothels from 1350 to 1450, also aimed at "sodomy" (l.2192-2309, pp. 47-49).
- The persecution of heresy turned into the persecution of witches: "the figure of the heretic increasingly became that of a woman" by the early 15th c. (l.1731-1733, p. 40).

**Ch. 4 (the great witch-hunt, c. 1430-1700).**
- The early Middle Ages did not hunt witches. The Germanic codes of the 7th-8th c. punished only *maleficium* that did harm, and the Church criticised belief in magic (l.8389-8404, p. 165; n. 7 on the Canon *Episcopi*, l.10634-10669).
- The first trials and Sabbat descriptions come in the 15th c. Twenty-eight treatises were written between 1435 and 1487, ending in the *Malleus Maleficarum* (1486) after Innocent VIII's bull (1484). The peak came in 1580-1630, run mostly by secular courts (l.8405-8448, pp. 165-166).
- The accused were mostly poor, old, often widowed women. The accusers were their richer neighbours and employers. Federici reads the hunt as class war fought by other means (l.8725-8748, l.9021, pp. 171, 176).
- Reproductive crimes (contraception, abortion, infanticide) became central. Midwives were suspected and then put under state control (l.9226-9377, pp. 180-184).
- The witch was also the "loose" woman and the woman who talked back. Laws of the same years punished adultery, prostitution, concealed pregnancy and female friendship (l.9395-9483, pp. 184-186).
- The Devil changed from a weak trickster into a husband and master. The witch could unman men, and non-procreative sex (between men, between young and old, between classes) was banned (l.9498-9615, l.9900-10016, pp. 186-197).
- The colonies and Europe exchanged techniques and images of the "devilish" other (l.10072-10148, pp. 198-200).
- The hunt ended when the ruling class felt secure, not because of the new science (l.10227-10432, pp. 201-205).

**What Federici says about the in-window eastern heresies.** Three points, all thin:
- The Paulicians are named once as "a sect of iconoclasts who rejected procreation", after Erbstösser 1984 (l.1490-1492, p. 35).
- The Bogomils "proselytized in the 10th century among the peasantry of the Balkans", taught that the visible world is the devil's work, and refused to bring children into this "land of tribulations" (l.1492-1503, p. 35). Through Browning she quotes Cosmas: "They teach their own people not to obey their masters, they revile the wealthy, hate the king" (n. 24, l.2650-2651, p. 56).
- She says the Cathars took two books over from the Bogomils, and that "buggery" (from "Bulgar") meant first heresy and then sex between men "by the 13th century" (n. 24, l.2643-2660, p. 56, after Bullough 1976).

She has nothing on Byzantine eunuchs, on Montanists, or on any Byzantine response to the Paulicians other than Cosmas's words.

## 4. Usable material

### 4.1 Heresy as a network (ch. 1)
- The sects had "contacts, schools, and safe-houses" across borders. They travelled by fairs and pilgrimages, and spread as refugees from the persecution kept crossing borders (l.1396-1406, p. 33). The Houses' `tm_send_on_effect` already sends residents of a closed House on to another; Federici's point is that the refugee *carries* the faith (§5 row 3).
- Women in the sects. Federici, after Volpe: "in the Church women were nothing, but here they were considered equal" (l.1647-1648, p. 38). Men and women of the sects shared houses unmarried, "like brothers and sisters" (l.1655-1658). Overstated: see §7.
- The Waldenses split in 1218. The Poor of Lyon lived on alms; the Lombards formed workers' collectives and kept private houses and marriage (n. 20, l.2554-2561, p. 54). This is a period model for two kinds of House economy (alms against work), which `D1`/`D2` already gesture at.
- The Beguines were laywomen living together by their own work, without a monastic rule. They were condemned at Vienne in 1312 and "forced out of existence by ecclesiastical reprobation" (Neel, quoted in n. 27, l.2691-2697, p. 57).
- The Church called those who opposed the death penalty heretics (1210). Every heretic who returned to the Church had to affirm that secular power may shed blood (n. 23, l.2634-2642).

### 4.2 Hostility to heretics: one charge, two shapes
- Heretics were "persecuted both as extreme ascetics and as libertines" (l.1527-1528, p. 36): too chaste (they refuse marriage and children) and too loose (they meet at night, men and women together).
- After the Black Death the charges grew sexual and reproductive: sodomy, then animal worship, night flights and child-killing (l.1722-1733, p. 40).
- The heresy charge was turned on labour and tithe revolts: the Ypres weavers (1377), the Bremen tenants (1234), women weavers threatened with excommunication for late delivery (l.1441-1451, p. 34).
- Cosmas on the Bogomils (via Browning): they also mock the elders, condemn the boyars and forbid serfs to work for their lords (n. 24, l.2650-2653, p. 56; paraphrase, the OCR is damaged there).

### 4.3 Church control of sex, in window
- From the 7th c. the Penitentials prescribed positions, days and partners (l.1586-1614, pp. 37-38).
- 1123 and 1139: clerical marriage banned; 1123 declared existing clerical marriages invalid, leaving priests' wives and children destitute (n. 25, l.2661-2674, p. 56).
- 1179: Lateran III condemned "the incontinence which is against nature" (l.1628, p. 38).
- 12th-century canons forbade marital sex in Lent, on feast days, during menstruation, pregnancy and nursing (n. 26, l.2675-2689).

### 4.4 Contraception and the "sterility" charge
- The Penitentials called contraceptives "Sterility potions" or *maleficia* and assumed women used them (l.1688-1693, p. 39, after Noonan 1965).
- Burchard's *Decretum* (c. 1010) set ten years' penance, but "it makes a big difference whether she is a poor little woman and" acted from hunger (l.1714-1715, p. 40).
- After 1347 that indulgence ended (l.1717-1733).
- By the witch-hunt, the old witch and the prostitute were both "symbols of sterility" (l.10024, p. 197). The saying "a prostitute when young, a witch when old" (l.10019-10020) is 16th-17th century.

### 4.5 The witch-hunt, as negative space
Almost all of ch. 4 is after 1453. It is useful as a list of what the mod should *not* project back:
- In the trials "ill repute" was evidence of guilt (l.9399, p. 184).
- The word "gossip", which in the Middle Ages had meant "friend", turned derogatory (l.9480-9483, p. 186). The Old English source word is in window; see §5 row 8.
- The Devil became a husband and master who renamed the witch (l.9536-9547, p. 187).
- The witch could unman men (l.9578-9584, p. 188). This is *Malleus* material (1486).
- Old women were denied a sexual life (l.9807-9873, pp. 192-194).
- Federici's own summary: "The witch-hunt, then, was a war against women" (l.9456, p. 186).

### 4.6 Things in the book about gender crossing (all brief)
- Clergy who tried to take over women's life-giving and magical powers by adopting women's dress (l.1577-1578, p. 37, paraphrase, after Condren 1989). This is an Irish early-Christian reading by Condren, not evidence. Do not use.
- Dolcinian women fought in men's dress beside the men, 1304-07 (l.1779-1786, p. 41; OCR damaged). Women in men's dress for war: reaction data only.
- Florentine prostitutes wore men's clothes to attract clients, in a city where sex between men was common (n. 32, l.2764-2765, after Rocke). In early-15th-c. Florence, preachers feared a city "in which females and males seemed the same" (n. 32, l.2794-2798, Trexler). Context for Rykener; nothing to build.
- Witches in morality plays and fabliaux are described "wearing male clothes" (l.9411, p. 184).
- In the colonies, "sodomy, cannibalism, incest, cross dressing" became charges against the colonised (ch. 5, l.11249, p. 221). This is after 1492.

None of these is a trans woman, and the book never discusses one. Eunuchs and castrates appear only as the *Malleus* fear of witches who castrate.

## 7. Cautions

**Federici's contested claims.** The book is a polemic of political theory. Its medieval chapters rest on older secondary works, not on archives. Do not cite it as the authority for a fact the mod states in the narrator's voice; find the primary source or a recent specialist.

- **Death tolls.** The text says "hundreds of thousands of women" were killed (l.8596, l.8328-8329). Her own note 3 gives Barstow's estimate of about 200,000 accused and at least 100,000 killed (l.10557-10570). Recent specialist estimates (Levack, Behringer) are roughly 40,000-60,000 executions across Europe, about three-quarters women, and fewer in Inquisition lands. She admits that men were up to 40% of the accused in the early phase (l.9196-9201). Nothing in the mod depends on these figures; do not quote any of them.
- **The midwife thesis.** She says midwives were singled out (l.9321-9377). Her own note 24 cites Monica Green's challenge (l.10860-10873). David Harley ("Historians as Demonologists: The Myth of the Midwife-Witch", 1990) found midwives under-represented among the accused. Keep midwives out of any hostile "witch" line.
- **Contraception as heresy and as birth control.** That the heretics' anti-natalism was a form of birth control is her conjecture ("it is conceivable", l.1544). Her claim that women's contraceptive knowledge vanished in the 17th c. (n. 26) is disputed. Row 5 rests only on the Penitentials and Burchard (via Noonan), not on her conjecture.
- **Primitive accumulation and periodisation.** Reading the witch-hunt as class war and as a policy of population is her thesis. She calls the population argument "a hypothesis" herself (l.9304). Macfarlane later withdrew the Essex enclosure link (her n. 14, l.10762-10766). Do not build an "accumulation" mechanic on it.
- **Women's place in the sects.** Volpe (1922/1971) and Koch (1962) are older works. Later studies (e.g. Abels and Harrison 1979 on Languedoc depositions) find women numerous among Cathar believers but under-represented among the perfected and rarely preaching. "Considered equal" is too strong. The Cathar "Lady of Thought" behind Dante's Beatrice (Taylor 1954, l.1654-1655) and a Cathar sexual sacrament ("Christeria", l.1525-1526) are unsupported in the text and should not be used.
- **Cathars and Kabbalah.** The claim that Cathar and Jewish thought fused into Kabbalah (Spencer 1995, l.1484-1486) is not the view of Kabbalah scholarship. Keep it out of Ha-Nehpakhot (row 9).
- **Paulician anti-natalism.** It rests on Erbstösser 1984 alone. Garsoïan (1967, 1971) argues that the Byzantine sources' "Manichaean" picture is largely polemic. Read before use (row 1).
- **Sodomy and heresy.** The "buggery"/Bulgar link is standard etymology, but the claim that the heretics' practices included "sodomy" as birth control is hers. The inquisitors' sexual charges were formulaic (she says so herself, l.9149-9152). Treat every sexual charge against a heretic as an accuser's charge.
- **Other weak points.** The *ius primae noctis* (l.920-922) is a later myth. The "faggot" and *finocchio* stories (l.10012-10016) are folk etymology. Rossiaud's reading of gang rape as tolerated policy, and of brothels as a "sex card" against revolt (l.2196-2311), is one historian's interpretation. Her Condren passage on clergy taking women's dress (l.1577-1578) is not evidence about any medieval clergy.

**Standing rules.**
- **No antagonist nation.** The Inquisition, the Albigensian Crusade and the witch-hunt are vanilla-world institutions. They may appear only as era pressure from existing faiths (rows 4-7). No "Inquisition" polity, no scripted crusade against the Sisters beyond the user-approved comedy casus belli (§0).
- **No suicide or self-harm content.** Barstow's prison deaths are quoted in n. 3 (l.10563-10567). Leave them out.
- **No torture or execution on screen.** This covers Dolcino and Margherita (l.1793-1797), the stake, the pricking and the Kirkcaldy bill (n. 9). None of it goes into loc, even paraphrased.
- **No romantic "third gender" framing.** The book has none. Do not create one from the cross-dressing passages (§4.6).
- **Trans men out of scope.** The Dolcinian and Taborite fighting women are women in men's dress; they are not a model for anything.
- **No living community as a faith.** WITCH (1968) and Wicca/Murray (l.9250-9264; n. 2, n. 22) are modern movements. Federici rejects Murray's "ancient fertility cult" thesis herself (l.9261-9264). No faith claims a witch-cult ancestry.
- **The narrator never claims historical figures.** Margherita, Dolcino, the Beguines and the Bogomils are not the Sisters' foremothers in the narrator's voice. A faith may name them in its own voice only if a proposal above says so, and none does.
- **She/her for trans women.** No passage in this book names one.
