# Goodall, "Rudragaṇikās: Courtesans in Śiva's Temple? Some Hitherto Neglected Sanskrit Sources" (2018): design reading

Source: Dominic Goodall, "Rudragaṇikās: Courtesans in Śiva's Temple? Some Hitherto Neglected Sanskrit Sources", *Cracow Indological Studies* 20.1 (2018): 91–143. DOI 10.12797/CIS.20.2018.01.06. Open access. PDF in `docs/research/sources_pending/south/india/`.
Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo. Page numbers are the journal's.

**What was read.** In full: the summary, §§1–6 and all footnotes (pp. 91–136). The Sanskrit texts and critical apparatus were read through Goodall's translations. Skipped: the bibliography (pp. 136–143).

**Relation to existing notes.** `ind_orr_temple_women.md` reads the Tamil *inscriptions* (850–1300) and finds donors, not dancers, with "daughter of God" (*tēvaṉār makaḷ*) an early Śaiva honorific. Goodall reads the Sanskrit *prescriptive* texts of the same Śaiva temples (7th–16th c.). He shows that the Śaiva ritual texts have their own "daughter of Rudra" and a real initiation rite. He also disagrees with Orr on dance and sex. `ind_merkle_tying_knot.md` already warns off the modern "always married" (*nityasumaṅgalī*) vocabulary. Goodall gives its textual history.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **Goodall's thesis.** Temple slavery, dance and courtesanship formed a single nexus that was "long been widespread across and beyond the Indian subcontinent" (p. 133), with much local variation. Goodall builds this from evidence that is scattered and individually inconclusive (pp. 96–98).
- **This is against Orr.** He names the two camps: those who will not project devadāsī practices into the Chola past (Orr) and those who think there are too many clues to deny them (p. 98). He leans to the second camp. He also says it is impossible to truly understand "how courtesans lived and were perceived" in 12th-century Madurai, Chidambaram or Srirangam (p. 131). Orr helped correct the article (p. 91 n.).
- **The South Indian Temple Āgamas** (from the 12th c.) are not neutral descriptions. They regulate big temple cities so as to justify the rights of the Ādiśaiva priests (pp. 106, 131–132). Their origin myths for the dancers are part of that rhetoric (p. 132).
- **The texts are badly transmitted.** None of the key passages has a critical edition (pp. 133–135). His longest passage is a 16th-c. quotation ascribed to a *Kāraṇāgama* and not found in the printed *Kāraṇa* (p. 133).

### 2.2 How a woman came to Śiva's temple (pp. 95–96, 114–117)

- ***Śivadharmottara* 2.165** lists five ways: given, bought, "entered [voluntarily]" (*praviṣṭāḥ*), produced as payment of a fine, or brought by force. All of these are "courtesans of Rudra" (*Rudragaṇikās*), women of Śiva's temple (p. 96).
- **2.166.** A Rudragaṇikā's children go to heaven "by the power of [her as] their mother" (p. 96). Goodall reads this as the mother's service being enough to save them (n. 8). **Merit runs through the mother.** This matches Orr's finding that temple women passed the merit of their gifts to daughters (Orr p. 82).
- **The "Kāraṇa" list has seven kinds**: Given, Bought, Servant, Devotee, Brought (from another temple), Alaṃkāradāsikā (a trained dancer placed by the king), and "Daughter of Rudra" (p. 114).
  - **"Devotee" is self-chosen.** She comes to Śiva's temple herself with the thought "I must be a Dāsī" (p. 116).
  - **Branding.** "Servant", "Devotee" and the others are branded (pp. 116–117). The Tamil gloss adds turmeric milk and branding for "Given" (p. 115 n. 31). It is not said whether the Rudragaṇikā was branded (p. 115 n. 31).
  - "Brought" covers women moved from one temple to another "because of a period of troubles" (p. 116). This echoes Rājarāja's transfer.

### 2.3 "Daughter of Rudra": the Sanskrit twin of *tēvaṉār makaḷ* (pp. 109–110, 121–125)

- In the 12th-c. *Uttara-Kāmika*, the women who dance in Śiva's temple are **daughters of Rudra**, not wives. Goodall links this directly to the Tamil *tēvaṉār makaḷ* (p. 110).
- **The myth** (73.4–7; pp. 123–125). Śiva enters the Deodar forest in a beautiful body. The sages' wives conceive merely by exchanging gazes with him. Their children are born on the spot and ask, "What will be our livelihood?" Śiva makes them his temple dancers.
- **Rudrakanyā and Rudragaṇikā merge.** "Daughter of Rudra" and "courtesan of Rudra" sound alike in Tamil-script spellings (*rutrakaṉṉikai* / *rutrakaṇikai*). This helped the Āgamas "re-frame Rudra's courtesans as both Rudra's daughters" and celestial nymphs (pp. 121–122). The *Śivadharma* corpus still kept the two apart (p. 122).
- **"Superior to brahmins."** Verse 73.3 says that others call the daughters of Rudra superior to brahmins. Goodall takes the attribution to "others" as marking an exaggeration (p. 124 and n. 43).

### 2.4 The initiation: Nandin's staff (pp. 110–112, 132 n. 56)

- **The rite** (*Uttara-Kāmika* 73.12–14; pp. 110–111). Śiva borrows the staff of his watchman Nandin and touches each woman on the head.
  - He names her Nanda, or with a name beginning in Nanda-.
  - He gives her the title *śirodaṇḍinī*, "she who was touched on the head with the staff".
- **This is the Tamil title *talaikkōli*.** Goodall says *śirodaṇḍa* is a literal translation of *talaikkōl* (p. 111). Orr found that title on Chola temple women (Orr pp. 60–61). It goes back to the baton venerated before Mātavi's dance in the *Cilappatikāram*, and to Indra's staff (*jarjara*) in the *Nāṭyaśāstra*. The Kāmika "cunningly draws together" that whole tradition and makes the staff Śiva's watchman's (p. 112).
- **Who performs it** (73.32–34; p. 132 n. 56).
  - The guru sets the staff down pointing east or north.
  - He venerates Nandin in it with scents and flowers. He wears fresh clothes, a garland and a turban, and has sandal on his body. He must be an Ādiśaiva and must have received a fee of five *niṣkas* or more.
  - He touches the head and other parts of each woman, and lays the mantra *śivāya namaḥ* on her head.
- **Age.** The *Uttara-Kāmika* allows initiation of gaṇikās from five to fifty. Goodall notes that this means "there is no need for them to be nubile" (p. 126 n. 45).
- **A low-status rite.** The rite needs no direct physical contact with the officiant, and that marks it as an initiation for people of low status in the priests' eyes (p. 112).
- No initiation is mentioned for the lower *dāsīs* (p. 112).

### 2.5 Their work, dress and rank (pp. 118–121, 127, 130)

- **Work.** Rudragaṇikās do "no scrubbing and such"; their work is waving lamps (*ārātra*), singing and dancing (v. 13; p. 118 and n. 34). The other devadāsīs scrub, sprinkle, grind, bring rice, clean vessels and play instruments (vv. 14–15).
- **Dress.** They dress like Brahmin ladies and wear the triple bar of ash from their Śaiva initiation (v. 20; p. 120).
- **Large troupes.** The *Pūrva-Kāmika* allows troupes of 10, 24, 34, 50 or 216 dancing women at the richest temples, dancing three times a day (p. 127).
- **Their part was auspicious.** The *Kumāratantra* warns that missing lamps at the spectacle cause blindness and faulty music causes crop failure. If a gaṇikā falls while dancing, the organiser suffers (p. 130).
- **They also served Viṣṇu.** Gaṇikās also appear in Vaiṣṇava Pāñcarātra texts as "gaṇikās of Viṣṇu", and in Skanda temples (p. 107). The Pāñcarātra texts also separate gaṇikās from devadāsīs (p. 120 n. 38).

### 2.6 Marriage to the god: the later layer (pp. 125–127, 135–136)

- **None in the 12th-c. myth or rite.** Neither the myth nor the initiation in *Uttara-Kāmika* 73 suggests that the dancers were "married" to the god (p. 127).
- **Later.** The "Kāraṇa" quotation (16th-c. witness) makes the first dancers the sages' wives themselves. It requires the marriage thread (*māṅgalyasūtra*) in Śiva's presence, which implies they are now his spouses (v. 19; pp. 120, 125).
- The undated "Sūkṣma" fragment has a wedding (*kalyāṇa*) at ages five to ten, before initiation (p. 126 n. 45).
- **Soneji's reading** (quoted at p. 126). The dedication was often a marriage to the god's weapon: the dāsī "marries his spear, just as a concubine does not marry King Śivājī, but his sword".
- **Against Brunner.** Goodall concludes that some Āgamic basis for marriage to the god does exist, contrary to Brunner (pp. 135–136). This is post-12th c. and of uncertain date.

### 2.7 Wealth (pp. 127–131)

- Goodall's picture is of women who were "respected, wealthy and cultivated benefactress[es]", freer than most women of their time, though morally disturbing to many and ritually impure to the strict (p. 131).
- *Kāmasūtra* 6.5 lists building temples, tanks and gardens, and endowing worship, as the surplus spending of the best courtesans. A commentator adds that brahmins took their gifts only through another's hand (pp. 129–130).

### 2.8 Kanchipuram in the article (pp. 108, 113)

- Fig. 3 shows the Deodar-forest myth on a panel of the Kailāsanātha temple at Kanchipuram.
- Fig. 5 shows Śiva and Nandin dancing there.

These are images only. Goodall cites no Kanchipuram text on Rudragaṇikās.

## 3. Corrections to the candidate

1. **"Daughter of God" is Śaiva twice over.** It is a 9th–10th-c. Tamil inscriptional honorific (Orr), and a 12th-c. Sanskrit Śaiva ritual-text title, Rudrakanyā (pp. 110, 121–125). The narrator should never present it as Vaiṣṇava.
2. **A real initiation exists, and it is Śaiva.** The touch of Nandin's staff on the head, the name and the title (*talaikkōli*) are 12th-c. prescriptive text (pp. 110–112). The faith may adopt the *form*, a staff laid on the head by a keeper of the gate, in its own voice. It must not adopt the priests' fee, the Ādiśaiva monopoly or the child-age clause.
3. **Do not import the "courtesan" frame.** Goodall's own argument rests on *gaṇikā* (courtesan), the *Kuṭṭanīmata* and the Meghadūta's "scratches" (pp. 96, 103–105). The brief forbids sexualising the temple street, and Orr's inscriptions show donors. Use Goodall for rite, rank and dating, never for the women's sexuality.
4. **Slavery is in the source.** "Given", "Bought" and "Brought by force", together with branding, are prescriptive categories (pp. 95–96, 114–117). The Garland's line must not originate there; Orr's note already rules out the slave-sale records. Only the self-chosen "Devotee" (p. 116) and the voluntary "entered" (p. 96) are usable, and both were still branded.
5. **"Marriage to God" is post-12th c. even in the texts.** Goodall moves Orr's "17th c." earlier only to an undated Āgamic layer quoted in the 16th c. (pp. 125–127). The Garland still cannot claim the temple women as brides in period.
6. **The ritual texts list eunuchs among Śiva's slaves** (*napuṃsaka*; 2.164, p. 95). This is the *napuṃsaka* category that Bhatt warns about. Do not read it as a home for the faith.

## 4. Design implications

**Gate step (Śaiva form inside the crossing).** "The staff on the head."
- After the dream, a keeper of the temple gate, framed as Nandin's office, lays a staff on her head and gives her a name. The faith's version is "a name the hymns give her". This is her public marking.
- Grade: *attested in period* as the Śaiva rite form (12th c.); *reconstruction* as a rite for the faith.
- Option text in the faith's voice: "Śiva's watchman lent his staff to name the daughters; we borrow it back."
- The narrator flags the rite as an Āgamic dancers' initiation.

**Title.** "She of the Staff" (*talaikkōli* / *śirodaṇḍinī*). It is attested on Chola temple women (Orr) and is the Sanskrit rite's name (p. 111). Grant it on the first public honour.

**Merit through the mother.** A modifier on a Garland daughter's children: "saved by their mother's service" (*Śivadharmottara* 2.166, p. 96). It fits the line, where shares pass to daughters.

**Rank among temple women.** An event can show the hierarchy: lamp-wavers and singers above scrubbers and grinders (p. 118). Option: a Garland daughter insists on taking the menial work too. The source lets her choose (vv. 16–17, p. 119, though Goodall is unsure of the sense, n. 37).

**Doctrine line (faith's voice).** "Śiva's priests call us his daughters; Viṣṇu's call us his bride. We answer to both." This leads into the crossing argued in `ind_govindacharya_azhvars.md` §5.

**Reaction lines.**
- Ādiśaiva priest: "The staff is ours to lay, and the fee is five *niṣkas*." (p. 132 n. 56)
- Strict ritualist: "When one of yours dies, how many days are we impure? Fifteen, or twenty?" (p. 121). This one is hostile.
- Garland dissenter: "They said the daughters of Rudra stood above brahmins, and then they branded them." (pp. 116, 124)

**Holy-site text, c_kanchipuram (narrator voice).** "On the walls of the Kailāsanātha, Śiva dances with his watchman Nandin, whose staff named the temple's daughters." (Goodall figs. 3, 5; pp. 110–112)

**Do not.**
- Use courtesan names (-*senā*) or *Kuṭṭanīmata* scenes.
- Use the slave categories or the child-wedding clause as mechanics.
- Use *nityasumaṅgalī* (see the Merkle note).
- Present Ādiśaivas or any devadāsī-descended community as the faith.
