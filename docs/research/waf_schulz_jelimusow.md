# Schulz, "'The World is Made by Talk'" (2002): design reading

Source: Dorothea E. Schulz, "'The World is Made by Talk': Female Fans, Popular Music, and New Forms of Public Sociality in Urban Mali", *Cahiers d'études africaines* 42 (168) (2002): 797–829. DOI 10.4000/etudesafricaines.167. OpenEdition open access.
Read from a text extraction of the OpenEdition PDF, kept outside the repo. Page numbers are the journal's.

**What was read.** In full, pp. 797–823, with all footnotes, the bibliography (pp. 824–829) and the abstracts.

**Relation to existing notes.**
- The brief expected an article on jeli praise, *jelimusow* and praise and shame. It is mainly about **girls who are fans of women pop singers** in Bamako, San and Segu, 1994–2000 (p. 797 n. *).
- Jeli praise appears in the opening footnote and in passing. Schulz's book-length treatment of praise is *Perpetuating the Politics of Praise* (2001b) and "Pricey Publicity, Refutable Reputations" (*Paideuma* 1999a). Neither was read.
- The note still matters for Badenya, for three reasons:
  - it gives the Bamana vocabulary of becoming a "complete" woman;
  - it describes a women's public built from talk and voice;
  - its *suman*/*kalan* (cool/hot) idiom matches Jansen's (`waf_jansen_kangaba_griots.md` §2.1).
- Everything here is **LATER** (1990s urban Mali). It is about cis girls and women; there is no gender-crossing material.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **The singers.** Malian women pop singers follow the conventions of jeli (griot) music. They became "emblematic of 'Malian' music and identity" through national radio from 1960 and television from 1983 (pp. 797–798).
  - Since about 1990 their position has been challenged by singers of "free birth", above all from the Wasulu (p. 801).
- **The girls.** Girls aged 12 to 25 adore these singers. Schulz explains this by their predicament of "postponed becoming": marriage and childbearing make a woman "accomplished", and the economy blocks both (pp. 804–811).
- **What the fans do.** Fandom is mostly talk: about the stars' lives, clothes and purchases. It creates an "intimate public" in which girls feel "touched" by a singer's voice and "complete" through her (pp. 812–823).
- **Mothers and daughters.** Middle-aged women value the songs' "educational" (*ladili*) and moral content. Girls value the singer's outfit, "air" and movement (p. 802).

## 2. What matters for the design

### 2.1 Jeli women and praise (the opening footnote)

- **Before colonial rule.** The *jeliw* are nyamakala specialists distinct from the freeborn (*hòròn*) and from descendants of serfs (*jon*). They lived with powerful freeborn families and passed down "their patron family's traditions and histories in exchange for material support" (p. 797 n. 1).
- **Jeli women's part.** "On festive events [...] jeli women were expected to praise their patron family's prestigious genealogy and heroic origins" (p. 797 n. 1).
  - The patrons repaid them with "grain, cattle, and captives" (p. 797 n. 1).
  - Jeliw also resolved conflicts and restored "order and harmony" (p. 797 n. 1).
  - Grade: an anthropologist's summary of pre-colonial practice, LATER. Its "until French occupation" framing is not a dated source for 1300.
- **Weddings and namings today.** Some jeli and other nyamakala singers "show up (often uninvited) at family celebrations, such as weddings and baptizing ceremonies, and bestow (and sometimes impose) their praise" for money (p. 801 n. 10).
  - This is the hook for **praise as a social weapon**: praise that is imposed and must be paid for.
- **Broadcast praise banned.** Since 1992, broadcasting jeli praise of politicians has been prohibited (p. 798).

### 2.2 The vocabulary of a complete woman

- **Name and respect.** A person's achievement is marked by a "name" (*tògò*) and "respect" (*dambe*) (pp. 805, 820).
  - Wealth, goods and favours given away are the "conventional repertory of strategies" to gain them (p. 820).
  - Compare Broqua's *màlo*/*dànbe*, shame against honour (`waf_contemporary_gender.md`).
- **Two Bamana phrases.**
  - Girls say "*an'w man dafa*", "we are not full, complete" (p. 811 n. 29).
  - Of a star who combines local beauty and cosmopolitan goods they say "*nin ye muso lakika de ye*", "this is a real woman" (p. 820 n. 58).
- **Marriage.** It is "the crucial criterion for 'achieving' adulthood" (p. 805). For women, childbearing "remains the principal path towards the realization of ideals of femininity" (p. 807).
  - Only a married woman gains "full access to social events, such as family ceremonies, in which women perform and thus create themselves as full-blown members of the community" (p. 807).
- **Shame.** Mothers call unmarried daughters "useless". They accuse them of lacking shame ("*u tè maloya*") and of going astray ("*yaala*") (pp. 810–811).
- **Voice.** A woman fan:
  - the singer's voice is "as if she carried herself over to me" (p. 817);
  - another said it "cuts right through the air" (p. 817);
  - a third: "She gives me the feeling of being complete" (p. 818).
- **Courage.** Women listen for "the courage it inspires" (p. 815).
  - A fifteen-year-old: "I gain new courage to continue with my everyday chores" (p. 818).
  - The courage here is a woman's courage, carried by a woman's voice. Nothing in it is cast as "manly".
- **Childbirth as women's work.** Oumou Dioubaté's song "Women of Africa" calls childbirth "the time of women", the "real work". The woman comes out "victorious" and "noble" (p. 803). The song is Guinean Maninka and was a 1998 radio hit.

### 2.3 Talk, cool and hot

- **Talk makes the world.** *Baro* (talk, conversation) is "talk-as-action" with the "capacity [...] to create sociality". Its opposite is *nyanafin*, the longing of isolation. The adage is "*duniya ye baro ye*", "the world is (made by) talk" (p. 812).
  - *Baro* across sexes can also mean courtship (p. 813 n. 39).
- **Cool.** A broadcaster or star is praised as "*kuul*". This adds to the Bamana *suman* (cool, deliberate), opposed to *kalan* (hot, quick to quarrel) (p. 816 n. 49).
  - A *kuul* speaker conveys a problem "without naming it in too explicit a manner"; her silences count as much as her words (p. 816).
  - This continues how "jeliw and [...] politicians" are judged as speakers (p. 816 n. 51).
- **Hot.** "Hot" (*chaud*) music is music that "makes one move" (p. 815).
- **Where men and women meet.** Men meet in the *grin*, the tea circle. Girls meet in or near a courtyard, out of their mothers' hearing (pp. 811–812).

### 2.4 Pop singers as models

- **Who sings.** Almost all the stars come from the southern Mande-speaking triangle (p. 801).
- **What they sing.** They lament women's burdens, co-wife rivalry and "money". They "laud women for their accomplishments as mothers and spouses" (p. 802).
- **The heroine of girls' anecdotes** "chooses her husband herself" and "keeps her family and in-laws at bay" (p. 819).
- **Distance.** Girls keep "a conscious and deliberate distance" from the stars. They imagine rather than imitate (pp. 819–821).

## 3. Corrections to the candidate and to the reading list

1. **Wrong source for "jelimusow, praise and shame".** For praise and shame, the Schulz items to obtain are 1999a (*Paideuma* 45: 275–292) and 2001b (*Perpetuating the Politics of Praise*, Köppe). Also Durán 1995, "Jelimusow: the Superwomen of Malian Music" (cited p. 825), and Durán 2007 (already on the list).
2. **"Women singers as keepers of the whole Mande word"** (`south_more_sources.md` B1.6) **is better supported by Jansen's Siramori tribute than by Schulz.** Schulz shows women's praise as patrons' publicity and modern stardom, not as guardianship of the epic.
3. **Status vocabulary.** The faith's status words should be *tògò* (name) and *dambe* (respect, honour), with *maloya* (shame), all attested here. Do not coin others.

## 4. Design implications

### 4.1 Gate, step 3 (the word)

- **The *jelimuso*'s praise, paid.** The praise is paid in goods, as patrons repaid jeli women with "grain, cattle, and captives" (p. 797 n. 1). For the mod: gold or cattle. Do not offer people as payment.
- **Praise can be imposed.** A jeli who arrives uninvited at the daughter's naming can impose praise and demand pay (p. 801 n. 10).
  - Event option: pay well, and the name is praised. Refuse, and the jeli's next song mocks it. That gives a "satirical name" penalty until the next feast, as in the candidate, now grounded in the 1990s practice.
- **Cooled by the word.** The faith's mistress of the word should be *suman* (cool), as Jansen's ritual idiom requires (pp. 816 n. 49; Jansen 2002 pp. 53–54). The gate ends with the daughter "cooled".
  - Loc: "She spoke it coolly, and the name cooled."

### 4.2 The line

- **Name and respect.** Track *tògò* and *dambe* rather than "piety". A daughter gains them by giving away what she receives (p. 820). That fits the gift logic of the Houses: a modifier "Has a Name (*tògò*)" granted by generosity events.
- **Ceremonies make a woman.** Family ceremonies are where women "create themselves as full-blown members" (p. 807). So a daughter of Badenya should be *invited* to perform at weddings and naming feasts.
  - Mechanic: a "Called to the Naming Feast" event chain that raises *tògò*.
  - This independently matches Broqua's góor-jigéen as master of ceremonies at weddings and namings. Use Schulz as the Mande-side support (`waf_contemporary_gender.md` §B1).

### 4.3 Faith voice and reaction lines (all LATER, all in characters' mouths)

- Faith: "The world is made by talk; we are made by it too." (after p. 812)
- Faith, to a new daughter: "This is a real woman." (*nin ye muso lakika de ye*, p. 820 n. 58). Spelling to be checked by a speaker.
- Daughter's inner line at the gate's end: "Now I am complete." This answers *an'w man dafa* (p. 811 n. 29). The narrator does not compare it to the girls Schulz interviewed.
- Hostile Mande matron: "She has no shame (*u tè maloya*)." (p. 811)
- Approving matron, on a daughter's courage: "She sings like one who gives women courage." (after pp. 815, 818). The courage is a woman's; do not call it manly.

### 4.4 Festival and holy sites

- Schulz adds no site or date. Its relevance to the festival is the *women's* side: women's courage, women's songs, women performing at ceremonies.
- The New Roof festival (`waf_jansen_kangaba_griots.md` §4.6) should end with a *jelimuso*'s song for the new age-set of daughters.

### 4.5 Do not use

- Any reading of the girls' fandom, unmarried motherhood or "rent-seeking" courtship as material about trans women.
- Real singers' names (Oumou Dioubaté, Oumou Sangaré, Kandia Kouyaté, Nana Kulubali) for in-game persons.
- The live praise-for-money scenes as a portrait of today's griots. Use the institution only.
