**Scope and bias.** This is a 1964 synthesis of German scholarship, written from the towns' side. The language is of its time: the Wends are "primitive pagan tribes" (p. 10), and the Hansa towns are "centres of Germanism in the east" (p. 130). Its subject is merchants and towns, not bands. Its one band-to-power case is the Victual Brothers. It is strong on institutions (membership, Kontor rules, sanctions) and on the 1150–1400 period, and it has dates for almost everything. It never uses the word *Likedeeler* (see §6).

**Relation to other notes.** Read with `byz_sophoulis_banditry.md` (bands turned landholders, pardon, Western free companies) and `norse_ahola_outlawry.md` (Icelandic outlawry, the band of the outlaw). Dollinger adds the **merchant company** half of the brief: sworn travelling fellowships, trading posts held by privilege, internal courts and expulsion. He also gives Gotland's medieval history from the faith's seat outward.

**Relevance:** high for Gotland and Visby (1080–1407), for Kontor rules, for the Victual Brothers as a band that took and held Gotland, and for treaties with Orthodox Novgorod and Smolensk. Moderate for the pagan Baltic, which appears as a crusade frontier only. Very low for women: there are no women traders, and the Kontore excluded women (§8).

## 1. The word: a hansa was a band before it was a company

- *Hansa* is an old Germanic word that appears in Wulfila's Bible "in the sense of a warrior band" (p. xix). In the 12th and 13th centuries it meant a tribute paid by merchants, or a group of merchants abroad (p. xix).
- The north Germans were first called a *hansa* in an English royal charter of 1267, on the model of the Flemish Hansa in London (pp. xix–xx, 39–40). The whole community was first called a hansa in 1343, in a letter from the king of Norway and Sweden "universos mercatores de Hansa Theutonicorum" (p. xx).
- The Hansa itself rejected the words "society", "league" and "corpus". Dollinger prefers "Hanseatic Community", after the medieval *communis mercator*, *der gemene kopman*, "the common merchant" (p. xx). In 1469 it told the English Privy Council that it was a "permanent federation" of towns and not a *societas*, *collegium* or *universitas*, so it bore no collective liability (pp. 106–107; Document 26, p. 411).
- **Design use.** A band becoming a merchant company is a change of meaning the word itself went through. One flavour line can carry it (proposal 7).

## 2. Gotland before the Germans (to c. 1160)

- In the early 12th century the Scandinavians were still the leading traders of both seas (p. 3). Baltic trade was "principally in the hands" of the Gotlanders. After the island's conversion in the 11th century it gained "great naval and commercial importance" (p. 7).
- **No town yet.** Trade went through several sites that were used from time to time, protected by earthworks and shared by several villages. One of these sites became Visby (p. 7). Dollinger calls the islanders "sea-going farmers" (p. 7).
- **Range.** They went to Sigtuna, England (shown by English coin hoards), the Oder ports and the Samland amber coast, and above all to Novgorod by the Varangian route (pp. 7–8).
- **The Gothic Court in Novgorod.** The Gotlanders founded a trading centre in Novgorod with a church of St Olaf, first mentioned around 1080. This "Gothic Court" later sheltered the Germans too (p. 7). They brought western cloth (Ypres cloth around 1130) and took home furs, wax and eastern luxuries that came through Byzantium, Kiev and Smolensk (p. 7).
- **Russians on Gotland.** Russian merchants were "particularly active in Gotland, where traces of their Orthodox churches have been found" (p. 8). The Novgorod Chronicle records Russian merchants plundered in Denmark in 1134, and again about twenty years later (p. 8).
- **Imperial privilege.** In 1134 Lothair III exempted the Gotlanders from customs and granted them his protection "throughout the whole Empire" (p. 8). He gave them privileges in the duchy of Saxony, "probably on reciprocal terms" (p. 12).
- **Design use.** Before 1160 Gotland is an island of farmer-traders with a court of its own in Orthodox Novgorod and Orthodox churches at home. That is a good world for the Bædling seat at early starts. The faith's seat would be a trading island, not an isolated one.

## 3. The Gotland Community and the 1161 peace

- **The peace of 1161.** "All who wished to trade in the Baltic had to come to an understanding with the Gotlanders" (p. 24). Henry the Lion "reconciled the warring Germans and Gotlanders, made them swear to keep the peace (1161)" and confirmed the Gotlanders' Saxon privileges (p. 24). Dollinger gives no detail of the earlier fighting.
- **The sworn association.** After that, the Germans formed "a sworn association" at Henry's instigation. Henry recognised their leader, Olderic, as his representative, with judicial powers over them like those the Gotlanders had in Saxony (p. 24). This was the *universi mercatores imperii Romani Gotlandiam frequentantes*, "in some sort … the birth of the Hansa" (p. 24).
- **Constitution.** Little is known. Each year the merchants elected an alderman and "swore an oath of obedience and mutual aid" (p. 24). Lübeck's oldest seal (1226) shows two men in a ship taking an oath, possibly this scene (p. 24). By the 13th century there were four aldermen, chosen by the merchants of Lübeck, Visby, Soest and Dortmund. They judged their companions and spoke for them to foreign powers (pp. 24–25). The Community had its own seal with a fleur-de-lis (p. 25).
- **Who joined.** Merchants of Lübeck and of Westphalian and Saxon towns. Men of little Medebach were trading in Russia by 1165 (p. 24). The 1229 Smolensk treaty was witnessed by men from Riga, Gotland, Lübeck, Soest, Münster, Dortmund, Groningen and Bremen (p. 30; Document 3, p. 382).
- **Travel in arms.** The early merchants travelled in groups (*cohors institorum*), armed with imperial permission and "ready to fight brigands, pirates or even dissatisfied customers", often with a travelling priest. Such were the merchants of the Gotland Community (p. 163).
- **Design use.** The founding act of the Hansa was a ruler making two hostile groups of traders swear peace and giving the newcomers their own leader and court. It is an oath of mutual aid, renewed each year. That fits a landless band's charter (proposals 1 and 2).

## 4. Visby: the double town, the rivalry with Lübeck, the fall

- **Two towns, two seals.** German merchants settled at Visby, where there was already a Scandinavian settlement with several churches by the mid-12th century (p. 25). The German town's seal read *Sigillum Theutonicorum in Gutlandia manentium*, and the law distinguished visiting Germans (*frequentantes*) from resident ones (*manentes*) (p. 25). The Scandinavian town had its own seal, a lamb. Visby was "a double town". Each community at first had its own council. The councils soon merged, but councillors were chosen "from among both nations", and the town law was common to both from the start (p. 25).
- **Size.** By the mid-13th century a stone wall of 11,200 feet enclosed about 90 ha. Eighteen medieval church ruins survive. St Mary of the Germans was built between 1190 and 1225 (p. 25). The wall is "the oldest and best-preserved" of Hanseatic town walls, with thirty-eight towers (p. 269).
- **Visby as a base for the east.** Merchants, settlers, missionaries and crusaders took in stores there before sailing for Russia or Sweden (p. 25). Riga was founded in 1201 under the Visby law code (p. 28). Visby held the Novgorod Kontor's treasure chest each winter. Its four keys were held by the aldermen of Visby, Lübeck, Soest and Dortmund, and the Visby council heard appeals from the Kontor (pp. 26–27).
- **The pagan east.** Meinhard, who came from Gotland in 1184, began the Livonian mission (p. 28). The Gotland Community "equipped hundreds of crusaders" for Bishop Albert's crusade and shipped them (p. 28). After the Ösel crusade of 1226, Riga gave the Community half its share of the booty: lands in Courland, on Ösel and in Estonia (p. 28). These were lost again when the Teutonic Order took over (p. 29).
- **Decline of the Community.** The Community was "a simple fraternity of travelling merchants". The towns came to distrust an association "which claimed to be a separate authority" (p. 43). In 1280 Lübeck and Visby agreed to protect trade together without mentioning the Community. Riga joined them later (p. 43). Each wanted sole authority, and Lübeck won. In 1293 the Wendish towns moved the Novgorod court of appeal from Visby to Lübeck. Only Riga, Osnabrück and perhaps a few Westphalian towns voted for Visby (p. 43). In 1298 the Community was forbidden to use its own seal (p. 44). In 1294 Zwolle thanked Lübeck for forbidding Frisians and Flemings to sail to Gotland (Document 15, p. 399).
- **Visby holds on.** When Lübeck was under Danish protection after 1307, Visby recovered the Novgorod appeal (p. 53). From 1346 Visby and Lübeck appointed the Peterhof priest and alderman in turn (p. 100). In the Bruges Kontor, Visby led the "Gotland-Livonian third" (pp. 51, 95–96). In 1388 Stockholm still called Visby "head of our third" (p. 128). Two of Lübeck's four great late-14th-century burgomasters were born in Visby (p. 130).
- **1361.** Valdemar IV "landed on Gotland, defeated a hastily assembled army of peasants, and seized and sacked Visby" (p. 67). The island stayed Danish for nearly three hundred years (p. 67). He was willing to renew Visby's charter and let it stay in the Hansa (p. 67). The Hansa still went to war (p. 68). After 1361 Visby "could no longer sustain its pretensions". It stayed a Hansa town into the 16th century, but in "an increasingly unimportant place" (p. 128). The burghers' refusal to help the peasants is well known, but **this book does not say it** (general knowledge; check before use).
- **Who held Gotland, 1391–1407.** The Mecklenburgers took Visby in 1391 as a pirate base (p. 79). The Teutonic Order took it "without difficulty" in 1398 with 84 ships and 4,000 men (p. 80). The OCR loses the sentence on how the Order "gained a footing in Gotland" (p. 291). In 1407 the Hansa forced the Order to return Gotland to Denmark (pp. 291, 295).
- **Later.** Gotland "gradually lost its importance as a port of call" (p. 237). Its cattle trade could not compete with Danish cattle (p. 238). Gotland stone fonts were a speciality until brass fonts from Lübeck replaced them (p. 273). A 15th-century saying called Visby "a warehouse for pitch and tar" (p. 129).
- **The Sea-Law of Gotland.** Visby was "the seat of the most important naval court in the Baltic". A Low German sea-law compiled from Hamburg law and the *Rôles d'Oléron* became known as the "Sea-Law of Gotland", a title fixed by the printed edition (Copenhagen, 1505) (p. 148).
- **Design use.** Gotland's lords change again and again: Gotlanders, then Germans and Gotlanders sharing a council, Denmark (1361), the Victual Brothers (1391), the Teutonic Order (1398), Denmark (1407). The countryside raised its own army in 1361. This is the frame for a faith that "stayed Bædling under Catholic lords for centuries" (proposal 5).

## 5. The Kontore: trading posts held by privilege and run by their own rules

**Common rules (pp. 98–99, 105, 108).**
- The Kontore arose "spontaneously" and were "accorded special privileges by the ruling powers wherever they settled" (p. 98). After the mid-14th century they came under the Hanseatic diet (pp. 63–64, 98).
- A great Kontor needed a big market a long way from Germany and a ruler willing to grant privileges. Stockholm had many Germans but no privileges, so it never had a Kontor (p. 98).
- Each Kontor had "its own leaders, tribunal and treasury" and the legal status of a corporation, which the Hansa itself never had (pp. 98–99). The seals were a two-headed eagle in London and Bruges, a cod in Bergen and the key of St Peter in Novgorod (p. 99).
- Every visiting merchant had to report to the Kontor, "submit to the strict discipline prescribed by the statutes", and lodge inside the walled precinct, except in Bruges (p. 99).
- Aldermen were elected yearly by the assembly of merchants. They "were obliged to accept the appointment" and were unpaid (p. 99). Revenue came from fines and the turnover tax (*schoss*) (p. 99).
- **Four make a company.** Cologne told its citizens in England that "as soon as four merchants found themselves together in a foreign town, they were to elect an alderman and obey him". Lübeck had the same rule (p. 105).
- **Expulsion.** A merchant could be excluded by his home town or by the Kontor where he had offended. This meant banishment from all Hansa towns and confiscation of his goods, with a share left to his heirs (p. 108). Christian Kelmer of Dortmund, a former alderman of the Steelyard, was expelled in 1385. He took English nationality and made trouble for his old colleagues, which showed that exclusion "could be a two-edged weapon" (p. 109; Document 23, p. 409).
- **The towns take control.** In 1356 the first full diet confirmed the Bruges statute and made the Kontor answerable to the towns (p. 63). In 1366 only citizens of Hansa towns could share the privileges, and no one could become an alderman in Flanders or Bergen without being a burgess of a Hansa town (pp. 68, 85; Document 19, p. 404). In 1378 the Bruges Kontor tried a secret exodus on its own authority, failed and complained: "Seeing that the citizens of the towns are our masters …" (p. 75).

**Novgorod, the Peterhof (pp. 26–27, 99–101, 182–183).**
- Privileges were granted by Prince Constantine in 1205–7, and a stone church was built before 1200 (p. 26). The 1180 treaty with Prince Jaroslav, made with the Germans and Gotlanders jointly and on reciprocal terms, is the oldest known treaty between the Hanseatics and a foreign prince (p. 26).
- **The route.** The fleet gathered at Kotlin, elected an alderman for the voyage, took Russian pilots up the Neva past "Swedish and Karelian pirates", kept a church and cemetery at Ladoga, and used a corporation of Russian boat-towers at the Volkhov rapids (pp. 26–27).
- **Winter and summer merchants.** Winter merchants stayed for the furs. Summer merchants came for a shorter stay. When the court stood empty, its two keys were held by "the archimandrite of Novgorod" and "the abbot of the Monastery of St George" (p. 27). **Orthodox clergy kept the keys to the German court.**
- **Inside the walls.** A palisade with one gate. The church of St Peter held the treasury, archives, scales and goods, and goods had to be forbidden on the altar. There were lodgings, a meeting hall, a malt-house and a prison (p. 99). At night the gate was sealed, guard dogs roamed, and watchmen were locked into the church. Masters and clerks lived apart. It was a "purely male group" of a few dozen or a few hundred, kept in check by fines and prison (pp. 182–183).
- **Officers.** At first the merchants' assembly (*Steven*) freely elected the alderman, whose powers were "more absolute than elsewhere" (p. 100). The priest also kept the Kontor's correspondence (p. 100). In the 15th century the real head was the *Hofknecht*, "servant of the Hof", appointed by the Livonian towns. He stayed for years, spoke Russian and was "widely respected" (pp. 100–101).
- **Rules about Russians.** By the late 13th century the statute (*schra*) banned partnerships or commission agency with Russians, on a fine of 50 marks (p. 200). The second *schra* (about 1295) banned credit sales (p. 205).
- **Violence.** In 1424, 150 Germans were arrested and 36 died in captivity (pp. 182, 295). In 1494 Ivan III deported the Peterhof's merchants (49 on p. 182, "some fifty" on p. 312). They were freed after three years and died at sea on the way home (p. 182). The Peterhof reopened in 1514, and its buildings were ruins by the mid-16th century (p. 312).
- **Size.** 160 merchants in the winter of 1336–7 (p. 55). Often more than 200 merchants and assistants in the early 15th century (p. 101).

**Bergen, the German Quay (pp. 101–102, 183–184, 297–298).**
- An enclosed, privileged row of *gaarde* along the harbour, with wooden halls, living quarters and the church of St Mary (p. 101). Mostly Lübeckers (p. 101).
- Outside the Quay lived German craftsmen, the *Schomaker*, who "far outnumbered" the merchants. The Kontor put them under its jurisdiction and tried to stop them trading (pp. 101–102).
- **The games.** Each year new journeymen faced three ordeals: smoke, water and whipping, in a "burlesque" initiation (p. 183). The Church and the Hansa objected. The Bergenfahrer kept the games because the ordeals "discouraged the sons of rich merchants", and most members "were of humble origin" (pp. 183–184). Denmark banned the games in 1671 (p. 184).
- **Violence and impunity.** In 1370 Haakon VI complained that the Germans settled crimes among themselves and "carry off the criminals in their ships", and that the "[German] tailors of Bergen" had killed the archbishop of Trondheim's cousin (Document 8, pp. 387–388). In 1455 the Germans burned the monastery where the bailiff Olaf Nielsson had taken refuge and killed him, sixty supporters and the bishop of Bergen. The crimes "remained unpunished" (pp. 297–298).
- The Bergenfahrer company in Lübeck had St Olaf as patron (p. 162). Most members were "of humble origin", and only 82 of 189 were married (p. 163).

**London, the Steelyard (pp. 6, 40, 102–103).**
- It began as the Cologne merchants' Guildhall on the Thames (Henry II's protection, 1157, p. 6). The three German hansas joined in 1281/1282 (pp. 40, 102). Its obligations were a rent of 40 shillings and guard duty at Bishopsgate (p. 40).
- **Two aldermen.** The merchants elected a "German alderman". There was also an "English alderman", proposed by the merchants and installed by the king. He had to be a London citizen and councillor and was "usually a native of Germany who had acquired English nationality" (p. 103).

**Bruges (pp. 41, 103–104; Document 17, pp. 401–402).**
- There was no enclosed precinct. The merchants lodged with landlords and met in the Carmelite refectory (p. 103). In 1252 Countess Margaret of Flanders approved a walled "New Damme" for them, but nothing came of it, perhaps because Bruges feared "a group of privileged foreigners" (p. 41).
- **The 1347 statute.** Three "thirds" (Lübeck-Saxony, Westphalia-Prussia, Gotland-Livonia-Sweden), each with two aldermen and its own treasury. Two thirds outvote the third. There are fines for sitting down or chatting while the aldermen speak, and for opening the door or leaving without permission (pp. 51, 104, 401–402).
- **Moving the Kontor as a weapon.** Bruges to Aardenburg in 1280 and 1307, to Dordrecht in 1358 and 1388 (pp. 48–50, 65, 76, 110).

**Smaller posts.** Copenhagen (1378), Polotsk, Tönsberg, Oslo and Boston ("Stalhof") (p. 105). The Skania *vitten* were fenced fishing concessions with a church, a cemetery and a town-appointed *Vogt*, active for about three months a year (pp. 239–240).

## 6. The Victual Brothers: a band that took Gotland (pp. 78–81)

- **Cause.** After 1375 the house of Mecklenburg lost Denmark to Margaret and turned to piracy. In 1376 a diet declared war on piracy, but Rostock and Wismar, both Mecklenburg towns, held back (p. 78).
- **The proclamation.** After Albert was deposed and imprisoned in 1389, the Mecklenburgers opened their ports "to all those who at their own risk would go to sea to harm the kingdom of Denmark" (p. 79). The book gives no exact year.
- **Who came.** "Knights, townsfolk, peasants and gallows birds", led by Mecklenburg nobles. Rostock and Wismar became bases where ships were armed, raids planned and booty "stored and divided up" (p. 79). The corsairs attacked everyone, not only the Danes (p. 79).
- **The name.** *Vitalienbrüder* comes from the French *vitailleurs*. These were supply troops in the Hundred Years War who supplied armies "by brigandage pure and simple", and then supply ships (p. 79). The OCR loses a line here. The extraction **never uses *Likedeeler***, the later name ("equal sharers"). The book's "divided up" (p. 79) and its remark that the Hanseatics maintained "a rigorous equality between members" (p. 185) are the nearest it comes. The "equal sharers" meaning is general knowledge and should be checked before use.
- **Land taken.** In 1391 they took Bornholm, **Visby** ("an excellent base"), Åbo, Viborg and strongholds in Finland. In 1393 they sacked Bergen, whose people swore loyalty to Albert. In 1394 they pillaged Malmö (p. 79).
- **Cost.** In 1392 the diet suspended all trade with Skania for three years. Herring tripled in price in Prussia and rose tenfold in Frankfurt (p. 80). Convoys of at least ten ships were required in the Sound from 1392 (p. 147).
- **Peace removes the licence.** The Peace of Skanör (1395) freed Albert and "removed the justification for piracy". Piracy stayed rampant "around Gotland" (p. 80).
- **End.** In 1398 the Grand Master took Visby with 84 ships and 4,000 men, and by 1400 the Baltic was clear (p. 80). Most of the pirates moved to the North Sea, where the count of Oldenburg and East Frisian lords received them (p. 80). They were defeated in Frisia in 1400. In 1401 Godeke Michels and Klaus Störtebeker were beheaded in Hamburg "together with hundreds of their companions" (p. 81). They became "legendary figures" (p. 81). A captain who fought them later became burgomaster of Hamburg (p. 153; his name is lost in the OCR).
- **Hanseatic war was itself piracy.** "In fact Hanseatic military action was essentially a war of piracy" (p. 111). Bremen's readmission in 1358 obliged it to send a ship with fifty armed men against "the pirates or other enemies" and to share booty in proportion to the men sent (Document 18, p. 403). Danzig pirates disrupted trade during the Thirteen Years War, 1454–66 (p. 293).
- **Design use.** This is the clearest band-to-power case in the book. A ruler at war licenses anyone to raid. A mixed band of nobles, townspeople, peasants and convicts takes an island and holds it for seven years. Peace removes the licence and the band becomes outlaw, until a great power clears the island by force. The band's last refuge is with petty coastal lords. All of it happened on Gotland, the faith's seat.

## 7. Novgorod, Smolensk and the pagan and Orthodox east

- **Treaties based on reciprocity.** The 1180 treaty granted protection and legal guarantees to the Germans and Gotlanders, and similar rights to Russian merchants (p. 26). The 1229 Smolensk treaty (Document 3, pp. 381–382):
  - no settlement by duel between Russian and German (cl. 10);
  - "If the Germans should fight among themselves … that is of no concern to the prince"; they settle it "in their own fashion" (cl. 10);
  - every German entering the town gives a piece of cloth to the princess (cl. 17);
  - a standard weight is kept in the Russians' church and another in the Germans' church (cl. 29);
  - each side is exempt from tolls in the other's lands, including Russians on Gotland (cl. 31);
  - the prince's wars are "in no way the concern of the Germans unless they ask to accompany the prince in battle" (cl. 32).
  Clause 20 is unreadable in the OCR.
- **Niebur's kissing of the cross (1392).** The peace was sworn by a Lübeck councillor in Novgorod. It renewed treaties based on reciprocity "for Germans in Novgorod and Russians in Gotland and Livonia". It guaranteed the Germans' safety even if Novgorod went to war with Sweden or the Order, and it was "for a century the charter" of relations (p. 78).
- **Embargo instead of war.** In 1277, after Russian and Lithuanian attacks on Livonia, travel to Novgorod was forbidden on pain of death (p. 48). There was a blockade in 1388 (p. 77). Novgorod arrested German merchants in reprisal for the Order's raids, as in 1367 (p. 77). In 1468 the Kontor moved to Narva for four years (p. 295).
- **Ambassadors.** Treaties with Novgorod and Smolensk set a double indemnity for killing an envoy (p. 110).
- **Pagans.** The Baltic peoples were "still pagan": Lithuanians, Livonians, Letts and Estonians. Trade there "went hand in hand with conversion, conquest and the founding of new cities" (pp. 27–28). The Brethren of the Sword (1202) were "open not only to nobles but also to merchants" (p. 28). The Lithuanians crushed the Brethren in 1236 (p. 29). The Prussians "were for the most part killed or deported" (p. 34). Pribislav, the Obotrite prince, rebelled, submitted in 1167 and was enfeoffed with Mecklenburg, which his line held "until the twentieth century" (p. 22). Henry, a Slav prince of Old Lübeck, settled merchants outside his fortress (p. 17).
- **Slavs in the towns.** In the new eastern towns Slavs "were not eligible for citizenship". Cracow's charter of 1257 excluded Poles for more than fifty years (p. 31). Guilds shut Slavs out from 1309 (Prussia), 1323, 1350 and 1354 (Riga), and Riga banned partnership with a Slav in 1399 (p. 132). In Reval in 1538 the merchant class was entirely German and day-labourers were 73% Estonian (p. 131). Sweden took the opposite course: in 1251 Lübeckers who settled in Sweden "should henceforward be called Swedes" (p. 37). Västerås had a council of six Germans and six Swedes, and a 1345 ordinance required councils to be half Swedish and half German (p. 36).
- **Design use.** The Orthodox east gives the faith a treaty partner that protects foreign traders, exempts them from its wars and lets them judge their own quarrels. The crusading towns give the hostile side: Gotland's own merchants equipped the crusade against Baltic pagans.

## 8. Women, gender and absence

- **No woman merchant** appears anywhere in the parts read. No woman is a member of a Kontor or a merchant company, a shipowner, a skipper or a pirate. The searched remainder turned up none either.
- **Women excluded.** "Although women were excluded from the Kontore, the preparation and salting of the fish was carried out mainly by Danish and German women" (p. 240). The Peterhof was a "purely male group" (p. 183).
- **Marriage as a qualification.** A skipper "had to be a married man and a father", his stake in the town "a guarantee of his honesty" (p. 152). The Danzig Artushof shut out men who "have married a woman of bad reputation" (Document 24, p. 410). The Artushof fined members who talked "scandal, particularly about women" (p. 181). Most Bergenfahrer did not marry (p. 163). The book states **no celibacy rule** for the Kontore. The Bergen ban on marriage is general knowledge and should be checked before use.
- **Women as the door to citizenship.** Nürnbergers "married Lübeck women and thus acquired Lübeck citizenship" (p. 197). The patriciate welcomed rich foreigners who "married one of their daughters" (p. 135). The great merchants' careers turn on marriages to burgomasters' daughters and widows (pp. 172, 174, 176, 178).
- **Women rulers as grantors.** Countess Margaret of Flanders granted the privileges of 1252–3, one of them "to those 'who are of the Roman Empire and frequent Gotland'" (p. 41). Queen Margaret of Denmark was regent, then queen of Sweden in 1389. She founded the Kalmar Union in 1397 and confirmed Hanseatic privileges in all three kingdoms (pp. 71, 79–80).
- **Other glimpses.** A princess receives cloth from each German arriving in Smolensk (p. 382). A pilgrim ship carried 500 men "not counting the women and girls" (p. 184). In a 1282 patrician tourney the prize was "a pretty woman there called Feie" (Document 16, p. 400). An East Prussian chronicle tells of a serving woman, the sole survivor of the Black Death on an estate, "strutting about in her mistress's finery" (p. 59). That is class dress, not gender. Members bet on whether a cook would name her master as father of her children (p. 181). None of these is usable as event content without care.
- **Eunuchs, gender crossing, disguise:** none.
- **Design consequence.** In this source the merchant company is a men's institution, and women enter it only by marriage, as labour at its edges, or as rulers who grant it privileges. A company of trans women is the mod's invention, and it runs against what the source shows. The honest frame for the Bædling is the faith's own voice: "the Kontor shuts its gate on women, so we keep our own house". The source also offers two real openings: women rulers who granted charters, and women who worked the *vitte*.

## 11. Risks and cautions

- **Ethnic and national framing.** Dollinger's 1964 text speaks of "primitive pagan tribes" (p. 10), "centres of Germanism" (p. 130) and German "technical superiority" (p. 130). Do not echo any of it in loc. No culture (Wendish, Estonian, Mecklenburg, Frisian, Russian) should be coded as pirate, primitive or untrustworthy. The Victual Brothers were a political licence open to all comers (p. 79).
- **Pagans and crusade.** The Gotland Community financed the crusades against the Baltic pagans (p. 28). For a pagan faith seated on Gotland that is hostile context, and a strong one. The narrator should not take the crusaders' view that pagans were savages.
- **Finance and Jews.** The book has nothing on Jewish merchants. Its finance chapter (royal loans, pledged crowns, the Westphalian consortium, pp. 57–58, 171–172) must stay out of Ha-Nehpakhot content. The Verdun slave trade (p. 8) is given without naming who traded. Do not fill that gap.
- **Women absent.** Any merchant company of trans women is the mod's invention against the grain of the source (§8). The Kontor's "purely male" world (p. 183) must not become a plot about a trans woman hiding inside it as a man. That is the deception trope, and the mod rejects it.
- **The Bergen games** (whipping a naked, drunk, blindfolded youth, p. 183) and the 1455 massacre (pp. 297–298) are not event content beyond a sentence of report.
- **Not in this book; check elsewhere before use:** *Likedeeler*; "God's friends and the whole world's enemies"; the Visby burghers shutting the gate on the peasants in 1361; the Bergen marriage ban; Eric of Pomerania's later years on Gotland. All of these are general knowledge, absent from the extraction.
- **OCR gaps.** Key sentences are damaged at p. 79 (the Vitalienbrüder passage), p. 81 (Störtebeker's legend), p. 291 (how the Order came to hold Gotland) and p. 382 (Smolensk cl. 20). Check them against a printed copy before quoting.
