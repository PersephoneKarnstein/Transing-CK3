## 0. Framing warning

- **Neither book is about the mod's people.** Baldwin is a social history of money, office, law and the schools. Barney's *Abel* is a preacher's word-book of Bible senses. Neither one mentions anyone crossing toward womanhood.
- **The Chanter's one relevant text is hostile.** *De vitio sodomitico* (*Verbum abbreviatum* ch. 138) is a polemic against sex between men.
  - Its line about men who "change themselves from males to females" (Boswell p. 377) is **how a Paris master classified sodomites**. It is not evidence about daughters, and it is never a daughter's description in the narrator's voice.
  - Daughters are women. They are never framed as gay men or as cross-dressers.
- **Hermaphrodites are not the daughters' story.** The Chanter's ruling concerns people with "the organs of both sexes" (Boswell p. 376). It is recorded here only because the user asked. Keep it separate from daughters, with no examination scenes and no anatomy in loc.
- **Persecution is never a mechanic.** Jews and the anti-Jewish frames in these sources are recorded only. The heresy material here (Arras 1182, Paris 1201 and 1210) never appears as scenes. Rule 25 applies throughout.
- **Thin for the mod.** Baldwin vol. 1 is useful for Paris institutions c. 1180-1215 (ordeals, prostitutes, jongleurs, heretics) and for dating the Chanter's works. It has nothing on gender crossing, hermaphrodites or eunuchs. *Abel* is thinner still: a handful of articles give hostile or neutral Latin clerical vocabulary.

## 1. The key question: the Chanter on hermaphrodites

**Short answer.** Baldwin vol. 1 gives **no Latin, no translation and no discussion** of the hermaphrodite passage or of *De vitio sodomitico*. "Sodomy" occurs in vol. 1 only three times, all on clerical celibacy (pp. 337, 339; see §2.6). "Hermaphrodite", "androgyne", "eunuch" and "effeminate (person)" never occur. Vol. 2 (notes) was not available, so whether its notes quote the chapter is **unchecked**. Goodich, who used both volumes, cites Baldwin for this subject only at 1:339 and 2:229, on Raoul Ardent and clerical celibacy (Goodich, *Unmentionable Vice*, n. 11, from the extraction). That suggests vol. 2 has nothing more, but it is not proof.

What Baldwin and Barney do give is **the text's date and recension**, which the repo lacked:

**The text itself (Boswell's translation, short version, pp. 375-376), recorded precisely:**
- **The frame.** Genesis: God made woman from the rib, and "lest any should believe they would be hermaphrodites" he said "Male and female created he them". This means no intercourse "of men with men or women with women" (pp. 375-376). The Latin is *androgynos* (n. 50). This is the "non-androgynous humanity" line that Goodich (p. 56) and van der Lugt (p. 106, n. 19) also report.
- **The ruling.** "For this reason the church allows" a hermaphrodite to "use the organ by which (s)he is most aroused or the one to which (s)he is more susceptible" (p. 376).
  - "More active [literally, 'lustful']" may wed as a man; "more passive" may marry as a woman.
  - If "(s)he should fail with one organ, the use of the other can never be permitted". (S)he "must be perpetually celibate to avoid any similarity to the role inversion of sodomy".
- **Boswell's notes on the Latin:**
  - n. 51: "(s)he" renders Latin verbs with no expressed subject. The Chanter leaves the gender open.
  - n. 52: "Text has 'et' for 'ut'", a textual crux in the clause about marrying.
  - n. 53: "role inversion" renders *alternitatis*.
  - n. 50: the Chanter treats the condition as inculpable. He is "one of the few Scholastics" to discuss its morals.
- **No Latin beyond these words** is given in any of the three books. The full Latin needs PL 205:333-335 or Boutry.
- **Not checked:** whether the passage is also in the long version (*Textus prior*). If it is, the date is 1191/92. If it is only in the short version, it is c. 1192-1197. Either way it belongs to the **1190s** and is posterior to Lateran III (1179).
- **Canonist parallels.** None of the three books gives one. The parallels already in the repo are Huguccio's behavioural test, the *Summa "Omnes homines"* oath and Hostiensis's Susa oath (`cmp_vanderlugt_sex_difference.md` lines 31-33; `comparanda_reading_list.md` line 45).

**Two further points from ch. 138 that the repo has not recorded** (Boswell pp. 375-378):
1. **Hostile classification.** "Such men ... who change themselves from males to females, abusing feminine coitus" are "kept as women by the pharaoh for his pleasure". They are called imitators of Sardanapalus (p. 377). It is aimed at men who have sex with men. **Never reuse it about a daughter**, not even in a hostile voice, because it is sexual. At most a Paris master may say "they change themselves from males to females" in a hostile line that the House answers.
2. **The chapter ends on the 342 statute.** "When a man marries as a woman, let the laws be armed" (p. 378; Boswell p. 123). It is the Chanter's precedent for secular punishment (p. 277). The Chanter also asks why "what the Lord punished severely the church leaves untouched" (p. 377; Latin at p. 277 n. 25).

## 2. Findings

### 2.1 The man and the works (Baldwin pp. 3-16; Barney pp. 9-15)

- **Life:**
  - Peter of Hodenc (Beauvaisis), of minor knightly family, trained at Reims, where he kept a prebend. He was a master at Paris by 1173 at the latest (pp. 4-6).
  - He was Chanter of Notre-Dame from 1183 (by 4 January 1184) to 1197, second in the chapter after the dean, and never above deacon (p. 6).
  - He was elected to Tournai (1191-92) but did not take it (pp. 9-10). He was elected Dean of Reims in 1196 and died at Longpont on 25 September 1197 in a Cistercian habit (pp. 10-11).
- **Works:**
  - Commentaries on every book of the Bible, from the 1170s to 1197 (pp. 12-13).
  - The *Summa de sacramentis et animae consiliis*, compiled after 1191/92 and left unfinished (p. 14).
  - The *Verbum abbreviatum* (above).
  - *Abel*: the earlier recension is "a good guess" between 1178 and 1188 (Barney p. 15), after 1174 (it calls Becket and Bernard saints, p. 15), in 88 manuscripts (p. 14).
- **For the design:** both *Abel* and the Chanter's teaching career fall inside a 1178 start. The *Verbum abbreviatum* and the hermaphrodite ruling come a decade or more later.

### 2.2 Prostitutes in the Chanter's Paris (Baldwin pp. 133-137)

The theologians held that public prostitutes could not be compelled to restore their earnings in court (*jus fori*). Chobham said "since these women labored with their bodies" they were "legitimately entitled to their wages" (p. 134; Chobham writes c. 1215).

- **Fraud was the exception.** If she painted her eyes, used cosmetics, pretended to noble birth or professed love, she owed restitution "to the extent of her deception" (p. 134). The Chanter thought the private courtesan more fraudulent than the street woman (p. 135).
- **In the confessional (*ius poli*)** the Chanter held that she must give alms from her earnings, to the church for the poor (p. 135).
- **Gifts to Notre-Dame.** "Certain women actively practicing prostitution" wished to give chalices and windows (Chanter, p. 135). Chobham reports that Paris prostitutes offered a stained-glass window, which Bishop Maurice of Sully refused, so as not to seem to approve their trade. The masters advised taking such gifts only privately (pp. 135-136).
- **In church.** They were barred from the Eucharist but not from the kiss of peace or prayer (Chanter, p. 136). Chobham: at Notre-Dame they joined other women presenting candles at Sunday Vespers, never at Mass (p. 136).
- **Harder lines:**
  - Courson wanted the incorrigible excommunicated, then expelled.
  - As legate at the Council of Paris (1213) he banished public prostitutes from the city and ordered them segregated "according to the custom for lepers" (p. 136).
- **Foulques de Neuilly**, the Chanter's protégé:
  - In 1198 he founded the Cistercian convent of Saint-Antoine for women leaving the trade.
  - He also raised a dowry fund so they could marry: scholars gave 250 pounds and burghers "over a thousand" (pp. 136-137).
  - Innocent III called marrying such women meritorious (p. 137).

### 2.3 Entertainers, "effeminate songs" and the two jongleurs (Baldwin pp. 198-204)

- **Infamy in law.** Canon law (Gratian, Rufinus, Huguccio) held actors infamous. They could not accuse in church courts, were refused the Eucharist and ordination, and bore the penalties imposed on prostitutes (p. 199).
- **Actors defined by transformation.** Rufinus: actors "by transforming their visage and clothing" told a story "with their bodies". Huguccio: one who "by bodily movement and transformation of the face" represented others' actions (p. 199).
- **The Chanter's question** (Baldwin's paraphrase; the Latin is in vol. 2 n. 192, not seen). Should the actor's definition extend to "those who recited monstrous or effeminate songs without bodily gestures"? And to those who followed princes' camps with shameful tales (p. 199)?
  - Here "effeminate" describes **songs**, not people.
- **Chobham's three kinds of actor** (p. 199):
  1. those who transform their bodies by shameful dances or gestures, wanton undressing, or terrifying costumes and masks;
  2. rootless court gossips;
  3. musicians, some of whom sing bawdy songs at drinking bouts.
- **The *Verbum abbreviatum* chapter** (p. 200):
  - Every trade has some use, "even brothels are necessary". Acting alone has none.
  - Notorious prostitutes and actors were to get no Eucharist but might enter church to pray. They were not to be fed at one's table except in extreme need.
  - This passed into Courson's councils at Paris (1213) and Rouen (1214), Langton's statutes (1213/14) and Lateran IV (p. 200).
- **Giving.** Give to an actor "only as human beings", out of charity (p. 202). To give to actors was "to sacrifice to demons", a saying attributed to Jerome (p. 202). Philip Augustus gave his cast-off robes to the poor instead (p. 202).
- **Concessions** (p. 203):
  - The Chanter separated music for recreation from music that provoked lust.
  - Courson allowed a lutist to keep a family by music. He condemned an organist who played "scurrilous and effeminate music".
  - The Chanter's two jongleurs: one "deforming the image of God" by "the wantonness of his body". The other sings deeds "with or without instruments" for recreation or instruction, and is exonerated.
  - Exemplum: a jongleur asked Pope Alexander (III?) if he could be saved in his trade (p. 203).
- **Repertoire.** Jongleurs who see the "chanson de Landri" fail switch to Antioch, Alexander, Apollonius or Charlemagne. The short version substitutes Narcissus (pp. 203-204).

### 2.4 Ordeals: the Chanter's campaign (Baldwin pp. 323-332)

- **The most important twelfth-century attack** on ordeals, by Baldwin's estimate (p. 326). It ran in lectures, disputations, the *Verbum abbreviatum*, a letter to a bishop and a plea before the cardinals.
- **Argument from experience.** Ordeals fail (pp. 327-328):
  - Alexander III's lost vessel;
  - an Orléans man hanged before the thief was found;
  - an English pilgrim hanged for murdering a companion who then came home.
  - Of three men carrying the same iron, "Innocence is too closely connected with calluses" (p. 327).
  - Disputes over whether the hair must sink; men taught to blow out their breath (p. 327).
  - Champions are picked for skill (p. 327).
- **Clergy.** No priest should bless iron or water, even where no blood would follow (p. 329). Paraphrased: even if the whole church commanded it under anathema, he "would quicker undergo the perpetual penalty" (p. 329).
- **Heresy.** "How can the heart ... be examined by such proofs?" (p. 328). He records an accused man at Paris who asked for assurance that the iron would not tempt God, and was burned (p. 328).
- **The cost of refusing.** One man accused of murder took the Chanter's advice and refused the cold water. He was hanged; the Chanter counted it a cause "worthy of martyrdom" (p. 326). **Not a model for an event.**
- **Outcome:**
  - Lateran IV (1215), canon 18, forbade clergy to consecrate water or iron, and renewed the bans on the duel (p. 331).
  - Unilateral ordeals left English, Norman and Danish law at once. The judicial duel lingered, especially in serfdom cases (p. 332).
  - "The water proof persisted in witchcraft trials as late as the seventeenth century" (p. 332). **Rule 25: never a mechanic.**
- **Within the circle:**
  - Courson yielded to custom under *Ad abolendam* (1184) when no rational proof existed (pp. 330-331).
  - Chobham allowed a defendant the duel as self-defence (p. 330).

### 2.5 Heretics and the death penalty (Baldwin pp. 320-323); capital punishment (pp. 318-319)

- **Theft.** The Chanter opposed hanging for simple theft: neither Scripture nor Roman law sanctions it (pp. 318-319).
  - Courson pushed the comparison further, and Baldwin sums up: "the burden of the theologians' conclusion was to transfer the death penalty from theft to sex" (p. 319).
  - **Caution for design: a reformer lenient on thieves could be harsher on sexual "sin".**
- **Heretics.**
  - The Chanter recorded the 1182 drive against the "Paterini" of Arras by ordeal and the stake. Officials extorted, and "orthodox women who refused the solicitations of lecherous priests were threatened with enrollment" on the lists (p. 321).
  - His remedy was life confinement, not death, citing Eon de l'Étoile (Reims, 1148) (p. 322).
  - Courson abandoned this. In 1201 the prince offered death or release for Evrard of Châteauneuf, since prisons could not hold "three or four thousand malefactors" (p. 322).
  - The Chanter's protest was "relegated to oblivion" (p. 323).

### 2.6 Celibacy, marriage, nuns, beguines

- **Celibacy.** "Clerical celibacy tended to foster the well-known ills of prostitution, concubinage, and sodomy" (Baldwin's summary of the circle, p. 337). Raoul Ardent and Courson argued for relaxing celibacy (p. 339). Courson reports that the Chanter wanted a general council to revoke continence for sacred orders (p. 341). Lateran IV refused (p. 341).
- **Marriage impediments.** The Chanter attacked the seven-degree rule as unstable law (pp. 332-335). Lateran IV cut it to four degrees, in language echoing his (pp. 335-336, 342).
- **Nuns.** Only one passage: an exemplum of nuns who maimed themselves to repel men, attached to a Plantagenet king (p. 256). **Not for use** (self-harm).
- **Beguines.** Only as Jacques de Vitry's tie to "Marie of Oignies, one of the founding saints of the Beguines movement", 1211-1216 (p. 38). **Thin.** Women's work and women's dress do not appear in vol. 1, except the Ovid line "a woman is the least part of herself", used against ornate books (p. 94), and the prostitutes' cosmetics (p. 134).

### 2.7 Jews (record only, never a mechanic)

- **The Chanter's informant.** He cites a learned Jew at Reims who asked why Christians allegorise the Gospels (p. 94). He approves the Jewish custom, via Jerome, of keeping Genesis 1, Ezekiel and the Song from readers under thirty (p. 94).
- **Philip Augustus.** He arrested, ransomed and in 1182 expelled the Jews of his domain. The Paris synagogue became Sainte-Marie-Madeleine after 1183 (p. 120). The king readmitted them in 1198 (p. 299).
  - The circle "by and large ... protested this royal mulcting" (p. 299). Courson held that princes could not seize Jewish goods (p. 299).
- **Usury.** The Chanter's "usurers as the prince's Jews" (p. 299) and his reading of the bitter-water test as "a specific concession to the malice of Jews" (p. 326) are **anti-Jewish frames, recorded only**.
- **The Reims drought.** After relic processions failed, the Jewish community offered to parade the Torah. The Chanter tells it as Master Alberic refusing a "contest which constituted a clear case of tempting God" (pp. 153, 328). The Jews are not wrongdoers in it, but it is a conversion wager. **Not for use.**

### 2.8 *The Abel Distinctions* (Barney)

- **E84 "There are eunuchs"** (p. 259). The three eunuchs of Matth. 19:12, "drawn from" the Glossa ordinaria (n. c):
  1. born so: "they don't have a reward, because they are continent by necessity, not by will";
  2. made so "by force by men", "not continent by will";
  3. self-made for the kingdom, "willingly devoted ... to chastity".
  - Etymologies: *castrati* from *caste nati*, "born chaste"; "spayed" from *spatha*, the cutting tool; *eunuchus* from *eu* + *nike*, "good victory".
  - **The Latin school classes eunuchs by continence, not by sex.** No gender is assigned to any kind.
- **Women** (pp. 60, 268, 410):
  - Women should not be unveiled before "an angel of the Lord, that is, a priest" (A40.e).
  - "Through a woman" death entered, John was beheaded, and so on (F9). The Adversary left Job his wife as "the consummation of his malice".
  - Women keep silent in church (M138).
- **"Man"** (V37, p. 634). *Vir* "sometimes denotes ... sex", among age, priestly dignity, strength and wickedness. The preceding article etymologises *vir* from *vis* (force) and *virtus*: "be a man ... from force, from virtue, and from greenness" (p. 634).
- **Virgins** (pp. 513-517, 635-636):
  - P100: in the nine gates of Jerusalem, virgins are the Valley Gate and will judge clerics. "We, who are the weaker sex and simpler by nature, have overcome the world, the flesh, and the devil" (p. 517).
  - V43: "Virgins are called drummers because of the affliction of their flesh". A drum is skin "stretched between two boards, that is, between hope and fear" (p. 636).
- **The visible body** (E39, p. 237, from a Comestor sermon). An "inscription on the body is visible, such as the tonsure of the head, meanness of clothing, gait, gesture, bearing". It closes with the Agnes antiphon: "He set a sign on my face".
- **Against nature** (F41-42, pp. 285-286; p. 495). "The sin against nature" is the gravest kind of fornication. In the Galatians list, uncleanness is sin "insofar as it is against nature". No detail.
- **Illusion** (I24, p. 312). A nocturnal illusion from "superfluity of humours" or weak nature is not blamed; from drunkenness or prior thoughts it is.
- **Creation** (F26, p. 277). Eve is "from a man without a woman", "from a rib". There is no androgyne in *Abel*.
- **Resurrection** (C160, p. 168). Bodies will be agile, refined, glorious and incorruptible. Sex is not mentioned.
- **Silences.** There is no hermaphrodite, androgyne, effeminate, magic, Sabbat or pact. Demons appear only in Bible senses.
