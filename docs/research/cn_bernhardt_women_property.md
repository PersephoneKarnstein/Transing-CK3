## 1. The argument

**The frame.** Inheritance had two separate tracks. **Household division** (分家) applied when a man had sons. **Patrilineal succession** (承繼) applied when he had none. Women's rights changed only on the second track, and that track generated most lawsuits. Roughly one family in five had no son who survived to adulthood (p. 2). "As many as one woman in three was either a daughter without brothers or a wife without sons (or both)" (p. 3). In her 430 Song-to-Qing cases, suits over adopting an heir "outnumbered those over household division by four to one" (p. 3).

### 1a. Song: daughters inherit only "by default", and the state takes a large share

- **No half-share law.** Bernhardt argues against Niida, Ebrey and Birge that the Song never had a law giving each unmarried daughter half a son's share. The evidence is two rulings by one judge, Liu Kezhuang, in 1244–48. He was misreading a late extinct-household regulation (pp. 24–39). The Song compiler of the *Qingmingji* even cut the half-share passage from one of the two cases (p. 39). **This contradicts Birge** (`cn_birge_women_property.md`, Table 2 row 1 and p. 76ff.). In the design, use only the extinct-household rules, which both authors accept.
- **"Extinct" had two meanings:** a dead patriline, and a dead **tax unit**. "A household headed by a widow with no sons, either natural or adopted, was called a 'female household' (*nühu*); and after her death, the household would become an extinct household" (p. 12). An undivided household of brothers was never extinct, because it still paid tax (p. 12).
- **Table 1 (p. 15)** is the same schedule as McKnight & Liu p. 297. Unmarried daughters take everything. Returned daughters take ½ and the state ½. Married daughters take ⅓ and the state ⅔. With a posthumous heir, unmarried daughters take ¾ and the heir ¼. A posthumous heir alone takes ⅓ and the state ⅔. Above a ceiling (3,000 guan, or 5,000 on estates of 20,000 and up) everything went to the state (p. 14).
- **What the Song state wanted** was fiscal and military, not ritual. The proceeds of extinct estates "were periodically earmarked for military provisions" (p. 19). Wars took 83% of cash income in the mid-11th century (p. 19). The rules also aimed to check the "aggrandizers" who swallowed land (pp. 18–19).
- **The state treated the land as its own.** The rules spoke of "giving" (*gei*, *yu*) extinct property to daughters, not of their "inheriting" it, a habit inherited from the Tang equal-field claim that the state owned all land (p. 20).
- **No one had to adopt.** "Tang and Song law, in fact, nowhere mandated the appointment of an heir for a sonless man." Judges spoke of it "always as a moral obligation (*yi*), never as a legal one" (pp. 20–21). Posthumous heirs could not succeed to titles and noble perquisites except by special decree (p. 23).
- **A late Southern Song regulation** (cited by Hu Ying, early 1240s) reached into undivided households. A sonless brother's share was split "between unmarried daughters and the state" (pp. 36–38). The case is Zeng Ergu's (McKnight & Liu pp. 287–291). The judge then waived the state's half.

### 1b. Song widows: real owners and free choosers of heirs

- **A widow inherited outright.** "Under Song law, a widow without sons possessed the right to inherit her husband's property. The custodial logic did not obtain" (p. 59). The 1240s ban on her selling land was fiscal and protective, not a matter of lineage (pp. 54–59).
- **She chose the heir, or chose none.** The working rule was "When the husband is dead but the wife is still alive, then follow his wife" (pp. 59–60). Across 20 widow cases in the *Qingmingji*, "no judge ... forced a reluctant widow to adopt an heir" (p. 60). Hu Ying upheld a widow's adoptee of another surname (taken under age three): "There is no principle permitting compulsion by lineage seniors or officials" (p. 61). Only her parents-in-law outranked her (pp. 61–62).
- **The female household was a tax category.** It sat beside "official households", "single male households", "**temples and monasteries** (*siguan*)" and the aged and infirm as categories exempt from labour service and partly from land tax (p. 58). Widows' exemptions were a magnet for fraud (p. 58).
- **"Calling in" a husband.** A widow who called in a second husband kept the land registered in her own name, never his, so that it stayed within reach of the extinct-household laws. In the 1220s this was capped at 5,000 guan (p. 57).
- **Widows were the main litigants.** Widows were principals "twelve times more frequently than the husband" (p. 52). Kinsmen who would not sue a man waited and sued his widow, a phrase recorded as "disrupting the funeral, fighting over the succession" (p. 52).

### 1c. Yuan, then Ming: the doors close

- **Yuan.**
  - Any adopted heir, appointed before or after the parents' deaths, had full rights again, so daughters lost their share against posthumous heirs (p. 40).
  - Married daughters still got only ⅓ of extinct property (p. 40).
  - Yuan and Ming law ordered officials to "confiscate (*ruguan*) the property outright if there were no male heirs and no surviving daughters" (p. 41).
  - The Yuan still did not require anyone to adopt (p. 42).
  - Bernhardt does not discuss the 1273 uxorilocal-husband rule Birge describes (Birge p. 227). The two accounts agree that daughters' claims shrank.
- **Ming, 1369: mandatory nephew succession.**
  - A sonless widow "is to receive her husband's share of property and must [*xu*], through the agency of the lineage head, select a nephew of the appropriate generation as heir" (p. 62).
  - The closest nephew became "the person who ought to succeed" (*yingji zhi ren*), with a legal claim to the estate (pp. 42, 64).
  - From then on, "a daughter's likelihood of inheriting property by default was very remote" (p. 4).
  - **This is inside the CK3 era (1369 < 1453).**
- **Why the Ming did it.** Hereditary military and artisan households needed heirs by law (p. 43). The Jinhua Neo-Confucians wanted the rites to "extend down to the commoners" (p. 43). Codified law also caught up with what people already did (pp. 43–44).
- **The chastity cult cut the other way.** From late Ming judges, and in Qing law from 1775 (Qianlong: "the widow's heart be followed"), a chaste widow could pick any lineage nephew she liked (pp. 65–72).
  - Qing jurists even spoke of **heirs for the widow herself**: Zhang Zhentao (jinshi 1769) wrote "No need for disputes over the appointment of heirs to women" (p. 69).
  - A late-Qing magistrate objected: "heirs are established only for sonless men, and most certainly not for wives or concubines" (p. 70).
- **Confiscation became a punishment only.** A 1740 Qing revision dropped compulsory confiscation of heirless estates: "unless there is some crime to be taken into consideration, it is not appropriate to speak of confiscation" (p. 41). By the 18th century the state had relinquished confiscation "except as part of the penalty for crimes committed" (p. 41). The earlier reader's finding, that confiscation followed only disgrace, is the **Qing** end state. In the CK3 era (Song, Yuan, early Ming) confiscation of heirless estates was routine law, though often waived.
- **Concubines.** From the start of the imperial chastity cult under the Yuan, widowed concubines were eligible for the same testimonials (p. 170). In the Song a concubine's security came only from being a mother (p. 172).

**Concrete legal events (sources cited by Bernhardt):**
- **Hu Ying and the adopted boy of another surname** (Hunan, early 1240s): the widow keeps her choice; "There is no principle permitting compulsion by lineage seniors or officials" (p. 61; *QMJ* 245–46).
- **Cai Hang and Mrs. Li** (late 1240s): "Since Mrs. Li is the family head, the establishment of an heir must lie with her" (p. 60).
- **The Tongcheng widow** (1250s): she keeps a son of another surname over eleven nephews (p. 61). McKnight & Liu (pp. 246–251) translate the same case as Mistress Mao's. There the commissioner also forces her to pick a second, same-surname heir and halves the estate. Bernhardt's summary leaves that out.
- **Hu Wujie (Fifth Sister Hu)** (Jian'an, 1240s): she must prove her marriage or the estate is confiscated as extinct (pp. 56–57). Usable as "prove she is his wife" pressure on a Yutai widow whose marriage the magistrate doubts.

## 3. In-world loc lines (attribute in-world)

- Song-era judge (Way-friendly): "When the husband is dead and the wife lives, follow the wife. No elder and no official may compel her."
- Magistrate (hostile, Song): "The household is extinct. The land goes to feed the army."
- Ming-era lineage head (hostile): "The nephew is the one who ought to succeed. She holds the land only for him."
- The Way's answer: "She holds nothing for him. The land was the society's before she came."
- Register clerk: "Temples and monasteries; female households. Write the House on the second line."
- Late voice, attributed to a sister-scholar: "An heir for a woman is no dispute."
