Chapter list, one line each:
- **Intro.** Four human images from Eddic poetry: warrior, prophetess/sorceress, avenger, whetter.
- **1.** Germanic-Norse continuum; Icelandic genealogies grafted onto Sigurðr and Ragnarr.
- **2.** Ancient female figures (fertility goddesses, *fylgjur*, *dísir*, valkyries, norns, sibyls, land-spirits) and women's cult.
- **3.** The pantheon mirrors patriarchy; seiðr's origin with Freyja; *ergi*; pagan misogyny; *gyðjur*.
- **4.** The warrior woman: shield-maidens, maiden warriors, maiden kings, the historical record.
- **5.** The prophetess/sorceress: the völva only uncovers fate; magic was first female, and men came late.
- **6.** The avenger: Guðrún, Signý, and how little female violence the sources record.
- **7.** The Continental whetter: Brynhildr and the queens' quarrel.
- **8.** The Norse whetter: kings' sagas, bloody tokens, verbal goading, and why authors multiplied her.

## 1. The argument in brief

### The völva and seiðr (chs. 2, 3, 5)

- **The völva uncovers fate; she does not change it.** The human völva, "a mere mortal, did not have direct access to the desired information but had to engage in a magic ritual involving seiðr". She "was not able to influence, but only to uncover, future events" (p. 116). Even the völva of *Vǫluspá* is "unable to change the outcome" (p. 43). Active manipulation is sorcery (*fjǫlkynngi*), which "was shared by mortals, both women and men" (p. 119).

- **Jochens's reading of Þorbjǫrg (Eiríks saga ch. 4).**
  - Þorbjǫrg is "unable to foretell the future at will. The time has to be right, and a magical ritual is required" (p. 115).
  - She needs her charms (*tǫfr*), her staff, and "female helpers who are to sing a chant called the Varðlokkur".
  - She "sits on a platform surrounded by other women" while Guðríðr sings.
  - Afterwards, the chant "had attracted numerous spirits (náttúrur) who, although not willing to have anything to do with her earlier, now revealed many things" (pp. 115-16).
  - Her meal is "the hearts of all the animals available" (p. 115).

- **Völur are documented outside Iceland.** "Human sibyls were limited to nordic areas outside Iceland, particularly Norway", which the laws confirm. Even Þorbjǫrg works in Greenland, but with an Icelandic woman "who had learned the requisite singing from her foster mother during childhood in her homeland" (p. 116). A generic passage: "It was the custom at that time that women who were known as vǫlur traveled around the country and told men their fate and the prospect of the season" (p. 116).

- **Two more rites of divination, both originally female.**
  - "Going to inquire" (*ganga til fréttar*): "in the majority of the cases the object of the frétt was a mortal woman who performed seiðr" (p. 116).
  - "Sitting outside" (*útiseta*) at night, preferably at a crossroads: applied to both genders later, but "originally this ritual was limited to females" (p. 117).

- **Men took over divination in Christian dress.**
  - Earl Hákon visits a Swedish *vísendamaðr* who "traveled from feast to feast", which casts a man in "the familiar role of the female vǫlva" (pp. 117-18).
  - The Scilly hermit-prophet converts Óláfr Tryggvason (p. 118).

- **Magic was female first, and women stayed stronger at it.**
  - In the sagas of Icelanders she counts "forty-one women and thirty men"; after discounting pupils and one-act figures, "eight powerful men, as compared to eighteen sorceresses" (pp. 123-24).
  - In the contest between the magicians Esja and Korpúlfr, the woman wins because she "was in control of more things" (p. 123).
  - Men entered magic late, perhaps through runes (p. 127).
  - Ironically, Iceland's Early Modern witch trials "chiefly involved men" (p. 131).

- **Eighty sorcerers burned, "whether they were women or men".** The oldest tradition of Óláfr Tryggvason's mass burning (Theodoricus; Sæmundr via Oddr) names women, "listing them first". Snorri "ignores them" by using the masculine pronoun (p. 125). This extends Blain's note (`norse_blain_nine_worlds.md`).

- **Textile magic.**
  - "Special shirts woven by female magicians offered protection in battle." Women could also "palpate a man (þreifa um) before combat" so that blades would not bite.
  - On division of labour: "women wove shirts while men prepared swords" (p. 122).
  - In Codex Regius the word *bók* "is consistently used about embroidery or weaving", and *skript* means both "writing" and embroidered "representation" (p. 127).

- **Snorri on seiðr and *ergi*.** "So much ergi accompanies it that men (karlmǫnnum) could not participate without incurring shame, and the activity (íþrótt) was therefore taught to the priestesses (gyðjur)" (*Ynglinga saga* 7, p. 73). Loki charges Óðinn with having done seiðr on Samsey and having struck "wands like witches" (*sem vǫlur*): "that I find is argr behavior" (*Lokasenna* 24, p. 73).

- **Jochens's staff reading is sexual, not textile.** She ventures that "the seiðr ceremony imitated heterosexual intercourse where the woman played her accustomed role of receiving, not the male member, but its substitute, the staff" (p. 74). She concedes "little evidence exists for these speculations" (p. 74). She does **not** read the staff as a distaff.

- ***Fordæða* became a man's insult.** *Fordæða* ("evil-doer", witch) is feminine in form. It became an insult against men demanding full compensation. In the older Gulathing law it stood "among sexual insults imputing female behavior to men" (p. 129). The Borgarthing law lets an accused woman clear herself, noting that "she does not herself cause that she is a troll" (p. 129).

### Women in cult (chs. 2-3)

- Women led the *dísablót* and *álfablót*.
  - Princess Álfhildr "reddens the altar with blood" (p. 46).
  - Sighvatr's Swedish housewife turns him away from her *álfablót*: "Do not come in ... I fear the wrath of Odinn—we are heathens" (p. 46).
  - In *Vǫlsa þáttr* the housewife keeps the cult object and leads the household ritual (pp. 46-47).
- "Old Norse prose literature mentions five cases of human women" holding the office of *gyðja*, "all ... associated with Freyr's cult". "A woman could inherit this office but not exercise the political function" (p. 79).
- Gunnarr helmingr met Freyr "attended by a priestess" in pagan Sweden (p. 48).
- Steinunn tried to convert the missionary Þangbrandr and told him Þórr had wrecked his ship (*Njála* ch. 102; p. 79).

### The warrior woman (ch. 4)

- **Thesis: the warrior woman was a male fantasy.** She argues, "apart from a few exceptions born of necessity, Germanic women engaged in war neither during the centuries of European migration nor during the later viking age" (p. 110). Maiden warriors "were invariably overcome, defeated, or even killed" (p. 105). Authors made them fail "perhaps as a comfort to men" (p. 111).

- **Women and weapons in the sagas of Icelanders.**
  - "Whereas men carried weapons at all times ... women were never armed" (p. 109).
  - Búi's mother tells him not to go "weaponless as women" (*slyppr sem konur*, p. 109).
  - The exceptions are defence of the farm (Þuríðr on Skúfey, "no worse than any man") and Kálfr's ramparts in 1212, held by "both women and men" (p. 109).
  - Women "dressed in male garb to make the group appear larger ... but they did so only on men's orders" (*Laxdæla* ch. 63, p. 110).
  - Women stopped fights "by throwing clothing over weapons" (*Vápnfirðinga* ch. 18, *Eyrbyggja* ch. 18, p. 110).

- **Marriage ends the career.**
  - "Only unmarried women and virgins engaged in outright combat" (p. 94); shield-maidens' martial life "lasted only until marriage" (p. 96).
  - The maiden king (*meykonungr*) must stay a maiden to rule as *konungr* (p. 102).
  - **Exceptions:** Þornbjǫrg returns to arms as a married queen to rescue her captive husband (pp. 102, 106). Áslaug "commands a large army in her husband's absence" (p. 106).

- **Maiden warriors take men's names.** Hervǫr leaves home "equipped as a man" as Hervarðr (p. 99). Þornbjǫrg rules a third of Sweden as "Þorbergr" and "forbids people to refer to her as maiden or woman" (p. 101). Ketill's flat-blade blow on her is called *klámhǫgg*, "one of the strongest sexual insults" (p. 101).

- **The historical record.** Plutarch on Germanic women in a lost battle; German graves with "wounded male and female soldiers ... side by side in battle dress"; an Irish "Red Maiden"; William of Jumièges on fighting women in France; a female warrior grave found in Norway in 1900 (pp. 107-8). Jochens writes before the Birka genomics (2017, 2019).

### The whetter (ch. 8) and the avenger (ch. 6)

- **The inciter is mostly literary.** Male authors "revived, amplified, and multiplied the ancient figure of the inciting woman in an effort to deflect the blame for men's unreasoning behavior" (pp. 174-75).
  - She is almost absent from *Sturlunga saga* (p. 194).
  - In *Njála*, inciters rise to 48 percent of all cases in her recount (p. 192).
  - She is "the perfect alibi" (p. 201).

- **Her real kernel is the widow without kin.** "It may also have served a social function among Icelandic widows who were without close kin and who thereby sought to preserve their position" (p. 210). For Þorgerðr and Hildigunnr, "revenge means not only restitution of honor but also economic survival through fines" (p. 202).

- **Many inciters were not kin.** Of 56 cases, "twenty-six, or more than one quarter, were not related to the victim", which she says is hard to explain "in other than literary terms" (p. 193).

- **Tokens.** Jochens tests Miller's ritual-token thesis and finds only eight bloody tokens:
  - Sigríðr's spear;
  - Þorgerðr's head;
  - Hróðný's body and cap;
  - Guðrún's shirt and drawers;
  - Hildigunnr's cloak (pp. 184-87).

  Þorgerðr digs up the head on a man's instruction and "even confesses that she does not see the point" (pp. 184-85). Þuríðr serves her sons stones with their meat (p. 188).

- **The verbal arsenal.**
  - "Does not dare" (*þorir eigi*);
  - "deed-less sons";
  - "memory like hogs" (*svínsminni*);
  - "more female than male nature" (*kvenna skap en karla*);
  - "you would scarcely be men";
  - *ragr* (pp. 189-91).

  Women also threatened their own bodies: a wife swears she "will not bear and raise (ala) another son" (p. 191).

- **The contemporary case.** In 1242 Steinvǫr Sighvatsdóttir told her husband: "I will take up weapons and see whether men would follow me, although it goes against my character, and I would leave you the keys to the pantry" (p. 195). He refused anyway.

- **The law punished men's words, not women's.** Lawmakers framed rules against dangerous words, and "the perpetrators of verbal transgression were invariably men". "If feuds were largely sparked by female inciting, it is difficult to believe that such behavior would have been omitted" (pp. 197-98).

- **Some women incited for peace.** A milkmaid incites "not to revenge, but to stop an act already in process", and is called *mannfýla* ("foul creature") for it (pp. 192, 196). "A large catalogue can be made of women who attempt to curb male violence" (p. 160).

- **Women's violence was rare and clumsy.** Grágás prosecutes a woman "if she succeeds in killing" (p. 158). The saga cases are few: Þórdís under the table with Gísli's sword; Auðr in *Laxdæla* wounding, not killing, the husband who left her (p. 159).

### Conclusion

- *Drengr* is applied to Bergþóra, Hallgerðr and Hildigunnr. *Skǫrungr* "is more frequently associated with women than men". The three women called "highly outstanding" (*mikill fyrir sér*) "were magicians" (pp. 212-13).
- This "gender blurring" runs one way, toward the male: "This state of affairs must be interpreted as the ultimate victory of patriarchy" (p. 213). "Men who were accused of activities associated with feminine biology incurred the highest scorn" (p. 214).
- Dress: "the law prohibited both sexes from wearing opposite clothing" (Gg 1b:47, 203-4). The norms "specified the clothes each gender was not to wear", and the *faldr* headdress was "the distinctive badge of female identification" (p. 212).

**Contradictions and flags.**
- **Distaff reading.** Jochens offers a sexual staff reading and no distaff reading (p. 74). It is a minority, self-confessed speculation. Our distaff reading (Price, Gardeła) is not undercut by evidence, only by her silence. Record it as dissent.
- **Þrymskviða.** Jochens builds her definition of *argr* partly on Þórr's bridal disguise in *Þrymskviða* 17 (p. 73). Per our rules, cite only *Lokasenna* 23-24 and *Ynglinga saga* 7 for *ergi*, never Þrymskviða.
- **Warrior women.** Jochens's "male fantasy" thesis (1996) predates the Birka genomics (2017, 2019). The faith keeps its claim in its own voice.
- **"Manly".** The source language is relentlessly "manlike". Our loc must translate the deeds, not the frame.
- **Iceland.** No living völva in Iceland itself (p. 116). This agrees with the companion note.
