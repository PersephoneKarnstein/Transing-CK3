## 1. The argument in brief

- **A unique text.** Rosen calls the passage "a transsexual wish". She says it is unique in Jewish literature and, as far as she knows, in European and Islamic writing of the time (R2000 p. 88; R2003 p. 169). The label is hers, in the language of 2000. Do not take it into loc.
- **Against the either/or.** Most critics labelled it "satire", "parody" or an "amusing piece", which she says spared them a serious reading (R2000 p. 88; R2003 pp. 169–170). Her thesis is that "it is precisely under this comic guise" that the text explores and crosses the limits of Jewish masculinity (R2003 p. 170).
- **The blessing.** The speaker says *shelo asani ishah* "meekly, with a faint voice". Rosen reads this as a man saying the men's blessing in the tone of the women's one, *she-asani kirtsono* ("who made me according to His will") (R2000 p. 89; R2003 p. 171). The women's blessing is first attested at about the same time, by Jacob ben Asher of Toledo (d. c. 1340). So the passage may be "one of the first historical evidences" of that blessing (R2003 pp. 171–172).
- **Maleness as defect.** The penis and foreskin, the 613 commandments and the scholar's curriculum are a burden and a lack (R2003 pp. 172–177). The legal term for the "fixed blemish", *mum kavua'*, is a Talmudic term for an irreversible injury (R2003 p. 248 n. 16).
- **The female sphere.** Spinning by moonlight, the hearth and embroidery are an idealised, male-imagined femininity: warm, bounded, uncompetitive (R2003 pp. 177–179).
- **Whose voice.** The man's voice turns into a woman's within a single sentence, by free indirect speech, with a slip from first to third person and back (R2003 p. 179, p. 250 n. 31). The imagined woman then dreams of a husband, a wedding, jewels, a son's circumcision: a "Cinderella" on the cinders (R2003 pp. 179–180).
- **The joke.** Talmudic double meanings (the Jerusalemites' "well-kneaded bread", Shabbat 62b) show "a man's voice imitating a woman's voice", joking with male readers over her head (R2003 pp. 180–181). The women's three commandments, "blood, fire and dough", are called "sweet", though the Mishnah says neglecting them brings death in childbirth and Genesis Rabbah 17 makes them penance for Eve (R2003 pp. 181–182).
- **Conclusion.** "Gender trouble": the text shows masculinity and femininity as a range, not poles (R2003 p. 183). But the reading stays androcentric. "Masculinity is not loathed or rejected, but momentarily deserted" (R2003 p. 184). The trip into femininity widens male subjectivity and comes back: "The circumcised prince has finally found the Jewish Cinderella inside himself" (R2003 p. 185).
- **Genre.** *Even Boḥan* is a Bakhtinian "seriocomic" mix, mostly penitential, with near-carnivalesque festival satires of the Provençal communities. Gender trouble mirrors genre trouble (R2003 pp. 185–186).

The direction of the revision is clear. **R2003 leans further towards a sincere reading** than R2000 did, while keeping the conclusion that the fantasy is androcentric and temporary.

## 3. Does Rosen support "a satire whose sincerity is debated"?

**Yes, as a description of the field. Less so as a description of her own view.**
- The debate is real, and Rosen documents it:
  - Habermann calls it the author's "humorous grievance" (R2003 pp. 250–251 n. 39).
  - Pagis calls it "a facetious grumbling" (same note).
  - Fleischer calls it "a true feat of humor" that fails as satire (p. 251 n. 40).
  - Another critic sees the envy of women as fake, a device to reaffirm masculinity (p. 183).
  - Rosen herself says the nature of the reaction, "whether sincere or satirical", is "yet to be discussed" (p. 172).
- Her own position is a third one. The text is **seriocomic**: a serious critique of Jewish gender made "under this comic guise" (p. 170). By 2003 she calls his sympathy for women "unequivocal" (p. 176).
- She never reads the speaker as wanting to *stay* a woman. The wish is a "momentary" desertion, an "excursion ... and back to masculinity" (pp. 184–185).

**Recommended wording for the narrator.** Keep "perhaps in jest" (the README and `tm_nehpakhot_desc` both use it). It is fair to the field and to Rosen's "comic guise". If a fuller line is wanted: "a lament written half in jest; scholars still argue how much he meant it." Never "Kalonymus was a trans woman", and never the narrator citing Rosen's word "transsexual".

## 5. Corrections to existing docs

1. **"Written for Purim laughter"** (`jud_kalonymus_even_bohan.md` §8, loc `tm_hipukh.0001.desc_touchstone`). Not supported; see §4.
2. **Place of writing.** Add to `jud_kalonymus_even_bohan.md` §1 that *Even Boḥan* was written in Catalonia (R2003 p. 177; p. 246 n. 1). Arles remains his birthplace and the sect's seat.
3. **Birth year.** Rosen gives 1286, Chotzner 1287. Use "c. 1286".
4. **"Exempt from military service".** The Kalonymus note §3 says Varady rejects the satirical gloss "exempted from military service". That phrase is **Rosen's own translation** (R2000 p. 87; R2003 p. 169). In 2003 she glosses it as figurative, the "service" of commandments and study (p. 246 n. 2). Rosen and Varady are closer than the note implies.
5. ***Massekhet Purim* is Roman, not Provençal** (R2003 p. 251 n. 52; R2000 p. 109 n. 38). The Kalonymus note §4 draws "a woman dances among the women on Purim" from Chotzner's summary of it. That custom, if used, is attested for Rome, c. 1320s, not Arles. Flag it as such or drop it.
6. **The women's blessing is contemporary** (Jacob ben Asher, Toledo; Abudarham, Seville c. 1340). The rabbinic note can add it beside *shelo asani ishah*.

## 6. Design implications

- **The women's blessing as the answered prayer.** This is the strongest new hook.
  - Rosen, via Tabory (R2003 p. 247 n. 10), records the following.
    - Women's prayer books in Romance languages, written in Hebrew letters, use "who made me according to His will".
    - A **15th-century Judeo-Provençal** translation reads "Blessed art Thou ... who hast made me a woman".
    - A 15th-century Hebrew-Italian siddur reads "who made me woman and not man".
  - Proposal: after a successful Turning (`tm_hipukh.0001.a.success`, or the follow-up `0002` or `0020`), she says the women's blessing for the first time, aloud. This is the exact reversal of Kalonymus's faint *shelo asani ishah*.
    - Before c. 1400: use *she-asani kirtsono* ("who made me according to His will"). It is attested by c. 1340, and Rosen dates the custom earlier.
    - After 1400: an event variant may use the Provençal "who made me a woman". *Unverified* beyond Rosen's report of Tabory; mark the date as approximate.
  - Example loc, in the mod's own words: "In the morning she says the women's blessing, not in a low voice: who made me according to His will."
- **"Blood, fire and dough".** Kalonymus's own triad (R2003 p. 181) can be the in-world name of the three commandments in loc lines, beside "The Three Lamps". For example, `tm_hipukh.0020.desc` could have the elder say "blood and fire and dough". A Rabbinic opponent may cite the Eve midrash (Gen. Rabbah 17) against the sect. The elder answers that Kalonymus called the three "sweeter than honey". That is Rosen's quoted phrase from the Hebrew; render it afresh from the Hebrew before use.
- **The spinning circle is not safe.** Mishnah Sotah 6:1 (R2003 p. 249 n. 26): what is said at the spindle can be carried to a court. This gives `tm_hipukh.0020.b` a risk branch. A small chance that talk from the circle reaches the worthies of the town, feeding `0003` or raising its chance next Adar. It keeps the sisterhood from becoming the male idyll Rosen criticises.
- **Do not write the Cinderella.** Rosen's critique is that Kalonymus's woman is passive, at leisure on the *kathedra*, defined by husband and son (pp. 179–180, 184). The built trait (learning, diplomacy) and the three commandments already avoid this. Keep it so:
  - no "chair of leisure" or exemption-from-work flavour for daughters;
  - no reward framed as a husband;
  - the elder's answer stays practice and company.
- **The clothes.** Before the lament, the speaker confesses a passion for fine clothes, including "fine linen of the kind that virgin princesses wear" (R2003 pp. 251–252 n. 53; Habermann p. 13). This is flavour for `0011` (the dress kept on) and for a court-amenity or artifact idea. Render from the Hebrew of Lemberg p. 13 first. That page was read in the Kalonymus note, but this line was not quoted there.
- **Dinah's cost.** Rosen notes that the lament "entirely ignores" what Dinah suffered (Gen. 34) and the blame commentators put on her (R2003 p. 170; Tanḥuma calls her a harlot, n. 7). Dinah is in the naming list (`tm_rite_effects.txt`, weight 10). No change is needed to mechanics. Any comment loc on the name should not be cheerful about her later story; "the one who was turned" is enough. Do not reference Genesis 34 in loc (sexual violence).
- **Purim as the licensed day for comedy.** Rosen reports Talya Fishman's argument that *Minḥat Yehudah* (Ibn Shabbetai, 1208) was read on Purim eve, "the only day ... when the reading of comical texts was licensed" (R2003 p. 231 n. 27). That supports the sect's choice of Purim as the day when what is said "for the joke" may be meant.
  - Do **not** use the same note's other thread: Purim satires on Christianity, and Horowitz on Purim violence (n. 60). It would breach the rule that gentiles are not shown as tormentors and that the narrator does not stage interfaith violence.
- **Catalonia.** Rosen places the book's writing in Catalonia under "Jewish and non-Jewish patrons" (p. 177). This is not a reason for a holy site. A translator event (`tm_hipukh.0030`) may mention that Kalonymus worked for patrons in Catalonia and for Robert of Naples. Both are already in Chotzner and Rosen.
- **Christine de Pizan** (R2003 pp. 169–170) is the mirror-image lament, a woman wishing to be born male. It is FtM-direction reaction data only. Out of scope; do not use.
