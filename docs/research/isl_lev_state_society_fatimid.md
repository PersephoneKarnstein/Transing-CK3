Source: Yaacov Lev, *State and Society in Fatimid Egypt*, Arab History and Civilization: Studies and Texts 1 (Leiden: E. J. Brill, 1991), x + 213 pp. ISBN 90-04-09344-3.
Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo. Page numbers are the book's printed ones. Where the extraction lost an even-page running head, the page was worked out from the odd-page heads either side.

**Relation to existing notes.** `later_starts/mukhannathun_fatimid_cairo.md` (§3.2) leans on Şen 2023 for Rasad and the civil war. `isl_el_azhari_queens_eunuchs.md` covers Fatimid eunuchs from the court-politics side. Lev adds three things:

## 0. Framing notes

- **No *mukhannathūn* anywhere in Lev.** The word does not occur. Lev does use al-Musabbiḥī p. 68, the Fusṭāṭ entry in which a *mukhannath* is beaten and paraded for procuring (Cairo note §1.2), but he paraphrases it as the police chief "inflicting a punishment on a pimp" at a brothel (p. 157). The gender term drops out in the paraphrase. That explains the Cairo note's negative search of Lev. Lev is a source for institutions, not for gender.
- **Lev's own words.** He calls the Egyptian-born Turks "half breeds" (*muwalladūn*, p. 94) and the black troops "the blacks". In docs and loc, use *muwalladūn* or "Egyptian-born Turks", and "Black troops" or *ʿabīd*.
- **No singers in the 1060s.** Lev never mentions singers, musicians or entertainers at the 1060s court. His morals material is al-Ḥākim's (996-1021) and al-Ẓāhir's (1021-36).

## 1. The argument in brief

### The state as household (ch. 4)

- **The caliph owned the city.** Citing Nāṣir-i Khusraw (in Egypt 1047-48), Lev says all of Cairo's shops (Nāṣir says 20,000), its caravanserais and bathhouses, and 8,000 rented buildings belonged to the imam (p. 65).
- **Royal women were rich and political.** They held land grants (*iqṭāʿ*) and ran their own offices (*dīwān*) and staffs (pp. 66-69; Ibn al-Zubayr, *Book of Gifts and Treasures*, 11th c.).
  - Sitt al-Mulk's grants in 1000 were worth 100,000 dinars a year (p. 67).
  - A slave woman, **Takarrub**, served as Sitt al-Mulk's confidante and informant. She "handled the petitions submitted to her lady" and died wealthy (p. 69; al-Musabbiḥī, obituary of 1024-25, p. 111).
- **What service earns, the sovereign owns.** Property gained in state service was held to belong to the sovereign, so it was confiscated at death or on disgrace (p. 72).
  - In 1024-25 officials tried to seize a third of the estate of Jawhar's grandson's wife, on the ground that Jawhar "had been a slave of the Fatimids" (pp. 72-73; al-Musabbiḥī pp. 32-33, 92-93). The attempt failed for lack of any legal basis (p. 73 n. 41).

### Eunuchs and slaves (pp. 74-78, 87-88, 154)

- **Supply.** The Ṣaqāliba eunuchs came chiefly from Spain, an inheritance from the Aghlabids (p. 74). The North African Fatimids also took "white slaves-Rūm", who came to Egypt with Jawhar's army in 969 "and established a quarter for themselves in Cairo" (p. 83).
- **Offices.** Eunuchs were often governors and commanders in Syria and Palestine. In the capital they repeatedly held the two key executive posts, chief of police and market inspector (*muḥtasib*) (p. 76; p. 154).
  - Before the civil war, police chiefs were "black and white eunuchs of the imam's household or Kutama Berbers" (p. 154). Some of them handed the actual work to their own slaves (p. 78 n. 78; pp. 154-155).
- **Rank.** Miʿḍād, a black eunuch who had tutored the young al-Ẓāhir for Sitt al-Mulk, was *muḥannak*, the highest grade, whose turban passed under the chin (p. 75; al-Musabbiḥī pp. 24, 71-72). Al-Ẓāhir's decree of 1024 presented his honours as the reward of loyalty (p. 77).
- **Scarce and dear.** Eunuchs were few and expensive (p. 77). Rulers gave them as gifts in small numbers:
  - al-ʿAzīz received 12 Ṣaqāliba, al-Ḥākim 10 and al-Ẓāhir 12;
  - Sitt al-Mulk gave al-Ḥākim 50 eunuchs, ten of them Ṣaqāliba (p. 77 n. 77; Ibn al-Zubayr; *Ittiʿāẓ*).
  
  They "neither dominated the ruling circles nor constituted a majority" (p. 78).
- **Armed eunuchs.** Groups of eunuchs "under patronage" appear to be those who carried arms, unlike the eunuchs of palace and harem (p. 88; al-Musabbiḥī, 1024-25).
- **The servile status could always be invoked.** After killing the regent Barjawān in 1000, al-Ḥākim told the court: "Barjawan was my slave and I employed him … Then he misbehaved, so I killed him" (pp. 26, 78; *Ittiʿāẓ* 2:27).
  - The other path was manumission. Zaydān, the Ṣaqlabī parasol-bearer, was freed and then signed as *mawlā amīr al-muʾminīn*, client of the Commander of the Faithful (p. 78).
  - Lev's summary: servile status "could be overlooked when convenient: slaves were nevertheless despised" (p. 78).

### The morals decrees and their reversal (pp. 28-29, 36, 144, 169)

- **Al-Ḥākim** (all from *Ittiʿāẓ* and al-Anṭākī):
  - **1008.** Singing was forbidden on the Nile banks and in riverside houses, and drinking on boats, during a low-Nile crisis (p. 169; *Khiṭaṭ* 4:70-71; al-Anṭākī pp. 193-195).
  - **1004-14.** Women's movement was progressively restricted, ending in house arrest in Rajab 404/Jan.-Feb. 1014, "with the exception of very young girls and very old women". Women's baths closed and makers of women's sandals were forced out of the trade (p. 28). Halm doubts the late report of a seven-year confinement (p. 28 n. 64).
  - **1010-12.** Gatherings in the desert and along the Nile were banned. Wine was forbidden and Christians and Jews were persecuted (p. 29).
- **Rajab 414/Sept.-Oct. 1023, under al-Ẓāhir.** A decree for the sacred months banned women staying in cemeteries after sunset and the gatherings at Jīza and Jazīra. It allowed music in public only on the flute (p. 144; al-Musabbiḥī pp. 14-15).
- **The reversal.** After al-Ẓāhir's accession (Dhū l-Ḥijja 411/March 1021), under Sitt al-Mulk's regency:
  - an edict cursed those who made al-Ḥākim divine;
  - "the drinking of wine and the listening to music were permitted, and women were allowed freedom of movement";
  - Christians and Jews forced into Islam might return to their faith (p. 36; al-Anṭākī pp. 236-238).

### Rasad, the regiments and the civil war (pp. 42-46, 93-96)

- **Rasad's origin.** She was "a black slave girl bought by al-Ẓāhir" from the Karaite Jewish merchant Abū Saʿd al-Tustarī, purveyor to the court (p. 42; *Ittiʿāẓ*).
- **Her office.** As queen mother she made her former master head of her own office, the *dīwān al-Sayyida* (p. 42). Turkish troops murdered him and "got away with their crime" (p. 42). Lev calls a Jew's heading her office "unprecedented" (p. 190).
  - Al-Yāzūrī, her former servant, became vizier in 1050. He also held the posts of chief *qāḍī* and chief *dāʿī*, and ran her office (pp. 42-43).
  - Lev names a structural "anomaly": her office grew in importance "at the expense of" the state's own organs (p. 43).
- **Chronology.**
  - **Jumādā II 454/June-July 1062.** Black and Turkish troops clash, the Turks prevail and some black units are driven out. Al-Maqrīzī dates *al-shidda al-mashhūra* from here (p. 43).
  - **1062-1066.** But this "was an isolated incident". **"Between 454/1062 and 459/1066-7, no clashes … are recorded."** Meanwhile:
    - the provinces stopped sending taxes;
    - viziers and chief *qāḍī*s turned over quickly;
    - the Turks took "a disproportionally large share" of a shrinking treasury (p. 44).
  - **459/1066-67.** Full battles break out in the capital, with the Turks under Nāṣir al-Dawla Ibn Ḥamdān (p. 44).
  - **460/1067-68.** His drive against the black regiments in Upper Egypt turns the fighting into country-wide war (p. 44).
  - **1071-1073.** Ibn Ḥamdān retakes Cairo in 464/1071-72 and is murdered in Rajab 465/March-April 1073 (pp. 44-45).
  - **Winter 1073.** Badr al-Jamālī arrives at al-Mustanṣir's invitation (p. 45).
- **The cost** (pp. 45, 163 n. 49):
  - a famine "taking a heavy toll … including members of the royal family";
  - much of the capital burnt;
  - the palace treasuries looted;
  - al-Mustanṣir "dependent on a subsidy paid him by one of the war-lords";
  - only one grain ship reached Egypt during the war, from al-Andalus.
- **Black troops.** They were the bulk of the infantry and low-paid, against well-paid Turkish cavalry (p. 94).
  - Their servile status and "ethnic barrier" kept them from marrying into local society, unlike the Turks and Kutama (p. 94).
  - In the 1060s they had their own quarters in Cairo. Every village had an area for them "outside the jurisdiction of the governor" (p. 94; *Khiṭaṭ* 3:29).
  - Named units included the Saʿdiyya, al-Ḥākim's sword-armed escort, which killed Ibn Dawwās for Sitt al-Mulk, and the **Faraḥiyya**, whose Cairo quarter housed purchased black slaves (pp. 88-89).
  - The Rayḥāniyya, probably from the 1030s, took its name from the eunuch ʿAzīz al-Dawla Rayḥān (p. 128).
- **The Turks** were mostly born in Egypt, yet kept the name of their origin (p. 94; Nāṣir-i Khusraw).
- **Rasad's policy.** Lev reads her backing of the black regiments as "a policy of despair", an attempt to restore balance against Turkish dominance. Because the black troops were spread across the country and outnumbered the Turks, no quick victory was possible (p. 95).
- **A Black commander.** In 458/1065-66 a black military leader killed monks and destroyed churches at Ashmūnayn (p. 188; Abū Ṣāliḥ p. 252).

### Police and market inspector (pp. 153-161)

- **The police forces.** Fusṭāṭ had an Upper and a Lower police; Cairo had its own (p. 153). The two Fusṭāṭ forces are last attested in 415/1024. After the civil war one force remained, and *shurṭa* gave way to the terms *wālī* and *maʿūna* (p. 153).
- **Status.** In al-Musabbiḥī policemen are called *raqqāṣūn*, a Geniza word also used for runners and errand-boys, so the service "carried little prestige" (p. 154, after Goitein).
- **Ceremony.** A police chief was invested publicly with sword, banner and horses (p. 155). In Rajab 415/1024 one received the *qāḍī*'s turban, a sign that the *qāḍī*'s authority over criminal law had waned (p. 156; al-Musabbiḥī p. 47).
- **Moral police** (al-Musabbiḥī, 1024-25):
  - the police chief punished a pimp at a brothel (p. 68 of the chronicle; the *mukhannath* entry);
  - a pimp-husband and his wife were punished because she had non-Muslim clients (p. 12);
  - two Christians found with two Muslim women were executed, and the women "flogged and put on public display" (pp. 50, 58).
  
  (Lev pp. 157-158.)
- **Weak in a crisis.** The police could not handle mass disorder (pp. 158-160). In 415/1025 the people of Fusṭāṭ were "authorized to kill black troops in self-defence" as starving soldiers and criminals rioted (p. 160; al-Musabbiḥī pp. 87-89).
- **The *muḥtasib*** was likewise drawn from eunuchs and Kutama. He had no legal training and judged "outside the pale of the religious law" (p. 161).

### Famine as a political stage (pp. 169-176)

- **1024-25, as al-Musabbiḥī saw it.**
  - The vizier's sugar figures, "seven in the shape of various palaces", were paraded "through famine-striken Fustat" (p. 172; al-Musabbiḥī p. 65).
  - Al-Ẓāhir went down to Fusṭāṭ to watch the Epiphany immersions and passed the unwashed dead (pp. 172-173).
  - Crowds cried: "Hunger, O Commander of the Believers, hunger. Neither your father nor your grandfather did such things to us" (p. 173; al-Musabbiḥī p. 73).
- **1054-55.** The state granaries held only "enough grain needed for the current consumption of the palace" (p. 175).
- **Byzantine grain.** Constantine IX agreed to sell al-Mustanṣir grain, but the Empress Theodora (r. 1055-56) revoked the promise (p. 163 n. 49; *Ittiʿāẓ* 2:227-231).

### Byzantium and the Melkites (pp. 40-41, 190-193)

- **The Melkite queen.** Al-ʿAzīz's wife, mother of al-Ḥākim and of Sitt al-Mulk, was a Melkite Christian. Her two brothers were made patriarchs of Alexandria and Jerusalem (p. 191; al-Anṭākī pp. 164-165). The Jerusalem patriarch negotiated with Byzantium and died in Constantinople (pp. 41, 191).
- **A Christian in the palace.** Under al-Ẓāhir a Christian dry-nurse held "high standing" in the palace (p. 191 n. 55; al-Musabbiḥī p. 79).
- **Treaty of 1027-28.** Al-Ẓāhir let the emperor pay for rebuilding the Holy Sepulchre. In exchange the Constantinople mosque reopened with the *khuṭba* in al-Ẓāhir's name (p. 40; al-Anṭākī pp. 270-271).
- **Epiphany.** The caliph watched the rites with his women from a Fusṭāṭ palace. The police chief kept Muslim and Christian bathers apart, and the Melkites processed from Qaṣr al-Shamʿ, "the old Byzantine town", to the Nile (p. 192; al-Musabbiḥī pp. 70-71; al-Anṭākī p. 196).
- **Nawrūz.** At the Coptic New Year, state employees received garments and food (p. 193; *Khiṭaṭ* 2:31-32). Lev calls Muslim-Christian festival sharing "religious syncretism typical of medieval Muslim Egypt" (p. 192).

## 4. Corrections / answers to open questions in other notes

Other notes were **not** edited. Proposed changes:

1. **`later_starts/mukhannathun_fatimid_cairo.md` §3.2, "Civil war, 1062-71 … In 1066 Cairo was at war with itself."**
   - Lev p. 44: the 1062 fight was isolated, and no clashes are recorded until 459/1066-67. At 15 Sept. 1066 the war is imminent, not under way.
   - The war ends with Badr's arrival in winter 1073 (p. 45). Rasad's seizure in 1071 (from Şen) is a mid-war event.
2. **Same §3.2, Rasad's origin.** "who gave her to al-Ẓāhir": Lev p. 42 says al-Ẓāhir **bought** her from Abū Saʿd al-Tustarī.
3. **Same §2.4 table, row "418/1027 Al-Ẓāhir allows wine and listening to singing again".** Lev p. 36 (al-Anṭākī pp. 236-238) puts wine, music and women's freedom of movement in the **accession edict of 1021**, under Sitt al-Mulk. The 1027 entry may be a re-affirmation. Add a 1021 row.
4. **Same §2.4, Rajab 414 "*qaḍīb*" gloss.** Lev p. 144 reads the exception as music on the flute (al-Musabbiḥī pp. 14-15). This supports the reed-pipe reading over "beating-stick".
5. **Same "Sources: searched with no hits … Lev 1991".** Confirmed, with the reason: Lev p. 157 paraphrases the al-Musabbiḥī p. 68 *mukhannath* entry as "a pimp" and drops the term.
6. **Same §1.2, *shurṭa* terms.** Lev p. 153: the two Fusṭāṭ police forces are last attested in 1024. After the civil war *wālī* and *maʿūna* replace *shurṭa*. *Shurṭa* remains correct for 1066 loc. Policemen were *raqqāṣūn* in al-Musabbiḥī (p. 154); see `isl_moreh_live_theatre.md` §4 on this word.
7. **Same §5, request 6 (Lev 1991).** Now read; strike it. Al-Ḥākim's decrees, al-Ẓāhir's relaxation and Rasad are covered above.
8. **`isl_el_azhari_queens_eunuchs.md` §1, "A dynasty run through eunuchs".** Lev pp. 77-78 is a counterweight: eunuchs were scarce and expensive and never a majority of the ruling circles. Both can stand. Note the tension.
9. **`isl_el_azhari_queens_eunuchs.md`, Rasad "a former Sudanese slave".** Lev p. 42 says only "a black slave girl" bought from al-Tustarī. "Sudanese" is El-Azhari's or his source's word; check before using it in loc.
