**Framing warning.** Every source is a Christian or Muslim outsider, and many East Slavic texts are sermons against "survivals" known only from fifteenth- and sixteenth-century copies (pp. 1, 5–6). The authors lean on Indo-European comparison (Dumézil, Benveniste, West) to argue for antiquity. Treat their parallels as hypotheses. The book itself relies on Rock, who rejects "double belief" (`dvoeverie`), while sometimes using the sermons' own phrase.

## 1. The argument in brief

### Fertility and the festival calendar (ch. 2)

- **Arkona's harvest rite** (Saxo 14.39.3–6; pp. 7–11).
  - Held in autumn after the harvest; William of Malmesbury's "Vindelici" put it on **the last day of November** (pp. 11–12).
  - The priest of Svantevit (the book spells him Sventovit) is set apart by "the length of hair and beard according to old traditions", while laymen shaved (p. 8). On the eve he sweeps the shrine without breathing inside it, going out to draw breath (p. 8).
  - He reads the level of mead left in the idol's horn from last year to foretell the harvest, pours it out, drinks a refilled horn "for himself, for the homeland and for the people", and refills it for next year (pp. 8–9).
  - A ritual dialogue: from behind a honey cake almost as tall as a man he asks whether the people can see him, and wishes for a cake next year big enough to hide him (p. 10).
  - A feast where "it was considered pious to violate abstinence" (p. 10).
  - Five-part structure: purification, auguries, triadic invocation, dialogue, banquet (p. 11).
- **Oracular lakes.** Glomuzi near Lommatzsch in Saxony foretold peace (filled with wheat, oats and acorns) or war (blood and ash) (Thietmar I.3; pp. 12, 60). Rethra's lake temple to Redigast (Adam II.21; p. 13). **Rethra's site is unknown: over thirty locations have been proposed**, the Tollensee traditionally, the Great Plöner See as an alternative (p. 13).
- **Wolin**: a revered spear and an early-summer festival with games and dancing (Ebbo III.1; pp. 15–16).
- **Rusalii** (pp. 16–23): a Pentecost-season festival of the dead and of spring among South and East Slavs, named from the Latin *rosalia*. Its first Balkan mentions are Balsamon's comment on **Trullo canon 62** and Demetrios Chomatenos, who describes "games, Bacchic dances and coarse theatrical plays" (pp. 16–17). Rus' sermons condemn its music, dancing and "pagan festivals" (p. 17). The Stoglav (1551, ch. 41 q. 24) describes night games of youths and maidens at St John's Eve (p. 22).
- **Koliada** (New Year, Christmas to Epiphany): ritual fights, knucklebones, and possibly a young man disguised as an aurochs. That last reading is the nineteenth-century editor Pavlov's guess (p. 19).
- **West Slavic spring rites.** Cosmas III.1 (1092): Duke Břetislav II expelled soothsayers and banned libations at springs, burials in woods, plays at crossroads, and jests over the dead "wearing masks on their faces" (pp. 23–24). Innocent III condemned "ghost masks" in Polish churches (p. 24). Kraków synod of 1408; Długosz's Pentecost *Stado* (pp. 24–25). A Hussite text says girls who never came to church all year gathered at Pentecost "to worship the idols" (p. 26).
- **Domestic offerings.** A "second table" of bread, cheese and honey for Rod and the Rozhanitsy, set in Rus' churches in the twelfth to fourteenth centuries (pp. 26–29). The Rozhanitsy become, in later folklore, women who pronounce a newborn's fate (p. 27). Szczecin's three lesser temples held tables for councils and feasting (Herbord II.32; pp. 29–30).
- **Weddings.** Brides thrown into water, rings and belts offered to water spirits (p. 38).

### Everyday rites (ch. 3)

- **Oaths.** Rus' swore by Perun and Volos and by their weapons (pp. 41–42, 72–73). The Wagrian grove of Prove at Oldenburg: a Monday court of prince and priest; no one enters but the priest, those sacrificing, and **"those in danger of death, who were never denied the right to refuge"**; no blood may be shed there "not even in times of war" (Helmold I.83; p. 43). Rügen envoys swore by throwing a stone into water (Saxo 14.25.2; pp. 44, 72).
- **Divination** (pp. 46–52).
  - Helmold I.52: the priest drank sacrificial blood to sharpen oracles; Svantevit was the most effective oracle; feast bowls blessed in the name of a good god and the Black God (Chernobog) (p. 46).
  - **Saxo 14.39.11**: Rugians took omens from the first animal they met; cast three sticks, white on one side and black on the other; **"And not even women were indifferent to this type of science"**: sitting by the fire, they drew random lines in the ash and counted them, even for success, odd for ill (p. 47).
  - Later Rus' and Polish rites: crow-cawing books, girls' Yuletide marriage divination in the bathhouse at midnight (nineteenth-century attestations), molten lead and wax (pp. 48–52).
- **Trade.** Foreign merchants had to give Svantevit a share of their best goods to trade on Rügen (Helmold I.6; p. 53).
- **Healing.** Old women healed children with knotted cords and measuring threads, condemned by a fourteenth-century sermon (p. 56).

### War (ch. 4)

- **The horse oracles** (pp. 61–62, 70–71). Three versions, which differ in detail:
  - **Szczecin** (Herbord, *Dialogue* II.33): a large black horse that no one rode, kept by one of four priests; nine lances laid on the ground a cubit apart; the priest leads it through three times; no stumble or touching a lance means go (p. 61).
  - **Arkona** (Saxo 14.39): a white horse, like "the clothes of the priests who took care of him"; only the priest may feed and mount it; it was found sweating at dawn, as if Svantevit had ridden it; three rows of crossed spears; right foot first at every row means war, left foot means abandon (p. 62).
  - **Riedegost/Rethra** (Thietmar VI.24): priests sit while others stand, cast lots, then lead the horse over two crossed spears; the horse must confirm the lots or the venture is dropped (pp. 70–71).
  - The horses were ridden only by the god (Anonymous of Prüfening II.11; p. 61).
- **A woman soothsayer at war.** In 1209 Duke Władysław III of Poland had "a fortune teller who drew water from the river with a sieve and did not drain it" and walked before the army as a sign of victory. The victorious margrave Conrad of Landsberg had her killed first (*Chronicon Montis Sereni*; p. 63). The authors read women's divination as the place where the old religion survived "away from power" (p. 63).
- **Cosmas's women.** A woman soothsayer prescribes an ass sacrifice (I.11), and a stepmother "one of the Eumenides" teaches a war charm. Cosmas uses "Eumenides" for "any priestly role assigned to women", including Krok's three daughters (p. 66 n. 38). Literary, Virgilian, "of doubtful historicity" (pp. 62, 66).
- **Banners.** Riedegost's banners never left the temple except for war (Thietmar VI.23). **The Lutici carried a goddess on their banners**: in 1017 one was pierced by a stone and they demanded reparation; they lost "another goddess" crossing a river (Thietmar VII.64; p. 65). **Stanica**, Arkona's great banner: carried before them, it let the Rugians "transgress human and divine law", so that "nothing they wanted to do was illegal" (Saxo 14.39.15; p. 65). Gerovit's untouchable gold shield at Wolgast (pp. 65–66).
- **After war.** A tithe of spoils to the temples of Szczecin and Arkona (p. 71). Votive victims chosen by horse oracle (p. 70). Bishop John's head offered to Redigast at Rethra, 10 November 1066 (Adam III.51; p. 69).
- **Hospitality and captives.** The *Strategikon* (c. 600): Slavs escort guests safely and make war on whoever lets a guest be harmed. "Prisoners of war are not kept as slaves for an indefinite time": after a set time they may pay ransom and go home "or stay there as free people and friends" (p. 72). By the tenth century Kyiv was a slave market (p. 72).

### The dead (ch. 5)

- Cremation, urns, mound and funeral feast (*tryzna*) a year on (pp. 76–81). Olga's revenge as a funeral (pp. 80–81, 86).
- **The *navii*** (spirits of the dead) are invited to the bathhouse at Radunitsa (Easter); ash is spread to catch their footprints (pp. 85–87).
- **Rusalki**: the souls of maidens who died untimely, or between betrothal and marriage; "any woman who did not marry ... had the potential to become rusalka" (quoting Dynda; p. 95). Village farewells with "girls dressed as rusalki" are nineteenth-century folklore (p. 96, citing Ivanits).
- Al-Masʿūdī: a bachelor who dies "is married after his death" (p. 84).
- **Metropolitan John "the eunuch".** When he arrived in Kyiv in 1090 people cried that a *navie* had come, a spirit of the dead. The authors suggest the label came from his being a eunuch, "a disabled person in his sexual reproductive function" (PVL s.a. 1090; p. 100).
- The restless dead appear only after Christian burial replaced cremation, and the cure is usually to burn them (pp. 101–109).

### B. What a Wendish expansion faith could draw on

- **Seat and head of faith.** Arkona and the priest of Svantevit, with the temple's tithe of spoils and the merchants' tribute (pp. 53, 71). The priest's beard is a problem for a trans-feminine head of faith, and a hook: a faith that keeps the office but lets its priestess "keep the old length of hair" and no beard would be a pointed, invented inversion. Say so plainly in the README.
- **Gate options, better than the horse.** The reading list's gate idea (a clean crossing of the spears means the god has seen a daughter) has three problems the book makes clearer: the oracle answers yes or no about *ventures*, usually war; it is a priest's rite with the god's own horse; and it repeats the Bædling "the seer sees, does not make" logic. Alternatives grounded in the same chapters:
  1. **The ash lines.** The women of the hearth draw lines in the ash; even means the god has seen a daughter (Saxo 14.39.11; p. 47). Women's own rite, domestic, and distinct from the völva's platform.
  2. **The sieve.** The candidate carries river water in a sieve to the temple; if it holds, the god has answered (1209 soothsayer, p. 63). An impossible task as proof, like the Vestal Tuccia. Dramatic and fail-able.
  3. **Horse as confirmation only.** As at Riedegost, lots first, horse to confirm (pp. 70–71): the women's ash lines ask, and the priest's horse confirms. Two-stage, so the faith's women and its temple both have a part.
- **Festival.** The late-November Svantevit feast after harvest (pp. 11–12) as the faith's great rite day, with the horn reading and the cake dialogue. A daughter declared at the feast "behind the cake" is a usable image.
- **Stanica.** A banner that lets its bearers do what law forbids (p. 65). In the faith's own voice: "Under Stanica nothing we do is unlawful." A doctrine or relic, and a strong war-banner artifact. The narrator should note Saxo is hostile.
- **Goddesses on the banners** (p. 65) and **Siwa** (reading list) give the faith female war deities without inventing them.
- **Refuge.** Prove's grove at Oldenburg admitted "those in danger of death", and no blood could be shed there even in war (p. 43). A Wendish House could be a sanctuary in this sense.
- **Hospitality and captives.** The *Strategikon*'s rule that captives go free after a time, or stay "as free people and friends" (p. 72), fits the mod's Arrivals and *Rescue the Daughters*. It is a sixth-century outsider's generalisation about South Slavs; use it as the faith's ideal, not as Wendish law.
- **Holy sites** (map keys *unverified*): Arkona (`c_rugen`), Szczecin (Triglav's four temples), Wolin (the spear), Wolgast (Gerovit), Rethra (unlocated; Tollensee or Plön), Glomuzi (Lommatzsch, near Meissen), Oldenburg in Holstein (Prove's grove).
- **Clock.** Rethra's crisis of 1057 and the 1066 revolt (pp. 14, 69); Arkona's fall in 1168 (p. 5). The book is inconsistent here: p. 7 says Valdemar "began the conquest" of Rügen in 1169. The usual date for Arkona is 1168; keep it.

### C. What the existing faiths' Slavic neighbours would do

- **Wendish pagan rulers vs. a Bædling raid.** Before any war the Wendish priest leads the horse over the spears (pp. 61–62, 70–71). An event for a Wendish ruler targeted by, or planning, a Bædling raid: the priest reads the horse; left foot first, the war is called off. Neutral toward the daughters themselves; the Wends would honour a guest and avenge harm to one (p. 72).
- **Pagan Rus' (before 988) and Kyiv as a Bædling holy site.** Oaths by Perun, Volos and weapons (pp. 42, 72–73). Sorcerers who stirred revolt were hanged from an oak and left unburied (1071; pp. 43–44). A Rus' prince would tolerate a seeress who keeps the peace and hang one who moves the crowd.
- **Christian Rus' reaction to Dorleyism.** The crowd called a eunuch metropolitan a walking dead man (p. 100). A Kyivan crowd event for a Dorleyist daughter of the knife: "A *navie* has come." Options: let the crowd jeer; have the sisters bring bread, cheese and honey to the bathhouse at Radunitsa, as for the honoured dead (pp. 26–29, 85–87), turning the insult into a rite.
- **Slavic funeral fears for a daughter.** Re-embodied daughters are infertile, and an unmarried woman who dies could become a rusalka (p. 95). In pagan Slavic counties, when a daughter dies, the locals want the old rite done properly (bath, pyre, feast) so she will not walk (pp. 86, 101–109). The House can give her the full rite (opinion gain with locals) or a Christian burial (unease). Avoid the widow-death and self-killing material in ch. 5 entirely.
- **Polish and Czech Christian courts.** Hostile to soothsayers: Břetislav II expelled them (1092; p. 23); the margrave killed the Polish seeress first (1209; p. 63). Fits the reading list's "Polish punitive castration" rung as a second Polish hostile option.

Say so; do not cite Rusalii as attested cross-dressing.
- **Finding 3, Metropolitan John.** "A dead man come" is a fair gloss; the word is *navie*, a spirit of the dead (p. 100; Cross translates "ghost").
- **The book's own slip.** The Conclusions attribute the Szczecin horse to Helmold and the Lutician horse to "Svarozhits" (p. 111). The chapter text has Herbord for Szczecin (p. 61). Cite the chapter, not the conclusion.
