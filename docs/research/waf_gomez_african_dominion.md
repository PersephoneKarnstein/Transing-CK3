**Relation to existing notes.** Gomez re-reads the same Arabic texts as `waf_ibn_battuta_mali.md` and `waf_ibn_khaldun_mali.md`, mostly through the English *Corpus of Early Arabic Sources* (Levtzion and Hopkins), and the epic largely through Niane, Conrad and Austen (`waf_conrad_austen_sunjata.md`). He is a historian arguing for the epic's partial historicity, more willing than Austen to read history out of it.

## 1. The argument in brief

Gomez sorts the oral corpus into three tiers (ch. 5, opening):
1. **High probability**, where independent sources agree: hunter guilds at the root of Mande polity, Soso domination and war, Sunjata's conquest.
2. **Plausible but unconfirmed**: dynastic rivalry, Sunjata's troubling character, and "the acceleration of gender-dominated political office".
3. **Context**: the values and protocols the epic teaches, such as caste.

He reads the epic as Mali's theory of empire. Its fourth and last principle "reengenders the formal political sphere as an exclusively male-dominated space" (ch. 5, opening). The Buffalo Woman is the key. In some versions her grievance is that her brothers had villages and slaves and she had none, or that she was excluded from sacrifices "simply because she is a woman" (ch. 5, "Sogolon, Sunjata's Birth, and the Echo of Female Disfranchisement"). The epic answers women's loss of office by remaking them as sorcerers and mothers, "alternative sites of power" (same section).

On Mali proper, Gomez sees:
- succession as contested from the start: royalists against the hunters' associations (*donson ton*), with adoption, "fictive scions" and royal slaves in play (ch. 6, "Succession");
- Mūsā as a possibly irregular, "Muslim candidate" ruler (ch. 6, "Motivations");
- Qāsā's revolt as the moment that recalls "a pre-imperial time when women were full participants in the political realm" (ch. 7, opening).

Slavery grows with empire and Islam; women at court appear mostly as enslaved attendants, wives and concubines (ch. 7, "Empire's Underbelly").

## 2. What matters for the design

### Ghana: the sister's son and the grove (ch. 3, "Sovereign Power in Ghana"; ch. 6, "Succession")

- Ghana had a royal town apart from the Muslim town, and its grove held the priests, the idols, the royal tombs and the king's prison. Gomez calls this Ghana's "existential center" and its cult "ancestral religion", his term throughout for non-Islamic practice.
- Only the king and his heir, "the son of his sister", wear sewn clothes. The paternity rationale is al-Bakrī's.
- Gomez adds a dated case: **Tunka Manīn succeeded his maternal uncle Basī in 455/1063** (ch. 6, "Succession"). This is the clearest PERIOD instance of the line in the region.
- Gomez does not repeat al-Bakrī's "adorned like women" in his paraphrase. The earlier caution in `south_reading_list.md` stands.

### Mali's succession and the sister's line (ch. 6, "Succession"; "The Curious Case of Sākūra"; "Transatlantic Voyages")

- **The first Abū Bakr.** Ibn Khaldūn: a son of Sunjata's **daughter**, made king "according to the custom of these non-Arabs, who bestow the kingship on the sister and the son of the sister". "Subsequent traditions" make him a **sister's** son, remembered in the Manden as **Bata Manden Bori**, with "Bata" marking descent through his mother (Gomez, after Niane, "Recherches"). Gomez wonders whether Kolokon was the mother; that is his speculation.
- **Gomez defends the matrilineal report** against scholars who say Mali's succession was father to son. His grounds: Ibn Khaldūn's informant knew Mande custom, Ghana gave the model, and Walata still practised it in 1352.
- **Mūsā's line is a brother's line in Gomez.** He reads Ibn Khaldūn and Ibn Kathīr ("Mūsā b. Abī Bakr") as making Mūsā the son, or in some traditions the grandson through Son Faga Laye, of **Manden Bukari (Abubakar I), Sunjata's younger brother**. He also says Mārī Jāṭa was "actually Mansā Mūsā's uncle", the brother of his grandfather (ch. 5 n. 15). This contradicts de Slane's "un fils de sa sœur" (`waf_ibn_khaldun_mali.md`, p. 112). See §3.
- **Royal slaves and the donson ton.** After Sunjata, the *jomba* (royal slaves) ruled an interregnum "without restraint". Kamanjan, champion of the hunters, lost and left for Kong. Khalīfa's murderous "archery" may be the hunters' faction in power. Sākūra the freedman (*mawlā*) may be real, or a safe figure for criticising Mūsā.
- **Adoption.** Traditions say Sunjata adopted his generals' sons, and "adoption and bastardy were common" in the imperial family (ch. 6, "Succession", n. 10). That gives a period-plausible precedent for adopted heirs in a Mande court. LATER as evidence, from 20th-c. traditions.

### Women at the Mali court (ch. 7, "Qāsā's Gambit", "Succeeding Sulaymān", "Empire's Underbelly"; ch. 6, "Cohering around the Center", "Motivations")

- **Qāsā.** Gomez retells Ibn Baṭṭūṭa (via Levtzion–Hopkins pp. 294–295):
  - Qāsā is Sulaymān's chief wife and "first cousin (daughter of a maternal uncle)". **De Slane's French has the paternal uncle** (`waf_ibn_battuta_mali.md`, p. 417). Gomez's further guess, that her father was the brother of Mūsā's mother Kanku, rests on the maternal reading.
  - She is replaced by the non-royal Banjū and released.
  - She rides daily with her slaves to the council gate.
  - She sends a slave woman to the exiled paternal cousin Jāṭil.
  - The farāriyya call for her death, and she takes sanctuary with the khaṭīb.
  - Her name was said with the king's in the khuṭba.
- **Gomez's speculation:** the "Qāsā, son of Sulaymān" who reigned nine months in Ibn Khaldūn's list "could have been Qāsā herself, depicted as a man in keeping with convention", or a regent. The narrator must call this a modern historian's conjecture.
- **Nakedness as submission.** The king's female cousins were made to appear naked before him for supporting Qāsā. Gomez reads female nudity at court as "a gendered act of political submission if not humiliation", as well as a mark of servitude.
- **Enslaved women** carried secrets and testimony: Qāsā's servant was "bound and shackled". Mūsā's caravan included thousands of enslaved women.
- **A freeborn daughter offered as a concubine.** Mūsā told Cairo's governor that a man of Mali who had raised a beautiful daughter would give her to the king as a concubine. Told this was unlawful, he said he would abandon the practice.
- **The mansa's wife received the Jenne-koi's tribute**, and the mansa did not see him. Gomez reads it as a deliberate humiliation of Jenne (ch. 6, "Cohering around the Center", from the *Taʾrīkh al-fattāsh*). This is 17th c. and LATER, but a good event hook: the queen's audience as a real office.
- **Mūsā's mother.** The *Taʾrīkh al-fattāsh* says Mūsā "accidentally killed his mother Nānā Kankan" and made the ḥajj in penance. "Kankan" is probably the woman's name **Kanku**, so "Kankan Mūsā" means "Mūsā son of Kanku" (ch. 6, "Motivations"). LATER, 17th c.
- **Gambian women mansas** (ch. 5, "Sogolon", n. 54, after Wright's Gambian traditions):
  - Niumi's first mansa was said to be a woman, Mansā Mama Andame Jammeh, followed by Mansā Wame, with twelve women in succession;
  - one account has the kingship rotating between Sonko men and Jammeh women.
  - LATER, oral, Senegambia.

### Eunuchs (ch. 2, "Islamic Conversion"; ch. 4, opening; ch. 7, "Mali and the Marinids"; ch. 11, "The Eunuchs of Songhay")

- **Gomez does not discuss "pages ou eunuques"** (Ibn Baṭṭūṭa p. 404). His paraphrase of the audience lists the farba, the farāriyya, the khaṭīb and the fuqahāʾ, with no pages or eunuchs (ch. 6, "Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's Account of the Royal Court"). So he gives no support for a eunuch corps in Mali.
- **The word is ambiguous in his own usage.**
  - He renders Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's Bornu export *fityān* as "young men slaves" (ch. 4, opening).
  - In 16th-c. Moroccan and Songhay contexts *fatā* is used of eunuchs: Pasha Jawdar is a "short, blue-eyed eunuch" (*fatā*), and a qāʾid is "ʿAmmār the Eunuch (*al-fatā*)" (ch. 14).
  - So if Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's word is *fityān*, it can mean either youths or eunuchs; context decides. Unresolved without the Arabic.
- **Eunuchs at West African courts, by date:**
  - **Gao, 10th c. (PERIOD):** al-Muhallabī says Gao's king had a palace "which nobody inhabits with him or has resort to except a eunuch slave (*khādam maqṭūʿ*)" (ch. 2).
  - **Kanem:** a reputation for exporting "black" eunuchs (ch. 4).
  - **Mali, 1340s (PERIOD):** the Marinid Abū l-Ḥasan sent the mansa, with his gifts, "a freed eunuch named ʿAnbar" (ch. 7, "Mali and the Marinids", from Ibn Khaldūn). This is **the only eunuch at the Mali court that Gomez reports**: a single foreign gift, not an office.
  - **Songhay, 16th c. (LATER for most of the period):** palace eunuchs under a *hu-kokorey-koi*; 700 in silk behind Askia Dāwūd; a 4,000-strong "eunuch cavalry" (*fārisān min khaṣī*); eunuch Kabara-farmas (ch. 11).
- **Gelding** appears in Gomez's list of what captives suffered in the trans-Saharan trade (ch. 4, "Fantasizing West African Women"). It is a violence of the trade, not a religious rite.

### Bards, Kamabolon, and the epic's date (ch. 5, "Transmission of the Traditions", "Orality and the Written Record")

- **Gomez dates the epic's crafting to the late 17th c.** He puts it under **Mansā Saman of Kangaba**, who began the seven-yearly Kamabolon re-roofing. Austen gives a mid-18th-c. founder, "Mansa Serna", in his table (Austen ed. 1999, p. 75). Note the disagreement: either way, it is after 1453.
- The Kamabolon is said to hold "holy books" deposited by Sulaymān after a ḥajj in 752/1352. Gomez rejects the ḥajj because Ibn Baṭṭūṭa, present in 1352, does not mention it (ch. 7, "Mali and the Marinids").
- Gomez accepts Wilks's songs-as-oldest-layer argument and the "first-singers". He grants that Sunjata "may have featured" in the bards' 1352 performance (ch. 5, "Transmission").
- **Dating Kirina:** using Ibn Khaldūn's 25-year reign and Walī's ḥajj under Baybars, Krina is about 630/1233 and Sunjata's death about 656/1258 (ch. 5, "Smiths and Societies in Motion").

### The epic's women, as Gomez reads them (ch. 5)

- **Do-Kamissa's grievance** (the section on Sogolon), in two versions:
  - "my twelve brethren" had villages, slaves and riches but gave her not one slave (after Frobenius);
  - she was excluded from her younger brother's sacrificial rites because she was a woman (Cissé and Kamissòko). In Kanouté's version she is "Dossigui", simply a woman, with no buffalo (n. 52).
- **Sogolon** has many names: Sogolon Kedju, Sogolon Kèjugu, Soukoulou-Koutouma, Sukulung Konte, Sira Nyading. She is Do-Kamissa's "double", "alike in sorcery" and "alike in power" (n. 60, citing a Condé text, "Sogolon and Sunjata"; probably Djanka Tassey Condé's version, unverified). In Gomez, Sunjata's *nyama* "and true Mande core emanates from his mother", his baraka from his father (section "Walking as Divine Sanction").
- **Sassuma Berete** also appears as Sama Bérété and Siga Demba (section "Sunjata and the Trope of Rejection").
- **Kolokon / Sogolon Kolokon** enters Sumaoro's chamber and learns that his *tana* is a white cock's spur. Gomez: her "(dubious) role is key in what is otherwise an entirely masculinist enterprise, her intervention a critique of that masculinity" (section "Sumaoro, Fakoli Koroma").
- **Namanje** is the third wife, a Kamara princess and mother of Manden Bukari (section "Exile").
- **Niuma Demba** is the wife of Niani's Kara Kamara. She gives away her husband's secret, then becomes Fakoli's wife and is taken by Sumaoro (section "Sumaoro").
- **Kanguba Kante**, Sumaoro's sister in some versions, is impregnated by a jinn and returns with the gift that raises his power (n. 125). In other versions this woman is Kosiya Kante (Conrad).
- **Shaving as a mark of submission.** Gomez doubts the tradition that Tiramakan and Maka Kamara shaved the heads of Soso men and made them soldiers. He reads it as a 19th-c. picture of servile armies projected back (section "Fate of the Susu"). That supports the rule that the faith's naming rite should involve no shaving or cutting.
- **Fakoli** leads the komo society of smiths (ch. 5, closing). Smiths rise to rule and command armies in the epic (Sumaoro, Fakoli, Fran Kamara). Gomez reads caste as "in formation" in the 13th c., not fixed (section "Smiths and Societies in Motion").

### Islam and "ancestral religion" (ch. 5 closing; ch. 7, "Clerical Towns")

- Gomez's frame: early Mali practised accommodation. "Islam tolerating rather than eviscerating ancestral religious practice", in which "the dali-ma-sigi is as generative as the ḥajj" (ch. 5). The *dali-ma-sigi* is the hunter's quest to sites of power.
- Mūsā's son Maghā is remembered as **"Maghan the Sorcerer"** (Maghan Soma Buréma Kéin). His short reign may have been a setback for Mūsā's pro-Islamic programme (ch. 7).
- **Dia/Diagha** was a city of jurists the sultan could not enter, where even the killer of the sultan's son was safe: "the city of God" (*Taʾrīkh al-fattāsh*, 17th c., quoted in ch. 7, "Clerical Towns"). Gomez reads it as a sanctuary within Mali.

## 3. Corrections to the candidate and to existing notes

1. **Mūsā's descent is disputed; drop it as a pillar of "the line".** Gomez (ch. 6), Conrad and Wilks (`waf_conrad_austen_sunjata.md` §3.6) follow Levtzion–Hopkins and Ibn Kathīr: Mūsā descends from Sunjata's **brother** Abū Bakr (Manden Bukari). De Slane's "fils de sa sœur" is the outlier. Correct `waf_ibn_khaldun_mali.md` §2 ("the stronger design fact") and §3.2. The defensible PERIOD evidence for the sister's line is:
   - Ghana (al-Bakrī; Tunka Manīn after his maternal uncle Basī, 1063);
   - Walata and Tagaddā (Ibn Baṭṭūṭa);
   - the first Abū Bakr, a **daughter's** son, explained by Ibn Khaldūn's informant as the custom of giving the kingship to the sister or the sister's son.
2. **Qāsā's cousinhood differs by translation.** De Slane has the paternal uncle's daughter; Levtzion–Hopkins as used by Gomez has the maternal uncle's. Leave the side unstated in game text, or follow de Slane, which was read directly.
3. **"Pages ou eunuques" stays unproven.** Gomez does not support it. The only period eunuch tied to Mali is ʿAnbar, a Marinid gift. A Mali court in the mod may have one or two foreign eunuch servants; it should not have a eunuch office. Gao in the 10th c. had at least one palace eunuch. Songhay's eunuch corps is post-1464 and mostly 16th c.
4. **The epic's date.** Gomez puts its crafting under Mansā Saman of Kangaba in the late 17th c.; Austen puts it in the 18th c. Either way the "Sunjata cycle unlocks after Kirina" design must have the narrator say the cycle was set down three to five centuries later.
5. **The narrator's vocabulary.** Gomez's "ancestral religion" is a better narrator term than "pagan" for the Mande cult. The faith can still be inside vanilla `west_african_religion`.

## 4. Design implications

### The gate, reassessed (period / epic / later)

| Step | PERIOD (867–1453 source) | EPIC (oral, set 13th c., recorded 19th–20th c.) | LATER (ethnography, 17th c.+ chronicles) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Smith-djinn contract; she gives her own manhood instead of a dependent's life | None. Smiths and sorcery are attested only as an Islamic outsider's "sorcerers" in Ghana's grove (al-Bakrī). Mali's Maghā is "the Sorcerer" in oral tradition only. | Jinn sell power for kin; a sister gives herself instead (Kosiya Kante, Conrad pp. 206–207). Sumaoro's sister and a jinn (Gomez n. 125). Smith-kings Sumaoro and Fakoli (Gomez ch. 5). | Brett-Smith pp. 67–76: the price is a dependent's life, or "he has given himself" and become impotent, "made himself a woman". Koné p. 5 denies that impotence makes a woman. |
| 2. A woman claims her as her mother's child | Women's political weight: Qāsā "partner in rule", her name in the khuṭba; the king's female cousins decide whom to honour (Ibn Baṭṭūṭa pp. 417–419). Nothing on adoption or sisterhood rites. | Mothers and sisters as *sabu*; the foster-mother Ma Tenenba; the sister-guide Kolonkan; badenya (Conrad, Johnson). Adopted heirs common in the royal house (Gomez ch. 6, traditions). | Koné: badenya, sinjiya, the mother's curse "through her womb and breastmilk", the mother's naming rights (pp. 2–3). |
| 3. Her name sung before the mansa | The *shāʿir*/intermediary before the throne (al-ʿUmarī, 1330s). Dūghā's praise-song with his singing wives. The masked jeli who admonish the king (Ibn Baṭṭūṭa pp. 412–414). | Tumu Manian, a female first-singer. A king renames a bard by shaving (Bala Fasseke). | The Kamabolon recitation (17th–18th c.). *Jelimusow* as stars (Newton). "Nyama is in speech" (Bird). |

**Verdict.**
- **Step 3** is the best-grounded and should carry the faith's claim to antiquity.
- **Step 2** has a PERIOD setting (powerful royal women) but its form, sisterhood or fostering as a claim, comes from the epic and from ethnography.
- **Step 1** is LATER at its core. The epic gives it a myth: a sister who stands in for her kin. It is not a period rite.
- Gomez's reading of the epic as recording women's loss of office gives the faith its historical voice: Badenya keeps the older, Ghana-and-Walata order that the mansas abandoned. That is a reconstruction the narrator must label as such.

### The line

- Doctrine text (faith voice): "Ghana knew its heir by his mother. Walata still does. The mansas forgot." Base it on Gomez ch. 3 and ch. 6.
- Mechanic: the preferred heir is a sister's or a sister's daughter's child.
- An optional "royal slaves hold the interregnum" event (the *jomba*, Gomez ch. 6) when a Badenya ruler dies without a named heir.
- Adopted heirs are acceptable. Gomez reports adoption in the royal house from traditions, so the faith's "Take as Daughter" sits well in a Mande court.

### Court events

- **Qāsā's chain**, from Gomez ch. 7. The demoted queen rides to the council gate. Her servant is forced to testify. The farāriyya demand death and she takes sanctuary. The khaṭīb's protection is the release. The king's female cousins choose whom to honour with dust.
- **The queen's audience:** a tribute-bearer who may see only the mansa's wife (*Taʾrīkh al-fattāsh*, LATER) can be a minor office for a Badenya queen consort.
- **The concubine-daughter custom:** the Cairo anecdote about Mūsā can be a hostile-Muslim reaction to a Mande court ("they give their daughters to the king without marriage"). Do not make it a mechanic.
- **"Maghan the Sorcerer":** a ruler epithet for a Mande king who favours the old cult. It fits a Badenya-friendly mansa.
- **Dia, the city of God:** a sanctuary town no king may enter. It could be a refuge for a hunted daughter or House mother. LATER (*Taʾrīkh al-fattāsh*).

### Eunuchs and the knife

- A Mali court of the faith should not offer a eunuch office (§3.3). At Gao, a single palace eunuch is PERIOD (10th c.). Songhay offices (*hu-kokorey-koi*) are for a post-1464 Songhay court only.
- Daughters of the knife from other faiths visiting a Mande court are therefore exotic. The court is more likely to read them as Maghribi or Egyptian palace servants, like ʿAnbar, than as anything local. Reaction line (Mande courtier): "The Marinid sent the mansa a eunuch once, with the silks."

### Holy-site text

- `c_awkar` (Ghana): "where the heir was the sister's son, and the grove kept the kings' tombs" (al-Bakrī via Gomez ch. 3).
- `c_walata`: "where a man is named for his mother's brother" (Ibn Baṭṭūṭa via Gomez ch. 6).
- `c_niani`: the capital of the oral corpus, though Gomez notes the capital probably moved, with Dakajalan, Sunjata's birthplace, as an early centre (ch. 6, "Cohering around the Center"). Holy-site text should say "the Niani of the songs".
