## 1. The argument in brief

- **Thesis.** For "the better part of a millennium, from 200–1400 CE," theologians, mapmakers, naturalists, lawyers, surgeons and alchemists used "hermaphrodites" and "androgynes" to think with (p. 4). Abstract thinking had real victims, such as Berengaria Castelló, examined by a surgeon in Perelada in 1331 and found to have "more the aspect of a man than a woman" (pp. 1–4). DeVun uses "intersex" for bodies and "transgender" as an analytic, not an identity (pp. 9–10).
- **Chronology.** "An embrace of the idea of nonbinary sex among authorities in early Christianity, its rejection at the turn of the thirteenth century, and a new enthusiasm" at the Renaissance (p. 11).
- **Ch. 1, paradise and resurrection.** Early writers (Gregory of Nyssa, Eriugena c. 860s) imagined Adam as an androgyne and the resurrected as sexless (pp. 25–26, 31–33). By the late twelfth century the androgynous Adam was tied to sodomy (Peter the Chanter; Stephen Langton; Hugh of St Cher) (pp. 28–29). **Otto of Freising** (*Chronicle*, 1157) has women rise as women, but intersex bodies, "whom a mistake of nature has badly joined or badly divided," rise corrected, like the lame and the short (pp. 33–34).
- **Ch. 2, monstrous races.** The Hereford Map (c. 1300) puts a turbaned, two-sexed figure on the Nile: "a race of both sexes, unnatural in many of their customs" (p. 40). Monstrous-race imagery split *sexus* into four: genitals, secondary traits, sexual role and **social labour**. The Westminster Abbey Bestiary figure holds a sword on one side and scissors on the other (pp. 59–61).
- **Ch. 3, Jews and the hyena.** See §2.1 below.
- **Ch. 4, natural philosophy and law.** Before c. 1250, Galenic-Arabic texts (*De spermate*, the *Prose Salernitan Questions*) placed sex on a spectrum with "manly women", "effeminate men" and people "of both sexes" in the middle, without moral outrage (pp. 112–115). After the 1250s the Aristotelian binary won: Albert the Great, the Franciscan *De corpore humano*, Peter of Palude (pp. 116–124). Jurists sorted people into "three types, but only two binary sexes" (p. 126) and were pragmatic. DeVun finds no documentary support for claims that intersex people were executed (p. 125).
- **Ch. 5, surgery.** Between c. 1250 and 1350, elite surgeons (Bruno Longobucco 1252, William of Saliceto c. 1270, Lanfranc of Milan 1296, Henri de Mondeville, Guy de Chauliac 1363) took over Arabic recommendations to cut "superfluous" genital flesh, and made themselves "arbiters of sex" (pp. 134–143). They paid no attention to the patient's own sense of self (p. 153). DeVun turns this against Foucault's claim that premodern people had "free choice" of sex (p. 156).
- **"Transgender" as the frame.** Medieval experts feared that such a person "might abandon their assigned sex and adopt a new sex," or alternate. "Gender inversion, and not just nonbinary anatomy per se, was the salient threat" (p. 158).
- **Ch. 6, alchemy.** From Petrus Bonus (*New Pearl*, c. 1330) to *Aurora consurgens* (Zürich MS, 1420s) and the *Book of the Holy Trinity* (early fifteenth century), the philosophers' stone became a male-female "rebis" and then a male-female Christ (pp. 167–186). Here "mixture" meant possibility, not pollution (p. 170).
- **Conclusion.** Medieval people were sometimes "far more comfortable with genitals that did not ostensibly match gender identity" than modern institutions, and sometimes far more vicious, above all in racial and religious exclusion (p. 203).

### 2.2 Catholic reaction: what a Latin court could say, and when

DeVun's book allows four Latin voices, each with its own dates. This refines Wingard's "neutral before c. 1150, unnatural after" (see §3).

| # | Voice | Dates | Page | What it says to a daughter |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Polemic and moral theology** | Hostile throughout: Gregory the Great (6th c.), Guibert (1111), Bernard of Cluny (c. 1140), Alan of Lille (1160s), John of Salisbury (1159) | 83–87 | The "neither man nor woman" figure is unclean and contagious. John of Salisbury's courtier-philosopher is "a monstrous thing" because he is both (p. 82). |
| 2 | **Natural philosophy** | Neutral spectrum c. 1100–1250; Aristotelian binary after the 1250s (Albert, *Questions on Animals* 1258, *De animalibus* c. 1263) | 112–121 | Albert: such a person is "a monster in nature" yet human, and "really" male or female (pp. 118–120). |
| 3 | **Law (canon and Roman)** | Pragmatic throughout, from Gratian and Azo (c. 1200) to Baldus (1390s) | 124–129 | The person's own choice counts; once made, it must be kept. |
| 4 | **Surgery** | c. 1250–1350, then Guy de Chauliac (1363) | 134–156 | "Nature erred seriously" (Lanfranc, p. 142); the surgeon restores the "natural form". |

New legal detail beyond van der Lugt:
- **Hostiensis** (mid-thirteenth century): a "perfect hermaphrodite" should **swear an oath to the bishop** to use only one sex, with no change afterwards (p. 128). This is the source for the oath that `cmp_vanderlugt_sex_difference.md` row 4 proposed for the canonist.
- **Baldus de Ubaldis on Giovanni Malaspina** (1390s): a sex-variant heir who lived as a man and asked to **succeed to his father's fief**. Baldus called his sex "mixed" or "confused", but let him inherit, because he was not a woman and his disposition suited "riding horses, handling weapons, and giving wise counsel". Had he been "fragile and feminine", he would have been judged female (pp. 128–129). The choice, made after puberty, was permanent.
- **Rolandus Passagerii's** notarial *Summa* (d. c. 1300) set the threefold division down to a binary one, "so that the threefold division can be reduced to a binary division." It was read in **Poland, Austria and Hungary** (p. 126). This means a notary in the German lands had a formula for registering sex.
- **Rolker**, cited by DeVun: jurists did not treat such people as legal "monsters"; "their humanity was never in question" (p. 129).
- The ***Summa "Omnes homines"*** (probably early fourteenth century) is harsher. Anyone who uses both members "according to the universal faith, he is unclean," and "ought not to be tolerated" (p. 131).
- ***Placides et Timeus*** (French, late thirteenth century) says that "the ancient judges" made people choose a nature, punished them bodily if they went back on it, and closed the unused organ surgically (p. 153).
- Some jurists **preferred to assign the male sex**. Augustine: "the prevalent usage" gave such infants to "the superior sex"; the *Secrets of Women* says "the worthier male species" (p. 129).

**The German lands, specifically.**
- **Cologne.** Albert taught in Paris and Cologne and "counted a number of physicians in Cologne among his friends." His anecdote of a child raised as a girl, cut open, found to have testicles, who later "took a wife and bore many children from her," may come from them (p. 120). From about 1260, a Cologne physician is the period-correct learned hostile voice in the Rhineland.
- **Freising.** Otto of Freising (1157): at the resurrection, defects are removed and "the natural state" is kept (pp. 33–34). A Bavarian bishop's answer to a dying daughter is that she will rise "as nature made" her.
- **Colmar.** The Annals of the Friars Minor of Colmar record the one known surgery on an apparently intersex person, in Bologna (p. 155, citing Miri Rubin). An Alsatian friar's chronicle reports a "correction" as a marvel.
- **Medical licensing** applied "in parts of Germany" by the later thirteenth century (p. 136). *Unverified* which territories.
- **A counter-tradition from the same lands:**
  - The eleventh-century **bronze doors of Hildesheim** show Adam and Eve without visible sexual difference (p. 178). A twelfth-century German copy of Ambrose's *Hexaemeron* shows them as one body with two torsos (pp. 179–181).
  - **Elizabeth of Schönau** (Rhineland, twelfth century) called Christ's humanity "a female virgin" (p. 192).
  - **Henry Suso's** *Exemplar* (Strasbourg MS, c. 1371) shows an androgynous Christ-Wisdom (p. 191).
  - **Hugh of St Cher**: "just as the hare is at once male and female, so the good prelate is both father and mother" to his flock (p. 191). The hare is an animal image, so it is for a Christian speaker only and never about the sect.
- **Constance, 1414–18.** The anonymous author of the *Book of the Holy Trinity* (*Das Buch der heiligen Dreifaltigkeit*), probably a descendant of the Spiritual Franciscans, presented it at the Council of Constance to **Emperor Sigismund**. It interested **Wenceslas of Prague**, and **Friedrich of Brandenburg** became his patron. The book says "Jesus-Mary is and was one being in the same substance." It survives in at least twenty manuscripts (pp. 185–186). It also pictures an **Antichrist hermaphrodite** (p. 186).

### 2.3 Other faiths, briefly

- **Dorleyism (eunuchs).** In the Latin West, eunuchs were "generally detested" and "virtually 'unsexed'" (Murray, p. 148). The *Catholicon*: "a hermaphrodite is said to be castrated, since [this person] seems to be neither man nor woman" (p. 148). Henri de Mondeville counted eunuchs and effeminate men among the "soft" parts of the body politic (p. 152). Huguccio did not bar eunuchs from the priesthood (p. 127). This confirms the README: a Latin court reads a Dorleyist eunuch daughter as a castrate, not a woman.
- **Jade Embryo.** Latin alchemists also used gestation. Albert compared the growth of metals to a foetus in the womb (p. 168). *Aurora consurgens* shows Wisdom pregnant with Mercury (pp. 173–174). These are parallels for flavour only; they do not show any contact between the traditions.

- **`cmp_wingard_trans_middle_ages.md`, row 1** ("Early-game Latin clergy should be milder than late-game"). This is only half right. Moral theology and polemic were already hostile before 1150 (Gregory, Guibert 1111, Bernard of Cluny c. 1140; pp. 83–86). The book's own turning point is "the turn of the thirteenth century" (p. 11). What hardened after c. 1250 was **natural philosophy** (pp. 115–116), and new **surgery** came in 1250–1350. **Law stayed pragmatic** throughout. Suggested rule: Catholic *theologian* and *preacher* characters are hostile in every era. *Physician* characters move from neutral (before 1250) to "monster in nature" (after). *Canonist* characters are pragmatic in every era, and accept an oath.

## 4. Design implications

1. **Loc style guide: banned Christian phrasings** (all faiths, all eras): "hyena"; "neither man nor woman" said of a faith; "neither faithful nor faithless"; "double-minded"; "half-men"; "turncoat" or "turned once, will turn again" about the Nehpakhot; *neutericum*; "unclean" said of Jews. Allowed hostile register (doctrine, not people): "The canons forbid Jews to hold office over Christians"; "The Church does not recognise this prayer."
2. **Catholic reaction, the canonist (all eras).** This makes the vanilla-faith oath option concrete. A Catholic liege's bishop asks a declared or turned daughter to **swear before the bishop to live as a woman only** (Hostiensis, p. 128).
   - If she swears, Catholic opinion of her rises a little, and she gains a modifier, `tm_sworn_before_bishop`.
   - The oath records her own choice, as the canonists did. No text should treat it as the bishop granting her womanhood.
   - Loc: "Swear it before me, and keep to it. The canons fear only a turning back."
   - The line uses "turning back". Do not fire this event for a Nehpekhet (see §2.1 row 2). Give her a variant without the word: "Swear it before me, and keep to it."
3. **Catholic reaction, the learned physician (after 1260).** A court physician cites Albert ("a monster in nature, though human"). The ruler may side with the physician (Catholic piety up; her stress up) or with the canonist (no cost).
   - No genital examination appears on screen. DeVun's York impotence "congresses" (pp. 144–146) show what such scenes were.
   - The surgical "correction" is never offered as an option on a daughter.
   - It may appear once, as a hostile threat that the player refuses: "The surgeons of Paris say nature erred, and that they can correct her." Her answer: "Nature made no error in me."
4. **"The Malaspina Consilium"** (1390–1453, a Catholic realm, a daughter with a claim). A jurist cites Baldus: a person whose settled choice after puberty is clear inherits as that sex, provided the choice is kept.
   - The liege may accept her as heir in her own sex, with no piety penalty from canonists.
   - This is the strongest Latin precedent the mod has for **inheritance**. Baldus himself judged by gendered duties (horses, arms, counsel). The loc should say that is the jurist's test, not the faith's: "The doctor of Perugia rules by her settled will. That is enough for the court."
5. **"The Alchemist of Constance"** (1414–1418, a ruler in the HRE or at the Council). An alchemist from the *Book of the Holy Trinity* circle sees a daughter at court and calls her a living emblem of Jesus-Mary, "both in one".
   - She answers that she is not both, she is a woman. Picking that answer gives prestige and a friendly alchemist.
   - This uses the German counter-tradition without the "third sex" framing Bhatt warns against. The alchemist's "both" is his idea, and the daughter corrects it.
   - The Antichrist hermaphrodite (p. 186) is the same author's hostile twin. It is available as a later threat from the same man if he is spurned, *optional*.
6. **German devotional flavour** for decisions and tooltips (Catholic speakers only; the narrator never claims these):
   - Hildesheim's doors, where "God made them one before He made them two";
   - Elizabeth of Schönau, for whom Christ's humanity was "a virgin";
   - Suso's Wisdom.
   - A Catholic friend of a daughter might cite them. These are mystics' images of Christ and Adam, not claims about any person's sex. Mark the loc as the character's opinion.
7. **Resurrection** (Catholic, any era, at a daughter's death or a deathbed visit). A Catholic priest says she will rise "as nature made" her, after Otto of Freising (pp. 33–34). Her faith's answer: "Then nature made me so." This is a theological dispute only. No suicide content; fire on natural death or illness only.
8. **`tm_hipukh.0030` "A Book in Arabic".** Keep "a book of physic, or of the philosophers". Add one optional line to the translator's offer: "She will put the whole book into Hebrew and the common tongue, save one chapter, on cutting, which she leaves in Arabic."
   - This is a design invention, not a historical claim. It marks the sect's refusal of the knife (Albucasis's and Avicenna's chapters, pp. 138–139) without making a Jewish character a surgeon.
   - If this reads as too pointed, drop it. The current text is safe as it is.
9. **Expulsion hook (*unverified* detail).** On 1306, a French crown-held Narbonne or Montpellier with Nehpakhot loses its *kahal*. The variable `tm_kahal` moves to Arles or the Venaissin, if Provence remains outside the French crown. The text names the crown as the actor ("The king's officers have closed the Jewish street of Narbonne"). It never shows violence on screen, and never portrays a living community. Check Shatzmiller before scripting.
10. **Rykener.** DeVun's only mention (p. 158) cites Henningsen and Karras-Linkinen. No change.
