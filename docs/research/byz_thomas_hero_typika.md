## 1. The argument and the evidence

### 1.1 Convents in the corpus (Constable, Preface)
- Only six of the 61 documents were written for women's houses. None is for a double house (p. xviii).
- Contact with men was unavoidable but kept to a minimum. Bebaia Elpis required its priest to be married (p. xix).
- Eunuchs were a peculiarly eastern problem: "policy seems to have varied." Some monasteries forbade them, "including nunneries." Attaleiates *required* eunuch monks. Kecharitomene required its priests, spiritual father, steward and physician to be eunuchs. One of the three Galesios houses was reserved for twelve eunuchs, "no objection in principle to eunuch monks" (p. xix).
- "The beardlessness of eunuchs showed their anomalous status, even if it did not exclude them from becoming monks or priests" (p. xxii).
- Entry to religion was "a second baptism … death to life in this world and rebirth" (p. xv). Tonsure, the cutting of "worldly locks" and the habit carried the meaning; formal consecration did not (pp. xxi–xxii).
- Novitiates were long. At Lips it was three years for outsiders and six months for mature women. At Kecharitomene it was shortened for women already "practicing the monastic life in secular clothing" (p. xxi).
- Spiritual and adoptive brotherhoods between monks and laypeople were banned again and again, "the number of prohibitions suggests that they were not uncommon" (p. xx).

### 1.2 Charistike and ephoreia
- Under the **charistike** (10th–11th c.), monasteries "were held in quasi-possession by institutions and private individuals, who often exploited them for their own advantage." The grants were conditional in theory and abusive in practice (p. xxviii; ODB "Charistikion").
- The reformers pushed **ephoreia** instead: "the protection and supervision of monasteries rather than their possession" (p. xxix). The ideal was "a powerful but distant protector" (p. xxxv). The ephoros was "often a ruler, a member of the founding family, or a great noble" (p. xxxvi).
- The ephoros's rewards were in principle spiritual. In practice ephoroi "increasingly exercised a variety of powers in their own names" after the 12th century (pp. xxix, xxxvi).
- Thomas's view is that the winning 12th-century model was Christodoulos's: "a constitutionally independent foundation with a powerful protector (minus the ideologically negative title of charistikarios)" (p. 446).
- Timeline that matters for the era arc:
  - 10th–11th c.: charistike defended by emperors and patriarchs;
  - late 11th c.: reform;
  - 12th c.: independent houses with private protectors;
  - Palaiologan period: protectors grow strong again.

### 1.3 Kecharitomene: the female line "in perpetuity"
- **Independence.** Irene governs the convent herself while she lives. After her death it is "independent and under its own control." It may never be given as epidosis or ephoreia "to any kind of person whatsoever" ([1], p. 667). The protectorate she creates is therefore a private, hereditary office inside her own family. Thomas calls it "a kind of private ephorate" (p. 654).
- **The office.** The holder is the *protectress* (*antilambanomene*) and her office is the ephoreia. The first holder was to be the nun Eudokia, resident in the convent ([3], p. 669). Eudokia died, and the added ch. [80] names Anna Komnene. After Anna the office goes to Maria jointly with Anna's daughter Irene Doukaina, then to Anna's other daughter, granddaughter or great-granddaughter, "as long as the female line continues … the one who is the eldest" ([80], p. 709).
- **Fallbacks.** If Anna's female line fails, the office passes to the daughter-in-law married to the eldest male descendant. Any widow who remarries "will at once be deprived of the ephoreia" (p. 710). When the daughters-in-law also fail, it passes to "the lady from the most distinguished of our family," chosen not by age but by "the nuns … with their superior." This holds "for as long as this present age lasts" (p. 710).
- **No ownership.** The protectress may not control property, change the rule, remove the superior at will, "enroll nuns, or introduce and expel them," demand accounts, or "try to issue instructions." Her task is "to support and look after it and its possessions, to pacify the nuns who are perhaps offended" ([3], p. 669).
- **Her real powers.** She seals the superior's election lots and breaks ties ([11]). She removes a superior who is corrupt or plays favourites ([13], p. 677). She confirms the steward ([14]). She is informed of any sale of movables ([10]). She expels a disruptive noble nun ([4]). She is buried in the exonarthex only if she takes the habit ([76]).
- **The buildings are a separate line.** The imperial residence adjoining the convent goes to Anna, then her daughter Irene. After that it passes to whomever Anna wills, "male or female." Failing a will, it goes to the oldest descendant of either sex, or to a daughter-in-law (forfeited on remarriage). It is protected even against debt and treason. If the line dies out, it reverts to the convent ([79], pp. 707–08). The clause carries its own gender rule: "even though we specifically mention the male sex only yet the female sex also must be understood" (p. 708).
- **The superior.** The superior and "all the community" pick three candidates. The spiritual father writes the names on slips, the protectress seals them, and the priest draws one at the altar ([11], pp. 674–75). The superior then appoints all the other officials ([18]).
- **Admission.** Admission is free, "as a gift … without any gift" ([7], p. 672). Irene's granddaughters must be admitted and may keep a private cell and two servants ([4], p. 670). Two girls are reared in the house ([5]).
- **Men.** The enclosure is "untrodden by any men and be for ever barred to them" ([17], p. 679).

### 1.4 Kecharitomene: eunuchs at the gate, not inside it
- **The steward** "must be a eunuch of godly life." He manages every estate, is confirmed by the protectress, and is invested before the icon ([14], pp. 677–78). The assistant steward is one of the two priests and normally succeeds him.
- **The priests.** "There must be two priests around the convent; they should be eunuchs and monks." If no monks can be found, they are still to be "eunuchs of proven virtue" ([15], p. 678).
- **The confessor.** One spiritual father "who is a eunuch very distinguished for virtue" ([16], p. 679). Gautier's Greek is softer; see §4.
- **The doctor.** "a eunuch or an old man" ([57], p. 696).
- **Yet the enclosure is shut to them.** In [80], when the protectress visits, "not a single man" may come with her, "because we decree that the convent is to be completely untrodden by men and continuously closed even to eunuchs themselves" (p. 710).
- **Reading.** The convent ran its estates, sacraments and medicine through eunuchs, and still drew its door between the eunuchs and the sisters. Eunuchs were the court's useful third thing: allowed nearer to women than men were, and kept on the far side of the wall. Both halves of the existing notes are true. The reading list says Kecharitomene "required" eunuch staff; beliefs G2 says it was "closed even to eunuchs." The real point is the combination.

### 1.5 The other women's convents: protection passes to men
- **Lips (39) [3].** "women of a gentle and weak nature … need strong protection, inasmuch as they are accustomed to staying at home." The ephoreia goes to the emperor, "we entrust its ephoreia to the emperor in perpetuity" (p. 1266). Female relatives may visit, "but only eunuchs or women of mature years may accompany her" ([16], p. 1270). On the steward: "It makes no difference whether he is a eunuch or not" ([25], p. 1272). Anargyroi (40) [5] copies this: "chosen from among eunuchs or otherwise respectable men" (p. 1292).
- **Bebaia Elpis (57) [18]–[19].** "the frail nature of women requires the ephoreia and guardianship of men" (p. 1528). The first ephor is the eldest son, then the second son, then the most powerful descendant ([19], p. 1529). Her daughter Euphrosyne is nonetheless "mistress and heir of this convent and all my property" ([124], p. 1558) and later calls it "her ancestral convent." The priest must be married, "not a eunuch as in (27) Kecharitomene" (p. 1514). The steward is a nun (p. 1519). The superior must "forget feminine weakness" and govern "in manly fashion" ([27], p. 1518). The officials are elected by all the nuns ([73]).
- **So the Kecharitomene matriline is the exception, not the rule.** Two later imperial women wrote typika that hand protection to men on the ground of female frailty. Irene alone wrote a protectorate that stays with women and ends in the sisters' own choice.
- **Philanthropos (47).** This house was adapted from Kecharitomene. Its editor notes "pronoun gender changes" in the text (p. 1385 n. 7, citing Trone): a rule written for one sex, re-gendered for another.

The bans run from the 970s to 1406. They grow, if anything, with the Athonite and Palaiologan periods. Manuel II's reason is the strongest native evidence that contemporaries could not tell a eunuch from a woman by sight. It fits Matrona/Babylas (reading-list finding 2).

**Contradictions to flag.**
- **beliefs G2 (g) and QG #35/#6.** "Passes the convent with estates, church and bathhouses to daughters … 'in perpetuity'" conflates two chapters. [80] passes only the protectorship, through the female line. [79] passes the residence, the church of St Demetrios and two bathhouses to Anna, then her daughter, then descendants of *either* sex. Neither passes the convent's estates.
- **QG #64.** Calling male guardianship "nominal" at Bebaia Elpis is wrong. Theodora Synadene really named her sons. The female protectress is Kecharitomene's, a different house two centuries earlier.
- **Reading list finding 1.** "Required its spiritual father … to be eunuchs" is too strong for the confessor. The Greek says *preferably* (§4). The doctor may be "a eunuch or an old man."

**Primary-source cases usable as characters or events.**
- **Irene Doukaina** (c. 1066–1123/33), **Anna Komnene** (protectress; wrote the *Alexiad* at the convent after 1148), **Eudokia** (the nun, died before her mother) and **Irene Doukaina the younger**. All of them are probably in vanilla 1066 history. The faith may cite "the empress's rule" in its own voice; the narrator never claims them.
- **Theodora Synadene and Euphrosyne** (c. 1300): out of the window. Flavour only.
- The Phoberos eunuch story involves the abuse of a child. **Do not use.**

**Net effect of the check.**
- The two load-bearing clauses for the design are verbatim in the autograph (Paris. gr. 384): the female line in perpetuity, and the exclusion of eunuchs even while eunuchs staff the house.
- One overstatement must be corrected: the confessor is "preferably" a eunuch.
- One usable line was added: the prologue's ἀρρενοῦσα.
