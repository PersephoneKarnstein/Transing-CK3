# South of the Mediterranean: a fifth faith (reading list and candidates)

The four built faiths sit in Byzantium, Scandinavia, China and Provence. This list records a deeper search (2026-10-19) in four regions the earlier comparanda sweep had judged weak: West Africa, the Horn, India, and island Southeast Asia. Each section gives findings, candidate faiths with an evidence grade, a reading list in the `china_reading_list.md` format, and what the user needs to find. Downloads sit in `sources_pending/south/<region>/` (gitignored), with `SOURCES.txt`. No extracted text beyond short quotes.

**Common result.** No region has a recorded pre-1453 case of a man living as a woman in a religious role. Every candidate's gate is a reconstruction the narrator must mark as such; what differs is how much of its setting is attested in period.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| India | The Worn Garland (Tamil bridal bhakti) | A Mārkaḻi vow kept with companions; the worn garland; a dreamed wedding to the god | Srirangam | Fair to good as a marked reconstruction: the women's voice of male poets, its temple performance, the theology and the landed "daughters of God" are all period |
| Horn | The Gold Beneath the Wax (a Miaphysite heresy) | A *qəne* poem, "wax and gold", whose hidden meaning names her a daughter; sung back to seal it | Lalibela | Weak to moderate: the poetry, the double monasteries and the 1434–68 persecutions are period; the crossing is invented |

## India and Sri Lanka

That sweep looked at law: paṇḍaka, napuṃsaka, klība, Jain debates, hijra historiography, and Sultanate eunuchs. This pass looks at **devotion**: bhakti poetry, temple service, cult, and myth. ### 1. Findings that matter

1. **In Tamil and Kannada bhakti, men sang as women in public cult, and the voice was institutionalised before 1453. Medieval theology explained it. What no source shows is a man who *lived* as a woman.** Grade: *attested in period (voice)*; *not attested (life)*.
   - **The poets.**
     - Nammāḻvār (9th c.) sings much of the *Tiruvāymoḻi* and *Tiruviruttam* as a girl in love, the "heroine" (*nāyakī*). The tradition calls this persona Parāṅkuśa Nāyakī.
     - Āṇṭāḷ, a woman (9th c.), sings herself as Krishna's bride (Venkatesan 2010, item 7).
     - Like the other Āḻvārs, Nammāḻvār "favored the female voice": mothers, heroines, friends, cowherd girls (Venkatesan's summary on the Harvard Divinity Bulletin and OCHS pages; check against item 7).
   - **The canon and its performance.** Nāthamuni (10th c.) is credited with compiling the 4,000 hymns. He is also credited with founding the *araiyar*: hereditary **male** temple performers who sing and mime the hymns, girl-voice included, before the processional image at Srirangam, Srivilliputtur and Āḻvārtirunagari (Narayanan 1994, item 9; Venkatesan 2022, item 8).
     - Nāthamuni's role is the tradition's own account.
     - A Srirangam inscription of c. 1085 on reciting the *Tiruvāymoḻi* is reported only by a devotional website (*unverified*).
   - **The theology.**
     - The Śrīvaiṣṇava commentaries are fully in period. Piḷḷāṉ's *Six Thousand* is 12th c. (Carman and Narayanan 1989, item 10). Periyavāccāṉ Piḷḷai is 13th c., Aḻakiya Maṇavāḷa Perumāḷ Nāyaṉār 14th c., and Vedānta Deśika 14th c. (dates from Anandakichenin 2019, DOI 10.12797/CIS.21.2019.01.01).
     - They read the girl's voice as the soul's true voice before God. The common gloss is that God alone is male and every soul stands to him as woman. That is the reading to verify in items 9 and 10 before the narrator uses it.
   - **Kannada.**
     - The 12th-c. *śaraṇa* (Vīraśaiva) poets address Śiva as husband.
     - Ramanujan (item 4) translates a Basavaṇṇa vacana in which the poet wears men's clothes "only for you" and is sometimes man, sometimes woman. He also translates Dāsimayya's vacana that the self "in between" is "neither man nor woman". Both wordings are from memory and *unverified*; check the numbers in item 4.
     - Harihara's 13th-c. Hoysala *Ragaḷegaḷu* is the earliest Kannada narrative of these saints (Ben-Herut 2018, item 11).
     - The *Śūnyasampādane* anthologies begin in the early 15th c., under Vijayanagara (*unverified*; Wikipedia only).
   - **For the design.** Bhakti gives an in-period language in which a man's womanhood before God is the true one. It does not give a single man who changed his life on that basis. A faith built here is a *reconstruction that takes the voice at its word*, as the Nehpakhot take the Purim dress at its word.

2. **Chola temple women were a real, propertied, unmarried, inscribed institution: "daughters of God". It was not a crossing role, and it is not the colonial "devadāsī".** Grade: *attested in period* (c. 850–1300).
   - Orr (item 1) reads the whole Chola inscriptional corpus.
     - The temple women appear mostly as **donors**.
     - The terms are honorific (*tevaraṭiyāḷ*, *tēvaṉār makaḷ*, *patiyilār*).
     - They "remained unmarried".
     - Their wealth came partly from "inheritances from their mother".
     - Their agency declines steadily under Vijayanagara and the Nāyakas.
     - The words in quotation marks are from Daud Ali's review, *Reviews in History* 169, 2001. Orr's own pages still need checking.
   - Rājarāja I's Thanjavur inscription assigns one or more "shares", each the produce of one *veli* of land, to "four hundred women". They were transferred from temples across the Chola country and settled in the temple streets (*taḷiccēri*). The same order also pays "a number of men" for temple services.
     - Some of the women bear royal names: Kundavai, Arinji, Kannaradevi.
     - Source: SII 2, no. 66; Hultzsch's introduction; **Downloaded**.
     - Hultzsch's gloss "evidently dancing-girls" is the colonial reading that Orr overturns.
   - Saroja 2012 (item 3), an Indian epigraphist, dates the first *devaradiyar* reference to a 9th-c. inscription and discusses *tēvaṉār makaḷ* and the *nakkaṉ* name element.
   - No source found puts men in the temple-woman role.
   - **For the design.**
     - It gives a *line*: settled shares, a mother's inheritance, and the title "daughter of God".
     - It gives a *world*: gifts recorded in stone, which is the period's own currency of piety.
     - **Do not** make the temple women into trans women or "third-gender" servants. Their descendants (the Isai Vellalar and devadasi communities) are a living, stigmatised group.

3. **The myths a faith could claim are old, widely known in 867–1453, and one is about choosing.** Grade: *text attested well before period*.
   - **Bhaṅgāśvana** (*Mahābhārata* 13.12, Ganguli Anuśāsana Parva sec. XII; **Downloaded**).
     - A sonless king bathes in a lake and rises a woman. She bears a hundred sons to an ascetic.
     - When Indra offers to restore her, she answers: "I desire to remain a woman, O Sakra".
     - Indra also asks which sons she would revive. She chooses the sons she bore, because "the affection that is entertained by a woman is much greater".
     - The text gives the pleasure argument as her reason for staying. That is a male author's frame, so the faith should lead with the children.
   - **Ilā** (*Rāmāyaṇa*, Uttarakāṇḍa; *Mahābhārata* 1.75, "Ila was both his mother and father", Ganguli sec. LXXV; **Downloaded**).
     - A king enters Umā's reed grove, Śaravaṇa, where Śiva has taken female form to please the goddess, and every male there becomes female.
     - Pārvatī softens this to alternating months.
     - As Ilā she bears Purūravas, founder of the **Lunar dynasty**, which ruled from Pratiṣṭhāna at Prayāga.
     - Grove, curse and dynasty details are from Wikipedia's summary of the Rāmāyaṇa and Purāṇas. Check them in Doniger 1999 (item 13) and Goldman's Uttarakāṇḍa.
   - Many medieval dynasties claimed Lunar (Candravaṃśa or Yādava) descent.
   - **For the design.** A faith can say that half the kings of India descend from a woman who was a man.
     - It refuses Pārvatī's monthly alternation as the cage of a "third thing".
     - It keeps Bhaṅgāśvana's answer as its creed.
   - Śikhaṇḍin (MBh 5.189–193; *not read here*) runs female-to-male: reaction data only.

4. **The famous "trans" sites of South Asia are later evidence, and the narrator must say so.** Grade: *later ethnography*.
   - **The jōgappa of Yellamma** (Karnataka).
     - Bradford 1983 (item 19) and Merkle 2016 (item 20, **Downloaded**) are 20th–21st-c. ethnography.
     - Merkle gives no early attestation.
   - **Koovagam.**
     - Aravāṉ's sacrifice is in the 9th-c. *Pāratavenpā* and in Villiputtūrār's 14th-c. *Pāratam*. Both dates are from Wikipedia's summary of Hiltebeitel and are *unverified*.
     - Krishna-as-Mohinī marrying Aravāṉ is the Kūttāṇṭavar cult version, known from modern fieldwork (Hiltebeitel 1995, item 18).
     - Koovagam as a trans festival is modern.
   - **Bahuchara Mātā.**
     - The shrine's first mention is said to be an inscription of 1280. The Bombay Gazetteer gives 1152. Both are *unverified*.
     - The hijra link is 19th-c. evidence.
   - **Sakhī-bhāva**, men living as Krishna's female companions, belongs to 16th-c. Vrindavan and later. The Gauḍīya and Sahajiyā texts are post-1453 (Dimock, "Also" under item 21). Jayadeva's 12th-c. *Gītagovinda* has *sakhīs* as characters, not as a male devotional practice.
   - **Sufis.** Shāh Ḥusain of Lahore (1538–99) is post-1453 (Kugle, "Also" under item 21). Musa Suhāg and the Sada Suhāg order have no scholarly source found. The dating is unknown and the lead is *unverified*.
   - **Sri Lanka.** The end of the bhikkhunī order c. 1017 is still sourced only to newspapers (*unverified*).
   - **Rule.** None of these may be presented as medieval. They can appear only as a character's claim.

5. **The Kākatīya queen Rudramadevī (r. c. 1262–89) ruled as "Rudradeva Mahārāja"** (Talbot 1995, item 15). This is the subcontinent's best *reaction* datum after Raḍiyya: a court that let a woman rule in a king's title. It is **female-to-male**, so reaction only.
   - Mahānubhāva (founded 1267, Maharashtra) drew many women disciples. No gender crossing was found (Feldhaus 1984, item 22).

### 2. Candidate faiths

#### Candidate A (recommended): *The Worn Garland* (working name; Tamil *Cūṭikkoṭutta*, "she who gave the garland she had worn", Āṇṭāḷ's epithet)

- **Family.** A new faith in `hinduism_religion`, a Vaiṣṇava bhakti heresy. It could instead be a sect of vanilla `vaishnava_faith` through a rite. A new faith is safer: it reads as invented, like Dorleyism, rather than as a claim about Śrīvaiṣṇavism.
- **Premise.**
  - The Āḻvārs sang as women. The commentators said every soul is a woman before the Lord. The *araiyar* danced the girl's part, and the temple streets held unmarried "daughters of God" who owned land.
  - The Garland takes the voice at its word: a man who sings the girl's part long enough, and is answered, becomes the Lord's bride in body.
  - Its scripture for bodily change is Bhaṅgāśvana and Ilā: "I desire to remain a woman."
  - Its polemic against third-sexing runs in Bhatt's direction. The *kimpuruṣa* (Pārvatī's half-mercy, the alternating sex) is "the cage of the grove". There is no third thing. There is one Lord, and everyone else is a bride.
  - *Risk to write in:* "One man and all the rest brides" moves the hierarchy into heaven. Let hostile Śaiva and Jain voices, and the faith's own dissenters, say so.
- **The gate: *Keep the Mārkaḻi Vow*.** A four-part rite, available only in Mārkaḻi (mid-December to mid-January), the month of the girls' dawn vow that Āṇṭāḷ's *Tiruppāvai* and Māṇikkavācakar's *Tiruvempāvai* were sung for (check against item 7).
  1. **The companions** (*tōḻi*).
     - She needs at least one companion who keeps the vow with her, as in the *Tiruppāvai*, where the girls wake each other house by house. A friend, sister or lover counts.
     - More companions and stronger bonds raise the odds.
     - This is the distinct mechanic: **a social gate.**
       - The knife needs a knifer.
       - The völva sees alone.
       - The embryo is gestated alone.
       - The Turning is learned.
       - The Garland is kept *together*.
  2. **The dawn bath.** Thirty mornings, as in the thirty verses. Each missed dawn (war, travel, illness) is a stress hit or a setback. This echoes Bhaṅgāśvana's lake.
  3. **The worn garland.** She wears the god's garland before it is offered. The temple may discover it, as Periyāḻvār found the hair in the garland in the hagiography (check the date of that story in item 7).
     - The choice: own it ("the Lord prefers it worn") and risk the temple's censure, or hide it.
  4. **The dream of the wedding.** Āṇṭāḷ's "Vāraṇam āyiram" decade (*Nācciyār Tirumoḻi* 6) is a dreamed wedding.
     - Success comes as a dream. On success she rises re-embodied, a bride of the Lord, and takes a name from the hymns.
     - On failure, "next Mārkaḻi". Each kept vow raises the next year's odds.
- **The line: *the temple street*.**
  - A daughter can be "settled on the street": given a share (an income-producing holding, like Rājarāja's *veli* shares) and the title "daughter of God".
  - She does not marry mortals. Āṇṭāḷ refused a mortal groom; check the verse in item 7.
  - She passes her share to a daughter she takes, as the Chola temple women passed wealth from mother to daughter (item 1).
  - "Take as Daughter" fits directly.
- **The world: *gifts in stone*.**
  - A patronage and diplomacy faith. The Garland's daughters endow lamps, land and festivals, and every gift is inscribed, giving piety and legacy.
  - A king of any Hindu faith may "transfer" daughters to his new temple, as Rājarāja did in 1010–14. That is prestige for him and a House for them.
  - The *araiyar* can be a court position: a man who performs the girl's part. This is how a seeker is first Marked.
  - **Era arc.**
    - Chola peak, c. 985–1070, temple women at their height.
    - Hoysala and Pāṇḍya patronage.
    - Malik Kāfūr's raid of 1311.
    - The Madurai Sultanate, 1335–78.
    - Then Orr's decline under Vijayanagara.
    - The Kōyil Oḻuku tradition that Srirangam's image was carried into hiding for decades, and the legend of the Muslim princess who became its bride (Bībī Nācciyār), can be event flavour if marked as later temple tradition (*unverified*; the chronicle is of uncertain date).
  - Skills: diplomacy and learning; martial is weak.
- **Seat and holy sites.**
  - Seat: **b_srirangam** (c_tagadur), the Raṅganātha temple, where Āṇṭāḷ is said to have entered the image.
  - Holy sites:
    - c_cholamandalam (Thanjavur, the four hundred);
    - c_madurai (Pāṇḍya country: Srivilliputtur and Āḻvārtirunagari lie near b_tirunelveli);
    - c_kanchipuram (shared with vanilla);
    - c_prayaga (Ilā's Pratiṣṭhāna and the confluence bath).
  - Possibly c_kalyani, if the śaraṇa voice is admitted as kin.
- **Date range.** Playable from 867. The Āḻvār corpus is compiled in the 10th c.
- **Evidence grade.**
  - *Attested in period*: the voice, the performance, the commentarial theology, the temple women, and the myths.
  - *Reconstruction*: any man living as the Lord's bride, and any bodily change.
  - The narrator says so. The faith claims more in its own voice.
- **Risks.**
  - **Living communities.** Śrīvaiṣṇavas and the Āṇṭāḷ cult are living and sensitive. A 2018 public controversy over remarks about Āṇṭāḷ drew mass protest in Tamil Nadu (from memory, *unverified*).
  - Make Āṇṭāḷ a figure the faith **venerates**, never its founder or a trans figure. She was a woman. Make Nammāḻvār a poet whose voice the faith claims, not one of its members.
  - Never make the temple women or the *araiyar* into the faith's institution. They are neighbours whose forms it borrows.
  - **Devadāsī stigma.** Do not sexualise the temple street.
  - **Bhatt.**
    - No third gender.
    - No "veneration". The faith's daughters are donors and owners, not alms-takers.
    - The *kimpuruṣa* is named and refused.
  - **Hijra.** Do not connect the faith to hijras, Bahuchara or Koovagam. That is a living community and later evidence.
- **Recommendation.** The best candidate in South Asia: fair to good. It moves the region's verdict from "poor" to "fair as a marked reconstruction". It is stronger than the Visayan option on *in-period* evidence, though weaker on any actual crossing.

#### Candidate B: *Ilā's House* (the Lunar line)

- **Family.** A new Hindu faith, or a dynastic sect open to Lunar-descended houses.
- **Gate: *Enter the Reed Grove*.**
  - Step 1 is a pilgrimage to the Śaravaṇa grove: a wilderness event and a hunting party gone astray.
  - Step 2 is the change.
  - Step 3 is the choice, Bhaṅgāśvana's: to remain. Choosing to remain is the rite.
  - Refusing the monthly alternation is doctrine.
- **Line.** Lunar dynasties (Yādava claimants such as the Sevuṇas of Devagiri and the Hoysalas, the Candellas, the Kalacuris; check each house's claim) gain legitimacy from the faith's genealogy.
- **World.** Dynastic legitimacy play.
- **Seat.** c_prayaga.
- **Grade.**
  - Myth: attested, ancient.
  - Faith: wholly invented. No institution at all.
- **Risk.** Low on living communities, but it reinterprets Hindu genealogical myth. Thin playstyle.
- **Recommendation.** Fold into A as scripture and as the c_prayaga holy site, not as a separate faith.

#### Candidate C: *Śaraṇasati* (Kalyāṇa, 12th c.)

- A Vīraśaiva-style current whose gate is taking the liṅga as husband. The phrase *śaraṇasati–liṅgapati* ("the devotee is wife, the liṅga husband") is recalled from the ṣaṭsthala doctrine; *unverified*, check in item 4 and item 11.
- **Seat.** c_kalyani. Dates: 1160s, dispersed after 1168.
- **Grade.** Voice attested; life not.
- **Risk: high.** Lingāyats are a large, politically active living community (the 2017–18 separate-religion campaign). Basava is a political icon in Karnataka. The vacana quotations could not be verified.
- **Recommendation.** **Do not build.** At most, a reaction or event (śaraṇa poets debating the Garland), or the Garland's kin in a footnote.

## The Horn of Africa

Scope: Ethiopia (Aksum, the Zagwe at Lalibela, the Solomonic state from 1270), Christian Nubia (Makuria, Alodia), the Muslim sultanates (Ifat, Adal, Shewa, Mogadishu, Zeila, Harar), Cushitic religion (Waaqa, the *qaallu*), Somali religion, the Beta Israel, the Agaw. 867–1453.

The earlier sweep (`comparanda_reading_list.md`, NW Africa section, items 16–18) already lists al-ʿUmarī on the castration ban and Hadiya, Davis 2002 on Hilaria, and the Tzadua/Strauss *Fetha Nagast*. They are not repeated. That sweep found "no male-to-female tradition" for Ethiopia and Nubia, and nothing in this deeper search changes that. What it does find is a set of **attested 15th-century institutions** that a faith could be built on: possession cults with male and female priests, a court eunuch office, coded poetry, women's double monasteries, and a written "law of the queens".

Downloads: `docs/research/sources_pending/south/horn/` (gitignored), with `SOURCES.txt`. Nothing was committed. The web-search quota ran out near the end, so the last checks used the Crossref, Open Library, Unpaywall and archive.org APIs directly.

Evidence grades: **PERIOD** = attested in a source from 867–1453 · **BACK** = plausible back-projection from a slightly later source · **LATER** = 19th–20th-c. ethnography only.

### 1. Findings that matter

1. **Possession cults are attested in the 15th century, with male and female priests, under the names Däsk, Dino, Gʷədale, Ṭäfänt and Mäqawəze. The sources are all hostile. (PERIOD)**
   - Zärʾa Yaʿəqob (r. 1434–68) attacks them in the *Mäṣḥafä Bərhan*, the *Ṭomarä Təsbʾət* and his homilies. He names the heathen priest as *mari* (masc., pl. *maryan*) and *marit* (fem., pl. *maritat*), and lists them beside Däsk and Gʷədale (Dickinson 2017, item 5, pp. 18–19 and glossary pp. 60–61, citing *Mäṣḥafä Bərhan* I, Conti Rossini–Ricci text p. 23).
   - The cult works by possession: the demon possesses a person and speaks through him, and that person is consulted as a soothsayer. Dickinson cites Kur's introduction to the *Acts of Samuel of Däbrä Wägäg*, a saint of Dawit I's reign who went round "expelling Däsk (and Gʷədale)" (pp. 17–18).
   - Mercier (*Art That Heals*, 1997, p. 38, via Dickinson p. 18) describes a ritual in which Gudale and Mäqawəzäy "ride" a priest who has been anointed with a sacrificial cow's blood, dressed in its peritoneum and has stepped into fire. Mercier's own source for this is *unverified*.
   - The chronicle says the emperor forced every subject to swear and to bear inscriptions, read in a later passage as tattoos (Dickinson p. 17, and Haber 1961):
     - on the forehead, "Of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit";
     - on the right hand, a renunciation of the devil;
     - on the left hand, "I renounce Dasak the accursed; I am the servant of Mary" (Perruchon 1893, item 2, p. 6).
     - The chronicle introduces this with the emperor saying Satan "has perverted our children" since he banned Dasak and Dino (p. 6). Offenders were flogged before the crowd.
   - **Design use:** this is a real, datable persecution machine (an oath, a mark on the body, spies, public flogging) for the faith's "world". There is **no gender content** in any of these texts. The *mari*/*marit* pair is the only gendered datum.

2. **Zār is LATER. Do not project it onto 867–1453.**
   - The first descriptions of a zār ceremony are by the missionaries Krapf and Isenberg in 1839 (Natvig 1987, item 14).
   - Natvig places a "proto-zār" cult in 18th- or early-19th-c. contact between Oromo and Amhara.
   - The word *zār* for a possessing spirit first appears in a 16th-c. Ge'ez manuscript (El Hadidi 2016, item 15, p. 57). The manuscript is *unverified*; El Hadidi also says it is unknown whether any rite went with the word.
   - The Agaw sky-god etymology (a god "Jar") is an early-20th-c. proposal, still disputed. Persian, Arabic (*z-w-r*, "to visit") and Hebrew etymologies have also been proposed.
   - The 15th-c. Däsk/Gʷədale cults (finding 1) are the honest medieval analogue. They are not "early zār", and the narrator must not say they were.

3. **The *Kebra Nagast* (early 14th c.) reports an older "law of the queens", but only in order to abolish it. It also gives Ethiopia a eunuch as its first evangelist. (PERIOD text; the "law" is its own fiction)**
   - "Now there was a law in the country of Ethiopia that [only] a woman should reign, and that she must be a virgin who had never known man" (Budge 1922, item 1, ch. 33, pp. 38–39). The queen then decrees that "a woman shall nevermore reign".
   - In ch. 87 (p. 146) the nobles swear by the heavenly Zion that they "will never make women queens".
   - Ethiopian Christianity begins with "the eunuch, the Deputy of Kandákes", who goes home and preaches (Acts 8:27; KN ch. 33 p. 39–40; Budge's intro p. lv).
   - Ibn Ḥawqal (10th c.) reports a woman ruling the Ḥabasha who killed its king (Andersen 2000, item 9). Ethiopian tradition remembers her as Gudit/Yodit, and later traditions make her a Jewish queen (Perruchon 1892, item 3, intro pp. v–xiii).
   - **Design use:** the faith may claim, *in its own voice*, the "law of the virgin queens" that the Solomonic oath overturned, and the eunuch whom a queen sent home. The narrator says plainly that the KN is Solomonic propaganda of c. 1314–22, and that it records the law only to replace it.
   - Gudit must **not** be used as a Jewish villain or a Jewish heroine. That overlay is late and antisemitic in effect.

4. **The Solomonic court had an office "eunuch-in-title and a sort of chamberlain", the *jan däraba*, beside a ban on castration inside the realm. (PERIOD office; the gloss is 19th-c.)**
   - Zärʾa Yaʿəqob's palace order lists the *Jân Darabâ* among the officers of the inner enclosure, carrying wax torches by day (Perruchon 1893 pp. 32–34). Perruchon's note glosses the term as "à la fois un eunuque en titre et une sorte de chambellan", citing d'Abbadie's *Dictionnaire* (19th c.), col. 804 (p. 32 n. 2).
   - Under Bäʾedä Maryam, "the Jân Darabâ named Mâtêwos" wakes the king at midnight to announce that Queen Romna has borne a son (p. 155). The office links the king and the queen's household.
   - Whether the medieval holders were in fact castrated is *unverified*. Al-ʿUmarī (already listed) says the Amḥara ruler banned castration and that eunuchs were cut outside his realm at Washlū and brought through Hadiya.
   - **Reaction:** an Ethiopian court that imports eunuchs, gives them a title, and bans the knife at home. It respects the office and refuses the knife, much as the 2026 Dorleyism notes have Byzantium do. A Horn faith built on eunuchs would **duplicate Dorleyism**, so leave this to reaction.

5. **Hagiography: only female-to-male crossing is praised (FtM, reaction only). Women's double monasteries and a whole movement of persecuted nuns are now documented. (PERIOD)**
   - The Ethiopic Synaxarium carries Hilaria, whom the brethren nickname "Hilarion the eunuch" because she is beardless (Ermakova 2022, item 12, p. 197). Clugnet 1905 (item 4) prints the Ethiopic Life of Marina, who lived as a monk (from BnF MS éth. 128; page in Clugnet *unverified*). **FtM.**
   - No Ethiopian saint, translated or native, lives the other way.
   - Ambu (2025, items 10–11):
     - the Ewostatean (Eustathian) movement kept "double monasteries" with powerful abbesses (14th–15th c.);
     - the Stephanite *Combat of the Holy Women of Tigray* (Gunda Gunde MS GG-006) counts about 1,408 nuns in the founder's lifetime;
     - Zärʾa Yaʿəqob's men stripped, whipped and mutilated them.
   - The Stephanites refused to prostrate before the king, the icon and the cross (Getatchew Haile, item 6).
   - Belcher's Walatta Petros (1672) has a lifelong partnership between two nuns. It is **LATER** (17th c.), but it shows that Ethiopian hagiography can hold women's intimacy (item 13).
   - **Design use:** a model for the "line" (an abbess-led double house that writes a *gädl* for its own women). The nuns were cis women and martyrs: never retell them as trans.

6. **Nubia and the Cushitic and Somali south are poor fits.**
   - **Christian Nubia:**
     - Medieval Arabic writers report succession to the king's sister's son (Tsakos 2021, item 22).
     - A letter from an eparch's daughter shows elite women writing in Old Nubian (van Gerven Oei and Tsakos 2018, item 23).
     - No gender-crossing or eunuch scholarship was found.
     - A late-Christian Nubian refuge (Dotawo at Qasr Ibrim) would only be a weaker Dorleyism.
   - **Oromo and Cushitic religion:**
     - Waaqa and the *qaallu* (with the female *qaallitti*) are documented from the 16th c. on: *Futūḥ al-Ḥabasha*'s "Wara Qallu" is the first datable trace.
     - The *gadaa* material is LATER (Asmarom Legesse 1973, Knutsson 1967).
     - The Oromo enter the highlands after 1522, outside the game's span.
   - **Maale *ashtime*** (south-western Ethiopia): male-born people who live as women, do women's work in the king's compound and may lie with the king before rites (Donham 1990, item 24). This is LATER (1970s fieldwork) and belongs to a living community. It is routinely cited as a "third gender", which is the framing Bhatt warns against. Use it at most in a README note on modern evidence, never as a faith.
   - **Somali religion and Harar:** Eebe/Waaq, *wadaad* and *saar* are LATER. Harar's walls are 16th-c. Nothing found before 1453. Vanilla CK3 already covers this ground with Waaqism (holy sites `kaffa`, `harar`, `danakil`, `makhir`, `mogadishu`).

### 2. Candidate faiths

#### Candidate A (recommended, but say what it is): "The Gold Beneath the Wax" (working name; *säm-ənna wärq*, "wax and gold")

- **Family.** A heresy faith in `christianity_religion` with its own rite, beside vanilla `miaphysitism` and its `ethiopian_rite`. Its sympathies run to the Stephanites (no prostration before king or icon) and to the non-Solomonic Zagwe.
- **Premise, in the faith's voice.**
  - Ethiopia once had a law that a virgin woman reigned (KN 33).
  - The Solomonic oath broke it (KN 87).
  - The first Christian of Ethiopia was a queen's eunuch, sent home to preach.
  - Under an emperor who brands his people, a daughter's name must be spoken so that the wrong people cannot hear it.
- **The gate: the Qəne.** Recognition through a coded poem, sung in church at a feast.
  1. **The commission.** A Declared seeker finds a master of *qəne*: a *däbtära* at court, or the master of a school. The master's learning sets the poem's depth.
  2. **The feast.** The poem is sung at a church feast:
     - Mäsqäl in late September, or Ṭəmqät in January;
     - so the gate fires once or twice a year, a different rhythm from the Turning's Adar.
     - The surface, the "wax", praises the saint of the day or her house.
  3. **The hearing.** Every listener hears the wax; those who know her hear the "gold", her name as a daughter. The roll compares the poet's skill with the learning of the most hostile listener present: a royal spy, the abbot, the emperor's *liqä däbtära*.
     - **Success:** sisters and allies who "hear the gold" gain opinion of her, and she bears the name from then on.
     - **Too plain a poem:** an accusation of Däsk worship, under the tattoo edict.
     - **Too deep a poem:** it goes "unheard". She may try again at the next feast, and the attempt is never reset.
  4. **The answer.** At the next feast a second *qəne* answers hers. Someone sings back to her in the gold, and that seals the gate.
  - **How this differs from the four built gates:**
    - It is not the knife.
    - It is not a seer's trance: no one goes into trance, and a human poet encodes.
    - It is not inner gestation.
    - It is not a prayer answered by heaven, as in the Turning: the community answers, in code.
  - Its odds are a duel of concealment, not a test of the seeker (Serano).
- **The line.**
  - **The school chain.** *Qəne* passes from master to pupil, as in the traditional chain from Yoḥannəs Gäbbälawi to Täwanäy. Teaching the gold to a ward is the counterpart of the varðlokkur and the three lamps.
  - **The double house.** A House under an abbess, on the Ewostatean model. When an Elder dies the House may "Write Her *Gädl*", as the Stephanite nuns had a *gädl* written for their whole movement. The *gädl* gives the House legacy and protection against closure.
- **The world.**
  - **Concealment play.** The skills are learning and intrigue (intrigue overlaps the Jade Embryo, so weight it to learning).
  - **The Emperor's Marks.** In a Solomonic realm from 1434 to 1468, every character must bear the three inscriptions or be flogged. A Marked daughter may bear them and keep her gold beneath.
  - **The Oath of Zion.** A woman ruler of the faith in a realm whose religion holds the *Kebra Nagast* suffers a legitimacy penalty. "The law of the queens" is the faith's answer.
  - **Refuge.** Zagwe and Agaw Lasta before 1270, and Stephanite and Tana hermitages afterwards.
- **Era arc.**
  - Zagwe Lasta is open (c. 1137?–1270; the start date is disputed, see Derat 2018).
  - 1270: the Solomonic restoration.
  - c. 1314–22: the *Kebra Nagast*; the oath bars women from the throne.
  - 1434–68: Zärʾa Yaʿəqob's terror (the tattoos, executions of "Däsk worshippers", the Stephanite martyrs).
  - 1468–78: Bäʾedä Maryam relaxes. By tradition, *qəne* and "the six crafts of magic" arrive in his reign with Däqä Esṭifa (*unverified*; Wikipedia "Qene"; check Levine 1965 and the EAe "Qəne" entry).
- **Seat and holy sites (CK3 1.20).**
  - **Seat:** Roha/Lalibela, `b_lalibela` in `c_lasta` (`d_lasta`, `k_abyssinia`). New holy site key, e.g. `tm_roha`.
  - **Holy sites:**
    - vanilla `aksum` (`c_tigre`, `b_aksum`), the queens' throne;
    - a Stephanite site: Gunda Gunde or Däbrä Gärzen in eastern Tigray, perhaps `c_intarta` (*check* the county against the map);
    - Lake Tana's hermitages, `c_dembiya` or `c_gojjam`/`b_bahir_giyorgis` (*check*);
    - `jerusalem`, for the Ethiopian community there.
  - **Avoid** `semien`, the eminent holy site of vanilla `haymanot`, and any Gudit-as-Jewish-queen material.
- **Dates.** 867–1453. The best evidence runs 1270–1478.
- **Evidence grade.**
  - The institutions are PERIOD: the cults, the tattoo edict, the eunuch office, double monasteries, the KN's queen law.
  - *Qəne* as a genre is BACK: legendarily 14th–15th c., and the manuscripts are later.
  - There is no male-to-female evidence at all. The faith itself is reconstruction, and the README must say so.
- **Risks.**
  1. **A living church and national canon.** The *Kebra Nagast*, Lalibela (a saint), Zärʾa Yaʿəqob and the Synaxarium are sacred to the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox churches, and the KN also to Rastafari.
     - Never make a real saint, Lalibela, Masqal Kebra, Krestos Samra or a Stephanite nun a daughter.
     - Use invented characters only.
     - Contemporary Ethiopia criminalises same-sex acts, and religious hostility is strong, so the mod should not read as a provocation aimed at a living church.
  2. **Gudit.** Treat her as the faith's contested figure, not the narrator's heroine. Never use the "Jewish queen" layer (the Beta Israel are a living community).
  3. **Colonial philology.** Conti Rossini and Perruchon worked in colonial and Orientalist frames. Prefer Ethiopian scholars (Taddesse Tamrat, Getatchew Haile, Selamawit Mecca) where possible.
  4. **Bhatt.** The *jan däraba* and the queen's eunuch are not a "third gender" and not daughters. The faith may claim the eunuch as its first preacher; the narrator never calls him a woman.
- **Recommendation.** Build only if a fifth faith is wanted from this region; it is the best of the three. Its strength is a gate unlike any other (coded speech) and a well-documented persecution machine. Its weakness is that, of the five faiths, it rests least on evidence of gender crossing. Grade: **weak-to-moderate**.

#### Candidate B: "The Ridden" (working name), an Agaw possession current

- **Family.** A new pagan faith (Agaw), or a sect of vanilla `kushitism_pagan`. Kushitism's doctrines already accept witchcraft and either-sex clergy.
- **Gate: the riding.** The seeker is ridden by Gʷədale or Mäqawəze. The spirit speaks through her mouth before the congregation, and it names her a daughter. The *mari* who keeps the rite asks: "*mari* or *marit*?"
  - In mechanics this is close to the NW Africa "drum night" and partly to Bædling.
- **Line:** priest lineages. **World:** hunted by saints (Samuel of Däbrä Wägäg) and by the tattoo edict.
- **Seat:** `c_agaw_meder` (`b_agaw_meder`) or `c_damot`.
- **Grade:** the cults are PERIOD, but only as hostile Christian lists. The gender content is pure reconstruction, and zār is LATER.
- **Risks:**
  - The sources call these gods demons, so the faith would be built on a church's own slur list.
  - Agaw communities and zār practitioners are living people.
  - It duplicates the drum candidate.
- **Recommendation:** do **not** build it as a faith. Use the cults as reaction and event colour for Candidate A ("They Say She Consults Däsk"). Merge them into the NW Africa drum faith if that is ever built.

#### Candidate C (not recommended): a Nubian refuge at Qasr Ibrim/Dotawo

### 1. Findings that matter

1. **The Mali court had a pre-Islamic rite of the spoken word, performed in masks, and the bards were licensed to admonish the king. (PERIOD, 1352–53.)**
   - Ibn Baṭṭūṭa, *Voyages*, ed. and trans. Defrémery and Sanguinetti, vol. 4 (Paris, 1874 printing), pp. 413–414. On the feast day, after Dugha the interpreter's sword-play, the poets enter. They are called *djoulâ*, singular *djâli* (the jeli, griot), each "dans le creux d'une figure formée avec des plumes", a feather figure like a bird with a wooden head and red beak.
   - They tell the sultan that past kings sat on this throne (*penpi*) and did good deeds, "so do good, that it be remembered after you". The chief poet then lays his head in the sultan's lap and on each shoulder. Ibn Baṭṭūṭa was told this is "une habitude très-ancienne, antérieure à l'introduction de l'islamisme" (p. 414).
   - On p. 412 Dugha's wives and slave women sing with him and "jouent avec des arcs" (play with bows). On p. 424 he lists the poets' "bouffonnerie" among the things he disapproved of in Mali.
   - Design use: the jeli's word before the throne is an attested medieval institution with its own power (later called *nyama*). A gate that ends in a bard singing a new name into the king's hearing has a period anchor. Nothing in the passage is about gender crossing.

2. **Three medieval Arabic writers report sister's-son or sister succession in Ghana, Mali and Walata. Al-Bakrī also says the king of Ghana dressed "like women". (PERIOD.)**
   - **Ghana (al-Bakrī, 1068).** De Slane trans. (1913), p. 329: only the king and his heir, "le fils de sa sœur", may wear tailored clothes. "Le roi se pare, comme les femmes, avec des colliers et des bracelets." The same passage (pp. 328–329) describes the king's town al-Ghāba ("the grove"). Its groves house the "magiciens" (priests) with the idols and the royal tombs, and guards let no one enter or learn what happens there.
   - Pp. 333–334: the king of Malal converts after a rain-prayer, breaks the idols and expels the priests. This is the medieval model for a Muslim ruler's reaction to the old cult.
   - Already downloaded: `sources_pending/comparanda/nw_africa/al-bakri_deslane1913_description_afrique_djvu.txt`.
   - **Mali (Ibn Khaldūn, c. 1380s).** *Histoire des Berbères*, trans. de Slane, vol. 2 (Algiers, 1854), p. 111 (the page number is OCR-damaged, between the headers for pp. 110 and 112). Mārī Jāṭa "soumit les Sousou" and reigned 25 years; this is the earliest written record of the Sunjata figure. A later king, Abū Bakr, "fils de la fille de Mari-Djata", was chosen "d'après le principe des nations barbares qui mettent la sœur [du monarque décédé] ou le fils de sa sœur en possession [du trône]".
   - **Walata (Ibn Baṭṭūṭa, vol. 4, pp. 388–389).** Among the Massūfa, each man traces his descent from his maternal uncle, and "l'héritage est recueilli par les fils de la sœur". The women go unveiled and keep male friends who are not kin.
   - **Queen Qāsā (vol. 4, pp. 417–418).** Mansa Sulaymān's senior wife is "l'associée du souverain" in government, and her name is spoken with the king's in the Friday sermon. He imprisons her and raises another wife; the court's women show their disapproval through how they greet each queen.
   - Design use: a line that runs through sisters and sisters' children is native to the region, not imported. The king "adorned like women" is the closest thing to a gender inversion in any medieval source for the region. It is a hostile Andalusi outsider's description of regalia, not evidence of gender crossing. The narrator must say so; the faith may read it otherwise in its own voice.

3. **The strongest MtF-adjacent material is 20th-c. Bamana ethnography (LATER). Master blacksmith-sculptors say they "become a woman".**
   - Brett-Smith, *The Making of Bamana Sculpture* (1994, open access), from fieldwork 1976–79 and 1983–84.
     - The sculptor Kojugu Cissoko, asked "Have you become a woman?", answers: "You have become a woman. … you cannot perform the work of men in the house of women" (p. 76; again p. 204).
     - Brett-Smith: artists accept "a form of invisible castration", "altering their gender identity, and becoming, at least partially, 'women'" (p. 203). The cause is a lifelong contract with a mostly *female* djinn familiar, together with medicine washings (pp. 75–77, 82).
     - The sculptor's "openness" (*dariya*) is the same word as a woman's (p. 229). After carving he washes and eats like a new mother (pp. 229, 234).
     - A woman carver of old put on trousers in the bush to carve and took them off before she came home (p. 243). This is **FtM**, reaction only.
     - Secret associations are spoken of as female, "mother" branches beget "daughter" chapters, and to found a Komo is to "marry the Komo" (p. 282 n. 1, after Colleyn 1985).
     - The first Komo could not have been made without a *griotte* girl (p. 212).
   - Koné, "When male becomes female and female becomes male in Mande" (*Wagadu* 1, 2004, open access). The author is a Bamana scholar writing about his own culture.
     - He gives the pejorative *cèmusonin* ("little manwoman"), the category *cètèmusotè* ("not male not female", used of homosexuals and hermaphrodites), and the insult "exchange your pants for my loincloth" (pp. 1–2).
     - Joking kinship lets an older sister's husband call his wife's younger brother "my wife" (pp. 4–5). The maternal uncle *bènkè* is "male mother" (p. 4). Childbirth is "women's war", and those who die of it are buried as warriors (p. 5).
     - *Badenya* ("mother-childness") and *sinjiya* ("breastmilkhood") are the hidden transcript preferred over patrilineal *fadenya* (p. 2).
   - Caveat for the design: in Brett-Smith the feminisation is a *price* (depletion, impotence, loss of wives and children), not a desired becoming. A faith may reclaim it ("the djinn took what I wished gone"), but the narrator must not present Bamana smiths as trans women. Nor should it adopt *cètèmusotè* ("not male not female") as the daughter's identity. That is the "third thing" that Dorleyism's Aristotelians already refuse.

4. **Yoruba "wives of the god" are real, but they are LATER for this mod and living religion.**
   - Matory's *Sex and the Empire That Is No More* (1994; 2nd ed. 2005) is fieldwork from the 1980s at Igboho and Ọ̀yọ́. It treats the Ọ̀yọ́ empire's royal possession cults of Ṣàngó and Yemọja, where every devotee, man or woman, is a bride of the god. Its chapter 6 is "Re-dressing Gender".
   - Johnson, *History of the Yorubas* (written by 1897, published 1921) records the Ọ̀yọ́ palace of the 19th c.:
     - the *Iwefa* (eunuchs), whose second officer keeps the Ṣàngó shrine at Koso (p. 59);
     - eunuchs addressed as "Baba", "father" (p. 60);
     - the *Ilari*, made by full shaving, incisions and a new name that states the king's will, each male Ilari paired with a female "mother" (pp. 60–62);
     - the Iyamode, whom the king addresses as "father" (p. 65);
     - the Alapini, who must be a monorchis (p. 30).
   - Pre-1453 grounding is thin.
     - The date of Ọ̀yọ́'s foundation is unknown; proposals run from the 10th to the 15th c.
     - Ife's classical period (c. 1000–1400; Ogundiran 2020) is archaeology without texts.
     - Gẹ̀lẹ̀dé, in which men wear female masks, emerged around Ketu in the 18th c.
   - Matory 2003 shows scholars bending these priests to their own agendas. Landes's matriarchy thesis helped Brazilian power-brokers marginalise male, reportedly homosexual, priests. Building a trans-women's faith on Ṣàngó's *ìyàwó* would be one more such agenda, on a religion practised today in Nigeria, Benin, Brazil and Cuba.

5. **Hausaland adds nothing pre-1453 beyond the earlier sweep's findings.** 'Yan daudu and Bori remain LATER (Gaudio, Okagbue, Masquelier). Online claims that 'yan daudu are "pre-Islamic" have no early source. The Kano Chronicle's eunuchs begin with Amina's era and Rumfa (late 15th c.). Akan *akɔmfoɔ* and the Asante court are 17th c. and later. Igbo "male daughters" (Amadiume 1987) are FtM, so reaction and structure only. Dahomey is 17th–19th c. Sande and Bundu masks are documented from the colonial period onward; I found nothing establishing pre-1453 antiquity.

6. **Griaule school: avoid.**
   - Van Beek (1991) could not corroborate Griaule's Dogon cosmology in the field, including the Nommo and the androgyny myths, and suggests leading questioning produced them.
   - Brett-Smith's informants "never mentioned the *wanzo*", Dieterlen's inherited impurity that circumcision is said to remove (p. 283 n.).
   - The Dogon dual-soul and androgyny circumcision myth (Griaule, *Dieu d'eau*; Dieterlen 1951) should not be used as a gate.

### 2. Candidate faiths

#### A. Badenya, the Mother's Children (recommended, as a reconstruction)

- **Family.** A new faith inside vanilla `west_african_religion`, beside `west_african_pagan` and `west_african_bidu_pagan`. A Mande current kept among smiths, bards and hunters, outwardly loyal to a Muslim mansa.
- **Name.** *Badenya* ("mother-childness") is an attested Mande term (Bird and Kendall 1980; Koné 2004, p. 2). Any other Bamana coinage (for example *musoya sira*, "women's road") needs a native speaker's check.
- **The gate: *Be Sung Before the Throne*.** A daughter is made by the nyamakala, the casted specialists, each in turn. She is not cut, she does not see in trance, she does not gestate, and she is not answered by God: she is made by other people's craft and word. Three parts:
  1. **The bush (the smith's road).** A *numu* (smith-sculptor) diviner leads her to the bush to meet a female djinn familiar, and a contract is struck (Brett-Smith, ch. 2).
     - The djinn names a price from a list: gold, a season's abstinence, a carved figure that must be commissioned, or a risk to a kinsman's health. That is the faith's inversion of Kojugu's "spirit payments".
     - In the faith's voice: "the djinn took my manhood, and I had asked her to."
     - Washings follow, like a new mother's.
  2. **The mother's house (badenya).** A woman of the faith claims her as her own mother's child. This uses the shared *Take as Daughter*, and they become "of one afterbreast" (*sinkò*). Without a mother the rite stalls.
  3. **The word.** At the feast after Ramadan, the bird-masked jeli sings her new praise-name before the mansa's *penpi* (Ibn Baṭṭūṭa pp. 413–414). The jeli must be paid in gold dust, as Dugha was (p. 412).
     - A jeli who is ill-paid or hostile sings it as satire instead, and the name does not hold until next year's feast.
     - It needs a Court Poet. That position is in the Royal Court DLC (`court_poet_court_position`), so a fallback is needed for players without it.
  - **How it plays differently.** It is an economy of patronage and favours across three characters (smith, mother, bard), tied to the calendar's two feasts. It is the most *social* of the gates: a court with no jeli cannot finish it.
- **The line.** The heir is a sister's child (al-Bakrī p. 329; Ibn Khaldūn p. 111; Ibn Baṭṭūṭa pp. 388–389). The faith's preferred heir is a sister's or a mother's other child, through the shared *Sisters Choose*.
  - Houses are "mother" chapters that bud "daughter" chapters, as the Mande associations do (Brett-Smith p. 282 n. 1). This fits the existing Sisters' Houses mechanic exactly.
  - The founder may *marry the House*: when a House is founded, its founder pays bride-wealth to the mother House.
- **The world.**
  - The mansa is Muslim and the court half-pagan. A Maliki qadi may preach against the jeli's "buffoonery" and the women's bare heads (Ibn Baṭṭūṭa p. 424). The Malal precedent (al-Bakrī pp. 333–334) is the threat: a convert king breaks the idols and expels the priests.
  - A queen like Qāsā can sit as co-ruler and fall to intrigue (pp. 417–418).
  - Gold, from Bure and Bambuk, pays every gate.
  - Hostile Islamic neighbours use the shared "trusted servant / mocked ruler" response.
- **Seat and holy sites (CK3 1.20 keys, checked in `00_landed_titles.txt`).**
  - **Seat: `c_sibi_mali`**, barony `b_kirina`, the field where the epic has Sumanguru fall. It is in `d_manding` (`k_mali`).
  - **Holy sites:**
    - `c_sibi_mali`, new site;
    - `c_niani` (vanilla holy site `niani`);
    - `c_awkar` (vanilla `awkar`, Ghana's grove-town al-Ghāba);
    - `c_walata`, new site: sister's-son inheritance;
    - `c_tabon` (barony `b_kangaba`), new site.
  - Note: `c_kankan` has a barony `b_jelibakoro`. Its meaning ("old jeli place"?) is *unverified*; do not gloss it.
- **Dates.** Available from 867 as a smiths' and hunters' current of Ghana's Mande south. The Sunjata cycle (Sogolon the buffalo-woman's double, Sumanguru the smith-king, his secret drawn out by Sunjata's sister) unlocks after Kirina (c. 1235) as the faith's own myth. The narrator notes the epic's texts are 19th–20th c.
- **Evidence grade.**
  - PERIOD for the setting: the masked jeli and his licence to admonish, matrilineal succession, the sacred groves, the queen co-ruler, and the king "adorned like women".
  - BACK for the Sunjata myth.
  - LATER for the gate's core, the feminised smith (Brett-Smith; Koné).
  - No male-to-female crossing is attested anywhere in the region before 1453. The narrator must say the gate is a reconstruction.
- **Risks.**
  - Komo, donso (hunters) and jeli institutions are alive and partly secret. Use only what is published, never Komo's secret objects or rites.
  - Brett-Smith's feminisation is a loss narrative; reframe it in the faith's voice only.
  - Avoid the Griaule school and avoid "not male not female" (Bhatt).
  - The Mande peoples are living. The faith is a fictional current with its own name, not a claim about Bamana or Malinke religion.
- **Recommendation.** The best southern candidate found. It is weaker in grounding than Bædling or Ha-Nehpakhot, but its setting is unusually well documented by eyewitnesses. Build it as a sect inside the West African religion, with the narrator's reconstruction note.

#### B. Wives of the Thunder (Ọ̀yọ́ / Ṣàngó *ìyàwó*): not recommended as a faith

- **Possible gate:** possession and marriage to the god, plus the Ilari-style shaving, incision and naming.
- **Seat:** `c_oyo` (`d_oyo`), holy sites `c_ife` (vanilla `ife`), `c_igbomina`, `c_ketu`.
- **Grade:** LATER (Johnson 1921, Matory 1994). Ọ̀yọ́ in 867–1453 is pre-imperial and undocumented.
- **Risk:** high. It is a living religion, and Matory 2003 is a warning against this exact use.
- **Instead:** use it as reaction flavour. Vanilla `west_african_orisha_pagan` already has `doctrine_gender_equal` and homosexuality accepted. Orisha courts could read a daughter of any gate as *ìyàwó*, a bride of an orisha, and think well of her. A late-game Ọ̀yọ́ court could offer Iwefa offices (*Ona'efa*, *Osi'efa*) to eunuchs and daughters of the knife. Flag both as LATER in the narrator's voice.

#### C. Bori sisterhood (Hausaland): no

This was already judged LATER in the earlier sweep, and this deeper search found nothing to change that.

### 1. Findings that matter

1. **Java has the region's only in-period (pre-1453) gender-variant category: the *kĕḍi* of the royal charters. The record treats it as a palace dependent and a defect, not a priesthood.**
   - **The charters.**
     - The Waharu I charter of Śaka 795 (873 CE) lists, among the king's dependants barred from entering a freehold (*sīma*): "… skar tahun, kdi, walyan, widu mangidung … hulun haji … watak i jro" (Boechari & Wibowo 1985/86, E.3).
     - The Majapahit Balawi charter of Śaka 1227 (1305 CE) repeats the list: "kdi, walyan, widu mangidung, sambal, sumbul, hulun haji, singah, pawrsi … pujut, bondan, jenggi, pandak, wwal, wungkuk, watek i jro" (same volume, E.80, p. 165).
     - OJED records kĕḍi "Passim in inscr." (OJO 36, 910 CE), and *walyan* adds OJO 39 (929): "kĕḍi walyan mahaliman widu … watĕk i jro".
   - **Who the watĕk i jro were.** OJED, s.v. jro, defines them as "persons attached to the kraton … comprising all the various groups of court-attendants". In the 1305 list the kĕḍi stand beside:
     - healers (*walyan*, "physician, healer, ḍukun")
     - singers (*widu*)
     - dwarfs (*wwal*)
     - hunchbacks (*wuṅkuk*)
     - *pujut*, a group of court dependants
   - This is the Javanese counterpart of the Bugis "monstrous" sacred kinds (albinos, dwarfs, bissu) that Andaya describes. In Java, though, the record shows a royal household, not a cult.
   - **What the word means.**
     - OJED glosses kĕḍi "emasculated, eunuch" (p. 838).
     - The *Ślokāntara* renders Sanskrit *klība* as "kĕḍi walawadi ṅaranya strī tan strī", "a woman and yet not a woman" (OJED s.v. walawadi, p. 2180).
     - The Śaiva *Wṛhaspatitattwa* makes it a karmic rebirth: "janmāntarapuruṣa … kĕḍi walawadi hara tĕmahanya" (OJED ibid.).
     - The *Wirāṭaparwa* (dated 996) has Arjuna "atĕmahana kĕḍi makasajña Wṛhannalā", becoming a kĕḍi named Wṛhannalā (OJED p. 838). It also uses *napuṅsaka* ("neither male nor female, hermaphrodite, eunuch", p. 1174) for the same disguise.
   - **What the record lacks.** No source here gives the kĕḍi a rite, a cult role, or a self-description. The literary frame is *wikāra*, "deviation from natural state; change, esp. for the worse" (p. 2273). This is Bhatt's "dumping-ground" pattern in Old Javanese.
   - **Other terms.**
     - *Wandu*, the modern word, is **not** an OJED headword. The comparanda list's "wandu/kĕdi" lead resolves to **kĕḍi** only.
     - *Ardhanārīśwara* in Old Javanese is the deified royal *couple* in one body (Sumanasāntaka 17.2; OJED p. 123). It is not a gender-crossing precedent.
     - Ken Dedes in the Pararaton is a *nāreśwarī* whose glowing body marks her as mother of kings. That is a cis female sovereignty sign. Do not use it.

2. **The Bugis chronology is firmer than the comparanda draft allowed, and it places the bissu world inside the game window. Every direct witness of the bissu is still 1545 or later.**
   - **The epic.** Koolhof: "no exact dating for the composition of the La Galigo can be established". Pelras (1996:56) and Fachruddin suggest it "first began to take shape in an oral form around the middle of the fourteenth century". The extant manuscripts are "no older than approximately two centuries", and the oldest datable one was acquired in 1784 (Koolhof 1999, pp. 379–380 n. 12).
   - **Writing and kingdoms.** Caldwell: writing developed in South Sulawesi "around 1400, at least one hundred years after the emergence of the first kingdoms". The chronicles "contain information dating from around 1300". Chinese navigators did not know Sulawesi before the 17th century. *Bissu* is one of the few Indic loans in Bugis (Caldwell 1995, pp. 394, 403 and n. 25).
   - **Luwu.** Archaeology puts Luwu's palace centre at **Malangke from c. 1300**: "the earliest keramik were imported to Malangke c.1300" (Bulbeck & Caldwell 2000, p. 69). The Wotu river is called *Sere Bissue*, "dance of the bissu" (c. p. 47, *page unverified*).
   - **Corrections.** This dating corrects Ismoyo's "written in the 13th to the 15th century", which is oral composition at best. Kennedy's informant's "600 years" (c. 1390) is not implausible as an order of magnitude, but it remains oral tradition.

3. **The Visayan asog rests on far thinner evidence than the Tagalog bayog, and none of it is from Cebu.**
   - Ngu counts seven early references to feminine shamans:
     - five Tagalog or Zambal: Plasencia 1589, the Boxer Codex 1590, Chirino 1604, Perez 1680, Salazar 1683
     - one Bikol: Lisboa 1628
     - one Visayan: Alcina 1668, from Samar and Leyte
   - Source: Ngu 2022, n. 1.
   - What Cebu itself has is Pigafetta's April 1521 hog consecration by "two very old women". They mark "the foreheads of their husbands" with the blood, "invite only women" to eat, and "no one but old women consecrate the flesh of the hog" (Blair & Robertson vol. 33).
   - Plasencia's twelfth office: "*bayoguin*, signified a 'cotquean,' a man whose nature inclined toward that of a woman" (B&R vol. 7, p. 192 per Ngu).
   - The Boxer Codex says rites in "Luzon, Panay, and Cebu … almost all agree" (via Ngu). This is the only warrant for moving bayog evidence to the Visayas.
   - Ngu (2022) argues against Brewer's "men who appropriate femininity". She holds that asog femininity "can be equally valid", which suits the mod better than Brewer.
   - There is no gender material for the Philippines before 1521. The pre-1453 record is the Laguna Copperplate (900 CE; Postma 1992) and Chinese trade accounts (Chao Ju-kua c. 1225–80, in B&R vol. 34). These attest polities and trade, not religion or gender.

4. **Every candidate requires back-projection or invention. The three differ in *what kind* of risk they carry.**
   - **Java: in-period, but invented.** The category is in period, but defined by deficiency. A faith would invent the rite, like Dorleyism. No living community's name is taken, but modern *kedi* is a slur-adjacent word for *banci* (bab.la gloss "hermaphrodite … wadam, banci").
   - **Bugis: best-described, highest risk.** The best-described ritual office (regalia, white blood, the trance-death *irebba*, the language of the gods). Direct witnesses date from 1545. It is a **living, persecuted community**: Operasi Tobat in the 1960s, and the eTropic 2024 and Song 2019 studies of present-day bissu. Its scholarship is split between Andaya's "outside male and female" and Davies's "five genders". Building a game faith on it is the clearest case of what Bhatt and the brief warn against.
   - **Visayan: medium risk, back-projected.** Evidence from 1521–1685 is projected back 70–650 years. A revival "babaylan" spirituality exists among Filipino and Filipino-American groups, so the risk is moderate. The third-gender risk is low if the faith follows Brewer and Ngu: the qualifying condition is womanhood and femininity.

5. **The castration gate is unattested across the islands.**
   - The OJED gloss "emasculated" for kĕḍi is a lexicographer's equation with Sanskrit *klība*. The charters give no evidence of cutting.
   - Bissu and asog sources record no castration (`east_sea_bissu_baylan.md` §5c).
   - Any faith here should refuse the knife as its own gate, as the Bædlings and Ha-Nehpakhot do.

6. **Reaction data for the existing faiths.**
   - A Hindu–Buddhist Javanese or Balinese court (vanilla Shaivism, Vaishnavism, Mahayana) would *enrol* a daughter as a kĕḍi among the watĕk i jro. She would be a palace dependent on royal revenue, a *wikāra*, the fruit of a past life's fault.
   - This is a ready event: "Counted among the Inner Court". It also supports the existing `tm_eastern_knife_opinion` (−15) and `tm_eastern_daughter_opinion` (−5) split, with a Javanese text variant.
   - Vanilla Tolotang (homosexuality a crime, male-dominated) should be *more* hostile to a daughter than its own bissu-free localisation implies. The game has no bissu exception. This is a vanilla gap, not the mod's to fill.

### 2. Candidate faiths

#### A. The Lambon (working name). Visayan, inside `dayawism_religion`. **Recommended.**

**Name.** The *lambon* is the long skirt to the feet that, by Alcina's account, the asog wore "so that they were recognized even by their dress" (Brewer ¶14).

**Religion.** A new faith `tm_lambon` in `dayawism_religion`. It keeps vanilla's gender-equal and homosexuality-accepted doctrines and witchcraft acceptance. It has no head. Clergy are female only, as in the other Sisters' faiths. It sits beside Dayawism the way Ha-Nehpakhot sits beside Rabbinic Judaism. Dayawist observers react mildly. The friction comes from datu politics, not doctrine.

**Gate: the Call, the Lambon, the Hog.** Three parts. Unlike the other four gates, it begins *involuntarily* and ends in a *collective recognition by women*.
1. **The Call.** A sickness event: catalepsy, mutism or a "brief period of madness".
   - Sources: McCoy pp. 144–145; Andaya n. 28 for the bissu parallel; Alcina's "mute indian".
   - It can fire on a Marked character or be sought by fasting. In line with the Serano rule, she can always refuse the call.
2. **The Lambon.** A season of women's work: weaving, pottery and the women's dance (Alcina, Brewer ¶14–15).
   - The progress variable is skills practised, not tests passed. Her house and the old women's opinion of her raise the odds.
3. **The Hog.** Two old babaylan consecrate a hog. The one with the horned kerchief speaks with the spirit, and the other answers.
   - They mark her forehead with the blood among the women, and she eats with the women only (Pigafetta 1521).
   - The rite needs two elder women of the faith or of Dayawism, at court or in the county. If none are present it waits; it never fails.

**Line.**
- The art passes from old women to younger women "only after they were married" (Bolinao, Brewer ¶29). Mechanically, a daughter must be married or widowed before she can *teach*.
- Paired siblings: "the sister in charge of the most powerful Anito" (Brewer ¶32).
- Spirit spouses: a minor, flavour-only "married to a diwata" status.
- Inheritance follows bilateral kinship (Brewer ¶4). A daughter inherits as any daughter does under vanilla gender-equal law. Take as Daughter stays the engine.

**World.** Datu politics: raiding, trading and feasting.
- The priestess blesses or curses a war: invulnerability amulets and *hiwit* cursing (McCoy pp. 153, 166).
- Healing as income: per-ritual fees (McCoy p. 153).
- Chinese trade at Butuan and Ma-i (Chao Ju-kua; B&R vol. 34).
- **Late pressure:** Islam reaching Sulu and Maguindanao in the late 14th–15th century (*date unverified*). Brunei is a regional power.
- There is no Spanish content: Spain arrives after 1453. The playstyle centres on diplomacy, learning and feasts, with a sacrifice economy (hogs as a resource).

**Seat and holy sites** (new `tm_` holy sites):
- Seat: **b_PHI_Sugbu** (c_PHI_bool_and_cebu). Pigafetta's women's hog rite, 1521. It needs a holding: empty in 867.
- **c_PHI_samar.** Alcina's asog of Sulat, Ibabao. Shares the county with vanilla Dayawism's `biri_larosa`.
- **c_PHI_panay** (b_PHI_Irong_Irong). Loarca 1582: "priestesses, or the men who perform this office".
- **b_PHI_Bolinao** (c_PHI_pangasinan). 145 priestesses and three bayog, 1679–85.
- **c_PHI_butuan** (b_PHI_Butuan). The Song-tribute port. It starts as Shaivism, which makes a contested site.

**Date range.** Present at every start (867), like the others. The narrator says the evidence begins in 1521. In its own voice, the faith claims the old women were always there.

**Evidence grade.** *Plausible back-projection.*
- The ritual is attested 1521–1685.
- The asog is attested in the Visayas only in 1668.
- Nothing on religion or gender before 1453.

**Risks.**
- **Back-projection:** 70–650 years without evidence. The narrator must say so.
- **Living revival movements:** do not call the faith "Babaylan". Vanilla already uses that word.
- **Spanish sodomy framing:** never voice it as narrator.
- **The asog's gender:**
  - Follow Brewer (femininity is the vehicle) and Ngu (asog femininity is valid).
  - Do not call asog a third sex.
  - Do not say all asog were trans women. Some Bolinao bayog dressed as women only for rites and had wives (Brewer ¶17, ¶22). The faith claims the ones who lived as women.
- **Vanilla overlap:** the Philippines in vanilla is already tolerant. The faith's distinctness has to come from the gate and the sacrifice and feast economy, not from persecution.

#### B. The Inner Court / Wṛhannalā's Year (working name). Javanese and Balinese, a sect inside vanilla Shaivism (Hinduism). **Alternative.**

**Religion.** A faith in the Hindu family (`hinduism_religion`), Śaiva, at a Kediri or Majapahit court.
- Polemic: "we are not *wikāra*", parallel to the Jade Embryo's "we are not eunuchs".
- It refuses the charter category, the kĕḍi as a royal dependent and karmic defect, while living inside the palace where that category put them.

**Gate: Wṛhannalā's Year.** A gate of *concealment and unveiling*.
- She takes service in the inner court (*jro*) for a year as dance-mistress to the princesses, as Arjuna-as-Wṛhannalā taught Uttarā (Wirāṭaparwa, 996).
- At the year's end comes the *unveiling* before the court, under a new name.
- Discovery events during the year (a jealous courtier, a royal summons) *delay* the year and never reset it (Serano rule).
- **Doctrinal twist, in the faith's voice:** Arjuna went back, and Wṛhannalā did not. The narrator must say the epic's disguise was a curse, not a choice.

**Line.** Palace patronage.
- The faith's houses are endowed as *sīma* freeholds. In the charters, the king's collectors, kĕḍi among them, are forbidden to enter a sīma. The faith turns its own exclusion into its sanctuary.
- Adopted heirs placed in court posts.

**World.** Inner-court faction play: court positions drawn from the watĕk i jro (dancer, healer, singer), charters as land.
- Era arc: Mataram (to 929) → Kediri → Singhasari → Majapahit.
- After c. 1400, Islam on the north coast (*unverified*).
- **Risk of overlap with the Jade Embryo:** both are palace-faction faiths. To differentiate, it would use dance and performance (intrigue and diplomacy) and land charters, not office-holding.

**Seat.** b_JAV_Daha (c_JAV_janggala) for Kediri, or b_JAV_Kahuripan. Holy sites:
- b_IDO_Bedulu (Bali).
- b_JAV_Tumapel (Singhasari).
- Optionally the vanilla Hindu sites.

**Evidence grade.**
- *Category attested in period* (873, 910, 929, 1305).
- *Literary precedent attested* (996).
- *Role and rite invented.*

**Risks.**
- The only in-period datum is a deficiency category, and the faith is wholly invented on top of it. Like Dorleyism, the narrator must say so.
- Modern *kedi* and *banci* are slurs.
- Weaker gameplay distinctness.
- The literary precedent (Arjuna) returns to manhood.

#### C. The Arajang Keepers (Bugis, Luwu). **Not recommended as a playable faith.**

**What it would be.**
- Gate: *irebba*, the shrouded trance-death and return in the regalia house.
- Line: Puang Lolo to Puang Matoa by apprenticeship and royal appointment; milk-kinship.
- World: guarding the regalia, the white blood, royal weddings, the forty.
- Seat: b_SUL_Malangke (c_SuMa_luwu, the Galigo kingdom, palace from c. 1300).

**Grade.** *Plausible back-projection*, with the strongest institutional detail and a datable kingdom.

**Why not.**
1. It takes the identity and a still-performed, clandestine rite of a living, recently persecuted community.
2. The bissu are not trans women. They include women and men who lived as men outside the rite, and the source tradition frames them as outside or beyond gender: Bhatt's third-gender trap.
3. It would sit in vanilla `tolotang_religion`, whose localisation has no bissu at all. The mod would be supplying the bissu to the game itself.

**Use instead.**
- Neighbour flavour: Luwu and Bone courts reacting to a Lambon or Dorleyite daughter, Andaya's "the gods do not ask what you were born".
- The regalia mechanic, *without* the name bissu.
