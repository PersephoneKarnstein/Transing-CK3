# El-Azhari, *Queens, Eunuchs and Concubines in Islamic History, 661–1257* (2019): design reading

Source: Taef El-Azhari, *Queens, Eunuchs and Concubines in Islamic History, 661–1257*, Edinburgh Studies in Classical Islamic History and Culture (Edinburgh: Edinburgh University Press, 2019), xix + 452 pp. ISBN 978-1-4744-2318-2 (hb), 978-1-4744-2319-9 (webready PDF).
Read from a text extraction kept outside the repo. Page numbers are the book's.

**What was read.** In full: ch. 3 (Abbasid eunuchs, pp. 142-195) and ch. 5 (Fatimid eunuchs, pp. 253-284). In part: the Introduction's aims and chapter plan (pp. 1-10); ch. 1's glossary and Umayyad eunuch section (pp. 60-61, 68-72); ch. 2 on the qahramana Thumal (pp. 120-121); ch. 4 on Sitt al-Mulk and Rasad (pp. 204-210); ch. 6's ghilman passage, Lu'lu' and the full "Eunuchs and their Influence under the Seljuqs" section (pp. 291-293, 308-309, 331-339); ch. 7 on Dayfa's council, Shajar al-Durr and the eunuch-atabegs (pp. 353-354, 373-375, 382-395). Everything else in chs. 1, 2, 4, 6 and 7 was not read.

**Relation to existing notes.** Ayalon (`hist_ayalon_eunuchs_caliphs.md`) already covers supply routes, prices, mortality and most of the named eunuchs. El-Azhari adds three things Ayalon lacks: the **Fatimids** (Ayalon left them out on purpose), the **alliance between royal women and eunuchs**, and the Seljuq/Ayyubid **eunuch-atabeg**. Where the two overlap (al-Amin and Kawthar, Mu'nis, Kafur, Qaraqush), this note records only what is new.

## 0. Framing warning

El-Azhari calls eunuchs "the third gender" throughout (e.g. pp. 70, 146, 168, 280). He calls the Medinan *mukhannathun* castrated under Sulayman "transsexuals" (pp. 71, 145). Both are his labels, not his sources'. His one supporting case is weak: fatwas on intercourse with eunuchs "look to eunuchs as a third gender" (p. 148). He reports that Serena Tolino rejects the label for Fatimid eunuchs (p. 254). This runs against Rowson, who calls the *mukhannathun* not-men, and Sanders, who shows the law refused a third category. It also runs against Bhatt's critique of third-sexing. **Use El-Azhari's data, never his categories, in loc or docs.** His own conclusion supports the faith's critique: the label was a demotion. Eunuchs were "always distinguished as a third gender, which affected their basic humanistic equality" (p. 280).

## 1. The argument in brief

### Eunuch supply and castration

- **Byzantium as source, in the Arab view.** Citing al-Jahiz, El-Azhari says Byzantine families castrated sons "in order to donate their sons to the church", removing "the testicles without cutting the male organ" (p. 143; *Hayawan* 1:124). He adds that "two eunuchs ... became Patriarch of Constantinople in the tenth century" (p. 143).
- **Why buy a eunuch.** He had "no family, no children and no biological future", so his loyalty was assumed "absolute". He could cross "any gendered space prohibited to others". He sold for "three or four times" a whole man (p. 144).
- **Slaves, not citizens.** Under Islam eunuchs "belonged to the mamluk slave institution, while in other civilisations they were free persons, natives" (p. 144; again p. 70). Byzantine eunuchs, by contrast, were "free citizens" (p. 275).
- **The legal evasion.** Castration was forbidden, yet rulers "were keen to buy and import ready-castrated slaves ... as long as they were not involved directly or physically in such a crime" (p. 144). "We have no evidence in any source of a Muslim ruler or jurist who clearly condemned the ownership of eunuchs" (pp. 144-145). No Shi'i or Sunni jurist criticised state use of eunuchs (p. 281).
- **Four types and their markets.** Al-Mas'udi's classes: Sudanese, Saqaliba, Rum and Chinese. Black eunuchs were cut in Abyssinia and Takrur. Saqaliba were cut at Pechina (Almería) "and in some other towns in France" by Jewish physicians. "There were other centres for castration in the vast Byzantine Empire" (p. 145).
- **Fatimid routes.** Saqaliba came "overland via France and Spain", with "Venetian slave merchants" in the trade, and "large numbers of Saqaliba came from the Byzantine Empire" (p. 254). Black eunuchs came across the Sahara and down the Nile (p. 254).
- **Castration inside Islam.** The cases are punitive or lustful: the Medinan singers; Hisham castrating a palace singer; Harun castrating the husband of his slave Kharshi's sister, married without his leave (p. 145). Seljuq rulers practised castration "for sexual and political motives within their dominions" (p. 332). Tughril castrated his beloved hajib Khumartakin and later had him killed. His vizier al-Kunduri was castrated, or castrated himself, over a bride (pp. 332-333). Zengi castrated sons of punished commanders and any ghulam he desired, "to prevent him growing a beard". His ghilman were "children of the stallions from Turkish, Armenian and Byzantine lords" (p. 334).
- **Two kinds.** *Khasiyy* (testicles removed; could have concubines) and *majbub* (all removed; used in the harem). "The sources do not distinguish between the two" (p. 145; pp. 146-147).

### Names and titles

- Eunuch names "were not given to full men": Masrur, Kafur, Mu'nis, Jawhar, Rihan. **Feminine names** were also used: Raja', Sawsan, Kawthar. **Shared names** too: Badr, Bishara, Wafa'. It is "extremely rare to find a eunuch ... named Muhammad or 'Umar" (pp. 148-149).
- Lu'lu' ("pearl") was given to beardless eunuchs, after the paradise boys of the Qur'an (pp. 166, 309).
- A childless eunuch received a *kunya* as an honour: Mu'nis was "Aba al-Hasan (father of Hasan, an honorary name given to a childless eunuch)" (p. 172).
- Under the Fatimids, calling a whole man by a eunuch's name "would have been insulting" (p. 254).
- **Named after a woman.** Iqbal al-Khatuni, eunuch adviser to Queen Dayfa of Aleppo, took his title from Dayfa Khatun. "He took great pride in being named after a woman" (p. 353).

### Abbasid eunuchs (ch. 3)

- **Al-Amin.** He took the eunuch Kawthar as "like a declared spouse" (p. 154) and formed the Jaradiyya (white Saqlabi) and Ghurabiyya (black) eunuch corps (p. 155). Abu Nuwas jibed that he raised eunuchs "Until impotence became a religion in the state" (p. 153).
- **Zubayda's counter-move.** El-Azhari's version: 100 maidens with boys' haircuts, "so they would look like eunuchs", called the Shakiriyya or Ghulamiyyat. They continued "under the authority of Zubayda", and al-Qahir copied them in 933 (p. 156). **Contradiction:** Rowson 2003, p. 48, says the intended look was "not the appearance of a eunuch" but an adolescent boy. Prefer Rowson, who argues from the poems.
- **The gap.** After al-Amin, eunuchs vanish from Abbasid politics until al-Mu'tamid (870-92) (pp. 158-159). El-Azhari calls this gap "some 160 years" (p. 158), which does not fit his own dates (c. 813-870). **Design fact for the 867 start:** Abbasid eunuchs are harem and prison staff in 867, and on the verge of a comeback.
- **The Byzantine frontier.** Ibn Tulun's eunuch Yazman held Tarsus 882-91. He was the "earliest eunuch on record to rebel", and he kept up the *fida'* (prisoner exchange) with Byzantium (pp. 159-160). Frontier eunuchs stayed through winters "as they had no families to return to" (p. 160). In 896 more than twenty eunuchs killed Khumarawayh, fearing exposure of their affairs with his *jawari* (p. 160).
- **Mu'nis and Shaghab (908-33).** Al-Muqtadir's court held c. 11,000 eunuchs (7,000 black, 4,000 Saqaliba), plus 700 eunuch hajibs under Sawsan (pp. 162-163). The queen mother Shaghab and the eunuch general Mu'nis formed a "trio of powers" with the boy caliph (p. 162). She called Mu'nis the "sword of the caliph" (p. 167). In 929 he turned on her household's spending, deposed and restored the caliph, then killed him in 932 (pp. 171-174). His men refused her grandson: "a caliph who had a dominant mother ... do you want us to return" to it? (p. 175). Al-Qahir tortured Shaghab to death (pp. 175-176).
- **Eunuch governors and a frontier emir.** Pp. 169-171 list eunuch governors. Thumal held the Byzantine frontier at Tarsus 917-31. Bronze coins read "the Emir Thumal", an honour "even Mu'nis did not enjoy" (pp. 170-171). His 931 raid "reached as far west as Ankara" (p. 173). Al-Jahiz said Muslim rulers posted eunuchs at Tarsus and Adana so they could avenge their castration on Byzantium (p. 170).
- **Byzantine parallels.** Leo VI's eunuch admiral Eustace, and the eunuch Samonas, godfather to Constantine VII in 906 (p. 178).
- **Kafur (946-68).** A Nubian eunuch, regent and then sole ruler of Egypt in 966. Al-Mutanabbi's satire: "The eunuch became the imam of slaves in Egypt" (pp. 179-187).

### Fatimid eunuchs (ch. 5)

- **A dynasty run through eunuchs** "from Jawdhar ... to Jawhar" (p. 254). Jawdhar, a Saqlabi from Spain, kept the secret of two successions (pp. 255-258). He wore the caliph's own gowns (p. 275), and was told "we never differentiate between you and ourselves" (p. 276).
- **Barjawan.** Regent for al-Hakim. He sent 1,000 knights to hold **Sitt al-Mulk** under guard in 996, when she tried to crown her cousin (pp. 255-259). Al-Hakim had him killed and announced: "Barjawan was a slave of mine" (p. 257).
- **Offices.** Eunuchs ran the police and *hisba* (pp. 269-270). Ghayn lost both hands and his tongue to al-Hakim and still held command (p. 270). The *muhannak ustadh* rank was "more than a thousand" strong, and its promotion was attended by the caliph (pp. 273, 277). There were eunuch battalions named for their commanders: Sa'diyya, Rihaniyya, Yanisiyya (pp. 266-267). Some 19 eunuch governors served across Syria and North Africa, mostly facing Byzantium (Table 5.1, p. 274).
- **Esteem, mostly.** Eunuchs were "viewed not with contempt but with esteem during most of the Fatimid period" (p. 278). Hostile verse was anti-Shi'i, not anti-eunuch in the main (pp. 278-280).

### Seljuq and Ayyubid eunuchs (chs. 6-7)

- **The eunuch-atabeg.** Lu'lu' in Aleppo (1113-17) was the first (pp. 308-309, 334). Tughril, a "Rumi eunuch", was atabeg of Ayyubid Aleppo 1216-31. He ruled from the citadel and "never left it", and handed power to the king at eighteen (pp. 394-395). Qaraqush was "a Rumi or of Byzantine/Balkan origin" (p. 383). William of Tyre's "toassin" (*tawashi*) made up 8,000 of Saladin's 18,000 men (p. 391).
- **A sultan who laughed.** In 1160 the drunken Seljuq Sulayman, among "clowns and entertainers", exposed the body of his eunuch commander Kardabadhu for laughs. "The eunuch killed his sultan and all those who had laughed with him" (p. 337).
- **A permissive chief judge.** Al-Mawardi (d. 1058), chief judge of Baghdad: "Castrated men should not be prevented from occupying any office ... Their mutilated sexual organs have no relationship with the capacity of the brain" (p. 333).
- **A couplet.** Abu Bakr al-Bayhaqi (d. 1066): "They (eunuchs) like women for a placid dweller / ... act as men if a long campaign calls them" (p. 332).

### Royal women acting through eunuchs

The book's core thesis is that "the pact between women and eunuchs or atabegs was a major development from Iran to Egypt" (p. 6). Cases:

- **Shaghab and Mu'nis** (Baghdad, 908-29).
- **Shaghab's qahramana Thumal.** She sat as head of the *mazalim* court, the only woman ever to hold it. Opposition was fierce, but Shaghab sent her back the next Friday with a judge (pp. 120-121). The eunuch Kafur later held the same seat without such opposition (p. 183).
- **Sitt al-Mulk** (Cairo, 1021). After al-Hakim's death she used the eunuchs Nassim and Mi'dad and "a battalion of Saqlabi eunuchs" to kill the regent Ibn Dawwas (pp. 204-205, 260). Eunuchs were "her arms or tools to control the realm" (p. 261). Her words to Ibn Dawwas: "I'm a woman, and I need to keep the rule for that youth" (p. 204).
- **Rasad** (Cairo, 1044-87). A former Sudanese slave, regent for al-Mustansir. In council she sat behind a curtain and "would send out a eunuch who would always support the opinion of" her vizier (p. 208).
- **Dayfa Khatun** (Aleppo, 1236-42). Her council sent Iqbal al-Khatuni into the harem "to ascertain her opinion and seek her permission" (p. 354).
- **Shajar al-Durr** (Mansura, 1249-50). She hid al-Salih's death with the chief eunuch Muhsin. The eunuch al-Suhayli forged decrees "in the same handwriting of the deceased sultan" (p. 374).

**Errors and contradictions to flag.**

- Zubayda's girls dressed "to look like eunuchs" (p. 156) conflicts with Rowson 2003, p. 48 ("adolescent boy"). Follow Rowson.
- The "160 years" gap (p. 158) does not match his own dates.
- The 921 Volga embassy is credited to "the geographer Ibn Batlan" (p. 166). It is Ibn Fadlan's embassy; do not copy.
- "1962" for 1062 (p. 332) is a typo.
- Calling the Medinan *mukhannathun* "transsexuals" (pp. 71, 145) is a gloss; Rowson's terms govern.
