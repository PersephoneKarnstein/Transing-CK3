# Kapitaikin, "David's dancers in Palermo" (2019): design reading

Source: Lev A. Kapitaikin, "David's dancers in Palermo: Islamic dance imagery and its Christian recontextualization in the ceilings of the Cappella Palatina", *Early Music* 47.1 (2019): 3–23, doi:10.1093/em/caz006.

**What was read.** All of it: the text (pp. 3–17), the appendix of dancers and standing musicians (pp. 18–19) and the notes (pp. 19–23). It was read from a text extraction kept outside the repo, and page numbers are the printed ones. Kapitaikin is an art historian. Everything Arabic here reaches us through him; none of it was checked against an edition.

**Relation to existing notes.** For the v2 Cairo house (`later_starts/mukhannathun_fatimid_cairo.md`), and in passing for the Baghdad troupe (`later_starts/mukhannathun_baghdad_1066.md`). His *mukhannath* material rests on sources we already hold: Rowson 1991 (`hist_rowson_mukhannathun.md`), Moreh (`isl_moreh_live_theatre.md`), Shoshan (`isl_shoshan_popular_culture_cairo.md`) and al-Ghazālī (Baghdad note). For Fatimid court women he relies on Cortese and Calderini (`isl_cortese_calderini_fatimid_women.md`).

## 0. Framing warning

- **The *mukhannath* reading is a question, not a finding.** He asks whether the beardless drummers and flautists of two ceiling panels "might" be effeminate accompanists (p. 11). His appendix marks five figures "Male or effeminate?" (pp. 18–19). The evidence is the instruments they hold, the *kūba* and the square *duff*, not anything about the figures themselves.
- **The ceiling is Palermo, c. 1140, not Cairo, 1066.** He argues that the painters were Fatimid-trained (p. 4, n. 13), so the dancers are a window on Fatimid court dance (p. 16). But the panels were painted for a Christian king's chapel, three generations after our start.
- **The dancers are enslaved.** He identifies them as *jawārī* and *qiyān*, singing slave-girls (p. 7). Any loc drawn from them must keep that in view (Cairo guardrails).

## 1. What the article says

### The *mukhannath* passage (pp. 10–11, 17)

- **The *kūba*.** He quotes al-Ghazālī (d. 1111): the *kūba* is a long drum, slender in the middle, and beating it is "a custom of effeminates [*mukhannathīn*]" (p. 10, n. 59, via Macdonald 1901, p. 213). **Already verified** in the Baghdad note (*Iḥyāʾ* 2:272). His explanation for the jurists' hostility, the drum's waist "evocative of a woman's waist or male genitalia?", is his own guess (p. 10).
- **Condemnation is late in his notes.** The "unanimous condemnation by religious authorities" cites Ibn Ḥajar al-Haytamī (d. 1566) (p. 10, n. 60). It is not an eleventh-century source.
- **The square drum and Ṭuways.** Jurists condemned the *duff murabbaʿ* because it was Ṭuways's instrument (p. 11, n. 62: the *Aghānī* iii, pp. 33, 37, and Rowson 1991). Rowson says Ṭuways played only the square *duff* (Rowson pp. 678–679). The claim that the jurists' condemnation of the drum came from Ṭuways has no jurist cited.
- **At Fatimid feasts.** "*Mukhannathūn* performers, who often played drums and flutes, also attended Abbasid and Fatimid celebrations, whether courtly or popular" (p. 11, n. 63). His sources are Moreh pp. 25–27, 30–32, 89–90 and Shoshan pp. 43, 46, 49. **Overstated for the Fatimids.** Our reading of Shoshan found no Fatimid *mukhannath* (Shoshan note, line 90). Moreh's only Fatimid case is the *muʾannathūn* at Nawrūz near al-Luʾluʾa (Moreh p. 50), which is already in the Cairo design. Moreh's pages cited here are Abbasid.
- **In the conclusion.** The drums "hint that some of them might be beardless effeminates", which "by no means contradicts the courtly ambience" of a *majlis* (p. 17).

### Fatimid court dance (pp. 5–9, 12)

- **The dancers' dress** covers the whole body except the palms and toes. Tight on the torso and arms, it opens into loose trousers split at the calf (p. 6, after Kalfon-Stillman). The knotted gold headband (*ʿiṣāba*) marks them as women; it is "the standard female medieval headdress" in the Geniza (p. 5).
- **Leaping and sleeves.** Most dancers leap; Arabic sources may call this *zafana*, "to dance, kick, push out a leg" (p. 8, after Moreh pp. 21–22). Some do the sleeve-dance, waving long sleeves (p. 8).
- **Men dance too.** A Fatimid wood carving in the Louvre (MAO 460) shows a turbaned man doing the sleeve-dance (p. 8, illus. 6). Cortese and Calderini also say Fatimid art has "male and female dancers" (C&C p. 88).
- **The spinning dancer.** The finest dancer on the ceiling spins. Al-Masʿūdī's description of Abbasid court dance is of legs "fixed on their axes" (p. 12, n. 69). Kapitaikin says the Byzantine courtier Kekaumenos (11th c.) calls the same movement *gurisma*, "turning" (p. 13, after Webb 1997). **Webb does not say this** (`byz_james_women_men_eunuchs.md`): Kekaumenos uses *gurisma* for "the characteristic motion of the female body", to be hidden from strangers, not for dance (Webb p. 123). Webb's word for the dancing movement is *lugisma*.
- **Accompanists.** Dancers were accompanied by a seated drummer and flautist, in Fatimid, Andalusi and Seljuq art. Al-Tifāshī (d. 1253) describes dancing slave-girls from Seville performing with other slave-girls to drum and pipe for them (p. 12, n. 68).
- **Behind a screen.** In standard practice the performer stayed hidden behind a screen and reached out a hand or a cup (p. 7, n. 37, after Sawa 1989 on the early Abbasid court). The source is Abbasid, not Fatimid.
- **The painters' contest under al-Yāzūrī (1050–58).** Painters competed in painting dancers who seemed to step out of the wall, or into it, from their niches (p. 8, n. 38: al-Maqrīzī, *Khiṭaṭ* ii, p. 318; Ettinghausen 1956). **Secondhand.** It is the closest Fatimid dance material to 1066 in the article.
- **Drum inscriptions.** Single words on three drums may be performance terms (*naẓm*, *ḍamr*) or the performers' own names. Court singing-girls and boys bore "concise and poetical resonant nicknames" (pp. 9–10).
- **Rulers and dancers.** Al-Ẓāhir (r. 1021–36) spent heavily on singers and dancers. In a procession he was "preceded by male dancers" (*raqqāṣūn*) (p. 7, n. 33: al-Maqrīzī, *Khiṭaṭ* i, p. 355, and *Ittiʿāẓ* ii, p. 164; C&C pp. 78, 89). One consort of al-Ḥāfiẓ (r. 1130–49) was a singer and lutenist (p. 7, after C&C p. 57).

### The Christian frame (pp. 13–17)

- **David dances.** The ceiling's harpist is David composing the psalms. The dancers around him become the women of Israel greeting him after Goliath (pp. 13–15).
- **Byzantine David dancing like a *jāriya*.** In a Byzantine-Latin psalter from Cyprus (c. 1150–1200), David dances the sleeve-dance before the Ark himself, "like an Islamic *jāriya–qayna*". Kapitaikin reads this as an "exhibition of David's humility" (p. 15, illus. 18, after Steppan 1997).
- **A king told to dance.** Alexander of Telese urged Roger II to follow David's humility in dancing before the Ark (p. 15, n. 88).
- **Sleeves in Byzantine art.** Byzantine scenes show the Israelite women in robes with exaggerated sleeves, and the Septuagint calls them dancing in a ring (*chorevousai*) (p. 15).
- **In Islam, Dāwud is a musician too.** Al-Kindī credits him with an eight-stringed instrument (p. 16, n. 90). Al-Yaʿqūbī's translation of the psalms puts flutes and drums in place of the harp (p. 17, n. 94).

## 2. Cross-checks

1. **Al-Ẓāhir's *raqqāṣūn* ("male dancers").** C&C p. 89, Kapitaikin's own source, says only "a procession of dancers" (C&C n. 65 is al-Maqrīzī). "Male" is Kapitaikin's gloss on the masculine plural. Lev (p. 154, after Goitein) reads the *raqqāṣūn* of al-Musabbiḥī's chronicle, which al-Maqrīzī used for al-Ẓāhir's reign, as policemen or runners (`isl_lev_state_society_fatimid.md` §4, `isl_moreh_live_theatre.md` §4 item 10). **Checked against the Arabic (2026-10-27), *Khiṭaṭ*, Būlāq 1853, vol. 1** (Princeton copy, HathiTrust page OCR, outside the repo):
   - **i, p. 355**, the page Kapitaikin cites, has no procession. On al-Ẓāhir's love of song it says the people of Egypt "took up **female** singers and **female** dancers" (*al-mughanniyāt wa-l-raqqāṣāt*). The word is feminine.
   - **i, p. 423** (*Khizānat al-bunūd*) repeats it: in his time Miṣr and Cairo vied in "songs and female dancers" (*al-aghānī wa-l-raqqāṣāt*).
   - **i, p. 89** uses the masculine for officials: people suffered "the oppression of the *raqqāṣīn*" in a tax office al-Nāṣir abolished (Mamluk). That supports Lev's reading of *raqqāṣūn* as runners or bailiffs.
   - So "male dancers" cannot come from *Khiṭaṭ* i, p. 355. If it is anywhere, it is in *Ittiʿāẓ* ii, p. 164, which we have not seen. **Do not build "male dancers" on Kapitaikin.** Female dancers under al-Ẓāhir are attested (*Khiṭaṭ* i, pp. 355, 423).
2. **"Mukhannathūn at Fatimid celebrations" (p. 11).** The claim is not supported beyond Moreh p. 50, as noted in §1. Cite Moreh, not Kapitaikin. The Nawrūz passage behind Moreh p. 50 is also in Būlāq vol. 1, p. 493: *al-muʾannathūn wa-l-fāsiqāt* gather under the Pearl Pavilion "so that the caliph sees them", instruments in hand. That agrees with the Sayyid reading already in the Moreh note.
3. **The *kūba*.** It agrees with the Baghdad note, which read al-Ghazālī directly. No change.
4. **The square *duff*.** It agrees with Rowson pp. 678–679. The jurist link is unsourced (§1).
