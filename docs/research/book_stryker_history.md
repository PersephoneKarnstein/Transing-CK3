## 2. Movement genealogy (waves, trans radicalism, left politics, trans/feminist conflicts)

### 2a. The waves of feminism (Introduction, "Feminist Frameworks")
- L461: "There are many feminisms, not all of them progressive. On the one hand, there have been Marxist, socialist, and anarchist feminisms, liberal feminisms, cultural feminisms, crip feminisms, ecofeminisms, indigenous feminisms, feminisms rooted in communities of color, and radical lesbian separatist feminisms."
- L463 (the book's rule of thumb): "the more a particular version of feminism concentrates on dismantling oppressive social structures rooted in misogyny and 'the patriarchy,' regardless of who is doing the oppressing or being oppressed, the likelier it is to imagine trans people as part of its constituency. The more a particular version of feminism roots its agenda in special rights and protections based on biological criteria, the more likely it is to drift toward racist affinities and reactionary positions on trans issues."
- L465 (all three waves): "'First-wave' feminism in the United States, from the late eighteenth through the early twentieth centuries, tended to focus on dress reform, access to education, political equality, and, above all, suffrage ... 'Second-wave' feminism, sometimes known as the 'women's movement,' took off in the 1960s ... A feminist 'third wave' emerged in the 1990s, partly in response to the perceived shortcomings of earlier generational inflections of feminism and partly to focus on emerging issues. Third-wave feminists considered themselves more sex-positive than their mothers and grandmothers—staging SlutWalks rather than Take Back the Night marches, making feminist porn instead of denouncing all pornography as inherently degrading to women, and supporting sex-worker activism and self-empowerment instead of imagining themselves as rescuing disempowered women from prostitution. They were more interested in contesting body-shaming politics, in having a subversive or ironic relationship to consumer culture, and in engaging in online activism through social media. Some feminists have long debated whether the 'wave' metaphor has ever been all that useful ... the fracturing and proliferation of various feminist tendencies and traditions are making it harder and harder to think in terms of 'waves' at all anymore."
- L467: "'The personal is political.' ... In this sort of feminism, gender itself—the social system for sorting bodies into unequal categories based on biology—could be understood as the enemy. If that's your understanding of gender, then it makes a lot of sense to call yourself 'gender critical.'"
- L469-471: origin of antitrans second-wave rhetoric: "a feminine person assigned male at birth, they thought, should work for the social acceptability of 'sissies' or 'queens' and be proudly effeminate instead of pretending to be a 'normal' woman, or a 'real' one." ... "it was easy for some feminists to imagine trans people as unliberated dupes or collaborators with an inherently unjust gender system."
- L473: intersectionality "Rooted in Black and Chicana feminist thought"; Crenshaw's debt to Pauli Murray, whose "intersectional analysis derived from their own lived experience of being of mixed racial heritage, with female reproductive biology and a masculine sense of self. A trans/nonbinary perspective has thus informed the concept of intersectionality from its very beginning."
- L475 (the only place "transfeminism" is defined in the main text): "One basic step in forging a better relationship between transgender and feminist politics—a step toward transfeminism—is simply to acknowledge that our individual experiences and gendered senses of self ... are very idiosyncratic, personal matters ... Being trans is kind of like being left-handed."
- L2345 (Reader's Guide): "How do you think third- and fourth-wave feminism differ from second-wave feminism regarding their approach to transgender issues? Is there a need to distinguish transfeminism from feminism more generally?"
- L2929 (Sources): "'Third-wave' feminism: Gillis, Stacy, Gillian Howe, and Rebecca Munford. Third Wave Feminism: A Critical Exploration. Palgrave, 2007; Hernandez, Daisy, and Bushra Rehman, eds. Colonize This! Young Women of Color on Today's Feminism."

### 2b. TERF / gender critical terminology (Ch. 1)
- L555: "Since the 2010s, there's been a marked resurgence of some long-standing but relatively minor strands of feminist thought that reject the framework of the gender concept ... Gender, in this view, is a false ideology that masks real material differences of sex and the oppression of women as members of a subordinated 'sex-caste.' This sense of being 'gender critical' links some secular and leftist feminists with right-wing, conservative, or reactionary social movements."
- L557: "Gender critical is often the preferred term self-applied by people others call trans exclusive radical feminists (TERFs). That acronym was coined around 2008 by online cisgender feminist activist Viv Smythe."
- L559: "TERF is not a term I personally use ... Some current terms ... include TARL (trans antagonistic reactionary liberal), coined by the literary critic Andrea Long Chu, and TUMF (trans unaware mainstream feminist), coined by the transfeminist activist and author Julia Serano."

### 2c. Trans radicalism and the left, chronologically
- **Hirschfeld as socialist; Marxist split in sex reform (Ch. 2)**, L899: "The World League for Sexual Reform splintered between liberal and radical factions (some members favoring the reform politics of Western democratic capitalism and others favoring Soviet-style Marxist revolution) ... Anti-Semites denounced Hirschfeld, who was a socialist as well as a gay man, as 'the most repulsive of all Jewish monsters.'"
- **"Case 13" (1909), a trans woman in the workers' movement (Ch. 2)**, L877: "In San Francisco I began as an itinerant bookseller ... I also had socialist writings and even took part in the workers' movement because I moved about as a man outside of my apartment ... I set up house and became their room mother." L879: "I have always been an activist for equal rights."
- **Reed Erickson (Ch. 4)**, L1147: "ran with a left-wing lesbian crowd ... Erickson came under FBI surveillance as a suspected communist during the McCarthy era ... Schuylkill Industries owned a large yacht, the Granma, which the company sold in the 1950s to Fidel Castro."
- **Compton's / Tenderloin organizing (Ch. 4)**, L1107: "inspired in equal measures by the socially progressive gospel preached by the Reverend Martin Luther King Jr. ... by the federal government's new War on Poverty programs, and by the vision of radically participatory democratic social movements outlined by Saul Alinsky in his activist handbook Reveille for Radicals." L1109: "the first successful multiracial gay-straight alliance for economic justice." L1111: Vanguard "the earliest-known queer youth organization in the United States ... The group's second leader even took a nom de guerre, Jean-Paul Marat, after a famous figure in the French Revolution." L1113: Glide Memorial "inspired by the Reverend Martin Luther King Jr.'s civil rights activism and by Latin American 'liberation theology.'" L1117: "'You've heard about Black Power and White Power,' the flyer proclaimed, before instructing its readers to 'get ready for Street Power.'"
- **Compton's as model (Ch. 4)**, L1127: "discriminatory policing practices that target members of minority communities, urban land-use policies that benefit social elites and displace poor people, the unsettling domestic consequences of US foreign wars, lack of access to health care, civil rights activism ... and political coalition-building around the structural injustices that affect many different communities."
- **Stonewall / GLF / STAR (Ch. 4)**, L1191: "the Gay Liberation Front, which modeled itself on radical Third World liberation and anti-imperialist movements." L1193: "In 1970, Stonewall regulars Sylvia Rivera and Marsha P. Johnson established Street Transvestite Action Revolutionaries (STAR). Their primary goal was to help street kids stay out or get out of jail and to find food, clothing, and a place to live. They opened STAR House, an overtly politicized version of the 'house' culture that already characterized Black and Latino queer kinship networks ... Some STAR members, particularly Rivera, were also active in the Young Lords, a revolutionary Puerto Rican youth organization ... Rivera also participated in the Revolutionary People's Constitutional Convention organized by the Black Panthers in Philadelphia, which adopted a plank calling for surgery and hormones on demand for trans people."
- **Angela Douglas / TAO (Ch. 4)**, L1199: newsletters "always interesting hodgepodges of eccentric political manifestos, psychedelic art, photographs, activist news, and occult beliefs. TAO was the first truly international grassroots transgender community organization." L1205: "She remained hostile to any suggestion of what later generations would call 'trans-normativity' and suspicious of all attempts to police trans lives into conventional molds."
- **1971 Trans Liberation manifesto (Ch. 5)**, L1221-1243. L1221: "Militant trans liberationists of the day rejected the idea that they were simply enacting gender stereotypes, and they resented the idea that they were expendable 'shock troops' for feminist and gay liberation struggles." L1231: "We unite around our oppression, as all oppressed groups unite around their particular oppression. All power to Trans Liberation." Demands L1235-1241: "1. Abolition of all cross-dressing laws and restrictions of adornment. 2. An end to exploitation and discrimination within the gay world. 3. An end to exploitation practices of doctors and physicians in the fields of transvestism and transsexualism. 4. Free hormone treatment and surgery upon demand. 5. Transsexual assistance centers should be created in all cities with populations of one million inhabitants, under the direction of postoperative transsexuals. 6. Full rights on all levels of society and full voice in the struggles for liberation of all oppressed peoples. 7. Immediate release of all persons in mental hospitals or prison for transvestism or transsexualism."
- **Neoliberalism 1973 (Ch. 5)**, L1247-1253: "Around 1973, a new political-economic system now referred to as 'neoliberalism' first clicked into place." L1253: "States are in the business of managing their populations to harness their productive capacities as workers and soldiers and students and mothers ... Trans issues are political precisely because trans lives confound the categories involved in a state's routine practices of administering its population." L1277: "'Genderfuck, Don't Fight'" and the 1973 "clone look."
- **Combahee River Collective (Ch. 5)**, L1289: "'We do not have the misguided notion that it is their maleness per se—i.e., their biological maleness—that makes them what they are. As Black women, we find any kind of biological determinism a particularly dangerous and reactionary basis on which to build a politic.'"
- **WITCH / Radicalesbians (Ch. 5)**, L1291: "Robin Morgan played an important role in launching Women's International Terrorist Conspiracy from Hell, or WITCH, in 1968, a loose network of socialist-feminist collectives." L1297: "'A lesbian is the rage of all women condensed to the point of explosion.'"
- **1972-73 San Francisco Pride split (Ch. 5)**, L1307: lesbian separatist signs "'Off the Pricks!'"; in 1973 "two separate San Francisco Pride events were organized ... the other by gays and lesbians who opposed drag and expressly forbade trans people from participating."
- **Sylvia Rivera 1973 speech (Ch. 5)**, L1311: "I have been to jail. I have been raped and beaten many times, by men, heterosexual men. I will no longer put up with this shit ... Come and see the people at STAR House. The people there are trying to do something for all of us, not [just] men and women that belong to a white, middle-class club ... Revolution! Gay Power!!"
- **Beth Elliott / Robin Morgan 1973 (Ch. 5)**, L1313-1323; Morgan: "'I charge him as an opportunist, an infiltrator, and a destroyer—with the mentality of a rapist.'" L1325: "the 'transsexual rapist' trope circulated in grassroots lesbian networks."
- **Sandy Stone / Olivia (Ch. 5)**, L1325: "In 1977 ... Sandy Stone, a trans woman and recording engineer who had worked with Jimi Hendrix ... became the target of an antitrans campaign among some women who threatened to boycott Olivia if Stone did not resign."
- **Janice Raymond (Ch. 5)**, L1327: "a Boston-based radical lesbian separatist named Janice G. Raymond, a former nun and graduate student in theology at Boston College ... under influential feminist theologian Mary Daly ... The Transsexual Empire: The Making of the She-Male. Though often assumed to be a work of secular social analysis, it lays out an argument couched in religious terms." L1333: "'All transsexuals rape women's bodies by reducing the real female form to an artifact.'" L1335 (eunuch/harem comparison): "comparing them to the eunuchs (castrated males) who once guarded the harems of Eastern potentates ... 'Will every lesbian feminist space become a harem?'" L1341: "'I contend that the problem of transsexualism would best be served by morally mandating it out of existence.'"
- **Trans-positive second wave (Ch. 5)**, L1353-1355: "Shulamith Firestone, a socialist feminist ... 'Just as to assure elimination of economic classes requires the revolt of the underclass (the proletariat) and ... their seizure of the means of production, so to assure the elimination of sexual classes requires the revolt of the underclass (women) and the seizure of control of reproduction.… the end goal of feminist revolution must be ... not just the elimination of male privilege but of the sex distinction itself.'" L1357: Jeanne Cordova and Rev. Freda Smith defended Elliott; Candy Coleman: "'I, like so many other women and Gaysisters, am proud to call her sister.'" L1361: C. Tami Weyant: "'only feminism can offer them safe harbor from that oppression.'"
- **1980s (Ch. 5)**, L1417: "The more radical activists spoke of being 'medically colonized' ... Antitrans feminist arguments increasingly dovetailed with right-wing politics, as when antipornography feminists in this period, such as Catharine MacKinnon and Andrea Dworkin, allied themselves with conservative government policies."
- **Leslie Feinberg's Marxism (Ch. 6)**, L1443: "Leslie Feinberg (who preferred the gender-neutral pronouns ze and hir) gave this expansive sense of transgender a political charge with hir influential 1992 pamphlet Transgender Liberation: A Movement Whose Time Has Come ... Feinberg's pamphlet took a Marxist approach to the question of the social, political, and economic oppression of nonnormative expressions of gender, and ze called for a 'trans-gender' politics that would link many struggles against specific gender-based oppressions together into one overarching radical movement."
- **Zines (Ch. 6)**, L1451: "the sex-worker-positive Gendertrash out of Montreal, the goddess-oriented cultural feminist publication TransSisters: The Journal of Transsexual Feminism from Kansas City, and San Francisco's TNT: The Transsexual News Telegraph ... replaced by online forums and chat groups, and soon thereafter by early social media outlets like LiveJournal and MySpace."
- **Queer / ACT UP / Queer Nation / Transgender Nation (Ch. 6)**, L1461: "a new kind of alliance politics ... which did not organize itself around identity categories but instead took aim at the overarching social structures that marginalized those infected by HIV, was queer." L1465: "'Queers Read This!' and urged 'an army of lovers' to take to the streets ... Transgender Nation, spearheaded by Anne Ogborn, originally formed as an affinity group within the San Francisco chapter of Queer Nation in 1992."
- **Sandy Stone's manifesto and Santa Cruz feminism (Ch. 6)**, L1475: "'The "Empire" Strikes Back: A Posttranssexual Manifesto,' first published in 1992 ... she used to help shift the old trans-exclusionary feminist debates into a productive new register." L1477: "The idea that transgender theory is derivative of queer theory is simply empirically wrong." L1479: "Anzaldúa's version of feminism was messier. It found its power in being mixed, in crossing borders, in having no one clear category to fit into—and in not disavowing an essential impurity. Haraway drew on this ... 'Cyborg Manifesto.'" L1481-1483: sex wars, Barnard 1982; "Trans people and practices were universally opposed by members of the 'sex-negative' side of the feminist sex wars, while many 'sex-positive' feminists were more welcoming ... The failure of cis-centric versions of queer theory and feminism to adequately account for the fullness of trans life fueled the subsequent development of both a trans theory that went beyond queer theory's emphasis on sexuality and a trans feminism that expanded the analysis of gender-based oppression."
- **Antigender ideology (Ch. 6)**, L1493: Beijing 1995 "an important 'super-spreader' event"; L1495: popes "characterized transsexuals as more dangerous than nuclear war"; "Mary Daly and Janice Raymond, after all, had been raised in the Catholic faith."
- **Michigan Womyn's / Camp Trans / Transexual Menace (Ch. 6)**, L1521: "expulsion of Nancy Jean Burkholder, a trans woman, from the Michigan Womyn's Music Festival in 1991 ... 'womyn-born-womyn.'" L1523: "a counterevent, 'Camp Trans,' near the music festival grounds. It offered ongoing protests, educational outreach, dialogue, alternative community formations, and networking opportunities to combat feminist transphobia ... until the festival finally folded in 2015." L1527: "Riki Wilchins and her new direct-action group, Transexual Menace (TM)—named in honor of the lesbian group Lavender Menace from the 1970s." L2047: "trademark image was a Goth-styled black T-shirt emblazoned with the group's name in blood-dripping red letters."
- **1990s left/anarchist/Marxist organizing (Ch. 6)**, L2041: "Sometimes these struggles were waged through radical groups like Critical Resistance, a prison abolition group, or within anarchist groups or sectarian groups of the Marxist Left."
- **Post-9/11 critique of transnormativity (Ch. 6)**, L2071: "Dean Spade's Normal Life: Administrative Violence, Critical Trans Politics, and the Limits of Law ... those aiming to gain access to what critics from the left called a 'transnormative' form of citizenship." L2087: hate-crimes law "a dubious accomplishment in the eyes of left-wing trans activists who thought its hate-crimes sentencing enhancements only strengthened the penal powers of the state."
- **2010s antitrans feminist / far-right convergence (Ch. 7)**, L2111: Deep Green Resistance "denounced ... as 'ecofascist,' primarily for the virulent transphobia of its founders Lierre Keith and Derrick Jensen"; "'Forbidden Discourse: The Silencing of Feminist Criticism of "Gender"'"; Jeffreys' *Gender Hurts* "dressed up Janice Raymond's thirty-five-year-old rhetoric ... in twenty-first-century drag"; "the Women's Liberation Front were openly collaborating with right-wing funders such as the Heritage Foundation and Alliance Defending Freedom."
- **Trans anarchism and Occupy (Ch. 7)**, L2137: "Anarchist movements, oriented against state-based power and prioritizing local and DIY approaches to change making, have offered congenial homes for a radical transgender activism that has embraced the 'prefigurative politics' of living in the present the way one ideally wants to live. A twenty-six-year-old trans anarchist named Justine Tunney ... along with other members of a Philadelphia anarchist collective that jokingly called itself Trans World Order, ran the servers that allowed the far-flung global Occupy movement to communicate."
- **Pinkwashing critique (Ch. 7)**, L2155: "criticism from some on the left who worried that trans-inclusion policies contributed to 'pinkwashing' objectionable US actions."
- **Women's March 2017 (Ch. 7)**, L2211: "pink 'pussy hats,' which some marchers felt recentered white, cisgender, and biological-essentialist notions of womanhood."
- **Epilogue**, L2301: "trans people have increasingly turned to one another in the growing conviction that we have nobody to care for us other than ourselves ... we are still connecting underground, like the mycelial networks that link mushrooms together." L2309: Nietzsche: "'Only those who are crushed by a present circumstance ... and who are determined at all cost to throw off their oppression,' have any need for a critical relationship to the history that has produced them."

## 4. Beliefs and practices recurring across the movements

Each item: the practice, then the strongest supporting quotes with location.

1. **Mutual aid / "nobody to care for us other than ourselves."** L485 (Intro): minorities "banding together to offer one another mutual support ... Others craft survival tools for living within conditions that can't, at that moment, be changed." L2221 (Ch. 7): "They organized mutual-aid networks, joined lawsuits, launched petitions." L2293 (Epilogue): a trans woman who "had established a mutual-aid network to help trans people escape political persecution." L2301: "we are still connecting underground, like the mycelial networks that link mushrooms together. We continue to find and to hold one another."
2. **Chosen family, houses, house mothers.** L647 (Ch. 1): "The 'house' or 'ballroom' subcultures ... have large balls in which participants 'walk the categories.'" L1017 (Ch. 3): William Dorsey Swann "called his informal network the House of Swann and described himself as 'queen of the drags.'" L1193 (Ch. 4): STAR House, "Rivera and Johnson, as 'house mothers,' would hustle to pay the rent, while their 'children' would scrounge for food ... they even dreamed of establishing a school for kids who'd never learned to read and write." L877 (Ch. 2, 1909): "I set up house and became their room mother." L1177 (Ch. 4, Suzy Cooke's Berkeley commune): "here I was with this family of like eight people all surrounding me ... All the girls would donate clothes to me."
3. **Street economies and the street as home.** L1045 (Rechy): "making it from day to park to bar to day." L1091: Tenderloin trans women "often worked in the commercial sex trade or as maids in the hotels where their friends sold sex." ## 5. Historical / premodern precedents (exhaustive)

Grouped by period; every item is in the file at the line given. Items marked (SRC) occur only in the Additional Reading / Sources lists, not the narrative.

### 5a. Prehistory and antiquity (Ch. 2 "The Very Long View," Ch. 1 "Religion")
- **Cave art and grave goods**, L671: "prehistoric cave paintings and other rock art persistently depict humanoid figures with a mix of male and female attributes ... Ancient religions and spiritual traditions are replete with supernatural beings who change or transcend sex ... numerous examples of female skeletons buried with grave goods usually associated with male skeletons, and vice versa."
- **Castration in the first city-states**, L673: "human agricultural laborers were sometimes castrated along with the nonhuman animals they worked with. That suggests that body modification has long been a practice for moving a person from one social category to another (for example, from potential husband and father to beast of burden toiling in the fields)."
- **Çatalhöyük**, L675: "the lack of significant distinctions between male and female skeletons ... Their anthropomorphic artwork de-emphasized rather than heightened anatomical sex differences."
- **Kama Sutra / hijra**, L677: "A 'third sex' status similar to modern hijra in South Asia is mentioned more than 4,000 years ago in the Kama Sutra."
- **Scythians / Amazons / feminized priestesses**, L677: "The ancient Greeks were fascinated with their so-called barbarian neighbors to the east, the Scythians. They were nomadic horse people of the steppes whose women warriors gave rise to the Amazonian myth and whose religion included feminized male priestesses and shamans." (Also L865: Krafft-Ebing "wrote about 'insanity among the Scythians.'")
- **Galli of Cybele**, L677: "Roman society had a similar cult of castrated galli priestesses who tended the temple of the 'Great Mother' goddess Cybelle."
- **Elagabalus**, L677: "an emperor, Elagabalus, who dressed as a woman, married a man, and reputedly promised half the empire to any surgeon who could construct a functional vagina for them."
- **Shamans / reincarnation / androgynous deities**, L637: "Shamanic practices in some cultures may involve shamans taking on other-gendered personalities during rituals or being inhabited by the spirit of a differently gendered power or deity; sometimes shamans may live socially in special gender roles as neither men nor women. Some religions believe in reincarnation and attribute present-day gender incongruity to past-life experience. Some pantheistic religions have deities that androgynously mix sexual characteristics, or change sex, or have both masculine and feminine manifestations."
- **Rabbinical Judaism**, L637: "Ancient rabbinical texts demonstrate that Judaism has recognized several distinct genders with different religious, social, and legal obligations." (SRC L2779/L2983: Strassfeld, *Trans Talmud: Androgynes and Eunuchs in Rabbinic Literature*.)
- **Qur'an and Hadith**, L637: "In Islam, the only mention of nonnormative gender in the Qur'an occurs in verse 24:3, in a passage that says Muslim women need not follow the usual rules of modesty when in the presence of male attendants who look and act like women and do not desire them sexually. - **Eunuchs of "Eastern potentates"** (hostile use by Raymond), L1335: "comparing them to the eunuchs (castrated males) who once guarded the harems of Eastern potentates ... eunuchs, too, 'can rise in the Kingdoms of the Fathers.'"
