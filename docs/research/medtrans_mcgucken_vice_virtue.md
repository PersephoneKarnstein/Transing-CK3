# McGucken, "Vice & Virtue As Woman?" (2019): design reading

Source: Stephenie McGucken, "Vice & Virtue As Woman?: The Iconography of Gender Identity in the Late Anglo-Saxon Psychomachia Illustrations," *Medieval Feminist Forum: A Journal of Gender and Sexuality* 55, no. 1 (2019): 42–63. DOI: [10.17077/1536-8742.2148](https://doi.org/10.17077/1536-8742.2148). Volume, issue and pages are verified from the PDF's footer. The DOI comes from the journal's issue page (scholarworks.wmich.edu/mff/vol55/iss1/3) because the PDF does not print one.

Read in full. Quotes are verbatim. Primary objects: the three late-tenth-century Anglo-Saxon illustrated Prudentius *Psychomachia* cycles (BL Add. MS 24199; BL Cotton Cleopatra C. viii; CCCC MS 23), with Ælfric, Gildas and *Beowulf* as comparanda. Framing is in `medtrans_kim_bychowski_visions.md`.

**Relevance: low to moderate, almost entirely for the Bæddel branch (Anglo-Saxon side).** The article reads gender in **allegorical personifications**, not in people. It gives the mod period visual vocabulary and the mechanics of period hostility, but no evidence about trans women's lives.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **Gender shifts from scene to scene, on purpose.** The artists set a "game of shifting genders": "Male transforms into female. Feminine becomes masculine" (p. 42). The Anglo-Saxon illuminators "were the first to conceive both the vices and virtues ... as (nearly) entirely female" (p. 46). Continental cycles drew the virtues as male warriors.
- **Dress and attributes decide gender, not the body.** Long gown plus unbound hair or veil reads as female. Short tunic and leggings read as male. A cloak clasped on one shoulder is male, and one clasped on both shoulders or at the centre is female (p. 48). Viewers follow Kessler and McKenna's rule: "see someone as female only when you cannot see them as male" (p. 48).
- **The virtue becomes masculine to win.** "Pudicitia wins, but only when her femininity is masculinized and her enemy's femininity highlighted" (p. 43). She gains a man's helmet for the killing blow, while the dying vice is made more feminine.
- **One woman warrior is acceptable, a company of them is not.** When Pax's troops fight they are drawn male, and when they rejoice they are drawn female. "Groups of women, however, are better off praying and rejoicing" (p. 51). A footnote cites Æthelflæd, nuns armed to defend their houses, and female sheriffs (p. 51 n. 19).
- **The defeated are feminised.** Libido is tied to unruly foliage, read as the "uncontrollable" female (p. 57). Gildas has the beaten Britons stretch out their arms "like women" to be bound (p. 59). *Beowulf* plays down the femininity of Grendel's Mother so that she cannot diminish the hero.
- **Gender flux marks a monster.** Avaritia moves between masculine and feminine dress and suckles the Crimes on "black milk". Budny describes her as "either an unveiled female or a male perversely suckling a brood" (p. 62). Her "permeable gender" is a sign of her monstrosity (pp. 62–63), and it ultimately reinforces the gender boundary.
- **Virginal motherhood is the positive counter-image.** Ælfric's Aaron's rod "blossoms without seed" as a type of Mary (p. 57).
- **Audience and reform.** The cycles come from the tenth-century Benedictine reform (Æthelwold, Canterbury, Malmesbury). Karkov links their production to "the curtailing of female power within the church" (p. 53). McGucken leaves the question of the audience open.

### Event / character flags
- **No primary-source person suitable for an event.** Æthelflæd, Lady of the Mercians, appears only in a footnote (p. 51 n. 19) as a woman warrior. She is not a trans figure and should not be co-opted. At most she is a precedent the Bæddel women cite ("the Lady of the Mercians rode with her own war-band").
- A possible **artefact event**: a Bæddel House acquires an illuminated *Psychomachia* from a sacked minster, and the sisters argue over whether the women warriors are them. It could be an artifact (book) with a small learning bonus and a choice: keep it, or scrape the vices' faces.
