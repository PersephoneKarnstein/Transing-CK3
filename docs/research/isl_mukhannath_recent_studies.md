## 1. The arguments in brief

### 1.1 Omar 2025: the burning report

**Thesis.** A Companion report has Abū Bakr burn a man "penetrated like a woman" (*yunkaḥu kamā tunkaḥu al-marʾa*). It is a third/ninth-century literary report that grew out of the story of the apostate al-Fujāʾa al-Salamī, burned in 11/632 for treachery and the killing of Muslims (pp. 455–456, 475–476).

**The stages of the report:**

| Stage | Date | What happens | Pages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Absent | before c. 850 | Not in ʿAbd al-Razzāq, Ibn Abī Shayba, Aḥmad's *Musnad*, Mālik, Saḥnūn, al-Shaybānī or al-Shāfiʿī | 456 |
| Forerunner | al-Jāḥiẓ (d. 868) | Abū Bakr burns "a group" who did *liwāṭ*. In another report he drops a wall on an offender | 456 |
| Full report | Ibn Abī al-Dunyā (d. 894), *Dhamm al-malāhī* | Khālid writes about one man in an Arab village. ʿAlī advises fire, and the Companions agree | 457 |
| Copied | al-Kharāʾiṭī (d. 939); al-Ājurrī (d. 970, Baghdad) | Al-Kharāʾiṭī adds "the evidence against him was established" | 458 |
| Rejected as weak | al-Jaṣṣāṣ (d. 981): *mursal*; "not a single jurist has argued for burning" | The man was perhaps burned for apostasy | 462 |
| Rejected as weak | Ibn Ḥazm (d. 1064), al-Bayhaqī (d. 1066), al-Khaṭīb al-Baghdādī (d. 1071) | Chains interrupted | 458–459 |
| Named | Ibn Ḥazm, citing al-Zajjāj (d. 923) | The man "is named al-Fujāʾa" | 462–463 |
| Merged | Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr (d. 1071, Mālikī) | Apostasy and passivity become one report, with a Mālikī stoning alternative | 466–467 |
| Back-dated | al-Zaylaʿī (d. 1360), then al-ʿAynī, Ibn Ḥajar, Ibn al-Humām | Al-Wāqidī cited as the source. Ibn Ḥajar calls the report "very weak" | 467–469 |

**The early historians.**
- Al-Wāqidī (pp. 464–465), al-Balādhurī (pp. 465–466) and al-Ṭabarī (p. 466) tell al-Fujāʾa's burning with no sexual charge.
- Al-Wāqidī's only sexual note is that he drew "sexually depraved" followers (p. 465).
- Abū Bakr on his deathbed: "I wish I had not burned al-Fujāʾa" (Ibn ʿAbd Rabbih, via Omar p. 463).

**How "like a woman" is built:**
- **An insult before a crime.** The phrase was first "a rhetorical insult" thrown at a traitor (pp. 455, 470, 476). The technical legal term was *mafʿūl bihī*, "the one done to" (p. 475).
- **Slander.** The earliest legal use of the formula is in a slander (*qadhf*) question put to Isḥāq b. Rāhawayh (d. 853) (p. 471). Ibn Rāhawayh preferred that the body be burned only after execution (p. 471).
- **Older roots.** The phrase echoes Leviticus 18:22 and the Midrash: "to be like a woman means to be penetrated" (p. 464 n. 50, secondhand via Satlow).
- **An insult against Rome.** Genesis Rabbah calls Rome a nation "penetrated like women", and so unfit to rule (p. 472 n. 81, secondhand via Brodsky).
- **The passive partner singled out.** The report asks only about him, which "is itself telling" (p. 475).
- **Pathology.** Physicians treated *ubna* as a disease, "some ... contending that it was treatable" (p. 470).

**Punishment debate in the window** (p. 461):
- unconditional death (Medina, Mālikīs);
- death on the *zinā* model (Kufa and Basra, many Shāfiʿīs and Ḥanbalīs);
- or discretionary *taʿzīr* (Ẓāhirīs, some Ḥanafīs).

ʿAlī is credited with contradictory penalties: stoning, a fall "from a high place", the *zinā* model and burning (pp. 473–474). Burning ran into the Prophet's saying that "no one punishes with fire except the Lord of the Fire" (p. 474).

**Egypt, Ismaʿilis, Fatimids.** Nothing. Omar does not cite al-Qāḍī al-Nuʿmān. The only Egyptian material is modern dialect: *manyūk*, *mitnāk* and *khawal* as insults for the passive partner (p. 472).

**Mukhannath material** (all in notes):
- **Ibn Qutayba (d. 889).** A *mukhannath* was brought to Abū l-ʿĀj, governor of Wāsiṭ, because "He is penetrated like a woman", "but he dismissed him" (p. 456 n. 8, secondhand).
- **Ibn Taymiyya (d. 1328).** He asks how women could marry *mukhannathūn*, whose "desire has transferred" (p. 472 n. 81, secondhand).

**Verdict: reliable.** This is careful isnād and source history in a top journal. Two cautions:
- It concerns the passive male partner in *liwāṭ*, not the *mukhannath* as such. The *mukhannath* appears only in notes.
- Omar's broader genealogy book is "forthcoming" (p. 461 n. 33). The medical chapter promised there (p. 470 n. 72) was not seen.

### 1.2 Hirsch 2023: dress, adornment and ritual

**Thesis.** The jurists "widened the binary" to make room for *mukhannathūn* and *khunthā* as "tolerated, but disapproved" groups. The room they made served patriarchy (pp. 387, 389, 397–399). The base is Sunni, "mostly from the Mashriq" (p. 389).

**The rules she reports.** Each source is the one she gives; none was re-checked here.

| Topic | *Mukhannath* | *Khunthā* | Her source | Page |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Covering the pubis | Not before men or women. If clothing runs short, women's pubis is covered first, then the *mukhannath*'s, then men's | Not even the soles shown, before sex is settled | al-Sarakhsī, *Mabsūṭ* 30:105, 107 | 393 |
| Organs | — | Male organs hidden from women, female organs hidden from men | al-Nawawī, *Majmūʿ* 3:182 | 393 |
| Silk, yellow | "permitted but not required" to wear silk and yellow | No silk; no men's or women's jewellery | Ibn al-Ukhuwwa 76; Ibn Ḥazm; Ibn Taymiyya; al-Nawawī 8:20 | 393–394 |
| Boys' dress | Al-Ghazālī: dress a boy in white, not "colourful or silk clothing" meant for women and *mukhannathūn* | — | *Iḥyāʾ* 3:70 | 393 |
| Return | Past attention to dress is no crime if he returns to "normative masculinity" | — | none given | 393–394 |
| Beard | Forbidden to shave it | No interference with facial hair | al-Shayzarī p. 88; Ibn Bassām p. 71; Ibn al-Ukhuwwa p. 51 (*khunthā*) | 394–395 |
| Henna | On palms, soles and nails, "in partial imitation of women" | — | al-Nawawī 1:295 | 395 |
| Prayer | Women's rules | Women's rules; head covered (*qināʿ*). Al-Nawawī accepts a bare head | al-Sarakhsī 30:106, 108; al-Nawawī 2:51 | 395–396 |
| Pilgrimage | Women's rules | A mixed *iḥrām*: seamless like men, covered like women; head or face, not both | al-Sarakhsī 30:106; al-Nawawī 2:53, 7:264 | 396–397 |
| Burial | Women's rules where not forbidden to men | Five garments; men nearest the qibla, then *khunthā*, then women | al-Sarakhsī 30:105; Gesink | 397 |

**Her conclusion.** The two groups are "another subordinate group ... standing under males, yet above females" (p. 398). The *khunthā*'s mixed *iḥrām* "suggests that the khuntha were clearly understood as a third sex" (p. 397). She later speaks of "a third gender" (p. 399).

**Reliability problems:**
1. **Thin and blended footnoting.** Most *mukhannath* rules in the ritual section are footnoted only to *khunthā* passages. Al-Sarakhsī's vol. 30, pp. 103–108, is his "Kitāb al-khunthā" (Sanders's base text). This applies to the prayer, pilgrimage and burial rows. The claim that "most of the jurists defined mukhannathūn's gender as not-male" (p. 395) has no note. **Treat every *mukhannath* ritual rule in her table as unverified.**
2. **Pilgrimage conflicts with Sanders.** Sanders reports that al-Sarakhsī "admitted that he did not know the answer" on the *khunthā*'s pilgrim dress (Sanders pp. 83–84). Hirsch has him rule (p. 396). Follow Sanders until *Mabsūṭ* 30:106 is checked.
3. **"Permitted" silk and yellow.** Her sources are rules *forbidding men* these things. That the *mukhannath* was "permitted but not required" (p. 393) is her inference.
4. **Citation errors:**
   - Sanders is dated 1992 (pp. 389, 402), not 1991.
   - Almarri 2016 is cited at pp. "251–252" (p. 390 n. 14); the TSQ pages are 578–586.
   - "Rowson 1991, 70" and "1991, 65" (pp. 392, 394) cannot be the JAOS article (pp. 671–693). They are probably Rowson 1991b (*Body Guards*, pp. 50–79). The beard quote on p. 394 is therefore unverified.
   - "Roswon 2003" (p. 398 n. 64).
   - "al-Shayzārī 1996, *al-Muhadhdhab fī fiqh al-Imām al-Shāfiʿī*" (p. 394 n. 43) is Abū Isḥāq al-**Shīrāzī**'s *Muhadhdhab*, not the *ḥisba* writer.
5. **Living-community projection.** The claim that a *mukhannath* could "revert" to manhood (p. 391) is footnoted to Wikan on the modern Omani *khanīth* (n. 32). A dress parallel also cites the *khanīth* (p. 393 n. 40). Medieval law is being read through a living community.
6. **"Third sex."** Her reading runs against Sanders, who shows the law "gendered" the *khunthā* inside two sexes. It also runs against her own sources' habit of assigning women's rules "as a precaution". It is her label.
7. **Dates.** Al-Nawawī (d. 1277), al-Tifāshī (d. 1253) and al-Shayzarī (12th c.) are all after 1066. Al-Sarakhsī (d. c. 1090) and al-Ghazālī (d. 1111) are just after it.

**Verdict:** useful as a finding-list of rules and sources, with the provenance of each rule checked before use. Her categories are not usable.

### 1.3 Aziz (BA dissertation): "gender diverse performativity"

**Thesis.** Social and legal views of the *mukhannathūn* were constructed and patriarchal, medical views essentialist. The combination supports Serano's "Intrinsic Inclination Model" and modern Muslim acceptance (pp. ii, 39). The framework is Butler and Serano. The sources are almost all secondary.

**Medical chapter** (pp. 29–38). Every primary citation is secondhand:

| Claim | Via | Check |
|---|---|---|
| ʿAlī b. Sahl Rabban al-Ṭabarī: seed from the left testicle reaching the right of the womb makes a "masculine female", and the reverse a "feminine male" (p. 33) | Kueny 2013, p. 61 (spelled "Keuny") | Not checked. Aziz dates al-Ṭabarī "838–970", which is impossible; he flourished in the mid-ninth century |
| Al-Rāzī (d. 925): sex and "femininity or masculinity" follow which of the two seeds prevails (p. 33) | Rosenthal 1978, p. 52 | Rosenthal, "Ar-Râzî on the Hidden Illness", *Bulletin of the History of Medicine* 52 (1978): 45–60, is real; quote not checked |
| Al-Rāzī: *ubna* comes from weak paternal seed and spermatic vessels near the rectum that itch (p. 34) | Ragab 2015, p. 449 | Consistent with Omar p. 470 |
| Al-Rāzī: "the mukhannathun were not perceived as needing a cure" (p. 34) | Rosenthal 1978, p. 49 | **Wrong.** A reproduction of Rosenthal's translation has al-Rāzī say a prolonged case "cannot be cured", especially when the patient is "obviously feminine and effeminate". Only early, ashamed cases are treatable (verified from the reproduction; Rosenthal's page not seen). Aziz turns "incurable" into "needs no cure" |
| Ibn Sīnā (d. 1037): *ubna* is "habituation" in men of "weak sexual potency" with "a strong desire created by the imagination"; they are of "vile psychological ... disposition" (p. 36) | Rosenthal 1978, p. 50 | Secondhand; plausible |
| Ibn Sīnā on the *khunthā*'s varied anatomy (p. 35) | Ragab 2015, p. 450 | Secondhand |
| Ibn al-Nafīs (d. 1288): the foetal soul, not seed side, determines sex (pp. 36–37) | Fancy 2019, p. 271 | After the window |

**Errors:**
- "The word mukhannathun derived from khuntha" (p. 35). Both come from the root kh-n-th; neither derives from the other (Rowson p. 672; Almarri triad).
- Ibn al-Nafīs worked "two decades after" Ibn Sīnā (p. 36). It was two centuries.
- Ṭuways "had a wife (Rowson, 1991, p. 179)" (p. 13). The JAOS page is 681 (`hist_rowson_mukhannathun.md` line 89).
- Sulaymān "castrated ... Ṭuways" (p. 25). Rowson shows Ṭuways was dead by 711, before Sulaymān's reign (Rowson p. 691 n. 152).
- Q 24:31 is read as an "explicit reference" to the *mukhannathūn* that shows their role "divinely-decreed" (pp. 23–24). That is exegesis turned into scripture.
- *Mukhannathūn* "less prominent ... from the tenth century" (p. 6, after Nielson 2012 p. 260). The Baghdad note's eleventh-century evidence contradicts it.
- He calls them "a third gender" (pp. 23, 36), after Serano.

**Verdict: unreliable as history.** Use it only as a pointer to Rosenthal 1978, Ragab 2015, Kueny 2013 and Fancy, which are the works to read. Its prescriptive aim (p. 39) is legitimate for a dissertation, but it shapes the readings.

### 1.4 Almarri 2018: the kh-n-th triad

**Author and venue.** Saqer A. Almarri, the author of the TSQ article in `tsq_almarri_khuntha.md`, in *Women & Language* (2018), reprinted on Stanford's *Arcade*. The essay is autoethnographic. Almarri writes from inside the Gulf community he describes, which uses *khanīth* in self-reference (Arcade §§1–2).

**Argument:**
- *Khunthā*, *mukhannath* and *khanīth* share the root kh-n-th and its stigma. They should be read together as "a triad of identities" ("Triad" section).
- The *khunthā* is known by the body, and the other two by "inhabitation of the body" ("Triad" section).

**New material** (none of it is in the repo):
- **Al-Isnawī (d. 1370), *Īḍāḥ al-mushkil fī aḥkām al-khunthā al-mushkil*** (MS Berlin Petermann II 673, p. 57). The word *khunthā* ends in a feminine *alif* but takes **masculine** pronouns, "even if its femaleness has become clear" (Almarri's quoted Arabic, n. 3, citing al-Māwardī). So the grammar assigns a gender that the legal sex-assignment does not change.
- **A pre-Islamic legend.** In Ibn Kathīr's *Bidāya* (14th c.), the herd-girl Sukhayla tells the arbiter ʿĀmir b. al-Ẓarib to settle a *khunthā*'s inheritance by watching urination. Almarri suggests the urinary rule "could be a development of Arabian custom" ("Triad" section, secondhand).
- **Translation history.** Bouhdiba and Schimmel rendered *mukhannath* as "hermaphrodite" ("Triad" section).
- **Feminine address** among the Medinan *mukhannathūn* (Rowson p. 689, via the triad). The repo already has this from Rowson 1991b.

**Projection of a living community.** This is the essay's core move, and the project cannot adopt it:
- The *khanīth* "is the current development of an earlier identity ... the mukhannath", citing Rowson p. 693 ("Triad" section). Rowson's p. 693 n. 165 only lists the Omani role as a comparandum.
- Almarri proposes asking how Medinan practice "may be taken up by contemporary khanith communities" as "an extension" (closing section).
- As an insider's claim about his own community's past, this deserves respect (memory: weight community voices). It is still a claim of descent for a living community, which the mod never models.
- His critique of Wikan's "transsexual" label for the *khanīth* ("Triad" section) supports the project's refusal of outsider categories.

**Verdict:** reliable on the *khunthā* texts and on the linguistic point. Unusable on the *khanīth*: it is a living community and modern. Its continuity thesis is an argument, not evidence about 1066.

### 1.5 Fortier 2025: introduction to a special issue

**Thesis.** "The figure of the 'third gender'" has been present in Muslim societies "from the Medinese mukhannath period until today" (p. 1). She says "third gender" is an etic term (p. 1), but uses it throughout as her own category.

**Historical claims:**
- **The *khunthā* in Mālikī law.** Khalīl (14th c., Mālikī *Mukhtaṣar*) gives the undecidable *khunthā* "half of a man's part and half of a woman's part". Fortier calls this "recognition of a third gender" and "a third legal status" (pp. 2–3). It is the same half-and-half fallback the Ismaʿili code gives (`tsq_almarri_khuntha.md`, para. 1378). Sanders shows such fallbacks are a way of fitting the *khunthā* to two sexes, not a third status. Fortier also says there is no "true hermaphrodite" (p. 2), a medical aside, not a source claim.
- **A dating error.** "Already in Medina, at the time of the Prophet, in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries" (p. 3). This is presumably the seventh and eighth centuries.
- **Marriage.** *Mukhannathūn* "could be married and have children" (p. 3, citing Mezziane 2008 p. 218, secondhand).
- **Living-community descent.** "Hijras in India and khusras or khwāja sirās in Pakistan identify themselves with the figure of the mukhannath" (pp. 3–4, citing Abbas and Pir 2016). This is a living community's self-account. **Not for use.**
- ***Khawal*.** She dates the *khawal* of Egypt to "the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries" (p. 2).
- **Eunuchs:**
  - Medieval eunuchs as matchmakers (Cheikh Moussa 1982 p. 415, via p. 6).
  - Castration done by Christian, Coptic or Jewish traders on non-Muslim slaves (p. 6).
  - "From the twelfth century onwards" the great sanctuaries were guarded by eunuchs (Höfert 2018 p. 22, via p. 19 n. 4).
- **Segregation.** Gender segregation "facilitates the existence of in-between gender figures" and makes them matchmakers (p. 11). This is a structural claim, asserted across a thousand years without dated evidence.

**Verdict:** a programmatic overview, not research on the medieval period. Its framing (a single "third gender" lineage from Medina to hijras and Turkish pop stars) is the one the project refuses (Bhatt). Its historical items are secondhand and add little to Rowson.

### 1.6 Hayat, Ali and Nabeel 2025: marital capacity in Pakistan

This is about Pakistan's living *khawaja sira* community and the 2018 Act. **It is not a historical source and is not a model for anything in the mod.**

**Medieval law it reports** (all secondhand):
- **Al-Nawawī, *Majmūʿ* 16:213–214.** Under the Shāfiʿīs, the marriage of a *khunthā mushkil* is invalid while doubt remains (p. 565). Their paraphrase is muddled.
- **Ibn Qudāma, *Mughnī*.** A *khunthā mushkil*'s own statement of sex has weight for marriage (p. 565; no page given). This matches the self-report rule in Sanders p. 78.
- **The hadith of the *mukhannath* barred from the Prophet's wives** (al-Bukhārī 5887; p. 565). The repo has it from Rowson p. 676.

**Useful line, in its own voice.** "Khunsa does not mean any one of the people who are part of the current transgender category" (p. 561). This agrees with the project's rule that the *khunthā* never stands in for a trans woman.

**Verdict:** nothing new for 1066. It answers "medieval *mukhannath* marriage law" negatively: the article has none.

## 4. Corrections and answers to open questions in other notes

This note does not edit other notes. Changes for the lead to apply or decline:

1. **`later_starts/mukhannathun_fatimid_cairo.md`, "Scholarship read".** It lists Hirsch 2023 but has no summary of her dress rules. The summary is §1.2 here. Add a pointer.
2. **Same note, §3.3.** "Hirsch (2023, p. 397) calls the *khunthā* 'a third sex', against Sanders 1991" is **verified** (p. 397). Add:
   - Hirsch also says "a third gender" (p. 399);
   - her pilgrimage ruling conflicts with Sanders pp. 83–84 (§1.2 above, item 2).
3. **Same note, §1.6 (no *khawal*).** "The word belongs to the Mamluk–Ottoman period" is broader than the cited source. Fortier dates the *khawal* to "the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries" (p. 2). Omar has it only as a modern Egyptian insult (p. 472). Say "attested from the late eighteenth century (Fortier p. 2); earlier use unproven". The ruling stands: **do not use it for 1066**.
4. **Same note, §2.1 (§1599, ʿAlī's stoning).** Add Omar pp. 473–474: ʿAlī is credited with several incompatible penalties, so §1599 is the Ismaʿili code's choice among competing attributions, not a fixed precedent. Add Omar p. 461 for the three-way punishment debate. The *Daʿāʾim*'s stoning of both partners belongs with the Medinan or Mālikī unconditional position.
5. **Same note, §6 Q3 (al-Shayzarī's *khunthā* versus *mukhannath*).** Not settled. Hirsch (p. 394 n. 47) cites al-Shayzarī p. 88 and Ibn Bassām p. 71 for a ban on shaving a *mukhannath*'s beard, and Ibn al-Ukhuwwa p. 51 "for *khuntha*". Her editions differ from the Cairo note's (Ibn Bassām, Baghdad 1968), and she does not quote the Arabic. This at least confirms that Ibn al-Ukhuwwa reads *khunthā*.
6. **Same note, §2.6 (*ḥisba* manuals).** Omar adds the *dhamm* genre as a separate, harsher, homiletic register next to law and *ḥisba*: Ibn Abī al-Dunyā's *Dhamm al-malāhī* lists "the presence of *mukhannathūn* among women" among the diversions (p. 460, via Robson). The repo does not have this register yet.
7. **`later_starts/mukhannathun_baghdad_1066.md`.**
   - §1.1 and the "living community" paragraph (line 213) cite Rowson for the *khanīth* link. Add Almarri 2018 ("Triad" section), Fortier 2025 (pp. 3–4) and Hirsch 2023 (p. 391 n. 32) as further works asserting *mukhannath* → living-community descent. **None is used.**
   - Baghdad-relevant items from Omar: Ibn Abī al-Dunyā (d. 894) and al-Ājurrī (d. 970) were Baghdadis writing *dhamm* tracts (pp. 458, 460). Al-Khaṭīb al-Baghdādī (d. 1071) was active in Baghdad in 1066 and rejected the burning report's chain (p. 458).
8. **`tsq_almarri_khuntha.md`.** Add a related-work line for Almarri 2018, *Women & Language* 41.1: 97–109 (the triad), and its al-Isnawī point (masculine pronouns for the *khunthā*). Hirsch's citation of it as vol. 40 (p. 400), and of the TSQ article at pp. 251–252 (p. 390 n. 14), are both wrong.
9. **`hist_sanders_khuntha.md`.** Two works now contest Sanders's "no third category": Hirsch (p. 397) and Fortier (pp. 2–3, on Khalīl's half-shares). Neither engages Sanders's argument. Hirsch misdates her (1992). Sanders's reading stands. Khalīl's half-and-half rule (14th c., Mālikī) is a further parallel to the Ismaʿili para. 1378, against the Ḥanafī lower share.
10. **`hist_rowson_mukhannathun.md`.** No correction. Aziz misreports Rowson twice: Ṭuways's marriage at "p. 179" (it is p. 681), and Ṭuways among Sulaymān's castrated (contradicted by Rowson p. 691 n. 152). If Aziz is ever cited, cite Rowson directly instead.
11. **The open question "medieval *mukhannath* marriage".** None of the six gives a ruling of law. What they do give:
    - Ṭuways married and had children (Rowson p. 681, via the triad);
    - Fortier's claim that *mukhannathūn* could marry, via Mezziane 2008 p. 218 (secondhand);

    For the *khunthā mushkil* alone: marriage is invalid while doubt remains, according to al-Nawawī as reported by Hayat (p. 565, secondhand).
