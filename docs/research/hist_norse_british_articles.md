# Norse / British Gender-Crossing Articles — Research Report for "Transbian Maximalism" (CK3 mod)

Purpose: test whether a **Norse or British homeland** for the faith is defensible, against the earlier conclusion (from Byzantine/Syrian material) that the homeland should sit east of Rome because that is where castrated men were called women. Five short sources, read in full. Planned mechanics under test: random "transgender" trait; ritual castration on the galli/gallae model; castrated men culturally regarded as women; adoptive matrilineal heirship; women-centred / trans-woman-centred priesthood.

Citation convention: every quote is verbatim from the plain-text files in `scratchpad/books/`. Line numbers (`l.N`) are line numbers in those text files. Where the source carries printed pagination it is added: Berry = OURJ printed pp. 134-154; Jakobsson = ANF 126 printed pp. 01-012 (proof pagination); Tommony = BILT printed pp. 2-9. Birch (Fashion Theory 29.5, 619-639) has no page breaks in the text file, so it is cited by line only. Short names: **Blog** = Cailleach's Herbarium; **Berry**; **ÁJ** = Ármann Jakobsson; **Birch**; **T&F** = Tommony & Forster.

Reliability ladder used throughout: ÁJ and Birch are peer-reviewed by specialists (ANF; Fashion Theory). T&F is a peer-reviewed *student* journal essay. Berry is an *undergraduate* research-journal paper (edited, lightly reviewed; contains factual slips, see below). Blog is a popular guest essay by a PhD candidate, explicitly self-described as "a working theory of mine" and "my own bias" — mark every Blog-only claim as popular/unverified.

**Cross-source contradictions to carry forward**
1. *Ergi = homosexuality?* Blog: "Old Norse texts describe ergi and argr as terms for homosexuality, deviance, weakness and effeminacy" (l.21). Berry fn 62: "The words used to describe a 'bottom' in male homosexual relationships are as follows: ergi, ragr, argr, and sorðinn" (l.193-194). ÁJ: ergi "can be used about sorcery, cowardice, same-sex relations between men, female lust, and about females in general... This means that it cannot be used (as one sometimes sees done) as a synonym for homosexuality" (l.256-262). Follow ÁJ.
2. *Is Lokasenna the only shaming of Óðinn's seiðr?* Blog: "This particular tale is really the only one where Óðinn's use of seiðr is questioned in an attempt to shame the deity" (l.53). ÁJ quotes Ynglinga saga's explicit statement that seiðr carries ergi and was therefore given to the goddesses (l.156-167) and notes it "is not the only instance" (l.205-208). Follow ÁJ.
3. *Men-with-men vs bestiality.* Blog: "13th century law texts [show] that sexual relations between men were felt to be more severe than bestiality" (l.19). ÁJ: Icelandic penitentials "list deviant sexual relations with a beast and with a person of your own gender side by side, along with masturbation and incest" (l.329-332, citing Gade). The Blog does not cite its text; ÁJ's framing is the safer one.
4. *Who taught seiðr?* Berry: Odin "according to the Poetic Edda, was responsible for teaching it to the other gods" (l.479-481). ÁJ's Ynglinga saga: "var gyðjunum kennd sú íþrótt" (the goddesses were taught this art) (l.161, 167). Compatible only if "other gods" means the goddesses; use ÁJ's wording.
5. *Was Odin queer?* Berry quotes Schjödt: "there is no indication that Odin should be seen as queer... He did perform seiðr because he had to" (l.500-510) but argues against him; ÁJ argues Óðinn does queer things but, as a god, "is not queer" (l.430-432); Blog accepts Óðinn as queer with discomfort. The disagreement is interpretive, not factual.
6. *Sutton Hoo.* Berry calls it "a Viking-age ship burial" (l.1010-1012). It is early 7th-c. East Anglian; this is a Berry error, not a source disagreement, but it means Berry's archaeology section should be cited for Suontaka and Bj 581 only.

### 2A. Norse / Scandinavian

**1. ergi / argr / ragr — the core concept** | 10th-13th c. Iceland/Norway (attested in 13th-c. texts) | A broad negative term for border-crossing: unmanliness, cowardice, being penetrated, sorcery, female lust, and women generally. Not a word for homosexuality. | ÁJ l.256-262: "It is a fairly wide and ambiguous concept, indeed the word can be used about sorcery, cowardice, same-sex relations between men, female lust, and about females in general, whatever their attributes. This means that it cannot be used (as one sometimes sees done) as a synonym for homosexuality". ÁJ l.268-274: "ergi is a negative term which covers a wide ground but most of its meanings have something to do with crossing borders... a penetrated man has been emasculated, or is a sorcerer, according to Ynglinga saga." ÁJ l.201-204: "queer is a good translation in the sense that if the examples are studied, unmanliness seems to be central to the word's meaning." Blog l.21 (popular): "Old Norse texts describe ergi and argr as terms for homosexuality, deviance, weakness and effeminacy." Berry fn 62 l.193-194: "The words used to describe a 'bottom' in male homosexual relationships are as follows: ergi, ragr, argr, and sorðinn." | The one Norse concept that *bundles* sorcery, penetrability and femaleness in a single word is the best available hook for a faith that treats the "unmanly" man as a woman-category person. But note: in the sources it is always an insult, never a self-claimed status. A tenet could invert it (reclaim *argr* as the blog's modern baedelism reclaims *bæddel*).

**2. nið and the three outlaw-words (Grágás)** | Iceland, law in force c. 930-1264; text 13th c. | Calling a man *ragr*, *stroðinn* or *sorðinn* carries full outlawry and licenses killing; nið "figures the insultee as a female." | Berry l.612-615 (quoting Clover p.9): "[T]he Icelandic Gragas establishes [...] full outlawry (exile for life) for the utterance of any of the words ragr, stroðinn, or sorðinn. Indeed, for these three words, one has the right to kill." Berry l.243-249 (Clover): "the nið taunts figure the insultee as a female and in so doing suggest that the category 'man' is, if anything, even more mutable than the category 'not man.' For if a woman's ascent into the masculine took some doing, the man's descent into the feminine was just one real or imagined away." Berry l.441-444: nið was "a legal insult only resolved by either a submission to the insult or by a violent retaliation." | Directly models (k) intra-queer hostility and (l) outlaw framing: a culture tradition "Níð" in which gendered insult is a crime and a casus belli; also shows that the *man-to-woman* slide was conceptually easy in Norse thought — useful for the castration-as-womanhood veneer even without castration evidence.

**3. trénið — the carved insult-pole** | Iceland, Gísla saga (events 10th c.; text 13th c.) | Commissioning a wooden carving depicting two men in a sexual act was itself grounds for a legal fight. | Berry l.615-631: "Gisli overheard another character commission a trenið, a wooden carving of an act of homosexuality used to demean the people depicted... In this instance, even the commission of a trenið is enough to legally allow for a battle." | Flavour for a hostile-neighbour event chain; a *trénið* raised against a trans-woman priest could trigger a duel or feud.

**4. seiðr as a woman's art; Óðinn's dishonour (Ynglinga saga)** | 13th-c. Iceland (Snorri, Heimskringla), describing the legendary past | Seiðr is the most powerful magic; its ergi is so great that men cannot practise it without shame, so it was taught to the goddesses; Óðinn practises it anyway. | ÁJ l.156-167 (Old Norse then ÁJ's translation): "En þessi fjölkynngi, er framið er, fylgir svá mikil ergi, at eigi þótti karlmönnum skammlaust við at fara, ok var gyðjunum kennd sú íþrótt." = "but this magic, in its execution, is so queer that men could not practice it without dishonour and thus the goddesses were taught this art". ÁJ l.169-172: "Óðinn is not just the alfather of Edda... but also a magician or, more aptly, a witch since his magic is perceived as a female quality." Berry l.270-274: "Loki insulted Odin by comparing him to a woman and linked him to the practice of seið magic, an art supposed to be practiced only by women." Blog l.43 (popular): "seiðr (a form of magic the myths consign to women because it is so shameful...)". | Foundation for a seiðr-priestesshood in which men who practise are *by definition* unmanned and therefore sorted with women: the Norse analogue of "castrated men regarded as women," but by rite (seiðr) rather than by knife.

**5. **7. **8. **11. "A god who is queer is not queer" — divine exemption and elite bigamy** | 13th-c. **Verdict — east vs north-west homeland.** The Norse material gives the mod things the eastern material does not: a native word (*ergi/argr*) that fuses sorcery, penetrability and womanliness; a magic (*seiðr*) that by its own 13th-century description unmans the men who practise it and belongs to goddesses; a top god who drums "like the völur," leads an army with "no males," and is "more mother than father" (ÁJ l.187-189); a law code that treats the man in women's clothes as a category (lesser outlawry, Grágás); a legend of woman-dressed bell-ringing priests at Uppsala; and one high-status XXY burial dressed as a woman. The English material gives a court case (Durham 1417), a lived trans-feminine figure (Rykener), a Latin-Christian mysticism in which monks lactate as brides of Christ, and a theology in which sex is a garment of the Fall (*exuisse*; Augustine via T&F). What **neither** gives is the mod's spine: not one castrate, not one eunuch, not one text in which a castrated or unmanly man is *called* a woman as a status rather than as a slur (§2C). In the north-west, feminisation of men is always demotion, insult or disguise; the only sanctified gender-crossing runs the other way, toward manhood (Jerome, the twenty saints, Saxo's shield-maidens). The east (Byzantium/Syria/Anatolia, per the earlier reports) remains the only place where a cut man is a social woman and a priest. Recommended synthesis: keep the **homeland and the castration rite in the east**, and let the faith's **Norse branch** arrive by the Varangian/Rus route, where it finds a ready-made local theology (seiðr, ergi, Uppsala, valkyrjur) to absorb — the Norse-heritage culture supplies traditions (seiðr-priestesses, níð as crime, Uppsala bells), the eastern faith supplies the knife and the word "woman." A **British** homeland is the weakest of the three: the English evidence is late (1395-1519), female-to-male, Christian-penal, and best used for a heretical offshoot (Durham, Rykener's Cheapside, the Pardoner) rather than a cradle.

**Which bundle for which culture.** A *Norse-heritage* culture is best served by rows 1-4, 6, 11-12, 14-15, 17 (seiðr-priestesses, níð as crime, Uppsala bells, valkyrie host, outlawry, shield-maidens, Type B solidarity, divine exemption, blunted sword, flyting): everything here is in the 13th-century Icelandic/Danish textual record quoted above, with Uppsala the one Blog-only item to be re-verified in Saxo. An *English* culture is best served by rows 7-10, 16 (Cistercian bridal mysticism, Theodora's prayer, Durham's inverted shrine, Cheapside margins, the striped hood), all of which are documented 1135-1519 but all of which run on female-to-male or spiritual-only crossing and so need the faith's doctrine to invert them explicitly. Rows 5, 13 and 18 straddle both. No row in either bundle can carry **castration**: that mechanic must keep its eastern (galli/gallae, Byzantine eunuch) justification and be presented in the north-west as an imported rite that local *ergi*/seiðr theology happens to make legible.
