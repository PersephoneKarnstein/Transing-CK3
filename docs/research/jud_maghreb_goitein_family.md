- In part: Preface (p. x); A, 1, names and surnames (pp. 5–7, 12–14); A, 2 (pp. 22, 26); B, 1 (pp. 55–57, 62–63); B, 2 (pp. 72–73, 88); B, 3 (pp. 98, 115, 117–118); B, 4 (pp. 124, 129–130, 137–138); B, 5, "Social Safeguards" (pp. 142–156, read through); C, 1 (pp. 166–171, 176–177, 183, 200–203, 211–212); C, 3, divorce and remarriage (pp. 260–266, 277); C, 4 (pp. 280, 289); the note to B, 3, n. 95 (p. 151 of the notes).
- Searched, not read through: the whole text and notes for Qayrawan, Tunisia, Mahdiyya, Gabes, Tripoli, Fez, Sijilmasa, Marrakesh, Maghreb/Maghrebi, Zirid, Almoravid, Almohad; and for eunuch, khuntha, hermaphrodite, effeminate, cross-dressing, mukhannath, Purim, castration, lesbian. Every hit in the main text was read in context.

**Where the evidence comes from.** Almost everything here is **Egyptian**, above all Fustat, 10th–13th c. The Maghrib appears through letters sent from Qayrawan, al-Mahdiyya and Gabes to Egypt (mostly early to mid-11th c.), and through Maghribi emigrants who went to court in Fustat. Goitein does **not** claim that Egyptian women's freedoms carried over to the Maghrib. In fact he says the opposite: visitors from the "religiously strict and zealous Muslim West" were shocked at how freely women moved in Cairo (p. 323), and Geniza women "cannot be regarded as a unified group, either geographically or in rank" (p. 323). What he does claim is a shared economy and law of marriage across the southern Mediterranean shore. For instance, a 150-dinar dowry was usual in Fustat in 982, and in the same century a 150-dinar marriage gift was usual among the Muslims of Qayrawan, which he calls "characteristic of the socioeconomic unity of the southern shores" (pp. 129–130). Each finding below is marked **[M]** (Maghribi evidence), **[E]** (Egyptian or Syro-Palestinian) or **[M→E]** (a Maghribi person documented in Egypt).

**Gender and the body: what is absent.** The volume has no eunuch except the one in the Book of Esther (p. 171). It has nothing on khuntha (intersex) law, cross-dressing, the mukhannathun, dress codes for Jews (ghiyar), or Purim customs, beyond weddings held on Purim. Goitein sends "playboys, gays, and lesbians" and "pleasure boys" on to vol. IV (Preface, p. x; p. 170). That absence is itself a finding: for the mod's subject, this volume supplies women's lives, names, courts and law, not gender crossing.

## 1. Women's names (10th–13th c.)

Goitein's main claim (pp. 314–319): women's names were "living words", and mothers chose them. About 70% of them speak of **ruling, overcoming and victory**, mostly compounds of *Sitt*, "mistress, lady who rules" (p. 316). Biblical and Hebrew names are **completely absent among the Jewish women of Egypt**. When they do appear, the family can be shown to come from "Palestine, Tunisia or other North African country, Spain, Byzantium, or Western Europe, where biblical names were occasionally given to females" (p. 315). That is the one place where he says Maghribi naming differed. He gives no list of biblical names for Maghribi women.

**What the names show.**
- **Names of rule.** In the Maghrib as in Egypt, the *Sitt*/*Mawlat* pattern is the norm (Mawlat, Sittuna, and Sitt al-Ahl on Qayrawan tombs). A faith that renames its daughters can take its "name of the street" straight from this stock. Goitein reads these names as mothers' wishes for their daughters' strength (pp. 316–317).
- **Names shared by both sexes.** Many names were "common to males and females": Baraka, Bushr, Hiba, Salama, Sa'ada, Baqa, Khalifa, Zayn (p. 318). Goitein takes them to show "the proud mother's contention that she did not care whether she had given birth to a boy or a girl" (p. 318). Some girls bore masculine forms: Muwanis, "Intimate Friend" (p. 317); Turayk, "Little Turk"; Khuzayr, "Little Khazar" (p. 318). He also lists Banuqa, "the boy-like daughter of the caliph Ma'mun", as a rare literary name (p. 315). None of this is gender crossing. It does show that a woman's name in this world was not always marked as female.
- **Scriptural names.** Esther or Dinah suit a Maghribi daughter better than an Egyptian one (p. 315). Krakowski independently notes that "Ester" is "unusual for Geniza women" (her ch. 5, n. 58; see `jud_maghreb_krakowski_coming_of_age.md`).
- **Spelling.** The forms above are Goitein's transliterations with the diacritics removed. Run `tools/check_glyphs.py` on any loc that uses them.

## 2. The argument in brief

- **"Privacy, but no purdah."** Men believed "the princess' place is in the innermost corner of the house" (p. 312). In practice, Jewish women in Fustat went to court, to notaries, to the synagogue, to the bathhouse, to the bazaar and to weddings and mournings. They received charity in mixed queues, held and traded real estate, and travelled. The houses of Fustat "had no women's quarters" (p. 359). [E]
- **A separate world of women.** Women "constantly flocked together" in the gallery, the bathhouse and the bazaar, and at visits (p. 359). But almost nothing survives that was written by women, or for them: not one poem, and no letter certainly in a woman's own hand (pp. 355, 358). [E]
- **Regional difference.** Women of Iraq and Iran lived under stricter seclusion. Rich émigrés' daughters in Tunisia and elsewhere were "more independent", and Maghribi visitors found Egyptian freedom shocking (p. 323). So for the Maghrib these Egyptian freedoms are an **upper bound**, not a description.

## 3. What matters for the design

### 3.1 Marriage and its conditions

- **Consent, and the Qayrawan custom.** A mature girl could not be married without her consent before two witnesses (p. 72). "According to Qayrawanese legal practice, dislike of the groom by the bride was a sufficient reason" to dissolve a betrothal (p. 72). [M]
- **A Qayrawan case** (p. 212). A father wanted out of his daughter's betrothal because the groom's family had lost money trading with the Rum. He pleaded her dislike of the groom. The Jewish judge refused, and the father went to the **Muslim authorities**, who "as usual" sent the case back to a high-ranking Jewish jurisconsult. "Old women were sent to the girl." When they found her "adamant in her refusal", the jurisconsult ordered the betrothal dissolved. [M] Krakowski treats the same query (ch. 6) and reads the girl's refusal as a legal performance staged by her father.
- **Betrothal in the synagogue.** A query from Qayrawan to Saadya Gaon (d. 942) describes the local custom: the father receives the betrothal gift for his daughter in the synagogue before the congregation, and the groom says the formula in the house of study before the scholars (p. 88). [M]
- **Weddings on Purim** were held "like carnival" by poorer couples and in second marriages (p. 115). An engagement draft of c. 1100 ends "Marriage on Purim, if God wills" (p. 143). [E]
- **No second wife, no slave girl without her consent** (pp. 147–150). Jewish law in this period forbade a master sex with his own slave girl. Marriage agreements still regularly added that the husband would not keep or buy a maidservant his wife disliked, and some have that clause without the clause against polygyny. In the oldest version the slave-girl clause comes first, which Goitein reads as the wife's greater fear (p. 147). The fixed "well-known conditions" appear only from the early 12th c., probably on the initiative of the Nagid Mevorakh b. Saadya (c. 1095–1112) (p. 148). The wife chose whether to stay on if he married again; if not, he paid her deferred marriage gift and freed her (p. 149). [E]
- **The "Qayrawanese condition".** The Islamic clause that let a first wife rule over or divorce a second wife was known as the **Qayrawanese condition** (p. 149). This is **Muslim** law, attested in 9th-c. Egyptian papyri and in Cordovan responsa. Goitein reasons that "the countries between Spain and Egypt followed a similar course" (p. 149). [M, Muslim]
- **The wife's word as two witnesses.** Her trustworthiness, "like that of two witnesses", was the foremost condition in countless contracts (pp. 144–146). [E]
- **Movement.** Clauses limiting a wife's movement are "rare" and arise from special circumstances, for example "She will leave the house only with his permission", from the 1130s (pp. 154–155). Maimonides grants a wife's right to visit her father, mournings and feasts, but would let her out "once or twice during a month" (p. 153). [E]

### 3.2 Divorce and leaving

- **Only the husband could end a marriage in Jewish law**, though the courts could press him (p. 264). In practice "the female partner was the initiator" in many, perhaps most, detailed cases, usually by giving up what was owed her (p. 265). [E]
- **Strong women in divorce.** Sitt al-Kull of Alexandria made her silk-weaver husband declare bankruptcy and vow never to remarry her (p. 266). Muna faced the bier and the ram's horns brought in to frighten her off the divorcee's oath, and said only "I swear" (p. 266). [E]
- **Divorce before a Muslim judge.** A woman of Sahrajt was **banned** because she had "divorced her husband" before a Muslim judge "according to her own wish and against his will" (pp. 265, 339). A Muslim book of responsa tells of a woman who tried to flee her village for **Qayrawan** to seek justice against her husband (p. 339). [E; M, Muslim]
- **Maghribi wives who would not move.** Ibn Sabra of al-Mahdiyya divorced his Tunisian wife by proxy while in Egypt, since she would not follow him; her family "was not angry" (p. 176). A Tunisian who had married in Constantinople had to divorce her on leaving Byzantium (p. 177). In a letter from Tunisia, a wife's brothers disapprove of her travelling to join her husband in Egypt (p. 202). [M]
- **Remarriage was normal.** About 45% of marrying women were marrying for a second time (p. 63). [E]

### 3.3 Property

- Women of all classes bought, sold, leased, inherited and gave **real estate** (pp. 326–329). [E]
- **Surura of Qayrawan** was paid with the upper story of her husband's Qayrawan mansion, worth 295 dinars, and then sold it to a man from Gabes (p. 203). **A mother in Qayrawan** divided a valuable upper story equally between a son and a daughter (p. 280). [M] (Not for a ruling-faith mechanic built on money; see guardrails.)
- **Daughters' shares.** Down to the early 13th c., daughters who were sole heirs, or who were given equal shares with sons, appear "in numerous documents", contrary to the Islamic half-share (p. 280). [E, with a Maghribi example]

### 3.4 Learning and prayer

- **Women's education** was the "ultimate source" of the gap between the sexes. "As a rule, the women were uneducated" (pp. 355–356). Women of all classes knew the basic Hebrew prayers (p. 355). Upper-class girls could get instruction. Samuel al-Maghribi (12th c.) says his mother and her two sisters could read and write Hebrew and Arabic (p. 355). (General knowledge, *unverified* here: Samuel al-Maghribi was born in Baghdad to a father from the Maghrib, and his mother was from Basra, so this is not Maghribi evidence.)
- **The schoolmistress** (pp. 344–346). A woman married at nine learned the Bible from her husband and taught it with her brother. Later she ran the school with her son "so that he could talk to the fathers … while she would take care of the mothers" (p. 345). The husband objected. Maimonides held that a husband may forbid her to teach, but that as a divorcee "she would have disposition over herself, she could teach what she liked and do what she liked" (p. 346). [E, late 12th c.]
- **The *mu'allima***, a female Bible teacher, usually a relative of the schoolmaster (p. 355). "The Son of the Schoolmistress" carried the ransom for the captives of Jerusalem in 1099 (p. 356). [E]
- **A pietist mother's letter.** Dying, she asks her sister to give her baby daughter formal instruction (*ta'lim*), to keep her from appearing in public, and not to part her from her old Sudanese nurse (pp. 353–354). [E]
- **Nazirite women** vowed abstinence from wine and perhaps meat. Some gave houses and donations to the community (pp. 352–353). [E]
- **The synagogue.** It was the first place a respectable woman could go. From the women's gallery she came down "through the 'secret door'" into the courtyard (p. 343). Female attendance was regular, and women donated Torah scrolls, oil, books and houses (p. 359). "The husband was the priest in the house" (p. 359). [E]
- **Al-Wuhsha and the Day of Atonement** (pp. 346–352). Karima, called al-Wuhsha ("The Broker"), was a divorcee and businesswoman of Fustat c. 1095–1105 who bore a son outside marriage. On the Fast of Atonement she went to the synagogue of the Iraqians, and the Nasi "expelled her". Goitein comments that this "seemingly was against the law, since even excommunicated persons are permitted to join the community on that day of forgiveness" (p. 351). The witnesses to her affair "do not feel induced to ostracize her" (p. 351). In her will she left that same synagogue the same gift as the main one (pp. 349, 351), and she was the only woman among over a hundred lists of contributors to a public appeal (p. 352). [E]
- **Esther as the model wife.** Goitein argues that women knew and heeded the Book of Esther. Esther is "outwardly pliable and accommodating, but strong and resolute in mind": "A woman must be an Esther if she wants to survive" (pp. 170–171). [E] This is the volume's only link between women and the Purim story.

### 3.5 Slavery and concubinage

- Christians and Jews "did not have the legal outlet of concubinage with slave girls" that Muslims had (p. 170). The fear of a husband's slave girl shaped the marriage contract (§3.1). [E]
- Women bought and sold slave girls, often minors raised for household service. Male domestic slaves were not usual in Jewish houses (pp. 330–331). Mubaraka of Gabes sold her maidservant for 20½ dinars (p. 183). [E; M→E]
- Freedwomen appear as wives and divorcees, among them the freedwoman Dhahab ("Gold") (p. 262) and a proselyte freedwoman (p. 358). [E]
- The Sudanese mother of the caliph al-Mustansir had first been a slave girl of the Jew Abu Sa'd al-Tustari, who later became her "vizier" (p. 357). [E]

### 3.6 Women before courts, Jewish and Muslim

- **Women were "as active in court as men"**, except teenage girls, "pearls still hidden in their shells" (p. 336). A judge "takes cognizance" of a woman before she acts, a phrase never used of men. Goitein thinks it was often a formality (pp. 313–314). [E]
- **Proxies.** Women used proxies for the same reasons men did. They used them especially to sue a husband or a brother (pp. 335–336). Mourners, noble women and the aged were visited at home by two witnesses (pp. 333, 336). [E]
- **Muslim courts.** "'I went to the qadi,' writes a widow from Alexandria quite casually" (p. 332). Jewish women in Fustat were "not required to veil their faces" (p. 332). [E]

### 3.7 Dress and the body

- **Qayrawanese attire.** A traveller from Qayrawan, landed at Alexandria, realised he had forgotten what his wife wanted: "a Qayrawanese robe, cap, veil, and mantle, that is, a complete attire". He begged his correspondents to send it with the first caravan, calling it "my most urgent request" (p. 167). [M] This is the one Maghribi item of women's dress in the volume.
- **Adornment in marriage.** A wife was expected to comb her hair, wear kohl and perfume herself (p. 167). A husband wrote to his wife: "Sell the wine and buy a silver bracelet … and put the bracelet on your wrist" (p. 167). [E]
- "To uncover their faces" was the phrase for poor women forced to appear in public (p. 324). [E]

### 3.8 Women and Muslim courts of power

- **"The prominence of women in the affairs of Fatimid Egypt and Zirid Tunisia is remarkable"** (p. 357). Al-Mu'izz (r. 1016–1062) was brought up by his aunt al-Sayyida (Mallal), regent until 1023, and "there were other prominent women of the Zirid court". Ibn 'Idhari gives a chapter to the topic (p. 357). [M, Muslim]
- **Jews reached rulers through the court's women.** The mother of the Nagid and court physician Mevorakh is praised for having "placated the ire of the King", probably through the women of the court (p. 357). In the Ayyubid period, women were asked to help through their relations with the wives of the Sultan's servants (p. 358). [E]

## 5. Guardrails and cautions

- **Money as the vehicle.** Women's moneylending is well documented: a woman lends at fixed interest in 1156 (p. 330), Jayyida's disguised loan (p. 329), and al-Wuhsha's collateral loans (p. 349). None of it should carry content (Creditor guardrail). Al-Wuhsha's story is usable only for the synagogue and the ban, not for her business.
- **Slavery.** Use it only as context. Do not model the purchase or sale of people. A monogamy-clause mechanic can speak of "no other wife" without slave girls.
- **Child marriage.** The woman married at nine (p. 344) and the levirate cases are not material for events.
- **The Nasi's expulsion of al-Wuhsha.** A Jewish leader shaming a woman would breach the rule that Jewish characters are never the wrongdoers in harassment events. If used, frame it as a judgment about law, as Goitein does (he defends the Nasi's motive, p. 351), and give the community's other voices their say.
