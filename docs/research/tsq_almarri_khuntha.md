# Almarri, "You Have Made Her a Man among Men" (2016): the *khuntha* in Fatimid law, design reading

**Citation (checked against the article text).** Saqer A. Almarri, "'You Have Made Her a Man among Men': Translating the Khuntha's Anatomy in Fatimid Jurisprudence," *TSQ: Transgender Studies Quarterly* 3, nos. 3–4 (November 2016): 578–586. DOI 10.1215/23289252-3545251. Duke University Press.
Read in full from a text extraction kept outside the repo: essay (pp. 578–584), references (pp. 584–585) and the appendix translation (pp. 585–586). Page numbers are the journal's.

**Primary text.** Al-Qadi al-Nu'man (d. Cairo 974), *Da'a'im al-Islam* (*The Pillars of Islam*), commissioned by the caliph al-Mu'izz c. 960. Vol. 2, "Kitab al-Fara'id" ("The Book of Inheritance"), section 7, paragraphs 1377–1380 in Fyzee's edition (Cairo: Dar al-Ma'arif, 1961–63). Almarri translates all four paragraphs (pp. 585–586). It is the law code of the Fatimid state, and Tayyibi Isma'ili communities still use it (p. 579).

**Scope warning.** Like Sanders, this is about the *khuntha*: a person whose genitals the jurists read as both male and female. It is about intersex people, not trans women. Almarri keeps the Arabic word on purpose. He avoids "hermaphrodite" (Greek myth) and "intersex" (modern medicine and the intersex rights movement) (p. 580). The mod must not use the *khuntha* as a stand-in for a daughter.

**Relation to existing notes.**
- `hist_sanders_khuntha.md` is the main companion. Sanders works mostly from eleventh-century Hanafi law (al-Sarakhsi). Almarri gives the **Isma'ili Shi'i** code, written a century earlier, about 960. He cites Sanders for the frame: a Galenic body inside a binary law (p. 581).
- Almarri adds three things Sanders lacks. The rib test is **in the code**, attributed to Ali. The fallback inheritance rule is **half and half**, not the lower share. And there is a full narrative case, translated whole.
- `hist_rowson_mukhannathun.md` and `isl_rowson_*.md` cover the *mukhannathun*. Almarri does not mention them.
- `byz_king_one_sex_body.md` tests Laqueur's one-sex model. Almarri uses Laqueur without question (p. 581). Read his Galen paragraph with King's caution.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **A gap in the field.** Islamic studies has work on gender and on homosexuality, but almost none on the *khuntha* (pp. 578–579).
- **Jurists as recorders.** The case stories may not be historical. Almarri, after Musallam, says they still show what jurists thought. They do not show how far the rulings "were applied in the lives of people" (p. 580).
- **Why it sits under inheritance.** Shares are set by sex: a brother takes twice a sister's share (Qur'an 4:11). So the law needs to fix a sex (p. 580).
- **The frame.** The body model is Galenic (one sex, sorted by heat). But the law is binary, and the boundary "cannot be 'crossed legitimately by an act of will'" (Sanders, quoted p. 581). The jurists assume "every person has a 'true' sex that must be determined and assigned" (p. 581).
- **Judging as translating.** Almarri borrows Vicente Rafael's idea of translation. The jurist takes an unfamiliar body and renders it in a familiar category. He still keeps its strangeness in view (pp. 581–582).
- **No surgery.** The tests use "a jurist's gaze", not the knife (p. 582).
- **The order of tests (pp. 582–583).**
  1. Which organ is present.
  2. Which orifice passes urine.
  3. If both, which flows **first**. (Al-Tusi, another Shi'i jurist, used the last.)
  4. If both start at once, count the ribs. Exegesis read Eve as made from Adam's rib, so men have one rib fewer.
- **The conclusion.** The jurists cared about "true" sex, and only they could let a person cross the line (p. 583). That logic may have stopped them asking why sex mattered in law at all (p. 583). Muslims today who rethink the inheritance will meet these methods (pp. 583–584).

## 2. The case of Shuraih and Ali (para. 1377, pp. 585–586)

This is the most usable thing in the article. In summary:

- A person comes to the judge Shuraih (served c. 634–661) and asks him to clear the court. The text calls the petitioner a woman. She says she has both organs and that urine flows from both at once.
- She is married to her paternal cousin. He gave her a slave-girl, and the slave-girl bore her child. She asks to be separated from her husband if she is in fact a man.
- Shuraih takes the case to the caliph Ali. Ali questions the husband, who confirms it all. Ali says the husband is "foolishly braver than a man who dares to castrate a lion."
- Two women count the ribs in a house: twelve on the right, eleven on the left.
- Ali calls a barber and orders "Cut this man's hair." He re-wraps the robe the way men wear it. He tells the petitioner to marry "whomever you desire among the women."
- The husband protests: "you have made her a man among men, wherefrom did you bring such a judgment?" Ali answers from Adam and Eve's rib.

Almarri reads this as a caliph's licence "to transition and live as a man in society" (p. 583). That is his word, and it fits poorly. The petitioner did not ask to live as a man. She asked the court to rule. The court then imposed the ruling and dissolved her marriage. The text does not say how she lived afterwards.

**The other paragraphs.**
- **1378.** If urine flows from both at once, the *khuntha* takes "half a man's inheritance and half a woman's inheritance" (p. 586). The gloss says the rib case was decided differently because of the child born of the petitioner.
- **1379.** Five brothers from Syria bring a sibling who claims a man's share. They say Mu'awiya could not decide. Ali has the sibling drink, and urine flows from the penis, so he inherits as a man (p. 586).
- **1380** repeats the first-flow rule.

## 3. What is usable for the mod

**Period fit.** The code dates to c. 960 and the Fatimid state to 909–1171. On the 867 map there are no Fatimids yet. But the rulings are given as traditions from Ali through Ja'far al-Sadiq (d. 765). So **any Shi'i jurist** could cite "Ali's judgement on the ribs" from the start. "Al-Qadi al-Nu'man" and "the *Pillars*" should only be named after c. 960. CK3 has a vanilla Isma'ili faith, and Twelver Shi'ism is on the map.

What the article gives the design:
1. **A Shi'i legal voice**, separate from Sanders's Hanafi one. Sanders calls the rib count a fringe test (p. 78). In the Isma'ili code it is Ali's own ruling. A Shi'i qadi would cite it with confidence.
2. **A creation-order argument for sex.** Ali judges "From my ancestor Adam", because Eve was made from his rib (p. 586). This is the same Genesis rib that Christian and Jewish hostile voices can cite. Eve-from-Adam is a common ground for all three Abrahamic neighbours.
3. **Sex judged by begetting.** The petitioner is declared a man after she fathers a child (1377, 1378). Fertility decides the sex, and the marriage falls with it. This is Bhatt's "regendering" in a period legal form: the regime sorts people by their part in reproduction (`book_bhatt_third_sex.md`, degendering and regendering).
4. **Re-dressing as a legal act.** Haircut by a barber, the robe re-wrapped "the way men wrap it", a new marriage rule (p. 586). This is the court's own ritual of gender: re-clothing and re-grooming. It is the mirror of the Sisters' rite of renaming and re-clothing (beliefs A2, H28). Theirs is chosen. The court's is imposed.
5. **Women do the looking.** Two women count the ribs, away from the court (p. 586). This matches Sanders's slave-girl examiner (Sanders row 8): a woman's look settles the case, but a man rules on it.
6. **The petitioner came on her own.** She asks for a closed court and speaks for herself (p. 585). The court takes her account and checks it with her husband.
7. **Divorce on regendering.** Once she is declared a man, the marriage ends. This confirms Sanders row 6: an Islamic court will call a re-embodied daughter's marriage void.

## 5. Cautions

- **Intersex is not trans.** The petitioner is a *khuntha*, not a trans woman, and not a daughter. Do not give her story to any of the six faiths as a foremother. Use it only to show how the neighbours' law sorts bodies.
- **Pronouns.** The text calls the petitioner "she" and "a woman" while she brings her case. It calls her "this man" after the ruling (p. 586). We do not know how she saw herself. Use "the petitioner", or follow the text and mark the change as the court's.
- **"Transition" is Almarri's word.** He calls the ruling a licence "to transition" (p. 583). It was an imposed reassignment. The mod should not cite it as a period acceptance of changing sex.
- **Sacred figures.** Ali is a holy figure for Shi'a, and Shuraih is a revered early judge. In-game, a jurist may cite the tradition. Never invent new words for Ali, and never mock the ruling in the narrator's voice. The polemic in row 3 is aimed at the *method* (counting ribs), and it belongs to the faiths, not the narrator.
- **Sectarian edge.** Para. 1379 mocks Mu'awiya, the Sunni-remembered caliph, as unable to judge. Do not reuse that. It would put Sunni-Shi'i polemic in the mod's mouth.
- **Laqueur.** Almarri's Galen paragraph follows Laqueur's one-sex model (p. 581). King (`byz_king_one_sex_body.md`) disputes it. Cite Sanders for the Islamic medicine, not Almarri.
- **Historicity.** Almarri says the stories show juristic ideas, not practice (p. 580). Loc should say "the jurists hold", not "it was done".
- **Glyphs.** Keep loc spellings plain: "Ali", "Shuraih", "al-Numan", "qadi". Run `tools/check_glyphs.py` on any new line.
