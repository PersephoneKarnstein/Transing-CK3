# Mortel, "Ribāṭs in Mecca during the medieval period" (1998): women's hospices and the deed above the door

Source: Richard T. Mortel (King Saud University, Riyadh), "Ribāṭs in Mecca during the medieval period: a descriptive study based on literary sources", *Bulletin of the School of Oriental and African Studies* 61, no. 1 (1998): 29–50. **Partly verified.** Author, title, affiliation, the © SOAS 1998 line and the page run 29–50 (running heads) are in the extraction (`sources_pending/akhi_ribat/src2.txt`, gitignored). Volume 61, issue 1 is not printed in the extraction. It is from general knowledge, so check it against JSTOR before it goes into the README bibliography. Page numbers are the article's own, read from the running heads.

**What was read.** The whole article (pp. 29–50), with the footnotes. Dates are given as Mortel gives them, Hijri/CE. Quotations are short and verbatim.

**Scope and bias.** The article is a catalogue of the 59 ribāṭs known to have been founded in Mecca before the Ottoman conquest of the Hijaz (923/1517). It rests almost entirely on two 15th-century historians of Mecca, al-Fāsī and Ibn Fahd. Al-Fāsī often copied the foundation inscription over the door, so for many houses we have the founder, date and rules but nothing about daily life (pp. 30–31). None of the buildings survives except the ribāṭ of al-Muwaffaq, which stood until at least 1980–84 (pp. 30, 39). The article is about **Mecca only**. North Africa appears in its first sentence and in footnote 2, and the Maghreb in two houses for Maghribis (al-Sabtiyya, al-Muwaffaq). West Africa, al-Andalus and the Sahel do not appear.

**Relation to other notes.** Read with `isl_negre_ribat_andalus.md`, which covers the armed frontier ribāṭ of the 9th century. Mortel's opening paragraph gives the shift Negre documents: from a frontier fortress to a hospice founded as a pious endowment (p. 29). Also read `jud_maghreb_hirschberg_north_africa.md` (Jewish endowments and community authority under Muslim lords) and `byz_thomas_hero_typika.md` (the Protectress, the model already used for Sisters' Houses).

**Relevance:** **high** for women's hospices: who founded them, who could live in them and on what terms, and how rulers treated them. This is the strongest model in the reading for a Sisters' House under a Muslim lord. The model is attributed, not claimed. **Nil** for gender crossing, eunuchs, armed bands, or the Maghreb and Sahel themselves.

## 1. What a Meccan ribāṭ was

- **From fortress to hospice.** The ribāṭ "appears to have made its initial appearance" on the North African coast in present-day Tunisia in the 2nd/8th century. There it was a frontier fortress garrisoned by pious volunteers for the jihad (p. 29). In the eastern lands it changed "as early as the fourth/tenth century" into a pious foundation set up by a ruler or, more often, a wealthy individual. It gave free lodging to travellers, the poor and, later, Sufis (p. 29). Marçais put the change in the 12th century. Mortel's earliest Meccan house, before 395/1004–5, is already a hospice (p. 29 n. 2).
- **Ribāṭ and khānqāh.** In Mamluk Cairo, Amīn distinguishes the khānqāh (a mosque with a Sufi hospice) from the ribāṭ (a hospice for the poor, Sufi or not). Mortel says this does not hold everywhere: Ramisht's ribāṭ of 529/1135 was for Sufis only, and Mecca had no khānqāh before 1517 (p. 29).
- **Al-Maqrīzī's definition** (15th c.), which Mortel holds good for Mecca. The resident, the *murābiṭ*, cuts his ties with people, worships day and night, and so becomes "a defender of the faith" by prayer. The ribāṭ is "the home of the Sufis and their halting-place" (pp. 29–30). The defender has become a man of prayer. The word keeps the frontier meaning only as metaphor.
- **The deed (*waqfiyya*).** Each house was an endowment (*waqf*). The deed set the term of stay, limited or open-ended, and who was eligible (p. 30). Most of what is known about each house comes from deeds carved in stone over the door (pp. 30–31).
- **Zāwiya = ribāṭ in Mecca.** Al-Fāsī treats Umm Sulaymān's *zāwiya* of 772/1370–71 as a ribāṭ (p. 43).

Also: the ribāṭ of Bint al-Ḥarabī in Ajyad took its name from a woman who lived there (p. 46). Al-Fāsī names several houses after a well-known resident (pp. 31, 35, 36), so this one was probably remembered for her.

**What this shows.**
- Women's houses were a recognised type in Mecca from 1098–99 to the 15th century. They cluster in Ajyad, south of the Haram, where most cheap land lay (pp. 41–42, 49).
- The residents named are **widows**, **women devoted to worship**, **Sufi women**, **women strangers** (not natives of Mecca), and the **poor**. Several deeds restrict by madhhab (Ḥanafī) or by "not a native" (pp. 38, 41–42, 44).
- Women **founded** houses for men as well as for women: the Khātūn (for male Sufis), Ibn al-Sawdāʾ (two sisters), Zumurrud Khātūn (pp. 35–37).
- Royal women abroad endowed houses in Mecca: Zumurrud Khātūn (Baghdad), Farḥat (Yemen), Zaynab (Cairo) (pp. 36, 44, 47).
- **Gaps.** Mortel never says who ran a women's house from inside: no *shaykha*, no elder, no house rule beyond the deed. Supervision is mentioned only through men (al-Baʿdānī for Farḥat, p. 44; Rabīʿ for al-Malik al-Afḍal, p. 38). A Sisters' House's Elder Sister has no counterpart in this source. (Women-headed ribāṭs with a *shaykha* in Mamluk Cairo, such as the Ribāṭ al-Baghdādiyya, are general knowledge, not from Mortel. Verify in al-Maqrīzī's *Khiṭaṭ* before use.)

## 3. Houses closed to women, and other eligibility rules

- **Explicitly closed to women:** Ramisht's house (529/1135), the deed of which "specifically disallowed women from residing in this ribat" (p. 33), and al-Arsūfī's (571/1175–76), where "Women were expressly excluded" (p. 35). Several others are for "male" Sufis or "men": the Khātūn (p. 35), the Ghurid sultan's house (p. 38) and Ibn Ṭāhir's (p. 46).
- **Origin.** Isfahanis for 40 days a year (p. 31); Prophet's descendants from Zarand (p. 32); natives of Akhlāṭ (p. 38); North Africans (al-Muwaffaq, p. 39); ten Iranians, with **Indians explicitly excluded** (al-Abarqūhī, 771/1369–70, p. 43).
- **School of law:** Ḥanafīs (Qaymāz, p. 36; al-Akhlāṭī, p. 38); Shāfiʿīs (Ibn al-Sawdāʾ, p. 37).
- **Marital status:** unmarried (Ibn al-Sawdāʾ, p. 37; al-Muwaffaq, p. 39); "married or unmarried" (al-Tamīmī, p. 40).
- **Numbers:** at most ten residents (the Ghurid sultan, 600/1203–4, p. 38; al-Abarqūhī, p. 43).
- **Term and forfeit.** At most three years, and a resident who travelled beyond the distance for shortening prayer lost his room (al-Arsūfī, p. 35). In Ibn Ṭāhir's house (843/1439) a resident could not be evicted but lost his room if away over six months. Residents of other hospices were barred, and he had to be truly homeless (p. 46). Mortel reads these rules as a response to misuse in the 15th century (pp. 46–47).
- **Conduct.** Piety, probity, renouncing worldly attachments, living "in isolation" (pp. 36, 39, 41).

## 4. Money: endowments and income

- Rents of houses, shops, a warehouse and arable land; a public bath (al-Tamīmī, p. 40); two rooms in the building itself (al-Aṣbahānī, p. 42); Aden waqfs for the "ribāṭ of the Indians" (p. 37); farmland in Wādī Marr and Wādī Nakhla (p. 40).
- **Later donors topped up old houses.** In 614/1218 a Sufi shaykh and a ship's captain each gave half a house to Ramisht's ribāṭ (p. 34). In Ramaḍān 809/1407 the sharif Ḥasan b. ʿAjlān gave farmland to several houses (pp. 38, 39, 42, 44). Merchants gave shops (p. 42).
- **Istibdāl** (substitution): a ruined endowed property could be sold if the money bought another property under the same deed. Qāʾitbāy's agent bought the ruined ribāṭ of al-Sidra this way for 3,000 dinars (882–884/1477–80) and demolished it with two neighbours for the sultan's madrasa and ribāṭ (pp. 47–48).

## 5. Rulers and ribāṭs

- **Who founded them.** Of the 46 founders known, 8 were merchants, 8 amirs or local rulers, and 7 ulama. Only four houses were Mamluk. Most were "acts of personal piety by private individuals", many not natives of Mecca (p. 50).
- **Foreign rulers** endowed houses for their own people: the Ghurid sultan Muḥammad b. Sām (600/1203–4, ten male Sufis, p. 38); Shāh Shujāʿ of Shiraz through his protégé (771/1369–70, ten Iranians, p. 43); Farḥat of Yemen (806/1403–4, p. 44).
- **Supervision (*naẓar*).** Al-Aṣbahānī (749/1348): the founder, then his offspring; if the line died out, the ruler of Mecca (p. 42). Ibn Baʿaljad (787/1385–86): the founder, his male progeny, then the chief Shāfiʿī qadi (p. 44). The qadi could also hand a ruin to a new restorer (al-Aṣbahānī's house, used as a stable by 826/1422–23, p. 43).
- **Seizure in bad times.** The 8th/14th century was a time of sharifian civil strife and a "breakdown … of religious and civic institutions, including charitable endowments" (p. 41). The amir ʿUtayfa (r. 1319–1336/37) made Zumurrud Khātūn's ribāṭ his house (p. 36). His brother Rumaytha did the same with al-Sharābī's, as Ibn Baṭṭūṭa saw in 1325–26 (p. 41). Only seven houses were founded in that century (p. 49).
- **Restoration under a stronger lord.** After Mamluk control in 826/1423, the Mamluk supervisor of the Haram rebuilt Ramisht's house (855/1451) and al-Sharābī's (856/1452) (pp. 34, 41). After the fire of 802/1400 the sharif Ḥasan b. ʿAjlān paid 200 mithqāls to rebuild Ramisht's (p. 34).
- **A wife's foundation depends on her husband.** The *khawand* Zaynab's house stopped when the sultan Īnāl was deposed (865/1461, p. 47).

## 6. Women, eunuchs, gender crossing: findings and absences

- **Gender crossing: none.** The deeds sort residents by sex as a matter of course ("male Sufis", "women", "men"). No case tests the line, and nothing in the article says what a house did with anyone whose sex was in doubt.
- **Eunuchs: none named.** The *qāʾid* is glossed as a slave or freedman of the sharifs (p. 37 n. 74). The *qāʾid* Shukr, a freedman, was the sharif's vizier (p. 47). Neither is said to be a eunuch. The *qahramāna* founder of al-Fuqqāʿiyya was a woman steward of the caliph's household (p. 32).
- **Women as learned founders.** Umm al-Ḥusayn's *ijāzas* (p. 43) are the article's one case of a learned woman, and Umm Sulaymān "the Sufi" its one named woman devotee (p. 43).
- **Design consequence.** A Sisters' House under a Muslim lord can borrow the **form** of the women's ribāṭ: a deed over the door, residents defined as widows, devotees or strangers, a royal woman's endowment, and a man supervising from outside. A daughter's place in it rests on the mod's premise that she is a woman. Nothing here supports or denies that a medieval house would have taken her. Any lord's question about it ("is she one of the women of the deed?") is the mod's invention and should be written as a question, not a precedent.

## 8. Design proposals: Sisters' Houses under Muslim lords

Existing mod pieces checked in `common/scripted_effects/tm_house_effects.txt`: the House is a set of county variables (`tm_house`, `tm_house_strain`, `tm_elder_sister`, `tm_house_tolerated_by`, `tm_house_protectress`). The yearly effect asks a lord of another faith to tolerate or close the House (`tm_house_yearly_effect`). `tm_close_house_effect` sends the sisters on (`tm_send_on_effect`) and never deletes them. The Protectress passes down the founder's female line (`tm_house_name_protectress_effect`). Internals beyond the comments were not read; read them before building.

No proposal claims that any medieval ribāṭ took in a trans woman, a Jewish woman or a woman of a West African religion. The ribāṭ is a Muslim institution. These are analogies, and the loc should say so or put the comparison in a character's mouth.

## 9. Risks and cautions

- **Not a precedent for trans women.** The sources sort by sex without comment. Do not write "medieval Islam had houses for women like her".
- **"Indians explicitly excluded"** (p. 43) and origin rules: reproduce an exclusion only as a deed's clause in a character's world, never as the narrator's judgement of a people.
- **Mecca is closed to non-Muslims.** No non-Muslim character should found or enter a Meccan house. Proposal 7 must target other cities.
- **Endowment money and Ha-Nehpakhot.** Keep any Jewish character's gift as alms or a pious foundation, never a loan, rent-farming or interest (standing guardrail).
- **The volume number** (61:1) comes from general knowledge, not from the extraction (see source line).
