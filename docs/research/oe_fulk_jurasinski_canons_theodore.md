# Fulk & Jurasinski, *The Old English Canons of Theodore* (2012): design reading for the Bæddel faith

**Citation.** R. D. Fulk and Stefan Jurasinski, eds., *The Old English Canons of Theodore*. Early English Text Society, Supplementary Series 25. Oxford: Oxford University Press for the Early English Text Society, 2012. ISBN 978-0-19-966818-2.

**Verified** from the title page ("Edited by R. D. Fulk and Stefan Jurasinski", "Published for the Early English Text Society by the Oxford University Press, 2012", "SS. 25") and the copyright page (© Early English Text Society 2012; ISBN as above; typeset by Anne Joshua, printed by TJ International). **One correction to the brief:** the volume is an edition, not a translation. It prints the Old English (Texts A, B, C and four appendices) with apparatus, Latin sources in the Commentary, and a Glossary, but no Modern English translation. The English renderings below are therefore mine, made from the edited text and checked against the editors' commentary and glossary. Base manuscripts: Y (Oxford, Bodleian Library, Laud Misc. 482, mid-11th c.) for Texts A and C; O (Cambridge, Corpus Christi College 190) for Text B; variants from Bx (Brussels, Bibliothèque Royale 8558-63, first half of the 11th c.).

**What was read.** Introduction §III (Date and Authorship, pp. xxxvi-xlii) and §V (Editorial Procedure, p. lx); Text A in full (pp. 1-14); Text B 1-26 (pp. 15-16); Commentary on A 10, 15, 28-30, 40-1, 52-64, 124-8 and B 4-13 (pp. 27, 33-5, 38-9, 62-3, 65-71); Glossary (pp. 85-101) entries *bædling*, *wæpnedman*, *sodomisc*, *forlegen*, *wiccung*, *galdorsang*, *galdorcræft*, *swefenracu*, *drycræft*, *hæðen*, *deofolgyld*. The OCR text was searched for every canon on clothing, castration, eunuchs and women with women. Extracted text is in the scratchpad only.

**Date.** The translation falls somewhere between 690 (Theodore's death; its Latin source, the *Paenitentiale Theodori*, is post-690 and pre-755) and about 1025, the date of the earliest manuscript. The editors argue that "there are probabilistic reasons not to dismiss the possibility of early composition" (p. xl), against Frantzen's 10th-century dating. For a game that starts in 867, the Latin canons are certainly current and the Old English ones may be.

## 1. The provisions

Canon numbers are the edition's (A = Text A, B = Text B). Penances are fasts unless stated.

### The *bædling*
- **B 6** (p. 15): "Se þe mid bædlinge hæme, oþþe mid oþrum wæpnedmen, oþþe mid nytene, fæste tyn winter." *He who lies with a bædling, or with another weaponed-man [male], or with a beast, fast ten winters.* The Latin (PT I, ii, 2) has only "masculus". The translator added the *bædling* and set him beside "another male" (Commentary, p. 66).
- **A 54** (p. 9) and **B 8** (p. 15): "Gyf bædling mid bædlinge hæme, tyn winter fæste." *If a bædling lies with a bædling, fast ten winters.* Here *bædling* renders plain Latin "masculus cum masculo" (PT I, ii, 5; Commentary, p. 68). So the word is not only the partner of a man. In this canon the *bædling* is the one penanced.
- **B 8** goes on: "hi beoð hnesclice swa forlegene" (p. 15), *they are, effeminately, like an adulteress.* The translator read PT's tariff "molles sicut adultera" as a description of the men (p. 69). *Forlegene* is a feminine singular form, so the canon files them beside the adulterous woman. The next clause sets one year "eallswa wif", *just like a woman*.
- **The editors on the word** (pp. 66-7). The translator "seems to have thought it natural that a 'masculus' with whom a male might fornicate would be a bædling, but that this was not the only possibility". The Cleopatra Glossary glosses *effeminati* as "molles vel bædlingas". But the translator "seems not to have regarded 'mollis' and bædling as the same": he renders *mollis* as *hnesclice* ("softly"), never as *bædling*. So the *bædling* "must have some more specific connotations than the general one of sexual passivity". They accept Frantzen's "a man who was known to have sex with other men" (Frantzen 1998: 24) but add that "some more distinctive trait" was probably needed.
- **What that trait might be** (p. 67). The editors point to the first wonder in the *Liber monstrorum* (Aldhelm's circle, 7th-8th c.): a person "utriusque sexus" who looked more male than female in face and chest and was taken for a man, "sed muliebra opera dilexit" (*but loved women's work*).
- **Etymology** (p. 67): "the likeliest etymology of the term ... seems to be a derivative of bæddel 'hermaphrodite'. See Fulk 2004a." They do not mention Frantzen's alternative *ge-bæd*, "oppressed one".
- **Glossary** (p. 87): "bædling m. homosexual (?): A 54, B 8 (see Commentary); -e ds. A 54, B 6, 8." The question mark is the editors'.

### Other acts between men
- **B 7** (p. 15): bestiality fifteen winters, "and sodomisce seofon gear". *Sodomisc* is glossed "like a resident of Sodom" (Glossary, p. 98). The editors take the Sodomite to be the insertive partner and *mollis* the receptive one. They read the translator's reordering as purposeful: the series runs from insertive to receptive offenders (p. 69).
- **A 55** (p. 9): "Se ðe þis werlice man deð, feower gear fæste." *He who does this manly sin, four years.* This renders the Latin "virile scelus" (p. 69). The editors note that the term *sodomia* was not yet in use (p. 68).
- **A 60-2, A 63** (pp. 9-10): boys together are to be beaten; semen in the mouth is "þæt wyrreste" (*the worst*); brothers together fifteen winters without meat. All are strict. The editors note that the translated penances are "at times even stricter" than Theodore's own (p. xl).

### Women with women
- **B 10** (p. 15): "Gif wif mid wife hæmeð, þreo gear bete." *If a woman lies with a woman, atone three years.* This is from PT I, ii, 12 (p. 71).
- **A 41** (p. 7): a woman alone with herself, three years. The editors observe (p. 38) that "autoerotic acts of women are to be regarded as more heinous than those of men ... whereas the opposite is the case in regard to homoerotic acts". The tariff is three years for women and ten winters for men or *bædlingas*.

### Cross-dressing and castration
- **Neither appears anywhere in the Old English Canons.** No canon concerns clothing, eunuchs or gelding. The only nearby gloss is a note on "nunquam mutat" (A 40, B 13, the penance for incest with a mother). Payer suggested it means changing clothes. The editors reject that reading: the phrase means the fast is never relaxed except on Sundays (p. 71). The English penitential tradition, in this text, has nothing to say about dress or the knife.

### Sorcery and heathen practice
- **A 28** (p. 6): "Gif hwylc wif wiccunga bega, and þa deoflican galdorsangas" (*if any woman practises enchantments and the devilish incantations*), she is to stop and fast a year and the three fasts. The Latin canon is aimed at women ("Si mulier", p. 33). The editors cite Ælfric, who ties witchcraft to healing and herbs, "witchcraft (gendered feminine)" (p. 33). The later laws drive witches out of the land (Edward and Guthrum). Alfred's "ne læt þu hi libban" (*do not let them live*) sits in his translation of Mosaic law and "was probably not meant to be applied as written" (p. 34).
- **A 124** (p. 13): sacrifice "deoflum and þam leasestum þingum" (*to devils and the falsest things*), one year. The editors suggest the translator may have had in mind creatures of folk belief that were not quite "demons" (p. 62).
- **A 125-6** (p. 13): a woman who sets her child on a roof or in an oven to cure a fever, seven years. Burning grain where the dead are buried "for lifigendra hælo" (*for the health of the living*), five years. The editors connect the second to the carbonised grain in a Saxon grave at Portway (p. 63).
- **A 127** (p. 13): "se ðe halsunga and galdorcræftas and swefenraca behealdað, þa beoð on hæðenra manna gerime." *Whoever heeds charms, spells and dream-reading are reckoned among heathen people.* The editors comment that the translator drops the Latin tariff because heathens are "persons to whom penitence is irrelevant" (p. 63).
- **A 128** (pp. 13-14): those who lead others into *drycræft* (sorcery) are to be thrown out if they are in a monastery. If they are among the laity, they make full amends.

### *Bæddel* or *bædling*: attestation and meaning

| | *bædling* | *bæddel* |
|---|---|---|
| Where | Canons of Theodore A 54 (twice), B 6, B 8 (twice); Cleopatra Glossary "molles vel bædlingas" for *effeminati* (F&J p. 66) | Two glosses, both in one glossary (Antwerp-London), beside *Hermafroditus*, with *wæpenwifestre* as an alternate (Wade p. 22-3 and n. 68, 74; not discussed in F&J beyond the etymology) |
| Kind of text | Law: a category of person in a penitential that a confessor applied | Gloss: a translation equivalent in a word list |
| Meaning | A male category in sexual law. It is the partner of a man (B 6), and it is also the offender himself (A 54, B 8). F&J: "homosexual (?)", with a further, unknown marker, perhaps "loved women's work" | "Hermaphrodite" |
| Relation | Derivative of *bæddel* (F&J p. 67; Fulk 2004a); *-ling* forms a noun for a person of that kind | The root |

**Verdict.** *Bædling* is the better-attested word, with five tokens in a working legal text plus a second glossary. *Bædling* is also the one that names the people the Bæddel faith is about: male-assigned people the church set beside women. *Bæddel* is rarer and narrower. It is a gloss for the Greek *Hermafroditus*, and it is the root word.

**Reconciliation with Wade** (`medtrans_wade_beast_two_backs.md`):
- **Agrees.** Wade's translation of B 6 and its "weaponed-man" contrast are confirmed. So is the derivation from *bæddel* "hermaphrodite". Wade's "no 'manly' way" is supported by the editors' reading of B 7-8 as insertive and receptive offenders.
- **Corrects Wade row 7 and its §3 line.** "The penance falls on the man" is true of B 6 only. A 54 and B 8 penance the *bædling* directly ("bædling mid bædlinge"). The wry line "The bishop has nothing to say to her at all" is false to the source.
- **Narrows Wade row 1 and the §3 naming line.** Wade reports Frantzen's two derivations as open. Fulk and Jurasinski give only the *bæddel* derivation as likeliest and do not mention *ge-bæd*. Wade herself warns against relying on Frantzen (row 10). "The oppressed one" is therefore a minority etymology. The faith may say it in-world as a folk etymology, but the mod should not present it as the meaning of the word.
- **Glossary event.** Wade's "The Glossary" places *bæddel* beside *Hermafroditus*. The Cleopatra gloss, *effeminati*: "molles vel bædlingas", is the better match for a faith of trans women, and it is the one this edition attests.
- **`norse_reading_list.md` finding 2** is confirmed for the Canons and Cleopatra. The **Harley** glossary attestation it lists is not mentioned in this edition and stays unverified here.

## 4. Naming recommendation: switch to **Bædling**

**Recommendation.** Rename the faith's display strings from "Bæddel" to "Bædling". The faith is "Bædling" and the adherents are "Bædlings". Event prose may use the Old English plural *bædlingas*. Keep "Bæddel" in one place only: a faith-description line naming it as the root word in the glossaries.

**Reasons.**
1. **Attestation.** *Bædling* occurs five times in the Canons, a working law text, and again in the Cleopatra Glossary. *Bæddel* survives as two glosses in one glossary (Wade n. 68). The faith's history claim (H23, "attested OE word") is strongest on *bædling*.
2. **Meaning.** *Bædling* names a class of persons that the church set beside "other men" and assigned "like a woman" penances. That is the faith's subject: male-assigned people placed among women. *Bæddel* glosses *Hermafroditus*. Using it as the name of a trans women's faith lands in the live dispute over whether perisex trans women may reclaim an intersex word (`web_baedelism.md` §§ on the intersex dispute). *Bædling* is also contested, but it is not a gloss for intersex people.
3. **Grammar.** *-ling* makes a noun for a person of a kind. "A Bædling" is a natural adherent noun, and "Bæddel" was being used as one against its form. The root and the derivative can both appear in-world: "*bædlingas*, from *bæddel*".
4. **The source movement.** The 2013-14 Tumblr group's name was "Baeddel". The 2020s revival and the url-holder already say "bædlings" (`web_baedelism.md`). The rename keeps the reference recognisable to people who know it. It also avoids making the faith's name the exact label of a group remembered for its 2014 collapse.
5. **Low cost in CK3.** Change only the values in `localization/english/tm_l_english.yml`: `tm_baeddel`, `tm_baeddel_adj`, `tm_baeddel_adherent`, `tm_baeddel_adherent_plural`, and the rite's `_adj`, `_adherent` and `_adherent_plural`. The keys `tm_baeddel`, `tm_baeddel_rite`, and the `faith:tm_baeddel` scopes in tenets, on-actions and events all stay, so saves are unaffected. Update the comments in `tm_faith_types.txt` and `tm_rite_types.txt`, `faith_design.md` §12 decision 1, and `beliefs.md` H23 and H18. CK3's fonts already render *æ*, since vanilla names use it.

**Alternatives considered.**
- **Keep "Bæddel".** It is familiar, and it keeps the root-word poetry ("the word the glossators wrote beside *Hermafroditus*"). Against it: weaker attestation, the intersex sense, and the exact match with the 2013 label. Acceptable if the user prefers continuity with the baedelism grounding.
- **Split the names:** faith "Bæddel", adherents "Bædlings". This is linguistically neat (root and derivative), but players will read it as two spellings of one word.
- **A non-slur name**, such as "the Spindle-Half" or "the High Seat". This loses the reclamation, which is the point of the faith.
