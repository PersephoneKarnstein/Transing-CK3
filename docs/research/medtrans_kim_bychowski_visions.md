## 1. The argument in brief

- **A Julian of Norwich frame.** The introduction opens by rewriting Julian's hazelnut showing around a hormone pill, and it is built in three parts: bodily (who was in the room), intellectual (terms) and spiritual (the issue's ethos). Smallness and precariousness are treated as "not insufficiencies but critical points of view" (p. 7).
- **The field's own history is a story of backlash.** It covers the MLA 2016 session, transphobic papers at Kalamazoo in 2017, and the New Chaucer Society email thread of 2018, where one scholar wrote that he "of course respect[s] men who want to wear women's summer dresses" (p. 16). Some scholars then left the NCS. The stakes are put plainly: "by saying that trans people have an acknowledged past, trans people can better imagine a future" (p. 10). Laverne Cox is quoted: "we exist and always have" (p. 10).
- **Anachronism is defended.** Neither "medieval" nor "transgender" was a period word, yet both "describe realities that may exist regardless of whether a person whom they describe possessed the terminology" (p. 20). Leelah Alcorn "was transgender before she knew she was transgender" (p. 20).
- **Scholars must be polyglots in period categories.** The field must learn "eunuchs, castrates, monks, saints, nymphs, fairies, sodomites, virgins, mothers, widows, loathly ladies" (p. 21), along with knights and "fabulously performative kings and queens."
- **Four misreadings come from the prefix.** Readers hear "trans" as transportation (moving between two communities, "reinforcing binary perspectives"), as "between" (non-binary only), as transcendence ("beyond gender"), or as transcription (trans identity as "an artificial product of technology", p. 23).
- **Trans women are caught in feminism's quarrel with femininity.** Following Serano, the authors identify three anti-feminine waves, which cast trans women as the "trap of misogyny (1970s), the artifice of fakery (1990s), and the persistent essentialism of gender (2010s)" (p. 25). In each wave, "anti-transgender rhetoric is a method rather than a goal" (p. 25), and trans lives become "blank bodies onto which cisgender fears ... are projected" (p. 25).
- **Trans exclusion is not feminism's oldest form.** "Feminist exclusions of trans lives is not the oldest, original form of the movement" (p. 27). The 1990s medievalists who first wrote about Eleanor Rykener used "outdated language" of transvestism and sodomy, yet they still brought "a premodern trans feminine figure into professional consciousness" (pp. 27–28).
- **The pill is a spirit in the flesh.** Bychowski describes her first estrogen pill as "an external prosthetic ovary" that "recalibrates the whole network" (p. 29), and she reads it through the humoral language of spirits flowing through the body (pp. 29–30).
- **The archive is the state's archive.** "The medieval archive is also the state archive" (p. 37). It was built for "social and resource management and control" (Chen, quoted p. 37). Hartman is quoted: the archive is "a death sentence, a tomb, a display of the violated body" (p. 38).
- **The method is critical fabulation.** Following Hartman, the editors ask readers to "imagine what cannot be verified" (p. 39) and to write "a history written with and against the archive" (p. 40). They suggest reading the issue backwards from Ellison and Hoffman's piece on Sylvia Rivera and Marsha P. Johnson, whom they call the "first transgender medieval scholars" (pp. 39–40).
- **The authors are candid about absences.** The issue contains no work by a trans medievalist of colour, and the introduction says race is "absent in this iteration" (p. 39).

**What the issue contains (pp. 35–36).** In order: McGucken (Anglo-Saxon Psychomachia), Lumbley (Empress Matilda as female king), Nestel (Christina of Markyate), Watt (Silence, read as a trans man), Gutt (pregnant men, Aucassin, Fanuel), Bychowski (Gower's Narcissus), Henningsen (Eleanor Rykener as a trans woman), Ellison & Hoffman (Rivera and Johnson). For the mod's scope (trans women; Byzantine, Norse/Anglo-Saxon and Chinese faiths), **Henningsen's Rykener piece is the most directly useful article in the issue.** It was read in a parallel batch: see `medtrans_henningsen_eleanor.md`. Watt's and Gutt's subjects are trans men and are out of scope except for method.

### Methodological cautions for how the mod presents history
- **Use period anachronism openly.** The mod may say that its characters are women whom no period text would have called that, as long as it does not claim the archive says so (pp. 20, 39–40).
- **Archives record trans women mainly through arrests, ledgers and medical treatises** (Hartman, p. 38). Events built on historical cases should come from the woman's side, not the clerk's.
- **Be honest about race and region gaps.** The issue itself is all-European and admits this (p. 39). The Yutai faith therefore rests on different scholarship (Chiang et al.), and the mod should not borrow this issue's European categories for China.
- **Older scholarship's vocabulary** ("transvestite", "cross-dresser") is a cue to re-read, not to cite as-is (pp. 27–28).
