## 1. Argument

### Shazhou 786–1036: the political frame

- **Tibetan conquest.** Shazhou held out under Yan Chao for ten years. In 786 it "sought alliance and surrendered" on condition that its people "would not be moved elsewhere" (p. 37).
- **Tibetan rule (786–848).**
  - The Tibetans organised Shazhou into occupational districts (*buluo* 部落), "including those of silk manufacturers, soldiers, Buddhist clergy, Daoist believers" (p. 39).
  - Taxes rose, iron tools were confiscated, coinage was abolished, and the Chinese ranked below Tibetans, Tuyuhun and Tongjia (p. 39).
- **Buddhism grew under Tibet.** Shazhou went from "nine monasteries and four nunneries" with 310 clergy to seventeen houses and "several thousand" clergy, out of a population of about 25,000. Because Tibet held it, "Dunhuang remained unaffected by the Huichang persecution" (p. 40).
- **848–851: the revolt.** Zhang Yichao drove out the Tibetan city head. The Guiyijun 歸義軍 was set up in 851 over eleven prefectures. Zhang "sorted out and reorganized monastic property" and created a Hexi saṅgha office (p. 41).
  - "Masses of local monks and nuns participated in Zhang Yichao's revolt", led by the monk Hongbian 洪辯 (p. 72).
  - Zhang kept his Tibetan teacher, the monk Facheng, teaching in Shazhou (p. 72).
- **861.** Liangzhou was taken. In 863 the Tang set up a rival commissioner at Liangzhou, fearing Zhang "would turn into another 'Tibet'" (pp. 41–42).
- **867, the game start** (p. 42). Zhang Yitan, the brother held at court as a hostage, died in Chang'an. "Complying with the order of the court, Zhang Yichao traveled to the court in person. He never returned", and he died there in 872.
  - His nephew Zhang Huaishen took over, but "the Tang court did not officially appoint him".
  - The Uighurs pushed into Ganzhou and Suzhou and attacked Guazhou. In 866 the Xizhou Uighur khaganate was founded, friendly to Shazhou (p. 42).
- **The Zhang family's end.**
  - Huaishen sent envoy after envoy. The title came only in 888, and in 890 he "and his wife with their six sons were assassinated" (p. 43).
  - In 892 Suo Xun seized power as guardian of the heir. In 894 **Lady Zhang 張氏, daughter of Zhang Yichao**, "led her sons against Suo Xun and killed him" (p. 43). The Li sons ruled until the aristocracy restored Zhang Chengfeng in 896.
- **910–914.** Zhang Chengfeng proclaimed himself "Emperor Baiyi 白衣帝" of the Jinshan state. In 911 he was forced to call the Ganzhou khagan "father" (p. 44).
- **The Cao family, 914–1036.**
  - Cao Yijin married a Uighur khagan's daughter. He defeated Ganzhou in 925 and married his daughter to the new khagan. In 931 he was titled "Great Prince", making the Guiyijun "both in name and reality, a regional kingdom" (p. 44).
  - In 934 he married a daughter to King Li Shengtian of Khotan (p. 46).
  - After 939, power under Cao Yuanshen "fell into the hands of Cao Yijin's Uighur wife who became called 'mother of the state'" (p. 46).
  - Cao Yuanzhong (944–974) presided over the last prosperity (p. 46).
  - In 1002 Cao Yanlu was besieged and killed himself (p. 46).
  - "In 1036 the Tanguts invaded Shazhou and with this the Guiyijun period essentially ended" (p. 46).
- **After 1036.** Tangut control was weak at first. The Guiyijun still sent tribute to the Song until 1052. Direct Xixia rule is dated by 1070 (p. 47).
- **The Mongols and after.** Mongol siege 1224; fall 1227 (pp. 47–48). The Yuan moved people out in 1291–92 (p. 48). The Ming left Dunhuang outside the Jiayu Pass, and it became pastureland (pp. 48–49).

### Daoism at Dunhuang

- **Early roots.** Celestial Masters material includes a talisman from the Great Wall and S.6825, the *Laozi* with the *Xiang'er* commentary (pp. 57–58).
- **Tang foundations.** Xuanzong established "the Zijigong 紫極宮, Shenquanguan 神泉觀 and other Daoist temples with the purpose of teaching disciples and copying scriptures" (p. 65).
  - Lecture 12 adds the **Kaiyuanguan 開元觀**: "local Daoist temples such as the Kaiyuanguan and Shenquanguan were established, and a large number of Daoist scriptures were copied" (p. 358).
  - Xuanzong had official Daoist canons sent to the circuits. Dunhuang copies are mostly from his reign, and one *Daodejing* bears the Liangzhou Area Command seal (pp. 364–365).
- **What survives.** The most-copied scriptures are the *Benji jing* 本際經 (119 copies) and the *Shengxuan neijiao jing* (22). There is also the anti-Buddhist *Laozi huahu jing* (pp. 359–361).
- **Under Tibet the Daoist establishment collapsed.** "Although there was an officially recognized group of Daoist followers, Daoist scriptures disappeared from contemporary manuscripts" (p. 72). The 790 district of "Daoist believers" (p. 39) is that group.
- **Under the Guiyijun Rong names no Daoist temple and no Daoist clergy.** Buddhism "developed in the direction of popular religion, while other religions also showed signs of vitality, gradually merging with folk culture" (p. 72). The Zoroastrian cult became a folk festival, and Nestorians appear (pp. 73–75). Rong points to Jiang Boqin's study 「道釋相激：道教在敦煌」 (1998) for the full history of Daoism there (p. 361 n. 32; not read).
- **The Xizhou parallel shows what a working Tang Daoist establishment looked like at the frontier** (pp. 362–364).
  - There were five named *guan* in Turfan under Xuanzong, with abbots (*guanzhu* 觀主), *weiyi* 威儀, *lianshi* 鍊師 and *daoshi* 道士, and "lay priests, i.e. local Daoist leaders".
  - Two economic details matter for the Way:
    - The Zijigong document concerns capital lent at interest: 本錢出舉 (p. 363).
    - The Tangchangguan report counts its **"long-life" (*changsheng* 長生) cattle and sheep** (唐昌觀申當觀長生牛羊數狀, p. 363). These are livestock held as perpetual endowment, the Daoist counterpart of Buddhist *changzhu*.

### Religion and society under the Guiyijun

- **Size of the saṅgha.** "The monastic community and its dependents made up more than one fifth of the population" (p. 301).
- **One controlled community.** The saṅgha was a single body under the chief controller (*du sengtong*), seated at Longxing Monastery. It had offices for donations (*chensi* 儭司), processions, ordination platforms, the library, lamps, buildings, granaries, the **permanent-property granary (*changzhu cangsi* 常住倉司)** and sheep (p. 301).
- **Holdings.** Each monastery registered its goods "in a list of permanent property". Dependents were *sihu* 寺戶 under Tibet and *changzhu baixing* 常住百姓 under the Guiyijun (p. 302).
- **Rong on Hao.** He accepts that "Dunhuang clergy lived outside their monastery, owned family property, paid taxes and were liable for service" (p. 304). He disputes two of Hao's proofs:
  - The flood circular P.4981 may show monks sheltering elsewhere *after* a flood, not living at home normally (p. 304).
  - There is no conclusive evidence for monk soldiers under the Guiyijun (pp. 304–305).
  - These are corrections to `cn_hao_monks_nuns_dunhuang.md` (§2 row 4: "corvée called on the sisters" is fine, "soldiered" is not proven for the Guiyijun).
- **She** (pp. 296–300, following Ning Ke and Meng Xianshi).
  - She were voluntary. Members were equal "as brothers" whatever their outside rank. The Three Officers were elected, and "the assembly was the highest organ of the she" (pp. 297–299).
  - She were formed "by Buddhist clerics, women, people living in the same alley, … in the military … in the same trade". They ranged from two members to more than 90 (p. 299).
  - "Apart from the extremely poor who were excluded from she associations, the ordinary people all had a chance to become members" (p. 299).
- **Local cults.** The Guiyijun government paid for sacrifices to water gods, to the god of Mount Jin'an, to a Zoroastrian deity, and to "a goddess called **Maiden Zhang 張女郎**, which is connected with a ceremony of praying for rain" (p. 306).

### The library cave and its sealing

- **Neither waste nor a hasty rescue.** Stein's "sacred waste" theory and Pelliot's 1035 "rescue from the Tanguts" are both rejected (pp. 109–118).
  - The core was 1,050 neat Chinese bundles plus 80 Tibetan ones, arranged by the *Kaiyuan lu* catalogue (pp. 112–113).
  - The disorder Pelliot saw was Stein's doing (p. 115).
- **It was the Sanjie Monastery 三界寺's library.** This small monastery at Mogao rebuilt its incomplete canon. The monk Daozhen 道真 from 934 "requested old and damaged sūtras from various collections" and repaired them, mixing canonical, apocryphal and Chan texts (pp. 119–123). Damaged items were kept "awaiting conservation rather than disposal" (p. 119).
- **Date and cause.** The latest dated document is 1002 (p. 131). Rong ties the sealing to **the fall of Buddhist Khotan to the Islamic Karakhanids in 1006**: news brought by Khotanese refugee monks "would have been more terrifying than the arrival of the Buddhist Tanguts" (p. 132).
  - Because the danger was not immediate, "the monks deposited the material in the cave in an orderly manner and, after walling up the entrance, painted murals over it to conceal its location" (pp. 132–133).
  - Rong grants that "I would not claim … that the problem is solved" (p. 136).
