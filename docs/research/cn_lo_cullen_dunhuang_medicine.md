# Lo & Cullen (eds.), *Medieval Chinese Medicine: The Dunhuang Medical Manuscripts* (2005): design reading

**Citation (verified from the copyright page of the downloaded epub).** Vivienne Lo and Christopher Cullen (eds.), *Medieval Chinese Medicine: The Dunhuang Medical Manuscripts*. Needham Research Institute Series. London: RoutledgeCurzon, 2005 (the copy read is the Routledge 2014 reissue). ISBN-13 978-0-415-34295-7 (hbk), 978-0-415-54662-1 (pbk). DOI 10.4324/9780203482247. Page numbers are the print pages carried in the epub.

**Read in full:** Foreword by Susan Whitfield (pp. xv–xix, on the cave and its sealing); Part III introduction by Vivienne Lo (pp. 207–25); ch. 10, Sumiyo Umekawa, "Tiandi yinyang jiaohuan dalefu and the art of the bedchamber" (pp. 252–77); ch. 11, Sakade Yoshinobu, "Daoism and the Dunhuang regimen texts" (pp. 278–90). **Read in part:** ch. 6, Donald Harper, iatromancy (pp. 134–64: method, demon talismans, *renshen*); ch. 7, Liu Lexian, love charms (opening and the P. 2610 text, pp. 165–67); ch. 8, Catherine Despeux, P. 3390 (the face-colour passage, pp. 179–80); ch. 9, Vivienne Lo, moxibustion charts (the chart table, p. 245 ff.); Appendix 2, Wang Shumin's abstracts (all entries on women, childbirth, *yangsheng* and grain avoidance).

**There is no chapter on gender, women's medicine or the embryo.** The brief hoped for one. The volume has no gynaecology chapter, and the abstracts list only scattered women's material (see §1, "Women's medicine"). What it does give is the Dunhuang regimen texts, a Dunhuang way of reading a pregnant woman's face, and the cave's own history, which together make good seat flavour for the gate.

## 1. What the book says

- **The cave itself.** The library cave opens off Cave 16, "built around 862 by the Buddhist priest Hong Bian, the leading cleric of this region"; on his death "in about 868" a stele and portrait statue were set in the side cave (p. xvi). Later the statue was removed and the space "filled with tens of thousands of manuscripts" (pp. xvi–xvii). The cave was "sealed in about CE 1035", as the Tanguts pressed south (p. xviii). Dunhuang had "over 15 Buddhist monasteries and nunneries", some with "around one hundred monks, nuns and novices" (p. xv); a few hundred of the manuscripts are medical (p. xvii). After the Tibetan century, Dunhuang was nominally Chinese again from the mid-ninth century but "in reality it was semi-autonomous" (p. xv).
- **Monasteries as clinics.** Buddhist monasteries "started to dispense drugs freely to those in need", and Tang cities founded hospitals (p. xvii).
- **Self-cultivation (*yangsheng* 養生).** Early nurturing-life practice "include[s] therapeutic gymnastics, dietetics, breath cultivation and sexual cultivation" (p. 209). Few Dunhuang texts are regimen texts, perhaps because such practice sat in "Daoist, rather than Buddhist, literature and practice" (p. 209). Sakade argues these few survive because the Buddhist church appropriated Daoist techniques (pp. 209–10, 278).
- **Grain avoidance with a Buddhist label (P. 2637).** Guanyin promises that the "miraculous fragrant pills" work by stages: "in one year his body would become lighter", then healthier organs, running like the wind, not sinking, not burning, and at fifteen years "Heavenly eyes" (p. 280). The method is "ethnically Chinese and not Indian" (p. 280): Daoist practice copied in a Buddhist library. Four grain-avoidance texts survive (App. 2 nos. 52–55, pp. 423–24), including a recipe "for removing the 'three corpses'" (S. 2438).
- **Breath and the three cinnabar fields (P. 3043).** The Six Character exhalation is chanted "as if voicelessly singing a song" while concentrating on "the three dantian" (p. 281). The note gives their seats: the upper field "was thought to be located in the head, and is also known as niwan 泥丸 (mud pill)" (p. 289 n. 13), after the *Huangting jing*.
- **A Dunhuang reading of an unborn child (P. 3390).** "If a yellow colour appears under the left eye of the pregnant woman, she will give birth to a boy; if it is under the right eye, she will have a daughter" (p. 179). The right side of the face "corresponds to Yin and the feminine" (p. 179). P. 3390 is "the only mantic text on the examination of the complexion available for the period" (p. 180).
- **Illness by the calendar (P. 2856, P. 2675).** Iatromancy treats illness as both "spirit world calamity" and "lucky and unlucky timing" (p. 135). For each day-branch on which an illness began, a named demon, its image and a talisman; one cure uses a "demon arrow" shot at the patient's chest (pp. 143–44). P. 2675 was copied by a Dunhuang prefectural official and "a specialist in the arts of Yin and Yang (no doubt at the prefectural school in Dunhuang)" (pp. 136–37). Its *renshen* 人神 systems track a "human spirit" that moves through the body by age and day, and must not be cauterised (pp. 150–51).
- **Moxibustion for everyone (S. 6168, S. 6262).** "The earliest extant moxibustion charts" (figs. III.1–3) are a "more popular guide" where diagnosis relies on "signs of illness which are at all times explicit" (p. 210). Their list includes "Women's Uterine Discharge" and a point for when "a Woman is Pregnant, [it] strengthens the body" (p. 245 ff.).
- **Women's medicine, scattered.** S. 4433 has "four sections of recommendations and prohibitions for women" beside bedchamber restoratives (App. 2, p. 410); P. 3930 treats "complications of childbirth" (p. 408); P. 2662 includes "peripartum disorders" (p. 410). S. 4329 is a domestic cosmetic book: skin creams, hair restorer, breath freshener (p. 408).
- **The bedchamber's "bad woman."** The sexual-arts literature (Ishinpō, after the *Plain Girl* tradition) lists women harmful to a male practitioner: "one with a big Adam's apple, one with a masculine voice ... one with a beard and moustache" (p. 271). Umekawa: these are not judgements of beauty but signs of "a disordered physiology of qi" (p. 271).
- **The *Songs of Great Satisfaction* (P. 2539).** Attributed to Bai Xingjian (776?–826). It covers "the anguish of the lonely and unmarried, both male and female", "hetero- and homosexual intercourse among Buddhist monks and nuns", and the emperor's love of a boy (p. 257). Umekawa: the bedchamber arts have no place for "Yin with Yin, or Yang with Yang" (p. 270).
- **Love charms (P. 2610).** "The Secret Art of Stealing Women" (攘女子婦人述秘法), mostly a wife's charms to make her husband love and respect her (p. 166). Han palace women were convicted for "taking up the female way of charming" (媚道, p. 165).

## 2. Design table

| # | Current design | Pressure | Proposed change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Seat at Dunhuang beneath Mogao. | The library cave was a memorial to Hong Bian (built c. 862, he died c. 868), and was sealed c. 1035 as the Tanguts came (pp. xvi, xviii). | Two seat events. (a) 867–868: "The Stele of Hong Bian" (the region's chief cleric dies; the Way's nüguan attend; opinion with the Buddhist sangha). (b) c. 1030–1040 under Tangut pressure: "Wall Up the Library": the Dunhuang holder may hide the Way's books in the cave (keeps a faith modifier through a Tangut conquest) or carry them east. Narrator states only the dates; the Way's hiding of its books is its own story. |
| 2 | Clergy female only; Sisters' Houses as mutual aid. | Dunhuang had 15+ monasteries and nunneries (p. xv); monasteries gave medicine free (p. xvii). | Seat flavour: a Sisters' House at Dunhuang can run a dispensary (small county development and opinion; stress loss for the Elder Sister if strain is low). Confirms Houses as care work, not reverence. |
| 3 | Gate: ten months, events at months 3, 6, 10. | The Dunhuang regimen texts are staged by time: P. 2637 promises results at years 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 10, 15 (p. 280). | Use the staged form for the gate's loc: each month heads with what the regimen promises ("In the first month the body grows light"). Grain avoidance (辟穀), breath (服氣) and the three-corpse purge (去三尸) as the month-1, month-2 and month-4 regimens. Flavour only; no tests (Serano). |
| 4 | Month 10: born from the crown of the head. | The upper cinnabar field is in the head, called *niwan* "mud pill" (p. 289 n. 13); the *Huangting jing* is the text Xue Xuantong chanted in her chamber (see Cheng). | Confirms the crown birth and gives it a period name: "born through the Niwan gate." Add 泥丸 to the month-10 loc. The *Huangting jing* is the adept's scripture in the quiet chamber. |
| 5 | Gate events at the seat; hostile and friendly readings. | P. 3390, a Dunhuang manuscript, reads a pregnant woman's face: yellow under the right eye means a daughter (p. 179). | Seat-only month-5 event "The Face-Reader of Dunhuang": a prefectural-school yin-yang master reads the adept's face. Always "yellow beneath the right eye: a daughter." Options: pay him (gold, piety), ask him to write it down (a witness for the month-10 naming), or send him off. Friendly event; never a test. |
| 6 | Hostile physician text. | The bedchamber texts' "bad woman" has "a big Adam's apple ... a masculine voice ... a beard" (p. 271): exactly the marks a trans woman is judged by. | One hostile line, used sparingly, for a bedchamber-minded courtier: "The *Plain Girl* says to avoid a woman with a man's voice and a man's throat." The faith's answer: "Then avoid me. I was not made for your longevity." No mechanic uses appearance. |
| 7 | Seat's cultural setting. | The regimen texts survive because the Buddhist church took in Daoist practice; P. 2637 puts Daoist grain avoidance in Guanyin's mouth (pp. 209, 280). | The Way at Mogao can be written as hiding in Buddhist dress: a doctrine or loc line that its regimen texts carry Guanyin's name on the outside. Fits the Lotus Sutra caves without claiming the sutra. |
| 8 | Omens and hostile reaction. | Dunhuang iatromancy names a demon for the day an illness began and shoots a "demon arrow" at the chest (pp. 143–44). | Optional hostile folk-healer event: a family calls in a day-demon talisman maker against the adept's "illness". Pairs with Cheng's ghost-fetus event; same rules (delay, never reset). Low priority. |
| 9 | Houses take discarded women. | Women's medical sections are few and mostly about childbirth (pp. 408–10). | No change; low relevance. |
| 10 | Trans men out of scope; no male-male mechanics. | The *Songs of Great Satisfaction* celebrates male-male love at court (p. 270). | Not used. |

Relevance: medium for the seat, low for the faith's theory of the body. No gender or embryo chapter exists; the useful pieces are the cave's dates, the staged regimen texts, *niwan*, and P. 3390.

## 3. In-world loc lines

- Month 1 (regimen): "In the first month the body grows light. Leave the grain; keep the breath."
- Month 4 (regimen): "Purge the three corpses. They report your faults to Heaven, and today they have nothing to say."
- Month 10: "She came out through the Niwan gate, the mud-pill palace at the crown, where the old books say the upper field lies."
- Face-reader (friendly): "Yellow beneath the right eye. The right is yin. It is a daughter."
- Sister (on the face-reader): "Write it down, master. We will need witnesses."
- Courtier (hostile): "The *Plain Girl* warns against a woman with a man's voice and a man's throat."
- Sister (reply): "Then keep away. I was not made to lengthen your life."
- Elder Sister at Dunhuang (c. 1035): "The Tanguts are coming. Wall the books in with the monks' sutras. Let them read the outside."
