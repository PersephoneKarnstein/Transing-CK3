## 1. The argument in brief

- **The text.** Harihara's *Ragaḷegaḷu* is the earliest written account of the Kannada Śiva-devotees (*śaraṇas*). The tradition itself later almost forgot it (p. 3).
- **The date.** Early decades of the 13th century (pp. 13, 43).
  - Harihara lived after the 12th-c. figures he wrote about.
  - Verses from his *Girijākalyāṇa* are quoted in the *Sūktisudhārṇavam*, "firmly dated at 1245 ce" (p. 43).
  - **No inscription or "hard" evidence links him to any Hoysaḷa court.** Later tradition names a king Narasiṃha Ballāḷa, but it is unclear which of three Hoysaḷa kings is meant (r. 1152–1173, 1173–1220, 1220–1235) (p. 43).
  - He was "of Hampi", where tradition says he wrote (p. 43 n. 5).
  - **Ramanujan 1973 misdates him to the 15th century**, perhaps confusing him with Harihara, the founder of Vijayanagara (p. 43 n. 3).
- **"Śaraṇas, not Vīraśaivas."**
  - The words *vīraśaiva* and *liṅgāyata* are absent or hapax in non-vacana Kannada texts until the 14th century. Harihara never uses them (pp. 14–16).
  - *Vīraśaiva* enters Kannada through Bhīmakavi's 1369 translation of the Telugu *Basava Purāṇamu* (p. 15).
  - Harihara says *śivabhakta*, *śaraṇa*, *jaṅgama* and *nūtanapurātana* (pp. 16–17). He never says *vacana* or *anubhava maṇṭapa*; he says "song" and "assembly" (*gōṣṭhi*) (p. 17).
- **Catholic but hard-edged.**
  - Harihara's world accepts "the queen and the prostitute" and "the Brahmin and the Untouchable" if their devotion holds (p. 232).
  - Its "equal conduct" (*samaśīla*) holds only among committed devotees and only in worship (pp. 123, 233). Ben-Herut repeatedly warns against reading modern egalitarianism or feminism into it (pp. 113, 233–234).
- **Sectarian edges.**
  - The hostile court Brahmins always bear **Vaiṣṇava names** (p. 187). Basavaṇṇa derides them as "dull-minded by the notion of karma" (*karmajaḍas*, p. 152).
  - Jains are the "intimate, wholly other" (ch. 6). Chapter 6 ends with "horrifically violent" scenes (p. 231).
- **Kalyāṇa is not the whole story.** Harihara's saints work at different times and places across the 12th century. The single Kalyāṇa community is a 16th-c. *Śūnyasampādane* myth (pp. 18–19, 235–236).

## 2. What matters for the design

### 2.1 *Śaraṇasati liṅgapati*: attested, c. 1200–1240

- **The formula.** "Śaraṇa is wife, liṅga is husband." It "epitomizes" the saint–god relation in the *Ragaḷegaḷu* and is "used several times" in the Ragaḷe about Basavaṇṇa (pp. 85–86).
  - The text it rests on: *Basavēśvaradēvara Ragaḷe* 4, prose, which reads "Saṅga as husband, Basava as wife" (*saṅgaṁ patiyāgi basava satiyāgi*; p. 86 n. 36).
  - The full lexicon definition ("thinking himself as wife and the liṅga as husband") is from a **modern** Vīraśaiva lexicon (Vidyāśaṅkara 2000; pp. 85–86 n. 33). Do not cite it as medieval.
- **Basavaṇṇa's initiation is a wedding in a temple** (pp. 86–87; Ragaḷe ch. 4).
  - At Śiva's temple at Kappaḍi, Nandi, Śiva's chief attendant, acts as priest. He gives Basavaṇṇa a personal liṅga and the five-syllable mantra.
  - The inner chamber looks like a wedding hall. Deities escort the groom; the senses escort the bride. The senses wave the lamp around the couple, and the "fluid of devotion" is poured over their hands.
  - Life and death hold the partition screen of *saṃsāra* between them. When it is lifted, Basavaṇṇa embraces Nandi, and the two are showered with cumin and jaggery.
  - Harihara uses technical wedding terms: wedding hall, bridesmaids, hand-washing (*kaidhāre*), the auspicious moment (*muhūrtam*) (p. 87).
  - **Basavaṇṇa is the bride.** The man is the wife in a temple rite, in a text of c. 1200–1240.
  - **Caveat.** Basavaṇṇa later marries two human women (p. 87). The bridal role is a devotional relation, not a life lived as a woman.
- **The wife who scolds** (pp. 87–88; *Kēśirāja Daṇṇāyakara Ragaḷe* 3).
  - Kēśirāja, a court administrator turned renouncer, loses his liṅga in a river and calls it back. It leaps out undecorated.
  - Harihara now calls him *śaraṇasati*. The "Śaraṇa-as-wife" scolds: "Why have you come out like this?" (p. 88). The liṅga obeys, goes back into the river and returns adorned.
  - Ben-Herut: the formula stretches "beyond a simple notion of complete submissiveness to the god" (p. 88).
  - *Do not use:* the note that a Śaiva who lost his liṅga was expected to kill himself (p. 87 n. 39).
- **"Already married to Śiva."** A later tradition, of uncertain date (Śivarudrappa 1976), says a rich courtesan came to marry Harihara. He refused because "he is already married to Śiva, as the god's wife" (p. 88). It is *later tradition*; use it only as a character's claim.
- **The god depends on the devotee** (*bhaktādhīna*). Śiva makes "guest appearances" and is often servile to his devotees (pp. 83–84).
- **The devotee is the god on earth.** Bijjaḷa sees Basavaṇṇa as Śiva (p. 89). Śaṅkaradāsimayya receives Śiva's third eye, and a king cries "O Lord! You are an incarnation of Rudra!" (p. 89).

### 2.2 Women saints and the limits Harihara keeps (pp. 111–123)

- **Many female figures.** Harihara gives several Ragaḷes to women saints. Their stories challenge the "virtuous wife" (*pativratā*) ideal (pp. 111–112). Leela Mullatti estimates about fifty early female saint-leaders (p. 112 n. 52).
- **In heaven, women serve Pārvati.** In every Ragaḷe about a female saint, the heavenly frame has her serving Pārvati, not Śiva (p. 114).
- **Mahādēviyakka, in Harihara's version.**
  - Her parents asked Śiva for a *daughter* (p. 114).
  - At ten she is initiated and receives her personal liṅga, as boys are (p. 114).
  - Four of the seven chapters concern her forced marriage to a non-Śaiva king, Kauśika (pp. 114–115).
  - She sets two conditions before marrying: freedom to worship, and the right to leave if he obstructs her three times. He breaks them, and she walks out (pp. 115–116). Harihara "formally sanctions" the divorce, yet the text knows no divorce rite (pp. 116–117).
  - She casts off her clothes, secludes herself in a cave at Śrīśailam and joins Śiva. **She never reaches Kalyāṇa in Harihara.** Her arrival there is a later (*Śūnyasampādane*) addition (pp. 19, 115, 117).
  - Later retellings deny the marriage altogether (p. 114).
  - Ben-Herut sets her beside Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār, quoting Pechilis: the ghoul body and the naked body both mark "ultimate departure from normative social life" (p. 117).
- **Vaijakavve.** Married to a Jain, beaten by him for feeding a Śaiva mendicant, she then initiates her husband and the town's Jains herself. She is "an antinomian role model" whom other female characters cite (p. 118).
- **Nimbavve.** A poor servant who performs her father's funeral rites herself (p. 119). Her sexual service to devotees makes her story "ethically troubling", and later texts drop it (pp. 119–121). **Do not use** her sexual material. One exchange is usable:
  - Pārvati rebukes Śiva: "Is this proper behavior for men? Is this a good example for women?" (p. 120).
  - Nimbavve answers the god: "O Lord! Is there a place on earth in which you do not reside?" (p. 120).
- **The conclusion of the section.** Nondiscrimination is complete only in ritual. Outside it, "one's gender dictates one's prescribed roles" (p. 123).

### 2.3 Crossing caste

- Harihara's devotees include Brahmins and Untouchables, artisans and ministers (pp. 99, 232).
- The vacana attributed to Mahādēviyakka that curses a hundred births "in a dog's womb" shows the tradition's anti-caste voice was not uniform (pp. 98–99).

### 2.4 Harihara's name: a bridge he refused

- "Harihara" can mean "Hari and Hara". A *smārta* cult of that name tried to reconcile the sects, with regional origins going back to the 6th century (p. 43, citing Desai).
- A town called Harihara has a temple to the joined Vaiṣṇava–Śaiva form (p. 44 n. 8).
- Harihara himself was "fervent[ly]" Śaiva. He glossed the name as Śiva, "the Lord of Hari", before whom Hari bows (p. 44).

## 3. Corrections

1. ***Śaraṇasati liṅgapati* is attested in period**: Harihara, early 13th c. (pp. 85–88). The earlier "unverified" can be lifted. The modern lexicon gloss is not medieval.
2. **Harihara is not securely Hoysaḷa.**
   - `south_reading_list.md` says "Harihara's 13th-c. Hoysala *Ragaḷegaḷu*".
   - Ben-Herut: the Hoysaḷa link is later tradition with no hard evidence (p. 43). Write "Harihara of Hampi, early 13th c.".
3. **Ramanujan's date for Harihara (15th c.) is wrong** (p. 43 n. 3). If any note uses Ramanujan for Harihara, correct it.
4. **The Ben-Herut point the review missed is in ch. 2, not in the review's summary.** `ind_orr_2013_anandakichenin.md` §3 item 6 can now point here.
5. **No man in this book lives as a woman.** The bride role is liturgical and devotional. Basavaṇṇa marries women; Kēśirāja is a "wife" in one scene. The narrator must keep saying "voice and rite", not "life".
6. **Mahādēvi in Kalyāṇa is a later story.** Any loc that puts her among Basava's assembly is quoting the 16th c.

## 4. Design implications

### 4.1 The cross-tradition current

- **Support.**
  - Ben-Herut confirms the bride theology on the **Śaiva** side in period, on the Kannada frontier (c. 1200–1240).
  - Together with Āṇṭāḷ and Nammāḻvār on the Vaiṣṇava side, the current has bride voices from *both* sects before 1300.
  - Harihara's name and the Harihara temple show that a Śaiva–Vaiṣṇava bridge was an idea people in the region knew.
- **Friction to write in.**
  - Harihara's own texts are anti-Vaiṣṇava (pp. 44, 187). Kannada Śaivas are the best in-period *hostile* voice against a crossing faith.
  - A Śaiva poet at court can say both "the śaraṇa is the bride" and "not *his* bride".
- **Hook.** b_kudala and c_kalyani are in Karnataka. If the Garland admits the śaraṇa voice as kin, the honest site is **Hampi** (Harihara's town), not Kalyāṇa: Harihara puts the saints everywhere, and the Kalyāṇa story is later.

### 4.2 The staff initiation (gate step 5)

- **In-period precedent for a man initiated as bride.**
  - The Kannada wedding-initiation (pp. 86–87) is the strongest text found so far for the step "staff on the head and a name".
  - In it, a man is initiated in a temple **as the bride**, with Nandi officiating.
- **Two rites, one officiant.** Goodall's 12th-c. staff rite uses **Nandin's** staff (`ind_goodall_rudraganikas.md` §2.4). The faith can join the two in its own voice: "Nandi gave Basava as a bride; Nandi's staff names the daughter."
- **Suggested step text (faith voice).** The lamp goes round, the screen is lifted, cumin and jaggery fall, and the staff touches her head. The narrator marks the fusion as reconstruction: Tamil staff rite plus Kannada bridal initiation.
- **Caveat for the narrator.** Chidananda Murthy (1983) likens Basava's initiation to *devadāsī* temple marriage. Ben-Herut notes the word never appears in Harihara (p. 86 n. 37). Keep §15's rule: no "bride of God" for *temple women*. Here the bride is the devotee, which is in period.

### 4.3 Events, modifiers, names, reaction lines

- **Event "The Wet Liṅga".** A daughter scolds her god for coming back undecorated, and he obeys (p. 88).
  - Options: scold (piety and stress relief) or wait in silence (piety).
  - Unlocked by the trait or the Mārkaḻi vow.
- **Event "Three Breaches".** A daughter married before her crossing sets Mahādēvi's two conditions (pp. 115–116).
  - If the spouse breaks them three times, she may leave without penalty.
  - Faith doctrine: "a marriage that bars the god may be walked out of".
- **Modifier "Bhaktādhīna"** ("the god depends on her"): small piety and prestige for daughters who keep the vow.
- **Names.** Harihara's register: *śaraṇe* (f.), *śivabhakte*, "new elder" (*nūtanapurātane*).
- **Reaction lines.**
  - *Kannada Śaiva poet* (c. 1200+): "Saṅga is husband, Basava is wife. Who is your husband, Vaiṣṇava?"
  - *Orthodox (borrowing Harihara's Pārvati, p. 120):* "Is this proper behaviour for men? Is this a good example for women?"
  - *Faith answer (Nimbavve, p. 120):* "Is there a place on earth in which He does not reside?"
- **Never.**
  - Do not cast the Garland as Liṅgāyat or Vīraśaiva. That is a living community, and the words are 14th c. or later.
  - No Nimbavve sexual material.
  - No liṅga-loss suicide.
  - Do not call the śaraṇas feminist or egalitarian (pp. 113, 233–234).
