Source: Delia Cortese and Simonetta Calderini, *Women and the Fatimids in the World of Islam* (Edinburgh: Edinburgh University Press, 2006), xvi + 270 pp. ISBN 0-7486-1732-9 (hb), 0-7486-1733-7 (pb).

**How C&C cite.** Nearly everything here is C&C's reading of chronicles (al-Maqrīzī, Ibn Muyassar, al-Musabbiḥī, al-Muʾayyad al-Shīrāzī, Nāṣir-i Khusraw) or of Goitein's Geniza work. ## 1. The argument in brief

### Method and hostile tropes (read this before writing any royal woman)

- **All male, all outside.** No source was written by a woman. The references reveal "men's perceptions of women" more than the women (pp. 1-2). The harem writers "had no access to the harem" and wrote for men (p. 79).
- **The anecdotal woman.** Following Malti-Douglas, C&C find three marks of the anecdotal woman: "eloquence, ruse and sexuality" (p. 76). Concubine stories serve to paint a ruler as feeble or virile; they say little about the woman (p. 76).
- **Rasad's stereotype.** With Rasad "come to the fore" the traits stereotypically pinned on female influence: "court intrigue, nepotism and personal vengeance" (p. 110). Chroniclers make female interference and *fitna* "bedfellows" (p. 112), on the pattern of Niẓām al-Mulk's attack on Terken Khatun (pp. 101-102). The Ismaʿili dāʿī al-Shīrāzī blames the palace women, "inflamed by hatred and rancour", for a vizier's death (p. 111). C&C ask whether historians were using "heroes versus villains" as a device (p. 113).
- **C&C's own verdict on Rasad.** She was "possibly one of the last royals" to work the old system of palace patronage, and her intervention may have **delayed** the shift to military viziers rather than caused the civil war (p. 113).
- **Famine tropes.** For the 1060s, chroniclers report harem women begging, women eaten alive, jewels traded for flour. C&C read these as the chroniclers' measure of a dynasty's failure, not as plain fact (pp. 19-20).
- **Other topoi.** The "fratricide" Sitt al-Mulk is a myth built on one hostile chronicler, al-Ṣābiʾ (p. 124). Al-Ḥākim as "Nero" and madman is an anti-Ismaʿili framing (p. 192). The countless harem is a numbers topos (pp. 78-79). The Tinnīs "effeminacy" may be a stereotype of a Christian town (p. 224).
- **Ismaʿili silence.** Ismaʿili authors largely ignore both Rasad (p. 113) and Sitt al-Mulk (p. 117).

### The palace and its women's quarters

- **Two palaces.** From al-ʿAzīz on, the Great Palace (*qaṣr al-dhahab*) and the Small Palace (*qaṣr al-baḥr*) faced each other across Bayn al-Qaṣrayn. Each was a compound with a public audience section and a private residence (p. 72). Al-Maqrīzī places the caliph's harem quarters (*maḥall ḥurumihi*) in the Great Palace, without dates (p. 74). Sitt al-Mulk had been given the Small Palace, with four *īwāns* and water pools (p. 117).
- **The harem.** Both a space and its people: the place "access to which was forbidden to most and limited to very few selected individuals", and its female and eunuch residents (p. 73).
- **Who lived there.** Wives, sons, daughters, mothers of the caliph's children, concubines and their attendants. Also the caliph's mother, aunts, female cousins, and unmarried, divorced or widowed sisters (p. 75). Caliphs' daughters stayed unmarried from al-Manṣūr on, by dynastic policy (pp. 91, 103, 151). Titles: *sayyida*, *al-sayyida al-malika* for the queen mother or senior women; *jiha* and *al-jiha al-ʿāliya* for leading consorts (p. 75); *malika* for al-Mustanṣir's mother (p. 109). Eleventh-century Geniza letters wish their recipients favour with "the ladies of the court" (p. 75, citing Goitein).
- **Numbers.** Under al-Mustanṣir, allegedly 12,000 servants and 30,000 residents (p. 83, citing al-Maqrīzī and Nāṣir-i Khusraw). Nāṣir-i Khusraw, in Cairo c. 1047-50, found the harem women impossible to count (pp. 78-79). C&C treat the numbers as "mostly notional" (p. 83).
- **A mixed harem.** Women came as booty, as diplomatic gifts and by purchase (pp. 77-78). In 454/1062 ʿAlī al-Ṣulayḥī sent al-Mustanṣir "eunuchs and slave-girls" from Yemen; in 462/1070 an Andalusi ruler sent male and female slaves (p. 78). The harem was "multi-ethnic, multi-cultural and religiously and linguistically diversified". "Many harem women were Christians and some are reported to have remained so" (p. 78).
- **Staff.** Palace women were *mustakhdimāt* or *quṣūriyyāt*. Some lived in (*muqīmāt*); others did not (*munqaṭiʿāt*) and lived in Fusṭāṭ or the Qarāfa (p. 80, citing al-Musabbiḥī for 415/1024). Top staff were often women of good family (p. 80). A head of personnel, the *zimām al-quṣūr*, oversaw them (p. 80). Twelfth-century records add:
  - a woman *zayn al-khuzzān* running the caliph's private wardrobe atelier with about thirty women (p. 81);
  - women treasurers (*khuzzān*) and standing attendants (*waqqāfāt*) (p. 81);
  - Sitt Ghazāl, keeper of the caliphal inkwell, who had a eunuch in her service (p. 81).
  Earlier and later there were wet-nurses (p. 82), women cooks (p. 81), and a *qahramāna* ("housekeeper") in the founding caliph's household (p. 74).
- **Eunuchs as the hinge.** In the harem their job was "to oversee the women, to guard them, to inform them and report on them"; they were "the link between the harem and the outside world" (p. 80). The external tailoring atelier was run by a eunuch, the private one by a woman. Only a eunuch could crown the caliph (p. 81).
- **How people moved.**
  - **Tunnels** (*sarādīb*) joined the palace parts. Women stable-hands (*shadādāt*) ran mules and donkeys through them carrying goods and people. The caliph used them to move unseen (pp. 82-83, citing al-Maqrīzī).
  - The **kitchen** stood just outside the palace and was linked to it by an underground passage. In al-Mustanṣir's time it had fifty staff (p. 81, citing Nāṣir-i Khusraw).
  - **Shopping.** After al-Ḥākim, "the palace slave-girls, escorted by the eunuchs" went shopping in Fusṭāṭ's jewellery, money-changing and carpet shops (p. 196, 1020s).
  - **Outings.** Caliphs took their harems to Nile resorts. Al-Mustanṣir "is also known" to have taken his women out of town (p. 91).
  - **Qarāfa.** The houses of the non-resident palace staff stood around Durzān's Qarāfa pavilion. It was a meeting point at festivals (p. 187, citing al-Maqrīzī).
  - In ordinary well-off houses, women came and went by a "secret door" onto a back alley (p. 191, citing Goitein).
- **Women's own voices** survive only as fragments. Concubines in al-Nuʿmān's household, crowded after children came, asked "not to be scattered" (p. 79). **Checked against the Arabic (2026-10-27):** al-Qāḍī al-Nuʿmān, *al-Majālis wa-l-musāyarāt* (Tunis 1978), pp. 545–6, read from the archive.org scan (AAlexandrina-015472). It is his own narration, not the women's words. "The sons and the daughters and some of the favoured women (*al-muqarrabāt*)" asked him to petition al-Muʿizz *li-yajmaʿa shamlahum* ("to gather them together"), so the women could visit one another veiled, "because in separation (*al-tafarruq*) there is loneliness and being cut off (*al-waḥsha wa-l-inqiṭāʿ*)". Al-Manṣūriyya, Ifrīqiya, 950s. Halm, *Empire*, p. 341, also gives the sons and daughters as the askers. "Do not scatter us" (*lā tufarriqnā*) is C&C's paraphrase, not in the text. A widow singer was summoned with her daughter and slave-girls, made to sing, then locked in a room until her host died (pp. 79-80).

### Entertainment, ceremony and festivals

- **Women musicians.** Al-ʿAzīz held a party with his mother Durzān, his favourite slave-girls, "the eunuchs and every woman musician (*mulhiya*) in the palace" (pp. 88-89). Malak al-Rāsiya was a leading court singer under al-Ḥākim and al-Ẓāhir (p. 89).
- **Nasab the drummer.** She sang and drummed in public for the 450/1058 victory over Baghdad. She "used to stand under the palace" and march with her group, singing in procession (p. 89, *secondhand* via Lev 2001, citing the *Khiṭaṭ*). Female palace drummers and insignia-carriers are "undisputed" and must have had a public role at parades (p. 94).
- **Gifts.** At mawlids palace women got the best pistachios. At the start and end of Ramaḍān each woman received new clothes and a plate of sweets. At New Year the caliph gave the first new gold coins to wives, female slaves and "high-ranking eunuchs" (p. 94). These are twelfth-century records.
- **Mid-Rajab 415/1024.** Al-Ẓāhir joined the night of lights "with the women of the palace" (p. 94, citing al-Musabbiḥī).
- **Christian festivals.** Imam-caliphs reportedly attended them "with their women" (p. 188, citing Lev).
- **Al-Mustanṣir's mother and sister, 471/1078.** They sat with him in the Golden Hall to receive the Coptic patriarch Cyril II. They offered him perfume and asked for his blessing (p. 95, *secondhand* via den Heijer).
- **Represented by servants.** At the twelfth-century *khatma* (end of the Qurʾān recitation), the servants of the *jihāt* carried water vessels before the reciters to take the blessing back to their mistresses (pp. 95-96).

### Rasad (pp. 45-46, 53-55, 103, 108-114, 151, 161-163, 180, 188, 233)

- **Origin.** A slave "variously reported" as Sudani, Abyssinian or Nubian. Al-Ẓāhir **bought** her from the Jewish merchant Abū Saʿd al-Tustarī (p. 110; also p. 53). Whether she was ever freed is unknown (p. 46). She was *umm al-walad* of al-Mustanṣir, who acceded aged seven in 427/1036 (p. 110).
- **From 436/1044-45.** After the vizier al-Jarjarāʾī died she "ruled over the 'state'" as queen regent (p. 110, citing Ibn Muyassar p. 14). She was "a force to be reckoned with" for "almost forty years" (p. 110).
- **Her *dīwān*.** It was "the gate to power" (al-Shīrāzī, p. 111):
  - She made her former master al-Tustarī head of her *dīwāns*. Geniza letters call him viceroy (pp. 110-111).
  - He was killed by eastern troops in 439/1047. No one was charged. Rasad had the vizier al-Fallāḥī jailed and killed (p. 111).
  - Service with her was dangerous. Abū Naṣr al-Tustarī and his wife refused her offer twice; he served three months (p. 111).
  - Al-Yāzūrī ran her *dīwān* and became vizier, chief qāḍī and chief dāʿī in 442/1050 (pp. 111-112, 162). Khaṭīr al-Mulk was placed in it by his father (p. 112). Her relative or protégé Abu l-Faraj b. al-Maghribī rose to vizier (p. 112).
- **Religious appointments.** She had the aged Qāsim b. al-Nuʿmān reappointed chief dāʿī over al-Shīrāzī (p. 112). Al-Yāzūrī claimed pressure from "the old women of the palace from the families of al-Hakim and al-ʿAziz" (p. 33). Al-Shīrāzī got the post in 450/1058 (p. 33).
- **Troops.** She bought "an enormous number" of Black slave soldiers to balance other ethnic regiments (p. 109), and kept 5,000 slaves in her personal service (p. 111, al-Maqrīzī after Ibn Muyassar). Viziers answered by recruiting more eastern troops, and the Treasury could not pay them all (p. 109).
- **Her people.** In 450/1058 her Jewish agent (*wakīl*) Ibn ʿUṣfūra gave charity and clothes to the poor of the Qarāfa. They thought it came from the caliph and his mother (p. 188, citing *Ittiʿāẓ* 2:245).
- **Her wealth.** Her possessions included:
  - diplomatic gifts from "the Byzantine emperor Michael";
  - a healing stone from Khaṭīr al-Mulk's grandmother;
  - 4,000 saddles with silver ornaments (p. 151, citing Ibn al-Zubayr and *Ittiʿāẓ* 2:289).
  She sponsored no buildings. Her wealth went to the Treasury and the army (p. 170).
- **The war years, 1062-1074.**
  - 454/1062: she backed riots against the Turks with money and arms for the Black troops (p. 112, Ibn Muyassar p. 13). She dismissed viziers who would not back the Sudanis and found an ally in al-Bābilī (p. 112).
  - 457/1064 to 464/1071: rebellions across the country and a low Nile (p. 112). The *shidda* ran from 457/1065 to 464/1072 (p. 170).
  - 459/1067: the Black troops were defeated. Unpaid Turkish soldiers looted the palace vaults (pp. 112-113).
  - 461/1069: the "Treasury of the queen Mother" was among the treasuries emptied and sold to pay the Turks (p. 180 n. 18, citing *Ittiʿāẓ* 2:289, 299).
  - 464/1071: Rasad was arrested and her assets confiscated (p. 113, Ibn Muyassar p. 21). C&C call the story of her flight to Baghdad with her daughters by 462/1069 "unlikely" (p. 113). On p. 161 they name Nāṣir al-Dawla as the man who confiscated her assets.
  - 466/1074: Badr al-Jamālī arrived and her influence ended. Between al-Yāzūrī's death and Badr there were 54 viziers and 42 qāḍīs (p. 113).
- **After.** In 471/1078 she wrote to Queen Arwa (pp. 108, 113) and received the Coptic patriarch (pp. 95, 113). After her no caliph's mother was regent again (pp. 113-114).
- **Women around her, by name or office:**
  - Al-Mustanṣir's full sister, unnamed. She was "very close" to him (pp. 54-55), wrote to the Ṣulayḥid al-Mukarram on his victories (p. 108), sat with Rasad in 1078 (p. 95), and in 1094 claimed to have heard his deathbed designation of al-Mustaʿlī (p. 38).
  - A daughter of al-Ẓāhir, so another sister. In 459/1067 she wrote Arwa a letter of condolence confirming her son's succession (p. 108).
  - Al-Mustanṣir's two main consorts:
    - the mother of Nizār (b. 437/1045), called "a Byzantine/Greek (*rūmiyya*)" in one Ismaʿili source (p. 55; *secondhand*: the *Dustūr al-munajjimīn* via Casanova 1922);
    - the *jiha ʿaẓīma*, mother of al-Mustaʿlī (p. 55).
  - Sitt Miṣr, al-Ḥākim's daughter, a rich patron, died 455/1063 (p. 53).
  - The princesses Rashīda and ʿAbda died in 442/1050 (p. 53).
  - Rasad had "daughters" (p. 113), unnamed.
- **Nothing on the curtain.** C&C never mention Rasad sitting behind a curtain or speaking through a eunuch. The only "curtain" in the book is the Imami jurist al-Mufīd's ideal of women hidden "behind the curtain" (p. 105) and al-Ḥākim's shoppers buying from behind a curtain or door (p. 196). In C&C, Rasad's go-betweens are her civil stewards, her *wakīl* and her troops.

### Sitt al-Mulk (pp. 117-127): the precedent Rasad's court remembered

- Born 359/970 to a **Christian *umm al-walad***, a Melkite. Her mother's brothers were made Melkite Metropolitan of Cairo and Fusṭāṭ and Patriarch of Jerusalem in 375/986 (pp. 53, 117, 119).
- She had 4,000 slave-girls (p. 117). Her chief informant was Taqarrub; after Taqarrub died, her wealth was held by another slave, al-Malīḥa (pp. 117-118). The Black slave ʿAṭūf founded the ʿAṭūfiyya corps and quarter (p. 118). She also kept an escort squadron (p. 118).
- Barjawān kept her under guard with 1,000 horsemen in 386/996 (p. 120). She gave the boy al-Ḥākim 50 eunuchs among other gifts (p. 121).
- After al-Ḥākim vanished in 411/1021 she ruled as regent. Her orders went out in writing through her slave Abu l-Bayān (pp. 124-125). She let women leave their houses again and restored wine and music (p. 125). She negotiated with Byzantium through the Patriarch Nikephoros (pp. 125-126). She died in 414/1023 (p. 126).

### Women's work, property, trade and learning (ch. 6)

- **Brokers.** There were women dealers or brokers (*dallāla*). "They would typically visit the women in their own house and sell them goods, especially fabrics" (p. 202). The Geniza's al-Wuḥsha ("the broker", fl. 1090s) was a rich Jewish businesswoman in Fusṭāṭ (p. 190).
- **Trades.** C&C list:
  - spinning, from princesses to paupers (pp. 200-201);
  - midwives and wet-nurses (p. 201);
  - in women's baths, fee-collectors, attendants and hair-removers (p. 201);
  - prison wardens (p. 201);
  - bridal hairdressers, corpse-washers and professional mourners (p. 202). A wailing slave-girl sold for twice the usual price (p. 202).
  - In the 1040s a woman owned 5,000 water pitchers and rented them at a dirham a month (p. 202, citing Nāṣir-i Khusraw).
- **Singers and sex work.** Outside the palace, singing-girls performed at functions and in tavern-like places. Their name was at times "synonymous with prostitute", and al-Maqrīzī's "taxes of the singing girls" were taxes on prostitutes (p. 203 and n. 69). Prostitutes worked in market areas. For 415/1024 C&C retell al-Musabbiḥī's report that the police chief "beat and publicly reviled an effeminate (*mukhannath*)" accused of pimping five women in his house (p. 203, citing al-Musabbiḥī vol. 1 p. 68). Punishment was flogging. It was harsher when Muslim women were involved with non-Muslim men (p. 203).
- **Slaves.** Slaves were sold at the Khān Masrūr (p. 203). In 470/1077 thousands of Berber women were sold in Cairo's markets (p. 203). Twelfth-century prices were about 20 dinars, while a labourer earned under a dinar a month (p. 204). The commonest slave-girls in the Geniza were Sudanese-Nubian, then European (p. 204).
  - In the slave-buyers' stereotype, "Byzantine slave-girls could be entrusted with valuables" (p. 204, from Ibn ʿAbdūn of Seville, 12th c., via Brunschvig).
  - Ibn Buṭlān, perhaps writing in Cairo, lists cooks, nurses, singers and dancers as skilled slave women. He divides musicians into lutists, pipers and percussionists (pp. 204-205).
  - Skilled slaves could keep their wages (p. 205).
- **Property.** Women owned shares in houses as investments (p. 191). Fatimid law let women inherit land, on the model of Fāṭima and Fadak, and gave an only daughter the whole estate (pp. 213-214). Qāḍīs sometimes cheated heiresses, and one heiress won on appeal to the vizier (p. 214). Royal women held land, tax farms and buildings. They served as "deposit banks" and financiers of the army (pp. 157, 162-163, 234). Confiscation hung over them all (pp. 159-161), but women were "normally" spared torture and prison (p. 161).
- **Religious education.** *Majālis al-ḥikma* were held separately for men and for initiated women. Women's sessions met "on specific days" in the imam's palace for his female household; ordinary women met at the chief dāʿī's palace or al-Azhar (pp. 32-33, 205).
  - Women paid for the teaching. Their names and fees were recorded, and the dāʿī waved a parchment with the imam's signature over each head (pp. 34-35).
  - Umm al-Khayr al-Ḥijāziyya preached to women at the ʿAmr mosque c. 1024 (p. 33).
  - Women's missionary work is absent from the Egyptian-phase sources (p. 36).
  - Fatimid law required a woman to wear jewellery at prayer "so that men can be distinguished from women", and henna: "never should [women] leave their palms like those of the men" (p. 34, citing the *Daʿāʾim*).
- **Daʿwa imagery.** The dāʿī al-Malūsī taught women through jewellery, spinning and dress (p. 30). Ismaʿili writing likened initiation to breast-feeding and the imamate to a bride (p. 35).

### Christians, converts and the Rūm

- **At court.** Imam-caliphs married non-Muslims, and Christian and Jewish nurses fed their children (p. 188). Many harem women stayed Christian (p. 78).
  - Al-Qāʾim's Umm Ḥabīb was a "Byzantine/Greek slave" (p. 50).
  - Al-ʿAzīz's consort was a Melkite (p. 53).
  - Halm thinks al-Ḥākim's mother was "an Egyptian Christian or a Greek concubine" (p. 67 n. 35, *secondhand*).
  - Al-Mustanṣir's consort, mother of Nizār, is called *rūmiyya* (p. 55).
  - Late in the dynasty the *zayn al-khuzzān* was "most likely" a Greek or Byzantine slave-girl (p. 81, *secondhand* via Lev).
  - Two nurses: in 415/1024 the nurse Ṣafwat al-Raqāma al-Naṣrāniyya was given one of al-Ḥākim's houses beside Sitt al-Mulk's palace (p. 82). The Zirid nurse Fāṭima was a Christian convert raised to princess (p. 82).
- **Rivalry with Byzantium.** Byzantine concubines in the harem brought news of Byzantine empresses' independence to Cairo. Fatimid ladies put on displays of wealth for Byzantine envoys (p. 179).
- **The grain affair, 446/1054.** After an Egyptian crisis the emperor promised al-Mustanṣir grain but died. Theodora then withheld the grain and demanded a military alliance. Al-Mustanṣir answered with force. Al-Maqrīzī saw this as a major cause of the *shidda* (p. 102).
- **The city.** In practice it was hard to tell a Jew or Christian from a Muslim (p. 188). Muslims, Christians and Jews rented from each other and did business together (p. 188). Historians rebuked mixing between Muslim women and non-Muslim men (p. 187).
- **Converts.**
  - A Christian woman who converted asked the qāḍī about her child's religion. He first ruled the child Christian, then gave way to public pressure and ruled it Muslim (p. 212).
  - Tinnīs and Damietta kept Christian majorities (p. 189).

### Gender-crossing in the book (all of it)

1. **The Fusṭāṭ procurer, 1024** (p. 203; see above). C&C list it under prostitution, not under any palace or gender topic.
2. **A *khunthā* at Tinnīs.** A man repudiated his bride after the wedding night, finding her to be a hermaphrodite (C&C's word). Under Fatimid law, the sex of a *khunthā* was decided "not [by] physicians, but [by] the imam himself" through divination with arrows (*qurʿa*) (p. 223; al-Maqrīzī; the law *secondhand* via Lo Jacono 1981). The date is not given.
3. **Tinnīs "effeminacy."** Ibn Riḍwān blamed the city's humidity for "the effeminate character of the people" (p. 224, citing *Dafʿ maḍārr al-abdān*, p. 115). C&C suspect a stereotype of a Christian town (p. 224).
4. **Barakāt's disguise, before 513/1119.** A dissident leader escaped prison and was smuggled into the palace "disguised as a slave-girl". He held meetings there until he died. His eunuch accomplices then told the head of personnel that "one of the old ladies of the palace had died" (p. 37, citing al-Maqrīzī, *Muqaffā* 2:571). This was a man in disguise, half a century after 1066.

There is nothing else: no palace *mukhannath*, nothing on eunuch gender, no Nawrūz, no cross-dressing festival.

**`later_starts/mukhannathun_fatimid_cairo.md`**
