# Aristotle, *Generation of Animals*, on castration and the "mutilated male", design reading

Source: Aristotle, *De Generatione Animalium*, trans. Arthur Platt, in J. A. Smith and W. D. Ross (eds), *The Works of Aristotle*, vol. V (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1912), with Ogle's *De Partibus* and Farquharson's *De Motu/De Incessu* (Internet Archive scan, `worksofaristotle005`; no ISBN). Read: every passage in GA hit by *castrat-*, *eunuch*, *mutilat-*, *female* in the relevant sense, with surrounding argument: I.2 (716a–b), I.4 (717a–b), I.20 (728a), II.3 (737a), II.8 (746b), IV.1 (765b–766b), V.3 (783b–784a), V.7 (787a–788a). Cited by Bekker numbers as printed in Platt's margins; line numbers approximate (OCR). Quotes are Platt's English.

## 1. What the text says

**The female is a privation of the male.**
- "the female is, as it were, a mutilated male, and the catamenia are semen, only not pure" (737a27–28; Greek *arren pepērōmenon*). What the female lacks is "the principle of soul".
- "a boy is like a woman in form, and the woman is as it were an impotent male, for it is through a certain incapacity that the female is female" (728a17–19). The incapacity is a failure to "concoct" blood into semen.
- Sex is decided in the embryo when the male principle "does not bear sway ... but is defeated", so that the material "change[s] into its opposite" (766a18–22). Female is what you get when the male principle fails.

**Castration moves the body *toward* the female, along that same line.**
- 716b3–9: "a small change in a first principle is often attended by changes in other things ... This is plain in the case of castrated animals, for, though only the generative part is disabled, yet pretty well the whole form of the animal changes in consequence so much that it seems to be female or not far short of it."
- 766a24–28: "This may be seen in the case of eunuchs, who, though mutilated in one part alone, depart so much from their original appearance and approximate closely to the female form."
- 784a6–11: "Eunuchs do not become bald, because they change into the female condition." They lose late-grown hair "with the exception of the pubic hair; for women also grow that ... and this mutilation is a change from the male to the female condition."
- 787b19–21: "All animals when castrated change to the female character, and utter a voice like that of the females". 788a6–9: "the voice and the form generally changes to the female character in castrated animals".
- 746b20–24: men sterile from birth "never grow a beard but remain like eunuchs".

**But the testicles are not where maleness lives.** For Aristotle the testes are "no part of the ducts but are only attached to them, as women fasten stones to the loom when weaving" (717a34–36). Their removal slackens the seminal ducts, and so the "principle" in the heart that gives the body its tension (788a3–16). He supports this with a bull that sired a calf "immediately after castration" (717b3). This loom-weight theory is exactly what Galen attacks (see `byz_galen_on_semen.md`).

**Precision on the claim.** Aristotle never says a castrated male *is* female or a woman. His verbs are of approach and resemblance: "seems to be female or not far short of it", "approximate closely", "change into the female condition/character" (*eis to thēlu metaballei*). There is no "third" anywhere in GA. The eunuch moves along a single axis whose lower end is the female, and the female is defined as a lack.

## 3. In-world loc lines

- "Cut one small part, said the Philosopher, and the whole body turns, and seems female or not far short of it. We have come the rest of the way." (Aristotelian sermon)
- "He called woman a maimed man. He saw the cut become women and called that a maiming too. He had the road right and the map upside down." (Mother of Mothers)
- "Her beard does not come back and her crown never goes bald. The Philosopher wrote that this is the female condition. So it is." (Rite of the Knife, after)
