## 1. The argument in brief

Fieldwork 1976–79 and 1983–84, mainly with four elders: the smith-sculptor Kojugu Cissoko, the Komo leader Nyamaton Diarra, the sculptor and healer Basi Fane, and the midwife and mud-cloth painter Salimata Kone (pp. 2–7). Brett-Smith's thesis: Bamana carving of ritual objects is modelled on women's procreation. Carving is "the same as" sex (p. 204) and, more deeply, the same as pregnancy and childbirth (p. 218). A great sculptor obtains his power from a mostly *female* djinn under a binding contract (*sarati*), pays for it in human lives, health, sexuality or years, and is progressively feminized: he "becomes a woman" (pp. 75–76, 203–204). Women, the primary creators, are born with spirits; a few secretly carved the most destructive objects of all (pp. 237–252). The book ends by placing the sculptor in "that dry, gray zone where masculinity and feminity interweave" (p. 234).

The evidence is the testimony of a handful of elite elders in the 1980s, read through Brett-Smith's structural equations. It is 20th-century, about living people and living institutions (the Komo, smith lineages), and much of it was told reluctantly.

## 2. What matters for the design

### Nyama and the smith (ch. 1, pp. 36–39)

- Nyama: Bird's "energy of action", released by any act, more often harmful than helpful (p. 38). It is finite and can be stockpiled; ritual specialists amass it; an old sculptor is a *nyama mògò*, "nyama person", whom it is dangerous to touch (p. 38).
- All *nyamakalaw* (casted people, smiths and griots) are "handles of nyama", human channels for it (p. 38, after Cashion 1982). A smith, by caste, is "the first child of the world" in one smith's origin account; the first smith fell from the sky like the first anvil (p. 38).
- A newborn smith is washed in the forge's quenching water; the first bath fixes a destiny (pp. 38–39).
- Nobles depend on smiths for tools, counsel, medicine and "ritual armoring", and fear them; smiths lived at the village edge in a hereditary host relationship (p. 37).
- Noble women give the gown they are wearing to a praising *griotte* and never wear it again (Introduction, note, p. 258). Griots and smiths are fed and seated apart.
- *Bula* smith lineages are said to descend from Fakoli, the smith hero who aided Sunjata (Introduction and notes, pp. 266–267, after Conrad 1992).

### The djinn contract (ch. 2, pp. 48–83)

- **Training.** Smiths' sons learn both forge and carving from age 6–7 (pp. 49–50). Gifted children are marked at birth (caul, birthmark) and washed daily in medicines from birth; the right to bathe such a child is taken from the mother and given to the father's mother or sister (pp. 48–49, 63–64). A great artist often chooses "a sister's son as his artistic heir" (p. 49).
- **No sculptor without a djinn** (pp. 53–56). Djinns are "things of the bush" (*kungo fèn*), found by pools, mountains, crossroads (p. 55). They live in villages, keep schedules, and quarrel with those who cross them at dawn (p. 54).
- **The gendering of spirits.** Personal djinns are most often of the opposite sex to their owner; in five years she met only one man whose familiar was male (p. 54). Kojugu: female spirits give power faster, because "the woman whom you like and who likes you, the secret that she possesses, she will tell it to you" (p. 55).
- **Access by gift, inheritance, friendship.** A master can "place you in correspondence with a djinn" (p. 59); djinns pass father to son; allied experts exchange them (p. 59). A lineage that owns the *Soro* object kills djinns for seven generations (p. 59).
- **Divination, medicines, dreams** are the three ways in (pp. 60–67). Sand divination's eight "houses" (p. 60). The medicines "change" you (*i b'i yèlèma*, "to transform, convert", p. 65), and Brett-Smith thinks some are hallucinogenic (pp. 65–66). Dream nights: experts sleep apart from their wives on **Wednesday, Thursday and Sunday** nights to meet their djinn (p. 66). The jealous female djinn made Kojugu kick his girlfriend out of bed (p. 66). Sculptors marry women from djinn-owning families who accept long abstinence (p. 66). Secrecy: "you tell it to everyone, you will not communicate with a djinn" (p. 67).
- **The price.** At the start the djinn asks "whether he is prepared to sacrifice other human beings, his own sexuality, or his life span" (p. 67).
  - *Human life first.* "My price is a person" (p. 67); Kojugu says he gave his first son (p. 67) and that a contract arranged by his master would leave him only three of seven sons (pp. 67–68). The man offers only a dependent he has legal power over: "his mother, first wife, or child" (p. 69). Kojugu: "nothing will succeed unless you give your child or your first wife or your mother" (p. 70).
  - *Madness and disability* in a relative, kept visible as proof the contract holds (Basi's nephew, pp. 72–73; others, pp. 73–74).
  - *Sexuality.* Two wives at most (p. 74); periods of abstinence of 3 days, 12 days or 3 months (p. 77); and finally impotence.
  - *Life span, blindness, or years of not carving* (pp. 77–78), even the day of death (p. 78).
  - *Refusal* is worse: the djinn kills the children or makes the refuser a *naloma*, a dim-witted person (p. 80).
- **The contract is a *sarati*** ("agreement, terms of payment", Bazin 1906; today a mortgage), witnessed by God: "Allah himself is aware of it" (pp. 68–69). The djinn must deliver "out of necessity!" once paid (p. 68). "It is she who gives me my personal name, it is she who gives me my family name [i.e., fame]" (p. 68).

### "Become a woman" (pp. 75–77, restated pp. 203–204)

- Kojugu describes the djinn's last clause: "The taking away of my virility" … "To take away my virility – to make me like a woman" (p. 75). He is not there yet: "My time has not yet come" (p. 75). When it comes he will give up women "Completely, because I myself I took the oath" (p. 75). "Yes, it's a contract" (p. 76).
- **It can be chosen instead of a human life.** Of a sculptor born impotent, people will think "That he has refused to give a person [to his spirits] and that he has given himself and agreed to become impotent" (p. 76). Brett-Smith: the sculptor "may deliberately choose his own sexual dysfunction" (p. 76).
- **It is done with medicines.** "There exist certain procedures … you can take away your virility completely. You won't have any need of women, women have no need of you" (p. 76).
- **The exchange quoted in the reading list** (p. 76): asked "But haven't you become a woman through that?", Kojugu: "Thus, you have become a woman, of course." Then: "You have become a woman. EE! You, you cannot perform the work of men in the house of women. Therefore, you have become a woman, you are not equal to any man." Asked whether Bamana people say such a person is a woman: "We say that he is a woman. He has made himself a woman" (p. 76).
- **It is known and unspoken.** "No one talks about it. That is an incantation hidden in one's belly"; one who gossips will be put "into that same state" (p. 76).
- Brett-Smith reads the djinn relation as demanding *dariya*, "receptivity", "quintessentially female stances" (p. 75). Sculptors come in two types: imposing giants who stay sexually active, and "dried, withered, and birdlike types with high, feminine voices, who are considered especially dangerous" (p. 77). Promiscuity is also "womanlike" (p. 77).
- Impotence does not weaken the work: "If you are not virile you can make one" (p. 77); it "may imply the ability to create objects more powerful" (p. 77).
- Ch. 6 restates it as "a form of invisible castration that effectively puts their sexual organs out of commission, altering their gender identity, and becoming, at least partially, 'women'" (p. 203), incremental with each Komo mask carved (pp. 203–204).

### Carving is "the same as" sex, then childbirth (ch. 6, pp. 203–234)

- "The root of all sacred secrets is the following: Man's penis and woman's vagina" (Nyamaton, epigraph, p. 203). Sex is "the first secret" (p. 213) and the first *jò*, "law" or association (p. 213); *ka jè*, "to unite", names both intercourse and assembly (pp. 213–214); *ka siri*, "to tie up" (a ritual act), shares a root with *lasiri*, "uterus" (p. 214).
- The left hand is "the man's hand" for sex and for carving ritual objects (pp. 209–210).
- **The Komo flutes are forged by a smith and his first wife, both naked, under a *lènkè* tree** (Nyamaton, pp. 210–212). The female flute holds the deadlier poison (pp. 212–213).
- **The first Komo** "required the presence of a small girl of the griot caste"; Kojugu said it grudgingly, and it is unclear whether she was killed or only present (p. 212).
- **Childbirth.** "Certain people think that it [making sculpture] is like a pregnant woman" (Kojugu, p. 218). Forge and labour share the phrase "your tomb is open" (pp. 219–220). Childbirth is "women's war" (*muso kèlè*), and a woman who dies in it is not buried in the ground but under stones and branches, for fear of drought (p. 221); sculptors carve on such tombs (p. 221).
- The husband of a woman in labour may not hunt or fight; his "luck is tied up" (p. 222).
- Seclusion: "you must hide yourself like a new mother hides" (p. 225); "When he is in the bush he is in a position equal to that of a young mother" (p. 226). Pregnant women never say they are pregnant (p. 224); sculptors never say they have a commission (p. 225).
- *Dariya*, "openness", is the same word for the woman and for the sculptor (p. 229).
- After carving the client gives the sculptor a meat dish no one else may touch, like a new mother's (pp. 229–230); he washes in leaves to remove nyama, like a new mother (p. 230).
- Carved human figures are "children": a figure costs a child of the same height (p. 231); figures are carved on the weekday whose children have the matching destiny (Thursday popularity, Monday knowledge, Friday rule, p. 232); the client's offerings match a birth's (p. 233).
- Brett-Smith's last word on the sculptor: "At the last, the sculptor is neither man nor woman; he is a means to an end" (p. 234).

### Women's knowledge (ch. 7, pp. 235–255)

- Uncircumcised boys are classed with women and carve the most lethal objects, without knowing their use (pp. 235–237).
- **Women carvers.** Kojugu: they existed "since before we were born", in secret, one here, one there (p. 237). Only the man she carves for and the next elder know; her husband takes the credit (pp. 237–238, 247). Some girls are **born with a djinn**, usually male; a female djinn instead gives other powers, like walking on coals to lift red-hot pots (p. 238). The signs are the eye sockets, the eyebrows, and words spoken in sleep (p. 239). Such a girl gets a boy's training and medicines (pp. 239–241).
- A woman of power "humbles herself" in public; if challenged she says "Whatever you wish – do it to me" (p. 241).
- **The trousers.** Kojugu's great-great-grandmother "put on trousers in the bush to sit down and carve", and took them off before coming home (p. 243). **FtM-adjacent; reaction only.**
- She carves on the ant heap where stillborn and deformed infants are left (*ntinkinyèso*) or at the village's open centre at midnight with her husband silent beside her (pp. 244–246). Kojugu's mother SiHawa carved a *Fara* that turns day to night in war (p. 248).
- The epic parallel: Sunjata defeats the smith-king Soumaoro when "his sister, Sugulun Kulunkan, seduces the sorceror and discovers the secret" (pp. 250–251).
- Ritual power forms a pyramid: feminized sculptors, then uncircumcised boys, then women at the top (p. 252). Men "live in a female world" (p. 253).

### Associations as female (ch. 3 note, p. 282 n. 1)

- Bamana, Malinke and Minianka speak of the secret societies "as though they were female", and "'mother' branches give rise to 'daughter' chapters" (after Colleyn 1985). Acquiring an association is compared to acquiring a wife. **"Marry the Komo" (*ka Komo furu*) is reported for the Minianka**, where the Komo is often bought from a Bamana village.

## 3. Corrections to the candidate

1. **Pages.** "Become a woman" is pp. 75–76 (the dialogue runs across the break), with p. 77 on sculptor types. The ch. 6 restatement is pp. 203–204. The griotte girl (p. 212) is confirmed. "Marry the Komo" is p. 282 n. 1 but is **Minianka**, not Bamana; the "mother"/"daughter" chapters are Bamana, Malinke and Minianka.
2. **The djinn's price list in the candidate is wrong in a way that matters.** The source's main price is a dependent's life (first wife, first child, mother; pp. 67–70) or a relative's madness or disability (pp. 72–74). The candidate's "a risk to a kinsman's health" is that same price softened, and it should be **dropped**: a gate that buys a daughter's womanhood with a sibling's illness repeats the source's worst feature. Sexuality, abstinence, years and wealth (sheep "every Friday", p. 81) are the usable prices.
3. **The usable core is the source's own alternative.** The sculptor who "has given himself and agreed to become impotent" did so *instead of* giving a person (p. 76). In the faith's voice that becomes: "I gave myself so that no one else would be given." This is better than "the djinn took my manhood, and I had asked her to", because it keeps the source's ethical logic and refuses its human sacrifice.
4. **The feminization is a demotion in the source.** "You are not equal to any man" (p. 76); the birdlike experts are "considered especially dangerous" (p. 77); the gossip is cursed into the same state (p. 76). The narrator must say so. The faith may reclaim it; the narrator may not present Bamana sculptors as trans women.
5. **Avoid Brett-Smith's conclusion as a doctrine.** "Neither man nor woman; he is a means to an end" (p. 234) and the "gray zone" (p. 234) are third-sex framing of the kind Bhatt criticises. The faith's line is the opposite: she is a woman, not a means.
6. **Washing like a new mother** is two things in the source: the sculptor's seclusion "like a new mother" (pp. 225–226) and his washing and meat dish after carving (pp. 229–230). The candidate's "washings follow, like a new mother's" is supported; cite pp. 225–230.
7. **The Komo flutes and the first Komo are secret-society material** told reluctantly (pp. 210–212). Do not use the flutes, the griotte girl, the Komo song (p. 205) or any Komo object in game text.
8. **The djinn's gender.** Men's familiars are female (p. 54); women's are usually male (p. 238). A daughter of the faith with a female djinn is, in the source's terms, *unusual* for a man and *usual* for a woman. That asymmetry can be used in the faith's voice: "the djinn knew what I was before the smith did."

## 4. Design implications

### Gate, step 1 (the smith's road), revised

- **Who:** a smith-diviner (a court physician or "learned" character with the smith flavour) who can "place you in correspondence with a djinn" (p. 59).
- **Where:** a pool, a hill or a crossroads in the bush (p. 55), at an hour that is not dawn, noon, dusk or midnight (p. 54).
- **The contract (*sarati*)**, chosen from:
  - **Abstinence:** three nights a week kept for the djinn (Wednesday, Thursday, Sunday; p. 66); or a season of 12 days or 3 months (p. 77).
  - **Herself:** the faith's distinctive option. She gives her virility to the djinn "so that no one else is given" (after p. 76). This is the faith's reading of the sculptor who "has given himself".
  - **Wealth:** a sheep every Friday (p. 81), a white bull (p. 68).
  - **Years:** a set term of carving or of life (pp. 77–78). Keep this abstract (a modifier on health or a stress cost), never a death date.
  - **Not available:** a wife, a child, a mother, a sibling's health. Hostile or rival smiths can be *rumoured* to have paid that way, as the source's community rumours do (pp. 69, 72), as a mark against them.
- **Breaking it** (the source's *tana*): the djinn makes you *naloma*, dim-witted (p. 80), or the work turns "empty" (p. 215). Use a stress and prestige penalty plus "the work is empty", never death.
- **Medicines:** "the tree with which you wash yourself can transform you" (p. 65). A washing event series can carry the bodily change.
- **Seclusion and return:** she hides "like a new mother" (p. 225), then washes and is fed the meat dish no one else may touch (pp. 229–230).

### Secrecy and reaction

- The source's rule, "no one talks about it" (p. 76), gives a social mechanic: in Badenya courts the change is known and unspoken; a character who speaks of it publicly gets a negative opinion or a curse event. Hostile courts speak of it.
- Smith reaction line, adapted, for a hostile or ambivalent elder: "He has made himself a woman, and everyone knows why" (after p. 76).
- Faith reaction line: "Nothing is what it appears; the sculptor who frightens the region is a woman of the djinn's making" (after pp. 253–254, rephrased in the faith's voice).

### Line and houses

- "Mother" branches beget "daughter" chapters (p. 282 n. 1): matches the Sisters' Houses mechanic. Drop "marry the Komo" (Minianka, and Komo); use "a House is founded as a daughter of its mother House" instead.
- A master's heir is often "a sister's son" (p. 49): consistent with the sister's-son line in `waf_ibn_khaldun_mali.md`.
- Naming by weekday (p. 232): a daughter named on Thursday is "for popularity", Monday "for knowledge", Friday "for rule". A small, period-neutral flavour for the naming step.

### Women

- Women born with a djinn, the signs in the eyes and in sleep (pp. 238–239), and the woman who walks on coals (p. 238) can be flavour for the faith's *cis* women: the House mothers who recognise a daughter. The trousered great-great-grandmother (p. 243) is FtM: reaction only.
- The woman who humbles herself and says "do it to me" (p. 241) is a strong event voice for a House mother facing a hostile court.
- Sugulun Kulunkan (pp. 250–251) belongs to the faith's Sunjata myth: the sister who draws out the smith-king's secret.

### Do not use

- Child or wife sacrifice in any form the player can choose; the madness of relatives as proof; the Komo flutes, songs and masks; the griotte girl; poisoning duels (pp. 64–65) beyond background; the menstrual-taboo apparatus as doctrine; Brett-Smith's "neither man nor woman" (p. 234); the informants' names with their stories.
