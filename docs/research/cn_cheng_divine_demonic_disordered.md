## Corrections and checks

1. **The "single yin without yang" line is Chen Ziming's (1237), drawing on Xu Shuwei.** The dissertation note quotes Xu Shuwei with 師尼寡婦，獨居怨曠. The book gives Chen's own definition: "such as Buddhist nuns and widows ... They are single yin without yang; they want [yu] men and yet cannot have one" (p. 23). Hostile loc can use either; the date matters (§2 row 3).
2. **Pei Xuanjing's parents' line is confirmed in the book** (pp. 156–57), dated 854: "If you devote yourself to the Dao and yet your practice does not bear fruit, there will be no place where you belong" (p. 157).
3. **Cheng's model of nuns as neither women nor men needs care.** She reads Buddhist sources as having "four genders: bhikṣu, bhikṣuṇī (great gentleman), nan, and nü" (p. 138), and a Jin history says renunciants 捨男女，為僧尼, "renounced manhood and womanhood" (p. 121). That is a third-sex frame of exactly the kind Bhatt warns against. The Way must not adopt it: its daughters take the robe *as* women. Use it as the Buddhist or official view the Way answers.

## 1. The argument in brief

- **Medicine invents "women without men" late, and briefly.** Chen Ziming cut Mencius's "old and without a husband" to "without a husband", turning 寡婦 *guafu* into "a new medical category defined by sex and sexuality" (p. 25). "Chen's linguistic innovation died with him, but this medical category remained" (p. 25). Before Xu Shuwei (jinshi 1132) and Chen (1237), no medical writer joined desire and procreation (pp. 22, 45).
- **And the Yuan physician answers him.** Zhu Zhenheng (1281–1358) held that "women did not necessarily desire men; they also did not have to have men" (p. 44). For Zhu the fault is a mind "not upright" (心念不正) that attracts ghosts (p. 51). The solution is "not marriage, sex with men, or medicine but self-discipline" (p. 44).
- **Who is lumped together.** Qi Zhongfu (1220) puts "court ladies, Buddhist nuns, and widows" into one class prone to ghost intercourse (p. 40). A Northern Song compilation instead says widows, nuns and monks "suffer from distressed thoughts" but are "not exhausted by the bedchamber" (p. 41): celibacy as partly a benefit, and nuns grouped with monks.
- **The ghost fetus (鬼胎 *guitai*).** A false pregnancy formed when "the jing of enchanting demons and ghosts" enters the body (p. 56). Song treatments were all to "free the blockage", "discharge" or "drop the fetus" (p. 56): abortifacient purges, many with toxic minerals (p. 48). Cheng suspects manless women were diagnosed with it more "because physicians were more willing to diagnose manless women" (p. 60): the risk of aborting a wanted child was lower.
- **The enchanted woman's symptoms are the adept's practice.** Women's ghost intercourse shows as being "unwilling to see people, talk and laugh on their own as if being with someone" (p. 49). The symptom *bu yu jian ren* (不欲見人, characters mine) applied "almost exclusively to women" (p. 54). The repertoire of the enchanted woman "overlapped with that of female transcendents in aspects concerning a woman's manlessness and isolation" (p. 152).
- **Celibate men are virtuous, celibate women suspect.** "Celibate men conformed to the expectation that a morally cultivated man should be immune to desire, whereas celibate women defied the norm that women's sexuality should be guarded by their husbands" (p. 140). Men "were rarely suspected of being demonic or possessed ... on account of indifference to their wives" (p. 152).
- **Divine or demonic: the same signs.** Such figures could be "an 'extraordinary person' (yiren)" or "'demonic and heretical' (yaowang)" (p. 151). Xie Ziran (d. 794) was "suspected of being demonic and heretic" by her father and the local official for fasting; each locked her in a chamber, and she came out a month later "radiating vitality and strength" (p. 151).
- **The quiet chamber (靜室 *jingshi*).** An oratory in the house; Song women's practice rooms "transcended the mundane boundary between inner and outer" (p. 158). Qi Xiaoyao asks for one, and her husband's family and neighbours "all deemed her to be demonic and manic [yaokuang]" (p. 155). Wang Fengxian's parents "suspected that she was enchanted by demonic spirits" (疑祅魅所惑, p. 153). Xue Xuantong "used illness as her pretext to stay in solitude" after twenty years of marriage (p. 135). In Hong Mai's *Yijian zhi* people call the condition of possessed women who turn medium "transcendent illness" (仙病 *xianbing*, p. 150).
- **Refusing marriage.** Song Yuxian (Ma Shu, late 6th c.) wore a Daoist robe under her bridal dress and at the groom's gate "changed her clothes into a coarse yellow robe ... and did not play the bride's role" (p. 131). In 1088 the nun Fawu cut her hair in the Buddha hall of her natal home and vowed "to never serve men" (pp. 142–43). Thirteen of the sixty-five *Lives of the Nuns* concern a clash with marriage (p. 140).
- **The state sorts renunciants as men and women.** In 972 Song Taizu separated nuns' ordination from monks', citing the *Book of Rites*: "'There are distinctions between men and women' [男女有別]" (p. 120). "As subjects of the state, monks and nuns were first and foremost nan (men) and nü (women)" (p. 121).
- **Daoist women were few in the Song, many under the Yuan.** In 1021 there were 731 registered Daoist nuns against 19,606 Daoist monks and 61,239 Buddhist nuns (p. 147). In the Kaiyuan era (713–41) about a third of Daoist abbeys were convents; under the Yuan, Complete Perfection had some twenty thousand clerics, "about one-third of whom were nuns" (pp. 147–48). The Tang Code treated clergy "as a distinct legal category" with financial privileges if they kept celibacy (p. 147).
- **Du Guangting's female transcendents (c. 923, Former Shu).** A separate female lineage under the Queen Mother of the West (p. 131). Assailants of women practising alone are struck down by divine power, and "the narratives do not blame their beauty or their female bodies for the assault" (p. 134). Husbands and parents who do not understand are "ignorant" (愚) (p. 135). Huang Lingwei's temple (711) ordained "seven priestesses" (p. 134). Wang Fengxian (c. 838–886) was "an influential sorceress and likely also a cult leader active in the late Tang" (p. 132).
- **Daoist ritual healing treats it as ordinary.** For Wang Xichao (1205), "dreaming of intercourse with ghosts leaks the shen-spirit", one of eight "leaks" like tears and sweat (p. 62). In a Tianpeng exorcism text (1344), the cured patient recuperates "as if a woman after childbirth" (p. 67).
- **Marriage never cures.** "None of the sources assumed that marriage solved the problem; marriage itself was never a cure" (p. 166).

Relevance: high. The best source yet for how kin, neighbours, doctors and officials would read an adept in a quiet chamber, and the ghost fetus is a ready-made hostile reading of the Jade Embryo.

## 3. In-world loc lines

- Physician (hostile, before 1130): "Her viscera are empty and her spirits weak. That is how the ghost got in."
- Physician (hostile, 1130–1280): "Single yin without yang. She wants and cannot have, and so she sickens."
- Physician (hostile, after 1280): "Her mind is not upright, so the ghost answers it."
- Physician (hostile, gate): "It is not a child. It is a ghost fetus. I can bring it down."
- Sister (reply): "It is not a child, and it is not a ghost. It is her. Leave the drugs on the table."
- Mother-in-law (hostile): "She will not see anyone, and at night she laughs in that room. Who is she laughing with?"
- Village (hostile): 妖狂, "demonic and manic."
- Official (hostile, 972 edict): "There are distinctions between men and women. Tell me which roll to write her on."
- Buddhist abbess (hostile): "You left womanhood at the gate when you took the robe."
- Sister (reply): "Your nuns left it there. We came in through that gate to find it."
- Family (after Declare Before Witnesses): "When she was their son, her chastity was a virtue. Now it is a symptom."
- The Way (after the Xiang'er commentary, p. 144): "Transcendents lack wives, and Jade Maidens lack husbands."
