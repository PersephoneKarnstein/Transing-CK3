# Bædling
*the High Seat* · [Back to the README](../../README.md#bædling-the-high-seat)

A Germanic pagan faith of Scandinavia and Anglo-Saxon England, seated on Gotland. Its daughters do not take the knife. A völva goes up onto her platform, someone sings the warding songs, and the faith holds that she sees a daughter waiting below. The faith is built from the Old English penitentials, Icelandic law, the sagas' seeresses and the archaeology of staff graves. This page sets out what those sources say and where the mod goes past them.

## The historical setting

**The word.** *Bædling* is Old English. It occurs five times in the Old English *Canons of Theodore*, a confessor's handbook translated from Theodore of Tarsus's Latin penitential at some date between 690 and about 1025 (Fulk and Jurasinski 2012, p. xl). Canon B 6 gives ten winters' fast to "he who lies with a *bædling*, or with another weaponed-man, or with a beast" (p. 15). The Latin has only "a male"; the translator added the *bædling* and set that figure apart from "another" man (p. 66). In A 54 and B 8 the *bædling* is the one penanced, and B 8 adds that such people are, "effeminately, like an adulteress", in a feminine grammatical form (pp. 15, 69). The editors gloss the word "homosexual (?)", question mark theirs (p. 87), and think it named "some more distinctive trait" now lost (pp. 66–67). One glossary puts *bædlingas* beside *effeminati* (p. 66). The root, *bæddel*, survives only twice, as a gloss for *Hermafroditus* (Wade 2020, p. 22 n. 68; p. 23); Fulk and Jurasinski derive *bædling* from it (p. 67). Sayers proposes a British (Celtic) root instead (2020, p. 10) and gives no support to the popular claim that modern *bad* comes from *bæddel* (pp. 9–10). The faith's name echoes the modern word *baedelism*; the etymology above is all the sources give.

**The law of the North.** Icelandic law, written down in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries and already Christian, punished the *word*, not the man. Anyone who called a man *ragr*, *stroðinn* or *sorðinn* ("womanish", "buggered", "fucked" in the translators' rendering) was fully outlawed, and "for those three words a man also has the right to kill", until the next General Assembly (*Grágás*, Add. §423, II p. 354; K §238, II p. 198). Meulengracht Sørensen calls this the least conditional right of vengeance in the code (1983, p. 17). The only Norse law that ever punished sex between men was a Norwegian clause of 1164, a crown-and-archbishop tool for confiscating land, which did not spread (Gade 1986, pp. 124–25, 131). *Grágás* did outlaw dress: a woman who wore men's clothes, cut her hair short or carried weapons "in order to be different" was a lesser outlaw, and "the same is prescribed for men if they dress in women's clothing" (K §254, II p. 219; K §155, II pp. 69–70).

*Argr*, the adjective behind the shout, fused three senses: sexually used as a woman, versed in sorcery, and cowardly (Meulengracht Sørensen 1983, pp. 18–20). In its sexual sense it "can only be applied to a man" (p. 18). Women could be called *org*, but there it meant lust (p. 18), and Skírnir's curse throws the noun *ergi* at a woman (Mitchell 2011, pp. 53–54).

**Which way the crossing ran.** In the sagas and in Saxo a woman could take up arms and be admired for it, though the story usually ends in marriage (Meulengracht Sørensen 1983, pp. 22–23). Saxo praises women captains at Brávellir and his shield-maidens as women who had become like men, and disapproves of them for it (Saxo, Book 7, p. 158; Book 8, pp. 172–75, in the edition read). Crossing the other way, toward womanhood, drew mockery. Clover once argued for a single sliding scale of gender (1993, p. 379), but Evans and Hancock call that model "far from universally accepted" (2020, p. 6): in the sagas sex was fixed, which is why likening a man to a woman cut so deep (Finlay, p. 181). Castration in the North was something done to enemies and outcasts. In 1135 Magnús was gelded so that he "could never call himself a king again" (Meulengracht Sørensen 1983, p. 82); *Grágás* let anyone castrate a vagrant without penalty (K §254, II p. 219); Frostaþing law gelded men for bestiality (Gade 1986, p. 127).

**Seiðr and the völva.** Snorri says seiðr brought "so much *ergi*" that men could not work it without shame, "and so this skill was taught to the priestesses" (*Ynglinga saga* 7, in Price 2019, p. 172). In *Lokasenna* 24 Loki taunts Óðinn for having beaten the drum "as witches do" (Frakes, in Acker and Larrington 2002, p. 167). Price finds that women carried "the primary role" in seiðr (p. 71) and that its practice by women was "socially acceptable" (p. 175). The fullest account of a rite is *Eiríks saga rauða* ch. 4. In a lean Greenland winter the seeress Þorbjǫrg, the last of ten prophesying sisters, is given a high seat with a hen-feather cushion and a meal of kid's-milk porridge and the hearts of every animal on the farm. The next day she goes up onto a platform raised for sorcery. Only Guðríðr knows the *varðlokkur*, learned from her foster-mother Halldís in Iceland. She refuses at first ("I am a Christian woman"), then sings (Kunz 2008, ch. 4). Price shows the high seat and the platform are two different things (p. 120), and that in every set-piece account the seeress goes into trance while the questioner waits below (pp. 74–75, 172). Jochens adds that the völva only uncovers fate; she does not change it (1996, p. 116).

How far to trust this scene is disputed. It was written down in the early thirteenth century about an event set c. 1000 (Gardeła, Bønding and Pentz 2023, p. 5). Tolley calls it "almost wholly unreliable" (2009, p. 589) and reads Þorbjǫrg as a pagan "anti-bishop" (pp. 491–92). But he keeps the word *varðlokkur*, "frozen within it, as if in amber" (p. 517), and reads the song as readying the völva's own guard before a light trance (pp. 504, 517). The archaeology is firmer. About forty staffs come mostly from women's graves (Gardeła et al. 2023, p. 422). Miniature chair pendants are found only with women where the sex is known (Jessen, p. 451). At Fyrkat a pagan seeress was buried with honour c. 980 inside a Christian king's fortress (Roesdahl, pp. 293–98).

**Gender in the graves.** Can we be sure most people in the staff graves "were gendered women"? "No, we cannot," Price writes; "a transgender reading is also possible", though he calls the term problematic for the remote past (2019, p. 341). At Klinta on Öland a person sexed male was cremated with a brooch, beads and a needle, and one sexed female with an axe (p. 103; Colwill 2021, p. 190). Picture stones from Lärbro on Gotland show bearded figures in women's dress (Price, p. 175). The earliest staffs, before 800, come from weapon burials read as male; women took the role over in the 800s (Delvaux, in Gardeła et al. 2023, pp. 440, 447). Colwill argues that the written shame around seiðr is a later Christian layer, while the graves honour their dead (2021, p. 184).

**Christianity.** The Old English canons say nothing on dress or castration (Fulk and Jurasinski 2012), but count those who heed charms and dream-reading "among heathen people" (A 127, p. 13). Wulfstan's sermon of 1014 lists "witches and valkyries" among the ruin of England (Damico 1984, p. 53). Norway's sorcery law turned capital around 1300 and Sweden's around 1350, though trials stayed rare (Mitchell 2011, pp. 125, 156–57, 204).

## What the faith holds

**The other road.** The Bædlings take their name from the word the confessors set apart from "other men". The clerks never wrote down what made someone a *bædling*. "The Bædlings say it was this road, and they keep it" (the faith's description in game). The claim is theirs; the narrator makes no claim about who the *bædlingas* were (Wade 2020, p. 23, holds the word open across several readings; Gutt 2019, pp. 188–89, separates recognising a figure from asserting what she was).

**Seiðr Unmans.** Seiðr is Freyja's art, which she taught (Tolley 2009, p. 422; Quinn, in Acker and Larrington 2002, p. 268). The Bædlings take Snorri at his word: if seiðr was given to the priestesses because it unmans, then whoever works it is no man, "and they do not call that shame". When *ragr* is shouted, the faith's answers include "Seiðr is women's art. I am a woman, and I do it" and "*Ragr* is said of men. It does not reach me", the second resting on Meulengracht Sørensen's reading of *argr* (1983, p. 18).

**The völva sees; she does not make.** "I did not make her. I saw her." This follows Jochens's völva who uncovers fate (1996, p. 116). It also follows the mod's rule from Serano that a gate should recognise a woman, not test her (Serano 2016).

**Óðinn's Host.** "The skalds fill Valhöll with dead men. The Bædlings say the Allfather's true host has none in it." This is polemic, in the faith's voice. It leans on Jakobsson's reading of Óðinn as "a leader of amazons, perhaps more mother than father", whose valkyrie army has no males (2011), and on Price's note that Freyja chooses half the slain (2019, p. 69). It runs against Damico, who shows that by the seventh century Óðinn's battle companions had become "dancing youths" (1984, p. 53), and it answers the far-right reading of the einherjar as a "violent, white, intensely homosocial masculinity" (Evans and Hancock 2020, p. 239). The faith points to the women Saxo puts at Brávellir: Hetha on the right flank, Wisna as standard-bearer (Saxo, Book 8, p. 174).

**Daughters of the Hall.** "Blood carries nothing the hall did not give." Icelandic law had no adoption rite (*Grágás* has no *ættleiðing*), but it had fostering, a contract to sixteen (K §141, II p. 46), and the inheritance-trade, *arfsal*, that made a stranger one's heir in effect (K §127, II pp. 26–27). The foster-daughter and foster-mother are two of the six women a man may kill for (K §90, I p. 154; Jochens 1995, p. 32). Miller finds the foster bond "as deep as, if not deeper than, that between parent and child" (1990, p. 171). Sworn brothers passed under a strip of raised turf and mixed blood (p. 173); the faith gives that oath to sisters.

**The knife is not theirs.** In the North the knife unmade kings and punished outcasts (see above). Meulengracht Sørensen likens castrating an enemy to *níð*: both aim "to make a no-man of a man" (1983, p. 84). The faith therefore does not cut. A daughter of another faith's knife is still a sister.

**Níðhöggr.** The vanilla Germanic faith's devil is Loki, who in *Lokasenna* is taunted for having borne children as a woman (Anderson, in Acker and Larrington 2002, p. 152). Making him the devil would cast the shape-changer as evil. The Bædlings' devil is Níðhöggr instead, whose name begins with *níð*: the enemy is slander.

**No third sex.** The faith refuses the reading of seiðr workers as a third gender (Solli 2008, pp. 197–98), which Price finds rests on a speculative etymology (2019, p. 177) and Colwill calls an "unnuanced" borrowing (2021, p. 183). Bhatt's argument that third-sexing is a feature of patriarchy, not a challenge to it, is why (Bhatt 2025). A daughter is a woman.

**Why these holy sites.** *Uppsala*: Saxo's Starkaðr left the sacrifices in disgust at "the effeminate gestures and the clapping of the mimes" and "the unmanly clatter of the bells" (Saxo, Book 6, p. 135). Saxo names no priests and no dress. *Trondheim*: Jarl Hákon's patron Þorgerðr Hǫlgabrúðr, who brought a hailstorm out of the north at Hjǫrungavágr in 986 (Price 2019, pp. 288–90). *York*: Eiríkr Blóðøx, "later to be king of York", burned his brother Rǫgnvaldr with eighty *seiðmenn* "and for this deed he was much praised" (pp. 80–81), though Tolley warns the label may be "no more than accusation spread by his enemies" (2009, p. 154). *Kyiv*: after the Rus' defeat of 971 the Byzantines found "the bodies of women in armour" among Sviatoslav's dead (Price 2019, p. 275). Kyiv sits on the Varangian road south toward Dorylaion. *Gotland*, the seat, has the Lärbro stones and chair pendants from its hoards (pp. 121–24, 175).

## How it plays

**The gate.** A Marked seeker, a eunuch or a declared sister of the faith takes *Ask a Völva to Sit for Me*. If no daughter of the High Seat is at court, a travelling völva is created and comes. The chain runs over three days, as a wedding does. First the feast: pay for the high seat and the full meal, offer plain hospitality, or send her away for the winter. Then the song: the best odds come from a courtier who already knows the *varðlokkur*. A woman of another faith who learned them as a girl may agree to sing (likelier if she likes the seeker) or refuse. Failing both, the women hum. Then the platform. Success gives the trait *Daughter of the High Seat* and, under the game rule, re-embodiment; failure costs some stress and nothing else, and the rite can be tried again next winter. Failure is the song's, not hers: nothing about the seeker counts against her.

**The name and the thing.** After the rite she chooses a name. Three days later she declares herself before the thing and someone shouts *ragr*. Verse is a diplomacy roll that can win the most prestige or lose some; steel brings prestige and dread and risks a wound; owning seiðr brings piety; "*Ragr* is said of men" brings prestige and eases stress; silence costs stress.

**The line.** Daughters bear no children, so the hall continues by fostering and adoption. A woman who knows the songs should teach them to her wards, since a known singer is the largest single bonus at the platform. *Take as Daughter*, *Declare Before Witnesses*, Sisters' Houses and *The Sisters Choose* work as for every faith (see [Shared mechanics](../shared_mechanics.md)).

**The flyting.** A Bædling can challenge anyone to a flyting, and hostile courtiers in northern and English halls challenge her. It runs three rounds of charge and answer, scored as Clover reads the genre: an accurate charge wins, an untrue one rebounds, and the best defence concedes the fact and fights over its meaning (Clover 1980). Against *ragr* the strongest answer is to own the word (Meulengracht Sørensen 1983, p. 18). Stakes rise with witnesses and double at the June thing, as *Grágás* doubles them at the Assembly. A lady of the hall may end it with the cup, as Wealhþeow does after the Unferþ flyting (Damico 1984, p. 10).

**Outlawry and the band.** From 1100, a Bædling at the court of a Christian lord of North Germanic heritage may be sued: for sorcery, by the church, if she is a daughter of seiðr or knows the warding songs; or under the dress clause. The lord may let the law run or quash the suit. Six housewives may swear for her against the sorcery charge. Otherwise she pays the life-ring and goes abroad for three winters, to a court of the six faiths outside the realm, or refuses the ring and is outlawed in full. A full outlaw who can fight leads a landless band of her own. Settlement with her accuser, a pardon for fighting in his liege's war, or land of her own ends it. Before 1100, and under pagan or English lords, no suit fires: the mod has no source for one there, and does not imply the pagan North was free. A landless band of any of the six faiths can answer the hall's call home, and a band that takes land has ten years' grace to hold it. A lord may also offer a band a county of his own, after it served him, camped long on his land or moved on when he asked; and a band whose leader fails may pass to a stronger companion ([docs/wave4/bands.md](../wave4/bands.md)).

**Gotland and the ports (wave 4).** A lord at war, of any faith, may open his ports to anyone who will harm his enemy at their own risk, as the Mecklenburg lords did after 1389 (Dollinger 1970, pp. 78–80). A Bædling band near his coast can take the letters, through vanilla's join-war contract, and while the war lasts it may seize an island seat. Peace ends the licence, and an island still held after that may draw a great power's fleet. No culture is coded as pirates: the band's men are knights, townsfolk and farmers' sons, and only the clearing power calls them sea-thieves. When Gotland passes to a lord of another faith, the faith's people there choose to keep quiet, to raise the island's levy as the farmers did in 1361, or to go to the ships. Gotland's ruler, of any faith, may have the island's peoples swear the peace of 1161: one town, one council, and less ill will between them. The design is in [docs/wave4/gotland.md](../wave4/gotland.md).

**Growth.** Each year a Marked character of another faith in Scandinavia, Britain or the eastern Baltic may hear the call and join, more likely with a member or a House nearby. Courtiers who are Marked, eunuchs or declared sisters may begin the rite on their own. The faith is meant to grow slowly. Members with no court, or only visiting one, who are Marked, daughters, declared or of a family of standing are gathered to the nearest court of the faith, an adventurer's camp included. Gotland's count is converted at the start, as Shazhou's is: the island is the faith's own. In one test run that count's line founded a Scandinavian empire; the start was then made a minority one, and in the next runs the faith never held land. The converted count is back, to be tuned if it blooms again (faith_design.md §14).

**Neighbours.** Christian, Jewish, Islamic and Indic courts react to daughters as described in the README. Built in wave 3: the outlawry tiers of *Grágás*, landless war-bands and *Spring the Cell*. Built in wave 4: Open Ports, the Island Changes Hands, the Peace of the Island, and a lord's offer of land to a band. Not yet built: the feud ledger and settlement (Offer Bót), reprieve by killing outlaws, the níð suit against a flyter, the níð-pole, the rest of the sorcery law's era arc, and Wulfstan's sermon as English reaction text.

## What is attested and what is invented

**Attested in the sources.**
- *Bædling* as an Old English word in the penitential, set apart from "other men" and likened to an adulteress; *bæddel* as a gloss for *Hermafroditus* (Fulk and Jurasinski 2012; Wade 2020).
- The Icelandic law that outlawed the speaker of *ragr*, the dress clause, the absence of adoption law, fostering and *arfsal* (*Grágás*).
- Seiðr as chiefly women's work, shameful for men in the written sources (Snorri; Price 2019; Tolley 2009).
- The words *seiðr*, *seiðhjallr*, *vǫlva* and *varðlokkur*, a raised platform, a light trance, a wandering seeress, and songs learned from a foster-mother, at least in saga (Tolley 2009, p. 517; Kunz 2008).
- Staff graves, chair pendants, mixed grave goods at Klinta, the Lärbro stones, and women in armour among the Rus' dead in 971 (Price 2019; Gardeła et al. 2023).
- Women warriors in Saxo and the sagas, as literature (Saxo; Jochens 1996, p. 110, reads them as a male fantasy).

**Disputed.** Whether *Eiríks saga* records a real rite (Price and Gardeła lean yes; Mitchell 2011, p. 97, thinks its overall impression probably right; Tolley rejects almost all of it). Whether the staff-bearers were all women (Price 2019, p. 341). What *bædling* meant, and where it came from (Fulk and Jurasinski against Sayers).

**Reconstructed or invented by the mod.**
- No source shows anyone assigned male becoming a woman through seiðr. Franks finds no Norse man taking a woman's name (Gardeła et al. 2023, p. 36). The gate is the faith's inversion of a real link between seiðr and *ergi*.
- No source ties *bædling* to seiðr, or the English word to the Norse rite. The faith joins them.
- "Óðinn's host has no men" is polemic; the evidence cuts both ways.
- The sworn sisterhood is borrowed from sworn brotherhood.
- The outlawry suit applies real Christian-era Icelandic and Norwegian law to the faith; the year 1100 and the odds are design. A band turning landholder is Balkan evidence (Sophoulis 2020), applied to the North.
- The Victual Brothers, the 1361 levy and the oath of 1161 are real (Dollinger 1970). The narrator never claims the Gotlanders, the farmers of 1361 or the Brothers for the faith; Henry the Lion is not named ("a duke of Saxony"). The Hansa never licensed a band of this faith, and the crusade line is spoken only by a hostile chaplain, in quotes.
- The three-day sequence follows one saga scene that may be largely literary; the odds, cooldown and bonuses are game design.
- The Bædlings never existed. No living community is meant by them.

## In the game: the summary that was on the README

Moved here on 2026-10-26, when the README's faith sections were cut to short overviews. It lists what the mod builds for this faith, feature by feature, as the README described it.

*Working name.* A Germanic pagan faith of Scandinavia and Anglo-Saxon England. It takes its name from *bædling*, a word of uncertain meaning that the Old English Canons of Theodore set apart from "other men" in their canons on sex between men; one glossary puts it beside *effeminati*, and the glossaries also keep *bæddel*. It joins that word to *seiðr* and *ergi*.

In the north the sanctioned crossing ran toward manhood. Anyone the law counted a man who went the other way was called *ragr*, yet Icelandic law outlawed not the one so called but whoever said the word, and the one so named could kill the speaker. The Bædlings keep the other road. In the north the law let vagrants be castrated without penalty, and men unmanned their enemies; it was never a rite, so this faith does not use the knife.

- **The gate: seiðr.** *Ask a Völva to Sit for Me* is a three-day rite, like a wedding, built on the seeress in *Eiríks saga rauða*:
  - **The völva comes.** She is given the high seat and a feast.
  - **The warding songs.** Someone must sing the *varðlokkur*, which girls learn from their foster-mothers. A woman of the faith can teach them to a daughter or ward.
  - **The platform.** The völva goes up into trance while the seeker waits below.
  - **The odds.** The faith holds that the völva sees a daughter rather than makes one, and the odds turn on whether the song holds, not on the seeker's merit; it may fail until next winter. The rite follows the seeress in *Eiríks saga rauða*, which scholars think was largely invented to honour her host; the warding songs are the part most likely to be old. Völur also travel the halls in winter and can be asked to sit for anyone at court.
- **The declaration.** A new daughter declares herself before the thing, perhaps with a name fastened by a gift or the woman's form of her own. When someone shouts *ragr*, she can answer in verse, with steel, by owning seiðr as women's art, with "*ragr* is said of men", or not at all.
- **The line.** The hall's daughters are fostered, sworn and adopted. Two may bind themselves as sisters by the old oath.
- **The world.** A martial faith. The skalds fill Valhöll with dead men; the Bædlings say the Allfather's true host has none, and point to the shield-maidens at Brávellir. English courts call valkyries witches. The hall raids to bring its daughters home, and its clergy fight. The faith's devil is Níðhöggr, the malice-striker: its enemy is *níð* itself, the slander shouted at the thing.
- **Tenets.** Seiðr Unmans, Óðinn's Host, and Daughters of the Hall.
- **The flyting.** Three rounds of charge and answer: a true charge wins, a false one rebounds, and against *ragr* the strongest answer is to own the word. Stakes double at the June thing, and a lady of the hall can end it with the cup.
- **Outlawry.** From 1100, under Christian Norse lords, a Bædling may be sued at law: for sorcery by the church, or under the law of dress. Six housewives may swear for her. Otherwise she pays the life-ring and goes abroad for three winters, or refuses and is outlawed in full. A full outlaw who can fight leads a band of her own. A settlement, service in the realm's war, or land of her own ends it.
- **The law changes with the North.** In Iceland the one who hired a seeress answers for her, and twelve jurors may clear her client; in Norway the seeress answers, and six housewives may clear her. From 1300 a bishop may offer penance and seven years on the pilgrim roads instead, under a condition that does not end. Where the law has become capital (Norway from 1300, Scania 1328, Sweden 1350), a woman who comes before a court again is handed to the lord's keeping. Trials stay rare. A fortnight after a sentence comes the confiscation court, and if it cannot sit, the sentence lapses. An outlaw in full may ride from hall to hall for pledges to buy a settlement, and a House that keeps her bears the strain and the accuser's summons.
- **The feud, finished.** A requital on someone of higher rank overbalances the Account, one on someone lower settles nothing. A house that owes may grant the other Self-Judgment: a modest award, a heavy one the neighbours call uneven, or none at all. A settlement can be sealed by fostering a child of the other hall ("The one who fosters is the lesser. The hall is glad to be lesser."). A Norse lord who wrongs a woman ruler may refuse to keep an Account with women, and her next blow against his house is answered with honour. When an outlaw of the faith is killed, the law counts no wrong, but her sworn sister may take up a private Account. Under lords not of the six faiths, a woman's suit must be carried by a man.
- **Lore.** Courtiers tell of the converting kings, and of a pagan king who burned eighty men he called *seiðmenn*; news comes from the islands of burnings on a bishop's sentence; a rival says a Norse lord keeps *seiðmenn*; a woman who knows the warding songs can weave a shirt no blade will bite; each holy site, and Gotland, has its story. From 1350 a daughter of any of the six faiths in England may learn the needle trade at an older woman's bench and take a woman's name there.
- **The band.** A landless Bædling band can answer the hall's call home (see *Bands come home* in [Shared mechanics](../shared_mechanics.md)).
- **Open Ports.** A lord at war, of any faith, may open his ports to anyone who will harm his enemy at their own risk, as the Mecklenburg lords did after 1389. A Bædling band near his coast can take the letters (vanilla's join-war contract) and, while the war lasts, may seize an island seat. Peace ends the licence. A band that still holds an island after that may draw a great power's fleet.
- **The Island Changes Hands.** When Gotland passes to a lord of another faith by conquest or grant, the faith's people there choose: keep quiet in this land, raise the island's levy as the farmers did in 1361, or go to the ships.
- **The Peace of the Island.** Gotland's ruler, of any faith, may have the island's peoples swear peace, as Gotland and German traders did in 1161: one town, one council, and less ill will between them.
- **Holy sites.** Uppsala, Trondheim, York and Kyiv. Kyiv sits on the Varangian road south toward Dorylaion.
