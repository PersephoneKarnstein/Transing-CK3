# Badenya
*the Mother's Children* · [Back to the README](../../README.md#badenya-the-mothers-children)

Badenya is a Mande faith inside the game's West African religion, seated at Kirina in Mali. In Mande speech *badenya*, mother-childness, is the bond of children who share a mother, and *fadenya*, father-childness, is the rivalry of children who share a father. The faith takes the first as its law. Three people make a daughter: a smith who bargains with a djinn, a woman who claims her as her mother's child, and a jeli who speaks her name before the lord. No source shows a man becoming a woman in West Africa before 1453. Badenya is a reconstruction built on institutions the sources do attest, and its god names and Mande terms still await a check by a Mande speaker.

## The historical setting

**Ghana and the sister's son.** The oldest layer of the faith's world is Ghana. Al-Bakrī, writing in 1068 and read here through Gomez, says only the king and his heir wore sewn clothes, and the heir was the son of the king's sister. The royal town's grove held the priests, the idols, the kings' tombs and the king's prison (Gomez 2018, ch. 3, "Sovereign Power in Ghana"). Gomez adds a dated case: Tunka Manīn succeeded his maternal uncle Basī in 455/1063 (ch. 6, "Succession"). That is the clearest instance of the sister's line in the region before 1453.

**Mali.** Ibn Khaldūn, writing in the 1380s from informants he names, tells how Ghana weakened as the Almoravids rose, how the Sosso then enslaved Ghana, and how Mali conquered the Sosso and spread to the Atlantic (*Histoire des Berbères* II, p. 110). The king who subdued the Sosso he calls Mārī Jāṭa, glossing "jāṭa" as lion, and gives him a reign of 25 years (p. 111). That this king is the Sunjata of the epic is a modern identification, though the gloss supports it. A later king, Abū Bakr, was a son of Mārī Jāṭa's daughter, chosen by what Ibn Khaldūn calls "le principe des nations barbares", which gives the throne to the dead king's sister or sister's son (p. 111). De Slane's French also seems to make Mansa Mūsā descend from the founder's sister's son (p. 112). Levtzion and Hopkins's English, followed by Gomez, Conrad and Wilks, reads it as a brother's line instead (Gomez 2018, ch. 6; Conrad, in Austen (ed.) 1999, p. 214). The mod does not claim Mūsā for the sister's line.

**Walata and the court of 1352.** Ibn Baṭṭūṭa spent about eight months in Mali in 1352–53 and disliked most of it (*Voyages* IV, p. 424). His hostility makes him useful: what he records against his will was not invented to flatter Mali. At Walata the women were held in more regard than the men (p. 387), no man was named for his father, and the heirs were a man's sister's sons (p. 388). The same people prayed, studied law and learned the Qurʾān (p. 388), so the mother's line there was Muslim practice, not a pagan survival. Women kept men friends who were not kin, and men kept women friends (pp. 388–389). The Berber sultan of Tagaddā was also to be succeeded by his sister's sons (p. 443). Walata's people and Tagaddā's were Berber, not Mande.

At the capital, speech to the king passed through the interpreter Dūghā (p. 405). On feast days Dūghā sang the king's wars while his wives and slave women sang with him and played with bows, and he was paid 200 mithqāls of gold dust before the court (pp. 411–413). After him came the bards, the *djâli*, each inside a feather figure like a bird with a wooden head and a red beak (p. 413). Their verses reminded the king that the kings before him did good, and told him to do good so that he would be remembered. The chief poet laid his head in the king's lap (pp. 413–414). Ibn Baṭṭūṭa was told the custom was older than Islam (p. 414). That is hearsay, and he says he was told it. Above the throne's parasol sat a gold bird the size of a hawk (p. 406). Al-ʿUmarī, in the 1330s, also puts a poet-intermediary before the throne (Wilks, in Austen (ed.) 1999, pp. 44–47).

Women held real power at this court: Sulaymān's chief wife, Qāsā, shared the government and was named with the king from the pulpit, until he set her aside and charged her with plotting (*Voyages* IV, pp. 417–419).

**No knife here.** Mali had no eunuch office. "Pages ou eunuques" in the French audience scene (p. 404) is the translators' double gloss of one word. The only eunuch Gomez places at the Mali court is ʿAnbar, a gift from the Marinid sultan (Gomez 2018, ch. 7). The *Tarikh es-Soudan* puts no eunuch at Mali; its palace eunuchs and eunuch cavalry are 16th-century Songhay (al-Saʿdī, pp. 149, 193, 199). The faith's gate is therefore not the knife.

**Smiths and *nyama*.** In Mande thought every act releases *nyama*, a finite and dangerous energy, and the casted smiths and bards are its "handles" (Brett-Smith 1994, p. 38). Smiths with women potters were "earth experts" who controlled fertility, and in the epic Sunjata's enemy "always is a blacksmith" (Jansen 2018, pp. 9–11). *Nyama* is safe to use for the Mande: van Beek found it absent from Dogon religion and possibly Bambara in origin (van Beek 1991, p. 151).

**The epic's date.** The Sunjata epic is set in the 13th century but recorded much later. Between Ibn Khaldūn and an 1882 mention, no written source names Sunjata (Bulman et al. 2023, pp. 12, 20), and what Ibn Baṭṭūṭa heard was praise of many kings, not an epic (Austen, in Austen (ed.) 1999, p. 70). Most scholars place the epic's present form in the 17th or 18th century around Kangaba (Bulman et al. 2023, pp. 12, 16); the Kamabolon ceremony there has its present form from 1880–1912 (Jansen 2002, p. 57). This page dates every epic episode by when it was recorded.

**What the record does not show.** None of the twenty accounts recorded from 1889 to 1959 has a man becoming a woman, wearing women's dress as a role, or a eunuch (Bulman et al. 2023; the project searched all twenty). The nearest thing is 20th-century Bamana ethnography. A smith-sculptor told Brett-Smith that the last clause of a sculptor's contract with his djinn takes his virility, and that one who gives it "has made himself a woman" (Brett-Smith 1994, pp. 75–76). In the sculptor's words this is a loss: such a man is "not equal to any man" (p. 76). Koné, a Bamana linguist writing about his own culture, says the opposite: an impotent man has "left manhood" but is not a woman, only "an incomplete man" (Koné 2004, p. 5). Both views exist. The faith takes the first and reverses its value.

## What the faith holds

**Mother-childness over father-childness.** Koné describes a public patrilineal ideology, summed up in a saying that the belt's two ends are closer than breast milk, and a hidden one in which everyone privately values *badenya* and *sinjiya*, "breastmilkhood", more (Koné 2004, pp. 2–3). Jansen glosses *badenya* as harmony and the siblinghood of children of one mother (Jansen, "The Younger Brother", 1996, p. 10). The corpus editors call the pair the epic's core subject (Bulman et al. 2023, p. 5). The House speaks the hidden transcript aloud. Its sayings come from the epic: "The father is everybody's, the mother is personal" (Conrad and Condé 2004, l. 2163). At a bride's arrival, a bard explains, men were not the closest relatives; women were (ll. 1749–50). Children of one mother should be in harmony (l. 3595).

**A woman may claim a child she did not bear.** In one bard's version, recorded at Fadama in 1994, Tenenba Condé, the senior wife childless for thirty years, takes in the infant Fakoli and says: "I have given birth to a child without getting pregnant!" (Conrad and Condé 2004, l. 3110). His praise names her, "Soma Tenenba's son Fakoli" (ll. 2688–89). Her brothers give her three months at home, as for a new mother (ll. 3172–79), and their descendants still call the Condé "uncle" though no Condé woman bore a Koroma (ll. 3180–81). The House claims Tenenba as its first claiming mother. The narrator notes that the episode is one bard's, from 1994, and absent from the 1889–1959 corpus (Bulman et al. 2023).

**The mother's name goes first.** Sunjata's name began as praise, "Sogolon's lion" (Conrad and Condé 2004, p. xxxv). Niane calls prefixing the mother's name "a very frequent practice" (Niane 1965, p. 15 and n. 27), and Jansen calls it common in Mande (Jansen 2018, p. 14 n. 50). It applies to daughters too: So'olon Kolonkan, Sunjata's sister (Conrad and Condé 2004, ll. 2192–96). A daughter of the House is named with her claiming mother's name before her own.

**The price of the crossing.** In a Kela text, Sogolon takes off two bracelets and gives them to a ferryman as the price of her son's river crossing, and bracelets have been given as that price since (Jansen 2002, p. 127). In the House, the claiming pays the bracelets.

**Hot until cooled.** While the Kangaba sanctuary's roof is off, "it is hot", and only one man may speak, because a wrong word could remake society wrongly (Jansen 2002, pp. 53–54). Heat is the Mande idiom for a society in change, and a Sunjata praise line says the Mande "has cooled" (p. 53). Bamana speakers set *suman*, cool and deliberate, against *kalan*, hot and quick to quarrel (Schulz 2002, p. 816 n. 49). A daughter between states is hot, and the word cools her. The House uses the idiom and none of the sanctuary's rites.

**The smith's bargain.** In Brett-Smith's fieldwork the djinn's first price is a person, a dependant the man has power over: his mother, his first wife or his child (Brett-Smith 1994, pp. 67–70). People say of a sculptor born impotent that he refused to give a person and gave himself instead (p. 76). The House builds its gate on that alternative: the djinn asks a life of her house, and she gives her own manhood so that no one else is given. In the epic the genies also ask kin and refuse strangers as substitutes (Conrad and Condé 2004, ll. 2835–83). The House does not accept Brett-Smith's own conclusion that the sculptor is "neither man nor woman" (Brett-Smith 1994, p. 234). It holds that she is a woman. In the sources the djinn's debt recurs: potters owe the bush spirits a yearly sacrifice for the first clay (Jansen 2018, pp. 9–10). In the game the djinn returns once.

**The word.** Of the three steps, the word has the firmest anchor before 1453: the bards before the throne in Ibn Baṭṭūṭa and al-ʿUmarī. Bards were "guardians of the Word" who could praise and could also admonish their patrons (Conrad and Condé 2004, p. xv). Jeli women praised their patrons' lineage at festivals and were paid in goods (Schulz 2002, p. 797 n. 1). In 1990s Mali some bards still arrived uninvited at weddings and namings and imposed praise that had to be paid (p. 801 n. 10). In the corpus's Foreword, Sumanguru sang his own praise on the bala and found it sweeter from another's mouth (Bulman et al. 2023, p. xv). The House draws its rule from this: no daughter names herself.

**The line, and the grave.** The line runs through a sister or a daughter, as at Ghana and Walata. Sogolon's burial in a foreign land, and the price of her grave, is in at least eight early accounts, from 1890 onward: "The earth here is not your own" (Bulman et al. 2023, ch. 3, p. 75; ch. 4, p. 87; ch. 5, p. 96). In one account the son must buy the grave from his own mother's brothers (ch. 5, p. 96). In the contemporary record, refused burial is the oldest religious exclusion: in 1935 Gorer noted that Senegal's góor-jigéen were refused religious burial (Broqua 2017, ¶3). Kin have also claimed the estate of the dead and quarrelled over how the dead should be named (Gaudio 2009, pp. 192, 200, 204). So the House buries its own, under her own name.

**What it refuses.** The House does not take the identity of any living community. In the scholarship the 'yan daudu of northern Nigeria saw themselves as "real" men (Gaudio 2009, p. 10), and Yoruba *ìyàwó* names a new initiand of either sex, whose male priests stay men outside ritual (Matory 2005, pp. 7, 216–217). Neither is a model. The House is no "third thing". When surveys offer both "woman" and "transgender", more people in West African samples choose "woman" (Larmarange and Broqua 2023, p. 11). Bhatt argues that third-sexing is how a gender hierarchy works, not how it is resisted (Bhatt 2025, "The Third Sex"), and Serano objects to third-gendering people without their consent (Serano 2016, ch. 7). Matory warns that scholars' models of "African tradition" have reshaped living religions and harmed some of their members (Matory 2003, p. 434). So the House claims no "African matriarchy", and its matrilateral claims rest only on the attested sister's line.

## How it plays

**The start.** In 867 the county of Sibi, which holds Kirina, keeps the faith. If its lord holds other counties and Sibi is not the capital, the county passes to a new woman of the faith as the lord's vassal, who keeps it with her own chaplain. Otherwise the lord keeps it, and the faith lives at that court. A *jelimuso* who has made the crossing sits at the court that holds Kirina, and a few Marked courtiers are placed there too.

**The gate.** *Go to the Smith* is a social gate, not a roll. She needs a smith, a claiming woman and a jeli. The bargain makes her Hot. Within days she needs a woman to claim her: first a mother, sister or aunt who does not dislike her, then any woman of the court who thinks well of her. If no woman will, the gate closes, she takes stress, and she may try again after the decision's two-year cooldown. Once claimed, she pays the bracelets or lets them wait, at the claimer's cost. Then the jeli speaks. Paying 50 gold makes it praise and brings 100 prestige. Paying what she can is a coin toss between modest praise and a joke that costs prestige and stress. Either way she rises a Daughter of the House and, under the shared rules, continues in a woman's body unless the game rule is off. A year later the djinn comes back once, and the debt follows her into the new body.

**The line.** The tenet *Mother's Children* lets adopted daughters inherit as daughters of the blood, and keeps adoption always open. With the shared *Take as Daughter* and *The Sisters Choose*, a Badenya ruler can pass her realm to a claimed daughter or a sister's child. Re-embodied daughters bear no children, so the line runs by claiming.

**Feasts and funerals.** Each year a daughter of the House may be called to a naming feast. She serves at her first three, then may host one for 25 gold and five years' prestige. When a daughter dies, her lord decides whether the House buries her. Burial by the House earns piety and the respect of daughters at court. Giving her to her blood kin earns a little prestige and their resentment.

**Growth.** The shared call reaches West Africa: each year a Marked person of another faith there may join Badenya, more likely with a member or a House nearby. In one test run a converted lord's line took Mali in 967 and Badenya ruled thirteen kingdoms; a faith this small should grow slowly, so Kirina now starts as a county and a court, not a converted lord. In a later run Badenya fell to one member in the 970s and recovered to 39 by the 1000s.

**Neighbours.** Most of the faith's neighbours are Muslim. Under the shared rules, Islamic courts value a daughter as a trusted servant and think less of her as a ruler. That reaction leans partly on Leo Africanus's Fez of the early 16th century, where, as Davis reads him, men in women's dress cooked for the army and lamented the dead but were barred from the mosques (Davis 2006, chs. 4 and 7). The Maliki jurist at the mansa's court is the hostile voice. Jewish and Indic reactions apply as for every faith.

**Designed, not yet built.** A seven-yearly "New Roof" that gathers new daughters into an age-set, after Mande re-roofing and age-set practice but not the Kamabolon's rites (Jansen 2002, pp. 45–47, 52); a refuge at the rock of Koulikoro, which from 1819 to about 1930 sheltered runaways of either sex fleeing a master or a spouse (Bulman et al. 2023, ch. 2, p. 63), without its shrine; and a warm misreading by the Orisha faith, which would see a daughter as a bride of the god (Matory 2005, pp. 7, 216–217).

## What is attested and what is invented

**Attested before 1453.**
- Succession through a sister or sister's son at Ghana (al-Bakrī, 1068, via Gomez; Tunka Manīn, 1063), Walata and Tagaddā (Ibn Baṭṭūṭa, pp. 388, 443), and one Mali king chosen as a daughter's son (Ibn Khaldūn, p. 111).
- A court where speech passed through a paid interpreter-singer and bird-masked bards admonished the king (Ibn Baṭṭūṭa, pp. 405, 411–414).
- A queen who shared the government and was named with the king in the sermon (pp. 417–419).
- Do as "Daw" (al-Bakrī via Bulman et al. 2023, p. 18).

**Epic tradition, set in the 13th century and recorded from 1889 to 1994.**
- Tenenba's claiming (one bard, 1994); the mother's name before the child's; Sogolon's grave-price (from 1890); the genies' demand for kin (1994).

**Ethnography and contemporary studies, 20th and 21st centuries.**
- The smith's djinn contract and the sculptor who "made himself a woman" (Brett-Smith, fieldwork 1976–84); *badenya* as a hidden transcript (Koné); hot and cool (Jansen; Schulz); the bracelets as a crossing price (a Kela text); the "mothers", the feast roles and the burial disputes of the contemporary record. These last come from Wolof, Hausa, Akan and Abidjan material, not Mande. They are used for the faith's texture as analogy only, and no community in them is the faith or its descendant.

**Invented by the mod.**
- That anyone crossed from man to woman in medieval Mali. No source shows it.
- The House, its three-step gate, and the gate's order.
- The value the faith gives the sculptor's loss: in the source it is a demotion, and the faith reclaims it.
- The djinn's single return, the coin-toss satire, and the gold amounts.
- The House's burial as a doctrine.
- Mande terms and god names: the high god "Ala", the spirits, the ancestors and the bush spirits all await a Mande speaker's check.

The design notes add a warning from van Beek's restudy of Griaule. A powerful outsider with a thesis, and informants who supply what he wants, produced an "intercultural fiction" the field could not find (van Beek 1991, pp. 153–158). A mod that wants trans women in medieval Mali is also an eager outsider. That is why this page marks every step.
