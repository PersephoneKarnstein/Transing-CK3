# Adelphai Dorleyaiou
*the Sisters of Dorylaion* · [Back to the README](../../README.md#adelphai-dorleyaiou-the-sisters-of-dorylaion)

A Christian heresy in Byzantine Anatolia, seated at Dorylaion in Phrygia. It sits inside the Byzantine court's long culture of eunuchs, and its argument is with that culture. The Byzantine court made its eunuchs into a third estate: honoured, useful, and owned. The Sisters say the cut are women. This page sets out the history the faith is built on, what it claims in its own voice, how each mechanic traces back to a source, and where the sources stop and the mod takes over.

## The historical setting

**Phrygia and the Mother.** Dorylaion, modern Eskişehir, lies in the old heartland of the Phrygian Mother. Her twenty-three rock-cut facades fall inside a triangle drawn between Eskişehir, Kütahya and Afyon (Roller 1999, p. 84). Her Phrygian name was simply *Matar*, "Mother"; *Kybele* was an epithet meaning "of the mountain" (Roller 1999, pp. 244, 328–29). She was a protector, not a fertility goddess. The stress on fertility came later, at Rome (Roller 1999, pp. 114, 318; Burns 2025, pp. 17–18).

**Pessinus, as it really was.** Pessinus, in the same county as Dorylaion on the game map, was the shrine Rome remembered as the oldest and holiest. Its sanctuary has never been found (Roller 1999, pp. 192, 341). It was a temple-state ruled by priestly dynasts who all took the title Attis (Roller 1999, pp. 193–94). A first-century inscription shows the chief priesthood passing from father to son (Roller 1997, p. 544), and in Roller's words, "the evidence for eunuch priests at Pessinous during this period is very slim" (Roller 1999, p. 342 n. 81).

**The galli.** The castrated priests of the Mother, the galli, are known almost entirely from hostile Greek and Latin writers, and most of the evidence comes from Rome, not Anatolia (Lightfoot in Tougher (ed.) 2002, pp. 72–73, 79). Roller warns that "in no case does the voice of a eunuch survive" (Roller 1997, p. 543). What can be said: the gallus castrated themself as an adult, removing the testicles only, after an apprenticeship and not in frenzy (Roller 1997, p. 542; Roller 1999, pp. 324–25; Burns 2025, p. 114). A relief from Kyzikos shows Soterides in a long gown and a veil, though the inscription uses the masculine *Gallos* (Roller 1997, p. 544; Roller 1999, p. 333). The feminine *Gallai* appears only in outsiders' poetry: a Hellenistic fragment and Catullus 63 (Roller 1999, pp. 230–31; Latham 2012, p. 99). In Apuleius's novel the galli call each other "sisters" (Lightfoot in Tougher (ed.) 2002, pp. 76, 79). Roman law made the gallus "neither man nor woman", a non-person, and antiquity never saw the gallus as a sacred third gender (Roller 1997, pp. 549, 555).

The famous March festival is Roman. Claudius's cycle ran from the carrying of the pine to the *dies sanguinis*, the Day of Blood, on 24 March, when devotees offered their own blood, and then the Hilaria (Latham 2012, pp. 107–8). Castration on the Day of Blood itself is "improbable" (Latham 2012, pp. 107–8); Burns places it, if anywhere, at the Hilaria (Burns 2025, p. 114).

**Montanism.** The one real Phrygian Christian heresy with women clergy began at Ardabau, which an early source places in a district that could lie anywhere between Philadelphia and Dorylaion (Trevett 1996, p. 21). Its prophetesses Priscilla and Maximilla spoke with the authority of the Spirit; the Quintillians later ordained women presbyters and bishops, citing Galatians 3:28 (Trevett 1996, pp. 185, 221). A grave from the Dorylaion region commemorates Mountane, very likely a Montanist (Trevett 1996, pp. 172, 204). Their ladder ran from the patriarchs of Pepouza, to regional *koinonoi* ("companions"), to bishops (Trevett 1996, pp. 186, 210–14). The empire suppressed them with legal instruments: books burned, assemblies banned, heretics' wills voided and their property given to orthodox kin (Trevett 1996, pp. 199, 228–29). Survival is secure in Phrygia to the mid-sixth century, doubtful in the eighth, and unattested by the ninth (Trevett 1996, pp. 227–31). Opponents had called Montanus a castrated priest of Cybele; Trevett treats this as late polemic (Trevett 1996, pp. 78, 154).

**The court and its eunuchs in 867.** By the start date Byzantine law counted three kinds of people: Leo VI's Novel 98 distinguishes men, women and eunuchs (Sidéris 2010, p. 89), and calls eunuchs "some strange sex" (Tougher 2008, p. 111). Philotheos's *Kletorologion* of 899 sets out eight eunuch ranks and ten offices reserved to eunuchs, from the *parakoimomenos* who slept beside the emperor to the *papias* who held the palace keys (Bury 1911, pp. 121, 124–28). Eunuchs could hold any other office except the city prefect, the quaestor and the domestics (Bury 1911, p. 145). The empress had her own chamber, and a woman *koubikoularaia* was made by the same rite and the same words as a eunuch *koubikoularios* (*Book of Ceremonies* II.24–25, R622–R627).

Most court eunuchs were cut as children. Of 196 named eunuchs, the 25 with any detail were nearly all castrated before puberty, some at seven (Ringrose 2003, p. 61). By the middle period the supply was largely home-grown: Paphlagonian farmers cut their own children for court careers (Tougher 2008, p. 61). The standard surgical manual gives a method for "children, still of a tender age", and its author says he performs it only because the powerful insist (Paul of Aegina VI.68). Foreign children still came: in 949 Liudprand gave the emperor four *carzimasia*, child eunuchs made by Verdun merchants (Liudprand, *Retribution* 6.6, p. 199). Adult self-castration was the despised kind (Ringrose 2003, p. 15).

The law forbade making eunuchs and tolerated buying them. Leo VI's Novel 60 softened the penalties and let a free person be cut on a physician's decision (Guilland 1943, p. 200; Tougher 2008, p. 29). Nicaea's first canon barred self-castrates from the clergy but not those cut by physicians (Tougher 2008, pp. 69–70). Leo's Novels 26 and 27 opened adoption to eunuchs and to all women, virgins included (Macrides 1990, p. 111).

**Angels.** The comparison of eunuchs with angels was real but it was not law. It begins in seventh-century saints' Lives, where angels appear *like* palace eunuchs (Sidéris 2013, pp. 555–57). By the ninth and tenth centuries heaven was imagined as a court, and eunuchs and angels were thought to look alike (Neville 2019, p. 54). Neville judges that Byzantines more likely placed eunuchs on a scale of virtue than in a separate gender (Neville 2019, pp. 52–53), and Ringrose, who argues for a third gender, is clear that eunuchs were still considered men (Ringrose 2003, p. 4).

**Decline.** Eunuch power did not end by repeal; Novel 60 was never withdrawn. From Isaac I in 1057, and above all under the Komnenoi from 1081, eunuchs left the front rank; Alexios I barred them from Athos in 1083 (Guilland 1943, pp. 230, 232). Gaul traces the later decline to household offices passing to the emperor's kin and to aristocratic women (Gaul in Tougher (ed.) 2002, p. 209).

## What the faith holds

**The cut are women, not a third thing.** This is the faith's central claim, and it is a heresy against its own era. The Emperor's law counts three kinds; the Sisters count two. They take the direction of travel from Aristotle, who wrote that eunuchs "change into the female condition" (*Generation of Animals* 784a; also 787b), and refuse his ranking, in which the female is a defective male (737a). Ringrose states the split in the terms the faith uses: Aristotle has eunuchs changed into a female state, while Galen entertains a third type of being (Ringrose 2003, p. 16). The doctrine follows Bhatt's argument that third-sexing is a feature of patriarchy, not a challenge to it, and that "veneration" of a third class is marginalisation (Bhatt 2025, "The Third Sex").

**"The court calls its eunuchs angels, and buys them as boys."** The Sisters' polemic joins two things the court kept apart: the angel imagery and the slave market. Both halves are attested (Sidéris 2013, p. 555; Ringrose 2003, p. 61; Liudprand, *Retribution* 6.6, p. 199). The polemic compresses, as polemic does: the angel image began with the monks, not the palace. The Sisters also refuse the court's honour. Ringrose shows that every negative trait in the category "eunuch" was borrowed from women, while the virtues were left to men (Ringrose 2003, p. 6); the Sisters say the palace calls its cut sisters "of a different kind" so it never has to call them women.

**Pessinus never closed.** The Sisters call the galli *gallae* and claim them as foremothers. They know the word came from Roman poets. They remember Pessinus kinder than it was, and the eldest admit it: the house belonged to men named Attis who had sons (Roller 1997, p. 544). The Roman record swings between Varro's tender "Naiads" and the contempt of Martial and Juvenal (Latham 2012, pp. 96, 109), so the faith's memory is one more selective reading of a selective record. Raha and van der Drift warn against waxing lyrical with nostalgia (Raha and van der Drift 2024), and the faith knows its nostalgia for what it is.

**A line from Pessinus to Pepouza to Dorylaion.** In their own voice the Sisters claim a succession: the Mother's people at Pessinus, then the prophetesses of Pepouza, then themselves. They say of Pepouza, "they were not us, but they kept the door open." The narrator says only what the record says: Montanism was a Christian movement of cis women and men, its link to Cybele is hostile polemic (Trevett 1996, pp. 9–10, 89), and it is unattested by the ninth century. The faith may also claim the "Honoured Eunuch" bishop Heortasius of Appia and the eunuch priest Aurelios Appa of Laodicea, two Phrygian epitaphs Sidéris calls perhaps Montanist (Sidéris 2010, p. 91). Trevett warns that epitaph markers do not prove Montanism (Trevett 1996, pp. 204–8), so this is the faith's claim, never the page's. So is their story that the archangel of Germia, near Pessinus, had another name before he had wings: Mango's theory that Michael's cult took over the Mother's sites is, for Sidéris, hypothesis and analogy (Sidéris 2013, pp. 541–42; Ringrose 2003, p. 147).

**The barren Mother.** The Mother protects; she does not breed. The faith counts barrenness no defect, which fits the Phrygian goddess as Roller and Burns describe her (Roller 1999, p. 114; Burns 2025, pp. 17–18) and answers Bhatt's account of how the reproductive order treats infertile women and trans women alike (Bhatt 2025).

**Daughters are chosen, not born.** The line runs through adoption, as Leo VI's law allowed. The tenet quotes the Emperor's reason: "it is not nature but the malice of men" that took eunuchs' children from them (Guilland 1943, p. 201).

## How it plays

**The start.** At 867 the county of Phrygia, with Dorylaion and Pessinus, is Kybeline and Dorleyaian. Theia Beatrike, the Mother of Mothers, an elder daughter of the knife, takes Phrygia as a vassal of the same liege and rules it as a theocracy. Her court starts with two Marked young women: trans girls the game codes as male, who have not yet taken the knife. In the first year the faith chooses its House of the knife by event.

**The gate.** Anyone of the faith the game codes as male can ask for the knife: a Marked woman, a sister who has declared her name, or a eunuch the court's knife already cut. The rite takes two days: blood and mourning on 24 March, then the knife and the name at the Hilaria. Under Aunt Bea's House a ruler can also take a violent prisoner into a three-year programme with a sponsor, whose "levers" events move them toward the knife at the end of the first year, or toward washout. Under Grandmother's House captives are cut and stay eunuchs. The Gift Alone takes only those who ask; it is the strongest doctrine over a long game, in piety, same-faith opinion and arrivals. Vanilla castration still makes ordinary eunuchs; only the faith's own rite makes daughters.

**The line.** Daughters of the knife are infertile, so the faith lives by adoption. *Take as Daughter* accepts adults and writes the heir clause; *Foster as Daughter* brings a girl or woman into the House without making her an heir. *The Sisters Choose*, the shared elective law, lets the ruler, the faith's clergy and its vassals choose among born and adopted children, women of the faith on the council, and faithful vassals. Sisters' Houses belong to the county, not the family; each has a Protectress, an Elder Sister drawn by lot every five years, and strain that can split it into a daughter House.

**The court.** Dorleyaian dukes and above can appoint daughters of the knife and eunuchs as Parakoimomene, Protovestiaria and Papias, and any woman as Praiposita.

**Neighbours.** Under a lord of another Christian faith the old laws climb rung by rung: the books, then the assemblies, then the wills, faster after 1081. A bishop may preach that the Sisters are Cybele in Christian clothes, and the answer the player chooses depends on her wing. *Rescue the Daughters* brings home daughters living under a neighbour of another faith, or held in a neighbour's prison, and is closed to Grandmother's House.

**Four children and an army.** In 949 Liudprand of Cremona gave the Emperor four *carzimasia*, children cut by the merchants of Verdun, as "the most precious" of his gifts (*Retribution* 6.6, p. 199). The Mother of Mothers can ransom them, and each knife House answers in its own way; none of the children is given a rite or a name. Dorylaion was an army camp on the road east (*De Cerimoniis*, App. A), so when the Emperor goes to war his army may halt at the Mother House. His chamber may ask for a daughter, offering a rank and a salary, and would count her a eunuch.

**The Galenic wing.** From 886 a ruler of the faith who is not the Mother of Mothers may found the Galenic Rite, which keeps her House of the knife and changes only the body doctrine: the cut are some third thing, as the Emperor's law counts them. Its rulers find Christian lieges easier and their Houses find less to pray for. Under the heresy laws they can answer with the Emperor's own count, or register their daughters as eunuchs to save the wills. The Mother of Mothers keeps the main rite and may call it "the old cage with a new name". Stepping back is honoured: a ruler may return to the main rite, and the Mother is glad of it. The AI founds it rarely, and only under a liege of another Christian faith.

**From the record.** A few dated events follow the Byzantine reading: Ignatios returning as patriarch in 867, the boys sold on the road to Tarsus before 990, Theophylact's book from 1107, the letter of Psellos (1063–78), the closing of Athos to eunuchs in 1083, and a convent's eunuch steward at a House's gate from 1120. The flavour budget applies to all but the first and Athos.

**Growth.** Each year a Marked character in Asia Minor or the Balkans may hear the call, more often with a daughter, a House or a holy site nearby. The faith is meant to grow slowly. In the project's observe runs it was the faith that struggled most: Phrygia was revoked or converted back in several runs, and it fell to a few dozen members. The theocracy and a pressed claim on Phrygia for the Mother House are the current answers. The author's stance is that it may struggle, so long as it stays playable.

## What is attested and what is invented

**Attested in the sources the project read.**
- The Mother's cult around Dorylaion; the hereditary Attis priests of Pessinus and the slim evidence for eunuch priests there; the galli as adult, voluntary castrates; *Gallai* only in outsiders' poetry; the Roman Day of Blood and Hilaria.
- Montanism in Phrygia, its women clergy, its *koinonoi*, and the codes that suppressed it.
- Leo VI's Novels on adoption (26–27), castration on a physician's word (60) and three kinds of people (98); the *Kletorologion*'s eunuch offices; one rite for the empress's woman and the eunuch chamberer.
- Child castration for court careers, the Verdun trade, and the decline from 1057 and 1081.
- The Kecharitomene typikon's female line of protectresses, election by lot, and an enclosure "closed even to eunuchs themselves" (Thomas and Hero 2000, p. 710).
- Aristotle's "female condition" and Galen's "third thing".
- Theophylact's *In Defence of Eunuchs* (c. 1100–07), Ignatios's two patriarchates, the eunuch trade through the Cilician frontier, the Athos ban of 1083, and Psellos's letters calling himself feminine "with regard to nature" (read by Papaioannou as a rhetorical persona).

**Reconstructed.** The two-day rite is the Roman cycle moved to Phrygia and set inside a Christian heresy; the Sisters know their Day of Blood comes from Rome's books, since Pessinus left none. The Protectress and the lot are the Kecharitomene rules applied to Houses of trans women; in the original, eunuchs ran the convent's estates and were kept out of it. The court offices are real Byzantine offices given to women the court would have called eunuchs. The Companions are the Montanist *koinonoi* with the top inverted: at Pepouza the patriarchate stayed male (Trevett 1996, p. 211); at Dorylaion it is a woman's.

**Invented.** The faith itself: no source attests a sect at Dorylaion, or anywhere in Byzantium, that held castrated people to be women. No source shows a person assigned male living as a woman or nun in the Byzantine saints' Lives the project read (Talbot 1996; Betancourt 2020, p. 205, notes that trans women are "all but absent" from the record). The line from Pessinus through Pepouza to Dorylaion is the faith's claim, not history: Montanism was a Christian movement of cis women and men, and the bridge to 867 is unattested. The name is a pun on Dorley Hall, and the three Houses of the knife are taken from Alyson Greaves's serial *The Sisters of Dorley*, not from Byzantium. Aunt Bea's rehabilitative knife has no period precedent at all. Theia Beatrike, the Mother of Mothers and her Houses are the mod's own. Where the faith claims a historical person, Heortasius of Appia or the archangel's Phrygian past, it does so in its own voice; this page does not endorse the claim.

## In the game: the summary that was on the README

Moved here on 2026-10-26, when the README's faith sections were cut to short overviews. It lists what the mod builds for this faith, feature by feature, as the README described it.

*Adelphai Dorleyaiou*, "the Sisters of Dorylaion." A Christian heresy in Byzantine Anatolia, centred on the house at Dorylaion. The name is a pun on Dorley Hall.

The Sisters are nostalgic for a sect almost nobody remembers: the Mother's galli of Phrygia, whom they call *gallae*. They say the house at Pessinus never closed, and they remember it kinder than it was; Pessinus in fact belonged to priests titled Attis who passed the office from father to son. In their own voice they claim a line from Pessinus through the prophetesses of Pepouza, the women-led Montanist church of Phrygia, to Dorylaion. What they refuse is what the Byzantine court offers instead. "The court calls its eunuchs angels," they say, "and buys them as boys." The Emperor's law counts three kinds of people: men, women and eunuchs. The Sisters hold that one who takes the knife rises a daughter, not a third thing.

- **The gate: the knife.** *Take the Knife* takes two days, as the Mother's festival did: blood and mourning on the Day of Blood (24 March), then the knife, the knifer's question and a new name at the Hilaria, the festival of joy. Each faith picks one of three houses for who may be cut:
  - **Grandmother's House:** any captive, man or boy, in the old cruel way. She called them eunuchs, not daughters: unmanning them was the point.
  - **Aunt Bea's House:** its cells take only grown men proven violent, each under a sponsor who walked the same road, with a washout path for those who will not change. A Marked or declared woman held prisoner is never put in the cells: the House welcomes her, frees her, and offers her the knife as her own choice. It holds that every woman who leaves its cells is a woman, claims no law for it, and admits that it keeps cells.
  - **Gift Alone:** only grown women who ask. "Even the Mother of God was asked." "A gift you cannot refuse is not a gift." The strongest doctrine over a long game.
- **The line.** Daughters choose their heirs rather than breed them. A woman of the House can foster a daughter, who belongs but does not inherit, or take her as a daughter with the written clause that makes her an heir. Each House has a Protectress from the founder's female line, as the convent charters passed protection of a convent "in perpetuity", and its sisters name three for Elder Sister and let the lot decide.
- **The court.** Dukes and above can appoint daughters of the knife and eunuchs to the offices the Byzantine court reserved for its eunuchs: Parakoimomene, Protovestiaria and Papias. Any woman can be Praiposita of the empress's chamber.
- **The Women's Chamber.** Under the emperor of the Romans, or any Byzantine throne of administrative government, the empress's chamber may write to a daughter of the faith anywhere and offer her a post at court. Its clerks would write her in their eunuchs' column; a notary of the empress's may write her among the women instead. The chamber pulls against the Patriarch's party, and a woman on the throne or in the regency draws it up. While it has the throne's ear the laws against heretics are not read; with its standing a daughter can win a House a chrysobull or ask the throne for an estate, which a later throne, as Basil II did in 996, may annul. When the Patriarch has the throne's ear, the synod writes.
- **The world.** The *Rescue the Daughters* war takes a neighbouring county where daughters of the faith live under a lord of another faith, or whose lord holds one prisoner. The women freed choose whether to join the attacker's court or go home. A bishop may preach that the Sisters are "Cybele in Christian clothes", and under a lord of another Christian faith the old laws against heretics climb rung by rung, faster after the Komnenian turn of 1081: the books burned, the assemblies banned, and, under a Byzantine lord, the wills voided.
- **Tenets.** Day of Blood, Daughters Chosen Not Born, and Unbending Sisterhood.
- **Head of faith.** The Mother of Mothers, Theia Beatrike, an elder daughter seated at Dorylaion, with up to seven Companions, as the Montanist prophetesses had theirs.
- **The Emperor's road.** In 949 the Mother of Mothers can ransom the four child eunuchs Liudprand gave the Emperor, each knife House in its own way. When the Emperor goes to war his army may halt at Dorylaion, and his chamber may ask for a daughter, whom it would count a eunuch.
- **The foremothers.** The Sisters keep five saints of their own, women who died before 867: *Stephania the First Mother*, who took the knife by her own will and founded the House at Dorylaion; *Bethania the Bookbearer*, who carried the House's books out of Dorylaion and died in a governor's prison at Ankyra rather than name a House; *Diana of Kyzikos*, who founded the first daughter House and took in widows and barren wives; *Christina the Physician*, who taught the Houses to keep the knife clean; and *Philippa Who Walked Beside*, who walked the novice years beside many daughters, Theia Beatrike among them. At the 867 start each lies at one of the faith's holy places, as vanilla's saints do. They are inventions, named after the women of *Welcome to Dorley Hall*. The Church's monk-saints who lived as men (Marinos, Euphrosyne, Pelagia) are not claimed: the Sisters call them the Church's double standard.
- **Holy sites.** Dorylaion and Constantinople, Kyzikos, Ankyra, and ruined Pepouza.
- **The Galenic wing.** The main rite is Aristotelian: the cut body changes into the female condition. From 886 a ruler of the faith may found the Galenic Rite, the rite that agrees with the Emperor: Leo VI's law counts men, women and eunuchs, and the Galenic Sisters call the cut some third thing. It buys peace with Christian lieges and costs piety and the daughters' names in the register. Its book, from 1107, is Theophylact of Ochrid's *In Defence of Eunuchs*. The Aristotelians call it "the old cage with a new name"; a ruler may come home to the main rite. Its founder's students take the rite with her, and in later years the school wins some sisters over, more readily where its sisters already sit at court or hold power.
- **The Tower on the Condition.** A sister at the court of a Christian lord in the Byzantine world may ask him for an empty tower, on the condition that the Sisters rebuild it and keep it manned, as monks were granted towers in the 14th century. If he grants it, a Sisters' House stands there by his leave. Five years manned and the tower is theirs to keep; two years empty and he may take it back. A later lord finds a kept tower's grant harder to set aside, and men who robbed the road may come to keep its flocks.
- **From the record.** In November 867 Ignatios, cut at fourteen, returns as patriarch, and each House answers in its own way. Before 990 a merchant's train of captive boys passes on the road to Tarsus, and the Houses buy them free, free them and jail the merchant, or (Grandmother's House) take only the boys already cut; no child is cut on screen. From 1063 to 1078 a copy of a letter by Michael Psellos, who wrote of his own soul as feminine, reaches the Mother House, and each wing reads it its own way. In 1083 the Holy Mountain closes to eunuchs, and from 1120 a convent's eunuch steward, barred from the enclosure, may ask a House for work.
