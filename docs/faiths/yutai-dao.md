# Yutai Dao
*玉胎道, the Way of the Jade Embryo* · [Back to the README](../../README.md#yutai-dao)

Yutai Dao is a Daoist faith of late Tang China, seated at Dunhuang beneath the Mogao caves. Its daughters become women through inner alchemy: ten months carrying an embryo that is born from them as their true selves. It refuses the palace's knife and the palace's names. It holds its land through women's temple and burial societies, which outlast any one sister and stand between her house and the magistrate. The faith is the mod's invention. The world it is set in, and the pieces it is built from, are documented below.

## The historical setting

**Daoist women in the Tang.** In the Kaiyuan period, 550 of the empire's 1,687 Daoist abbeys were convents, about a third (Jia 2018, p. 50; Despeux and Kohn 2003, p. 119). The law named the women clergy as one of four distinct religious groups: Daoist priests, monks, nuns and *nüguan* 女冠 (Jia 2018, p. 14). They kept their hair, wore their own vestments, received the same seven ranks of ordination as men, and took a new name at ordination (Jia 2018, pp. 4–5, 13, 15, 166). Women reached the top ranks. Yet in every case Jia documents, the registers came down from male masters, and no source shows a woman conferring them on another woman (Jia 2018, pp. 5, 35, 70, 82). A fifth-century code did send a daughter who refused marriage to study "preferably with an unmarried woman master" (Despeux and Kohn 2003, p. 112).

**Dunhuang.** Xuanzong founded the Shenquan Abbey 神泉觀 and other Daoist houses at Dunhuang "for teaching disciples and copying scriptures" (Rong 2013, pp. 65, 358). The town's Chongxu convent had an abbess, Song Miaoxian, whom the capital called to copy the canon (Jia 2018, p. 72). Jia counts 37 priestesses at Dunhuang in 758 (Jia 2018, p. 73). Ordination certificates of the same year for the Hexi region list 33 Daoist nuns among the new clergy, and both surviving copies are made out to Shazhou men (Gernet 1995, pp. 55–56). Then Tibet took the town in 786 (Galambos 2020, p. 7). The Tibetans organised Shazhou into occupational districts, one of them for "Daoist believers", and Daoist scriptures vanish from the manuscripts of the time (Rong 2013, pp. 39, 72). Tibetan rule also spared Dunhuang the Huichang suppression of the 840s (Galambos 2020, p. 8; Rong 2013, p. 40). Zhang Yichao's revolt of 848 created the Guiyijun, and the town's monks and nuns marched with him (Rong 2013, pp. 41, 72). Rong names no Daoist temple from the Guiyijun period. The faith therefore begins as the remnant of the Tang abbeys, without a working temple.

The game opens in 867. Zhang Yichao's hostage brother has died in Chang'an, Zhang himself has gone to court at the emperor's order, and his nephew Zhang Huaishen holds Shazhou without the title (Rong 2013, p. 42; Galambos 2020, p. 10). The Tanguts took Shazhou in 1036 (Rong 2013, p. 46).

**The women's societies.** Dunhuang's *she* 社 associations were voluntary mutual-aid groups with elected officers, and funeral aid was their "most important activity" (Hao 2021, p. 70; Rong 2013, pp. 297–299). Women had their own. The bylaws of a women's *she*, renewed in 959 (British Library S.527), adapt a men's model. Where the model says "the older as elder brothers", the women wrote "the older ones as their elder sister, and the younger ones as their younger sister" (Ning and Hao 1997, pp. 24, 55; Galambos 2020, p. 204). Galambos reads the bylaws as making the bond between members as strong as blood ties, if not stronger (Galambos 2020, p. 205). Another women's bylaw, P.3489, levies a *dou* of flour from each member for births and deaths alike (Ning and Hao 1997, pp. 27–28). A loan ledger of about 945 shows women of member households borrowing flour and oil from a society's common store, the *yiju* 義聚 or "righteous store", repaying at autumn with about half again (Ning and Hao 1997, pp. 15, 484–486).

**Eunuchs and the palace.** Tang eunuchs were adopted into the service by senior eunuchs, married, and adopted heirs of both sexes. An edict of 791 let eunuchs of the fifth grade and above adopt one son (Rideout 1949, pp. 55–56, 68 n. 9). The record calls them "beardless men"; the category is still man (Rideout 1953, pp. 47–48). Chiang argues that the eunuch as a "third sex", and China as a "castrated civilization", are constructions of the 1870s–1930s (Chiang 2012, pp. 23–24). Citing Jennifer Jay, he finds that most eunuchs showed no shift in gender identity (Chiang 2012, p. 44).

**The inner embryo.** The embryo is older than inner alchemy. The *Laozi zhongjing* puts a Red Child in the adept's body, with a mother at the right and a father at the left. Shangqing scriptures of the late fourth century have the adept re-live their own gestation month by month (Pregadio 2019, pp. 15–17). Inner alchemy (*neidan*) itself appears only from the late eighth century (Pregadio 2019, p. 21). Zhang Boduan's *Wuzhen pian* of about 1075 says "in ten months the embryo is complete" (Wang Mu 2011, §2.3). The fixed three-stage scheme, whose middle stage is the Barrier of the Ten Months, dates from the 11th–12th centuries (Pregadio 2019, p. 66). In these texts the embryo is born *from* the adept and becomes "a perfect likeness" of her: the true self (Pregadio 2019, p. 52). The middle field where it grows is called the Mysterious-Female, but the goal is pure yang (Pregadio 2019, pp. 29, 39, 58, 61; Wang Mu 2011, §2.1). A text of about 420, the *Santian neijie jing*, says that Lord Lao took the shape of Mother Li and sheltered in his own womb. "There was never a real Mother Li" (Despeux and Kohn 2003, pp. 51–52). Qing manuals say that men must "achieve the state of pregnancy" and both sexes must realise a yin body before the last stage refines it to yang (Despeux and Kohn 2003, pp. 223, 234). Women's alchemy (*nüdan*) matures only in the late 18th century, and its "beheading the red dragon" is first attested in 1310 (Valussi 2008, pp. 47, 62, n. 45).

**The long arc.** Song law gave the estate of a household with no sons (*hujue* 戶絕) to its unmarried daughters, and the magistrate took a share only when there were none. An adopted daughter's "status is the same as that of a natural daughter" (McKnight and Liu 1999, pp. 244, 297). Judges often waived even the state's lawful share (McKnight and Liu 1999, pp. 153, 291). Birge argues that Song daughters held a half share; Bernhardt denies it (Birge 2002, p. 111; Bernhardt 1999, pp. 24–39). The mod builds only on the extinct-household rules, which both accept. Meanwhile women fell from a third of the Daoist clergy to three or four percent in the Song registers (Despeux and Kohn 2003, p. 201). From 1264 the Yuan confiscated heirless estates outright, and from 1273 a daughter could inherit only through a husband brought in to carry the household's service (Birge 2002, pp. 220, 227). In 1313 women lost the right to sue where a male relative could act for them (Birge 2002, pp. 268–269). In 1369 the Ming made a lineage nephew the heir of a sonless widow (Bernhardt 1999, p. 62), and barred women of childbearing age from orders (Despeux and Kohn 2003, p. 156). The protection a religious house could get was an official name-panel (*e* 額), "the safeguard against all future confiscations" (Gernet 1995, p. 43). Houses without one were closed first, in 727, 845 and 955 (Gernet 1995, pp. 43–45).

**Omens.** The Five Phases treatise of the *Han shu* reads "a man becoming a woman" as a portent about the court. An old gloss adds 男化為女，宮刑濫也: "a man becoming a woman means castration is used to excess" (*Han shu* 27C1). In 1124, the *Song shi* records, a tavern-worker's wife grew a beard "just like a man", and "by special edict was ordained a female Daoist" (*Song shi* 62). In the records the project read, she is the only person a court took in.

## What the faith holds

**The Jade Embryo.** The Way takes the alchemists at their word. The masters teach that an embryo is carried ten months and born from the adept as her true self (Pregadio 2019, p. 52). Late manuals add that a man must first reach "the state of pregnancy" and take a yin body (Despeux and Kohn 2003, p. 234). The Way stops there, at the mother, and does not go on to pure yang. It claims Lord Lao as its warrant: he made himself into his mother's shape and was born from his own womb, and the Way's daughters do as he did (Despeux and Kohn 2003, pp. 51–52). That is the faith's reading. The text treats Mother Li as an appearance, and the god stays male. The Way also speaks of the gestation through mothers, never fathers. She carries her daughter, and the daughter is herself. Gutt warns that male pregnancy has long been read as patriarchal self-generation (Gutt 2019, p. 200).

**Not eunuchs, and no contempt for them.** The palace counts the cut among its men and would count these women among them too. The Way's polemic is aimed at that classification, not at the people the palace cut. A eunuch who seeks to be born again is received as a sister. The design first read "we are not eunuchs" against Chiang's warning about third-sexing (Chiang 2012, pp. 23–24, 44). It then turned the polemic after Bhatt, who calls the third sex a "dumping-ground" and treats distancing oneself from the castrated as the respectability she rejects (Bhatt 2025, "The Third Sex").

**Rectified Names.** Confucius said that if names are not correct, speech fails, and in the end "punishments will not be properly awarded" (*Analects* 13.3). The Way turns this on the palace: a daughter named before witnesses is a daughter, and a register that files her as a eunuch is the disorder Confucius warned of. This is polemic, not exegesis. In the *Analects*, persons are made to fit their names: good government is when "the father is father, and the son is son" (12.11). And the omen gloss above shows the tradition naming such a change a wound.

**The Metal Mother.** Du Guangting's collection of women's lives, finished soon after 913, makes the Queen Mother of the West the head of every woman who attains the Way. "All women who ascend to transcendence and attain the Way are her dependents," and she decides who may be ordained (Cahill 2006, pp. 40, 46, 52). The Way makes her its high god. It has no head of faith.

**Temple Societies.** "The magistrate takes what a house with no daughter leaves, so every sister has a daughter." The tenet stands on the Song extinct-household law (McKnight and Liu 1999, pp. 244, 297), the Dunhuang societies' dues and righteous store (Ning and Hao 1997, pp. 15, 27–28), and the name-panel (Gernet 1995, p. 43).

**Its claimed foremothers.** In its own voice the Way claims women the sources never place outside womanhood. The faith claims them; the narrator does not, and none of them is a precedent for a trans woman. They are Huang Lingwei, the priestess who rebuilt Lady Wei's altars at Linchuan and left only her shroud in the coffin (Kirkland 1992, §II); Xue Xuantong, who left her marriage to practise and told a divine escort "Not yet! … Don't rush me!" (Cahill 2006, pp. 189–191); and the bearded woman of 1124, whom the court named a nüguan.

## How it plays

**The gate.** Any adult of the faith the game codes as male can take *Gestate the Jade Embryo*. The AI takes it mostly for Marked characters and eunuchs. About three months in comes the first bathing and the censor. About three months later comes the second bathing and the letter from home, and at ten months the birth. A physician may arrive in between with a purge. Holding firm usually works, and learning helps. If it fails, she loses time and gains stress but never loses the embryo. She can always set the work down by choice. The options that skip the roll are social or costly: sisters standing at the gate, the guarantor's word, a bribe, or Xue Xuantong's refusal at a cost in stress. After the birth the shared mechanics take over: re-embodiment, the trait of a daughter of the embryo, and the name, which she may already carry from Declare Before Witnesses.

**The line.** Daughters bear no children, so the line runs through *Take as Daughter*, the Sisters' Houses and *The Sisters Choose*. The Temple Societies add a money layer. Dues fall due when a sister of a House dies and when a daughter is born from the crown. The righteous store lends at half again. A House without a name-panel is exposed to the magistrate's inventory, which grows likelier as the game moves from Song law into Yuan and Ming. The name-panel is the answer, and it costs prestige as well as gold, because it is a favour from the throne.

**Growth.** The faith starts with Shazhou, a nüguan who is a daughter of the embryo at the count's court, and two young Marked women who have not yet risen. Each year a Marked character in China or the Tarim may hear the call. Courtiers who are Marked, eunuchs or declared sisters may begin the gestation on their own. In the third observation run (867–1060) it was the largest of the six faiths, with 233 members, four counties and a stable seat at Shazhou (faith_design.md §14). It grows by contact, not conquest.

**Neighbours.** The rite is female-dominated and pluralistic, with female clergy who may marry and an alms-and-pacification clergy. It has no head of faith. Prodigies turn up in its rulers' lands, and the ruler chooses between the law, silence and the Way. Kings and emperors of the Way can ordain by edict. Between 882 and 900 its rulers receive the rescript for Huang Lingwei. Buddhist, Hindu and Jain courts think a little less of its daughters; Jewish and Islamic courts react as described in the README's shared mechanics. A palace faction to contend with the scholar-officials is planned but not built. Its design follows Raha and van der Drift's warning that office is the price of inclusion: court office would give power and send the estates back to the crown, while the societies give less power and keep what they hold (Raha and van der Drift 2024).

## What is attested and what is invented

**Attested in the sources read:**
- Tang nüguan as a legal estate, about a third of the Daoist clergy, ordained with new names (Jia 2018).
- Daoist priestesses and abbeys at Dunhuang before 786, and the collapse of Daoism there under Tibet (Jia 2018; Gernet 1995; Rong 2013).
- The inner embryo: Shangqing re-gestation in the fourth century, *neidan* from the eighth, "ten months" by about 1075 (Pregadio 2019; Wang Mu 2011).
- The *Santian neijie jing* passage on Lord Lao and Mother Li (Despeux and Kohn 2003).
- Women's *she* at Dunhuang, their dues at births and deaths, and lending from a common store at about half again (Ning and Hao 1997; Galambos 2020).
- Name-panels, the Song extinct-household law with adopted daughters, and the Yuan and Ming tightening (Gernet 1995; McKnight and Liu 1999; Birge 2002; Bernhardt 1999).
- The omen gloss on castration, and the bearded woman ordained in 1124 (*Han shu* 27C1; *Song shi* 62).

**Reconstructed or invented by the mod:**
- **The name.** *Yutai*, "jade embryo", is the faith's own coinage. Neidan speaks of a holy embryo (*shengtai* 聖胎) or an embryo of the Dao (*daotai* 道胎) (Wang Mu 2011, §2.2–2.3). *Yutai* does not appear in Pregadio.
- **The faith itself.** No such faith existed. Stopping at the mother is a deliberate reading against the grain: every source the project read sets the goal at pure yang, and Cahill reads Du as seeing a woman adept as "a somewhat deficient male" (Cahill 2006, p. 30). The explicit "state of pregnancy" line is from Qing manuals (Despeux and Kohn 2003, p. 234). The faith uses it in its own voice; the narrator anchors only on older texts.
- **The two trials.** Wang Mu names no events in month three or month six (Wang Mu 2011, §2.3). Those are the mod's, set inside the attested "bathings". The censor's threat rests on Hinsch's private nuns, but the scene is constructed. "Subduing the white tiger", as the men's counterpart of the red dragon, comes from a 1906 preface (Valussi 2008, p. 62); dragon and tiger themselves are older emblems of yin and yang (Pregadio 2019, pp. 47–48). "Red dragon" was dropped from the month-six event because it is first attested in 1310.
- **The crown birth.** It follows the Zhong-Lü texts. Zhang Boduan places both conception and delivery in the Mysterious-Female (Wang Mu 2011, §2.1), and the crown-exit picture Pregadio prints is Ming (Pregadio 2019, p. 17).
- **Women ordaining women.** This is the Way's choice. Jia found no Tang case of it.
- **Temple Societies** joins three things that never met: Dunhuang's lay *she*, Song inheritance law from the interior, and name-panels granted mostly to Buddhist monasteries. The *she* had no names of their own (Galambos 2020, p. 199). The Song law belongs to the interior, and to a century after 867.
- **The holy sites and high god.** Making the Queen Mother a high god, and the Southern Marchmount the women's mountain, are design choices built on Du's text. Mogao itself is a Buddhist site, whose library cave belonged to a monastery (Rong 2013, pp. 119–123); its Daoist link is the town's abbeys.
- **The claimed women.** Huang Lingwei, Xue Xuantong and the woman of 1124 are claimed only in the faith's voice. Nothing in the sources makes any of them trans, and the narrator never says so. The same holds for Lord Lao: the faith's reading reverses the text's own priority.
