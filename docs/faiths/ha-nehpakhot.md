# Ha-Nehpakhot
*the women who were turned* · [Back to the README](../../README.md#ha-nehpakhot-the-women-who-were-turned)

A small, fictional current inside Judaism, set among the real Jewish women of medieval Arles. Its daughters are turned by prayer at Purim, never by a knife. Its law is the rabbis' own law, read against the grain. It holds no land and lives under other lords. It has to win each daughter in an argument before the baylons and council of its street, and it can be shunned only if the lord allows it. This page sets out the sources behind it, how each became a mechanic, and where the sources stop and the mod's invention begins.

## The historical setting

**Arles and its Jews.** An eleventh-century responsum already calls Arles "a great assembly" whose ordinances bind the towns around it (Ben-Shalom 2024, p. 25). Benjamin of Tudela counted about 200 families there around 1165 (Gross 1897, pp. 77–79). In 1144 the archbishop received jurisdiction over the Jews of Arles, and from 1251 most of it passed to the Angevin count (Ben-Shalom 2024, pp. 26, 29). A charter of 1215 let the community choose three rectors each year at Sukkot. In time these became the four *baylons*, the street's real leaders, beside councillors and auditors (Ben-Shalom 2024, pp. 27–28; Stouff 2014, "Chrétiens et juifs" §§6, 9). Jews usually lived in one or two streets in the parish of Saint-Martin, though they were not compelled to (Ben-Shalom 2024, pp. vii, 131).

**A self-rule licensed from outside.** Provençal communities could not ban one of their own on their own authority. Abba Mari wrote from Montpellier around 1305 that "we have no right to excommunicate except with the consent of our lord" (Shatzmiller 2023, p. 86). At Arles in 1405 the archbishop's vicar licensed the baylons to pronounce the ban for one year only, on two named matters, over "every Jew and every Jewess" (Hildenfinger 1900–1904, *REJ* 41: 85–86; Shatzmiller 2023, p. 87). Even a confraternity's statutes needed a Christian magistrate's approval (Ben-Shalom 2024, p. 55). Lords also granted privileges for a term: at Gordes in 1312, four Jewish householders could live and lend for ten years, renewable (Shatzmiller 2023, p. 182).

**Women in the street.** The Arles synagogue had two rooms in one building. A deed of 1452 sells "two in the men's school above" and "one below in the school of the Jewesses" (Hildenfinger, *REJ* 48: 75–76). A seat sold in 1409 "in the men's places, on the upper side" confirms the layout (Stouff 2014, "Chrétiens et juifs" n. 24). In October 1401 the baylons reported that the community had begun calling five women to its council, "because what concerns all must be approved by all" (Stouff 2014, "Chrétiens et juifs" §10). In 1407 two women, Estes de Cailario and Regina, the widow of a physician, sat in the assembly of householders. The donor's own Occitan terms summoned every head of household, "men and women" (Hildenfinger, *REJ* 41: 87–88; Stouff 2014, "Isaac Nathan" §20). Regina also became a governor of the new school for poor children (Hildenfinger, *REJ* 41: 90). The street kept several alms, among them an alms of the synagogue lamp and an alms for poor girls to be married (Stouff 2014, "Chrétiens et juifs" §19). Provençal communities more widely kept a *ma'or* society for the synagogue's lights (Ben-Shalom 2024, p. 55). Women lent money, kept shops and owned books. Venguessona Nathan, a widow, signed 326 loan contracts in 1431–40 and owned books in Hebrew and Latin (Stouff 2014, "Isaac Nathan" §§11, 14; Ben-Shalom 2024, pp. 120–121). Jewish women physicians appear in "dozens of the documents", styled *magistra*, *medica* or *fisica* (Shatzmiller 1994, p. 109).

**Kalonymus ben Kalonymus.** He was born at Arles (c. 1286; the year is disputed) and studied at Salon (Gross 1897, pp. 84–85). He made his Hebrew translations at Arles between 1307 and 1317 (Renan and Neubauer 1893, p. 74 [420]). He finished *Even Boḥan*, *The Touchstone*, in Tevet 5083, the winter of 1322–23, in Catalonia (Renan and Neubauer 1893, pp. 101 [447], 105 [451]; Shatzmiller 2023, p. 317 n. 13). Robert of Naples employed him to translate from Arabic into Latin. Through him the Jews of Rome obtained privileges from the king, and in 1329 the king's agent at Arles paid him a yearly stipend (Ben-Shalom 2024, pp. 293–294; Shatzmiller 1994, p. 48).

The *Touchstone* contains its speaker's lament at having been made a man (Lemberg 1865, pp. 15–19). It wishes "had the craftsman who made me made me a worthy woman" (p. 16). It imagines spinning with friends by moonlight (p. 16) and keeping the three women's commandments (p. 17). Then it prays through God's turnings, Dinah among them, and asks "would that You turn me from male to female" (pp. 17–18). The speaker would have been "lady of the house", but ends by saying the men's blessing "in a low voice" (p. 18). Scholars do not agree on how much of it he meant. Ben-Shalom says "there is no consensus among scholars regarding its meaning" (2024, p. 112). Davidson called it "humorous" (1907, p. 28). Rosen reads a serious critique made "under this comic guise" (2003, p. 170), but a momentary one: the speaker leaves masculinity and returns to it (pp. 184–185). The festival satire of the same book has the young men on Purim put on anklets and necklaces, and "this one puts on a woman's dress" (Lemberg p. 29).

**The law.** The rabbis divide the eunuch made "by the hands of heaven" from the one made by human hands (bYevamot 80b; Strassfeld 2013, p. 136). Strassfeld notes that "created by God" is the rabbinic way of saying "natural" (p. 137). The rabbis classified unusual bodies in order to fix the line between male and female. In the material Strassfeld surveys, no ruling lets anyone pass from male to female (Strassfeld 2013). Online lists of "four to eight genders in the Talmud" are, in Lev's word, "overstated" (Lev 2024, p. 31). Fonrobert's summary is "Sex is variable but gender is not" (2007, p. 289). Castration is forbidden outright, even of animals and even by asking a gentile to do it. A sterilising potion is forbidden to men too (Maimonides, *Mishneh Torah*, Issurei Bi'ah 16:10–13; *Sefer ha-Ḥinukh* 291). The aggadah, though, tells of Leah, who prayed, after which the child in her womb "was turned into a daughter", Dinah. The Talmud answers at once that "one does not cite miracles" (bBerakhot 60a). Purim cross-dressing was "technically an infringement" (Horowitz 2006, p. 252). Its permission, "for mere merriment", is Isserles's 16th-century gloss (*Shulḥan Arukh*, Oraḥ Ḥayyim 696:8).

**The blessing.** Around 840 women skipped the men's line "who did not make me a woman". By about 1340 they said "who made me according to His will" (Kahn 2011, pp. 66–67). Kahn and Baricci both treat Kalonymus's faint blessing as the earliest allusion to a women's alternative (Kahn 2011, p. 67 and p. 164 n. 33). Only at the end of the 15th century do books write the blessing down in its own words. A Judeo-Provençal siddur copied near Avignon has *qui fis me fenna*, "who made me a woman" (Baricci 2023, pp. 64, 66). Two Hebrew books copied in Italy in 1478 and 1480 have "who made me a woman and not a man", which Kahn calls "beyond the pale of what any rabbinic figure of the day could have accepted" (Kahn 2011, p. 71).

## What the faith holds

**Only heaven turns.** The sect takes the rabbis' own distinction and keeps the side it wants. The law counts what a human hand makes as a man maimed; what heaven makes is heaven's work. So a daughter is turned by prayer, "as Dinah was", and the knife and the potion are both refused (bYevamot 80b; Strassfeld 2013, p. 136; Issurei Bi'ah 16:10–12; *Ḥinukh* 291). Strassfeld also notes that the rabbis had no slot for Matthew's third eunuch: "The third type - eunuch for God - does not appear in rabbinic literature" (2013, p. 3 n. 6). The sect agrees, and asks for no such slot.

**Prayer is answered in its time.** Leah prayed and Dinah was turned (bBerakhot 60a). Rabbi Yitzḥak says the matriarchs were barren because "the Holy One longs for the prayer of the righteous". He also says prayer turns God's measure from anger to mercy, as a pitchfork turns grain (bYevamot 64a). Both texts use the root *h-p-kh* that gives the sect its name. Rashi says of Sarah that "a female organ was made for her" (Rashi on bYevamot 64b). The sect quotes all three in its own voice. The narrator says only that these are aggadot about prayer and fertility, not about a change of sex.

**Kalonymus meant it.** The sect claims Kalonymus as one of its own, in its own voice only: "the learned call it a jest; the women of the lamp say he meant it". Ben-Shalom's remark that readers "are free to understand and interpret the text as they see fit" (2024, p. 114) describes what the sect does. It is no claim about the man himself. His turnings list (Abraham from the furnace, Dinah in the womb, the staff, the hand, the sea, the rock) is the sect's liturgy (Lemberg p. 17). "Would that You turn me from male to female" is its motto (p. 18). It reverses his low voice by saying the petition aloud.

**A woman is made by what she keeps.** The three women's commandments Kalonymus wished for are the days of separation, the portion of the dough and the Sabbath lamp (Lemberg p. 17, after Mishnah Shabbat 2:6). In the midrash, Isaac knows Rebecca as Sarah's heir because the lamp, the dough and the blessing return with her (*Bereshit Rabbah* 60:16). The sect reads that as its tenet. Rosen's line that a man "is not born a man but becomes one" (2003, p. 176) points the same way. Rosen also records the cost the sect must face: the Mishnah ties the three to death in childbirth, and *Genesis Rabbah* 17 makes them Eve's penance (2003, pp. 181–182). A daughter whose days do not come keeps them as the *zekenah*, the old woman past them, keeps them. This is the sect's own reading. Grossman shows that much medieval niddah practice was women's custom, handed "from mother to daughter" (2004, ch. 1 §5).

**Daughters, not a third thing.** A moderate rabbi may offer her R. Yose's status for the *androginos*, "a created being of its own". The sect refuses it. Strassfeld reads the phrase as the clause that puts the *androginos* outside the law (2013, p. 162). In 2022 he adds that it also ties the *androginos* to creation (*Trans Talmud*, ch. 2). The same formula is used of the *koy*, a half-wild beast (Tosefta Bikkurim 2:1; Strassfeld 2013, p. 147). That is reason enough for the sect not to wear it. Bhatt's argument that third-sexing serves the hierarchy rather than challenging it (2025, "The Third Sex") is why the mod frames the refusal as the sect's position.

## How it plays

**The gate.** *Keep the Fourteenth of Adar* is open to any adult of the sect assigned male who is not yet a daughter (the game codes every seeker as male until her Turning), and to a Marked or declared seeker of another Jewish faith. It is shown all year but valid only in Adar (February or March). It costs 50 piety, and it can be taken once a Purim. A courtier offered the rite by the growth engine waits for Adar.

The chain runs in five steps. In the scroll she may go down to the women's synagogue (+10). In the revel she may keep the dress on (+20). The prayer is a roll of 30 against 60 for failure. It adds her learning and the elder's, +30 if she is Marked, +15 if she was taught the prayer, +10 in a county with the kahal and +15 in a licensed county. Every Adar that went unanswered adds +10, so patience is the real engine of the gate. A seeker of another Jewish faith who is turned joins the sect. Success gives the trait *Daughter of the Turning* (learning and diplomacy) and, under the game rule, re-embodiment. Then comes the naming, and a week later the judgment before the baylons.

**The judgment.** The learned dispute rolls 40 plus twice her learning, with +10 from 1401 if women sit on the council, against 50. A win gives +75 prestige and a ten-year licence for the county. A loss means *niddui* for three years, unless the county's lord has taken her people under his protection. Then he refuses the ban and the sentence falls. Claiming the house as its lady gives piety and peace of mind but accepts the shunning. Citing the *Touchstone* is safe and only possible after 1323. So the gate plays as a long game of learning, patience and patrons, not a single throw.

**The line.** Each year a daughter may keep the Three Lamps, spin with the women by moonlight (the women at court think better of her), or teach the prayer to a Marked courtier. A pupil gets +15 to her own Turning. The seed at Arles is an elder daughter of the Turning (learning 16, patient) and two Marked young women not yet turned, all at the count of Venaissin's court, as the Jews of Arles served its lords.

**The family and the door.** A daughter of the Turning may be sent a prayer book by her own kin, after the women's prayer book Leeds MS Roth 32, a wedding gift whose title page gives Rebecca's blessing as "my sister" (Baricci 2023, pp. 64–66). The choice is the kin's, and a refusal costs her nothing. In the Days of Awe she may stand at the door of the women's synagogue and give the prayer to the women outside, as the women's prayer leaders of the Rhine did (Grossman, ch. 8; Marienberg 2004). **The kahal keeps its children:** a guardian of another faith may teach a child of the sect but not convert her; in three observe runs the Arles household's children were all raised into the lord's faith.

**Growth.** In the project's observe runs the sect died out more than once, because daughters bear no children and a Marked girl, whom the world takes for a son, is born to about one family in a hundred. Three changes keep it alive:
- **The household.** A physician, his wife and three children start at the same court. Each child has a parent as guardian, since in one run all three grew up Christian. Children of members are born into the sect, and each year a Jewish adult at a court with two or more members has a 4% chance to join.
- **Matchmaking.** Each year an unmarried, fertile member aged 40 or under who is neither a daughter nor Marked has a 15% chance of a match with a Jewish spouse from outside the courts. The spouse joins the sect and the court, and a woman marries matrilineally. In the third tracked run the sect grew to 70 by matches alone, but it had no daughters after about 900.
- **The pilgrimage to the kahal.** The kahal is the court with most of the sect. Each year a Marked young Jewish woman (16–26), not yet turned, has a 10% chance to arrive there: +5 at 10 members, +5 at 20 and +10 at 40, since a larger kahal is known further. The eldest woman of the sect at least sixteen years older, a daughter first, adopts her, and the courtier rite may then offer the Turning. Marked members and daughters living elsewhere have a 10% chance a year to come to the kahal.

The shared *call* also sends Marked characters of the Jewish faiths to Ha-Nehpakhot. A member at court or a daughter nearby makes it likelier.

**Neighbours.** Jewish courts think less of a daughter of any faith ("what came forth male is male"), and a Jewish lord may be asked to rule on one. He can say she is a man, answer "one does not cite miracles, but she keeps her place", or let the worthies decide, which licenses his capital for five years. The sect's own +10 cancels the Jewish malus. A lord of any faith with a daughter of the Turning at court may commission a translation from Arabic, or license her as a physician. Christian hostility in the mod targets doctrine, never Jews as a people.

**Tenets and doctrines.** The Turning, The Three Lamps and Legalism. Gender equal, clergy of either sex (the *zekenah*), no head of faith, as in vanilla Judaism.

## What is attested and what is invented

**Attested in the sources.**
- Kalonymus's lament, his turnings list with Dinah, his petition, the three women's commandments and the men's blessing in a low voice (*Even Boḥan*, Lemberg pp. 15–19).
- Young men in women's dress on Purim, in one satirical text of 1322–23 (Lemberg p. 29).
- The women's synagogue below the men's at Arles (1452), five women called to the council (1401), and women in the binding assembly (1407).
- The lamp alms, the baylons, the ban licensed by the lord for a term, and Jewish women physicians.
- Kalonymus as a translator whose service won privileges for his people.
- The women's blessing "who made me a woman", written down from 1478 on.

**Reconstructed: real pieces put together by the mod.**
- *The women of the lamp.* No medieval women-only confraternity is attested. The *holim* of 1401 had eighteen members, all men, and its sick-watch was two men by night (Hildenfinger, *REJ* 41: 80–81). Stouff names the lamp alms but no members (§19). The sect as women householders who keep the lamp alms is the mod's frame, built from the council, the assembly and the alms.
- *The elder reads the scroll.* No document shows a woman reading Esther aloud in the women's synagogue. The nearest witnesses are Ashkenazi women's prayer leaders (Grossman 2004, ch. 8 §4; Baumgarten 2016, pp. 47–48) and Caslari's vernacular Esther.
- *The blessing.* The blessing is written down only after the game ends in 1453. That it is older is the sect's claim, made possible by Kahn's and Baricci's argument that women said it before scribes wrote it (Kahn 2011, pp. 70, 73; Baricci 2014, p. 236).
- *The woman translator.* Every documented translator is a man (Shatzmiller 1994; Shatzmiller 2023, pp. 22–24). Women physicians are well attested, which is why the physician event sits beside the translation.
- *The street names* are real women's names from Provençal and Arles documents, used for fictional daughters.

**Invented.**
- The Turning itself, every one of its five parts, the patience mechanic and the licence of ten years won by argument.
- The sect's whole exegesis: that heaven's hand turns, that Kalonymus meant it, and that a daughter keeps the days as the *zekenah* does.
- Its presence from 867. All its textual ground falls in c. 1320–1332 (Ben-Shalom 2024), so the early centuries are a game convention.
- The household, matchmaking and pilgrimage numbers, which are tuning.
- Jewish lords ruling on daughters.

**Left out on purpose.**
- The narrator never says a historical figure was trans. Kalonymus is claimed in the faith's voice only.
- The narrator never says the Talmud recognised "six genders" or a third sex. The rabbinic categories are legal classes of bodies (Strassfeld 2013, pp. 2–3; Lev 2024, p. 31).
- No Jewish character trades in slaves or eunuchs. That charge is a historiographic motif, not a finding (Phelan 2023). The sect has no Khazar seat.
- No Christian Purim accusations and no Haman effigy (Horowitz 2006, pp. 158–159, 267). A 1306 Purim case at Manosque survives only inside a Christian court's charge, so it is not used as precedent (Horowitz 2006, pp. 265–268; Ben-Shalom 2024, p. 197).
- None of the medieval "male menses" tropes (Resnick 2000).
- No beast, demon or "turncoat" imagery for Jews, since Christian bestiaries tied Jewish faith to changing sex (DeVun 2021, pp. 77, 84; Wingard 2023, pp. 950–951).
- No masks in the medieval revel (Davidson 1907, p. xiv).
- No expulsions are modelled.
