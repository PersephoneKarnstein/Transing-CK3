## About the project

*Crusader Dolls* adds six faiths built around trans women to Crusader Kings III. (The repository is *Transing-CK3*; the mod is *Crusader Dolls* in the launcher; the Byzantine faith is *Adelphai Dorleyaiou*.) Each is grounded in a different historical setting and plays differently. Their tenets come from contemporary transfeminist writing: baedelism, third-wave transfeminism and transgender Marxism. A history pass then gives each a veneer of plausibility in its own place and period.

The six faiths answer the same three questions in different ways:

| Faith | How one becomes a daughter | How the line continues | How it meets the world | Home |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Adelphai Dorleyaiou** | The knife, as a sacrament with a sponsor | A founding charter passes land to adopted daughters | Refuge, intake and church politics | Dorylaion, Phrygia |
| **Bædling** | Seiðr: a völva goes into trance for the seeker | The hall's fostered, sworn and adopted daughters | Outlawry, raiding and insult duels | Gotland |
| **Yutai Dao** (the Way of the Jade Embryo) | Ten months' inner gestation of one's own body | Adopted heirs, and temple societies that hold property in common | Palace factions against the scholar-officials | Dunhuang, at the Mogao caves |
| **Ha-Nehpakhot** | The Turning: a prayer said aloud at Purim | The women of the lamp, and the three women's commandments | The street's licence or its shunning; patronage by translating or medicine | The Jewish street of Arles |
| **Cūṭikkoṭutta Mālai** (the Worn Garland) | The pāvai vow, kept through Mārkaḻi with companions; the staff and a name | A share on the temple street, recorded in stone | Temple endowments; orthodox pandits | The temple street of Kanchipuram |
| **Badenya** | A smith's bargain, a woman's claim, and a jeli's word that cools | Through a sister or daughter; the House buries its own | Praise and satire; the smiths' power; naming feasts | Kirina, in Mali |

What the mod is not:

- **No antagonist.** It does not add a hostile "anti-trans" nation or stage an allegorical clash of superpowers. It adds the faiths, and vanilla Christendom, the Caliphate and the Tang court react as they would.
- **Not trans men.** Trans-masculine content is out of scope. Someone else is very welcome to make it.
- **Vanilla castration still works as before.** Castrating someone with the base game's interaction does not turn them into a daughter.

> [!NOTE]
> **Content note.** The mod deals with castration, with a penal path that transitions violent prisoners (the "Dorley precedent", after Alyson Greaves's *Welcome to Dorley Hall*), and with the transmisogyny of medieval law and custom. Event text is written without gore, but the subject matter is there.

> [!IMPORTANT]  
> **These faiths are fiction.** All six are invented for a game. Their places, institutions and texts are drawn from historical scholarship, and the bibliography below lists it, but no *known* historical people held these beliefs. Some, like the Byzantine Sisters' heresy, sit close enough to the record that someone might once have believed something like them, but we cannot know that, and the mod does not claim it. Nothing here is a claim about anyone's real ancestry. The mod does not present any living community, such as hijra, khwaja sira, bissu or two-spirit people, as one of its faiths or as a forerunner of a modern movement. Where a faith in the game claims a historical figure or a past people as its own, that is the faith speaking in character, not the mod making a historical argument.

## Playtesters wanted

The mod is in alpha. Most of it has never run in a real campaign, and we need people to play it and tell us what breaks. You don't need any modding experience.

> **Playtesters: start with the [test guide](docs/TO-TEST.md).** It lists specific scenarios to set up, from a five-minute smoke test to long observer runs, with console helpers to skip the waiting, what you should see, and exactly what to send back. Pick any scenario; partial results and "it worked" are both useful.

### Prerequisites

- **Crusader Kings III 1.20.x.** The mod is written against 1.20.1.
- **DLC.** *Roads to Power* is required: the faiths often end up landless, and some start that way. We test with all DLC enabled; reports from players with fewer DLC are especially useful.
- **A new game.** The faiths are placed in the world at game start, so an existing save will not have them.

### Loading the mod from this folder

1. **Get the files.** Clone the repository, or download it as a ZIP from the green *Code* button and unzip it anywhere.
   ```sh
   git clone https://github.com/PersephoneKarnstein/Transing-CK3.git
   ```
2. **Find your mod folder.**

   | OS | Mod folder |
   |---|---|
   | Windows | `%USERPROFILE%\Documents\Paradox Interactive\Crusader Kings III\mod` |
   | macOS | `~/Documents/Paradox Interactive/Crusader Kings III/mod` |
   | Linux | `~/.local/share/Paradox Interactive/Crusader Kings III/mod` |

3. **Create a launcher file there.** Copy `descriptor.mod` from the repository into the mod folder and rename it `transing_ck3.mod`. Then add one line at the end giving the full path to the folder you cloned or unzipped. Use forward slashes, even on Windows.
   ```
   path="C:/Users/you/Documents/Transing-CK3"
   ```
4. **Enable it.** Open the Paradox launcher, go to *Playsets*, add **Crusader Dolls** to a playset, and make that playset active.
5. **Start a new game.** At 867, the **Crusader Dolls** bookmark lists a head of each faith: Theia Beatrike at Dorylaion, Steinunn of Gotland, Huijing of Dunhuang, Cantirâdêvi of Candragiri, Djeneba of Kirina, and Bonadonna, an elder of the women of the lamp, who keeps a landless scholars' camp at Arles (Roads to Power). In the Ruler Designer you can choose the Kybeline culture and any of the six faiths. You can also convert to one in a normal game.

To update later, pull or re-download into the same folder. The launcher file does not need to change.

### What to test and how to report

Good first tests:

- **Ruler Designer.** Do all six faiths and the Kybeline culture appear?
- **The six rites.** Play a Marked woman (the game still codes her as male) and take each faith's decision: *Take the Knife*, *Ask a Völva to Sit for Me*, *Gestate the Jade Embryo*, *Keep the Fourteenth of Adar* (February or March only), *Keep the Pāvai Vow* (December or January only) and *Go to the Smith*.
- **Re-embodiment.** After a rite, do your titles, marriage, children, adoptive parents and skills carry over?
- **Adoption.** Try *Take as Daughter* on a courtier, a ward and a guest.
- **Arrivals and Houses.** Found a Sisters' House. Over a long reign, do Marked travellers, cast-out women and friends of the House arrive? Does the House choose an Elder Sister, and split when it is strained? Give the county to a ruler of another faith and see whether they close it.
- **Declare Before Witnesses** and **The Sisters Choose** (an elective succession law): does the declared name survive the rite, and does the election pick sensible heirs?

When something breaks, [open an issue](https://github.com/PersephoneKarnstein/Transing-CK3/issues/new?labels=bug) and include:

- **What you did** and what you expected to happen.
- **Your error log.** It is `error.log` in the `logs` folder next to your mod folder.
- **A save**, if you can attach one.

**[docs/TO-TEST.md](docs/TO-TEST.md) lists specific scenarios to set up, with what to expect and what to send.** The game's console can run test scripts from `tools/run/`. Copy them into the `run` folder next to your mod folder, launch the game with `-debug_mode`, open the console with `~`, and type, for example, `run tm_census.txt`.

## Adelphai Dorleyaiou: the Sisters of Dorylaion

*Adelphai Dorleyaiou*, "the Sisters of Dorylaion", are a Christian heresy of Byzantine Anatolia, seated in 867 at Dorylaion in Phrygia; the name is a pun on Dorley Hall. Dorylaion lies in the old heartland of the Phrygian Mother, a protector rather than a fertility goddess. In their own voice the Sisters claim a line from the Mother's house at Pessinus through the prophetesses of Pepouza, in the women-led Montanist church, to themselves. They call her castrated priests *gallae* and remember Pessinus kinder than it was: as Roller shows, it belonged to priests titled Attis who passed the office from father to son.

Their quarrel is with the court of their own day. Leo VI's law counted men, women and eunuchs, and the palace reserved offices for eunuchs it had mostly cut as boys. "The court calls its eunuchs angels," the Sisters say, "and buys them as boys." With Aristotle, who wrote that eunuchs "change into the female condition", they hold that one who takes the knife rises a daughter, not a third thing. The rite borrows Rome's March festival: blood and mourning on the Day of Blood, 24 March, then the knife and a new name at the Hilaria. The faith follows one of three Houses of the knife: Grandmother's House cuts captives in the old cruel way and calls them eunuchs; Aunt Bea's House puts only grown men proven violent through its cells, each under a sponsor who walked the same road; the Gift Alone takes only grown women who ask, since "even the Mother of God was asked."

Daughters are chosen, not born. A woman of the House may foster a girl or adopt her as heir by written clause, as Leo's laws allowed, and each House keeps a convent charter's rules: a Protectress from the founder's female line, an Elder Sister chosen by lot. The Mother of Mothers, Theia Beatrike, sits at Dorylaion with up to seven Companions. Lords may give daughters the palace offices once kept for eunuchs, and in 949 the Mother of Mothers may ransom the four child eunuchs Liudprand gave the Emperor. Against them stand bishops preaching "Cybele in Christian clothes" and the old laws against heretics, which climb from burned books to banned assemblies to voided wills, faster after the Komnenian turn of 1081. From 886 a Galenic wing may break away, agreeing with the Emperor that the cut are some third thing; the main rite calls it "the old cage with a new name".

What sets the Sisters apart is that they refuse an honour: Byzantium made its eunuchs a useful, owned estate, and the Sisters will not be one. No source attests such a sect, and their five foremothers, Stephania the First Mother among them, are invented.

## Bædling: the High Seat

The Bædlings are a Germanic pagan faith of Scandinavia and Anglo-Saxon England, seated on Gotland, with holy places at Uppsala, Trondheim, York and Kyiv. Their name comes from the Old English *Canons of Theodore*, a confessor's handbook translated from Latin between 690 and about 1025, which sets the *bædling* apart from "other men"; its editors, Fulk and Jurasinski, think the word named something now lost. The faith joins it to the Norse *seiðr*, a trance-craft the written sources call women's work and shameful for men, and *ergi*, the unmanliness it was said to bring. In the North the admired crossing ran toward manhood, and Icelandic law outlawed whoever called a man *ragr*. The clerks never wrote down what made a *bædling*; the Bædlings say it was the other road, and that they keep it.

Their daughters do not take the knife, which in the North unmade kings and punished vagrants. A seeker instead asks a völva, a seeress, to sit for her, in a three-day rite modelled on the seeress of *Eiríks saga rauða*. The völva is given the high seat and a feast, someone sings the warding songs that girls learn from their foster-mothers, and the völva goes up onto her platform into trance while the seeker waits below. She sees a daughter rather than makes one: if the song fails, the failure is the song's, and the seeker may ask again next winter. The new daughter declares herself before the þing, and when someone shouts *ragr* she may answer that "*ragr* is said of men". The hall continues through fostered, sworn and adopted daughters.

It is a martial faith with fighting clergy, all women. The Bædlings say the Allfather's true host holds no dead men. Their halls raid to bring daughters home. Their devil is Níðhöggr, because their enemy is *níð*, the slander shouted at the þing, which they meet in the flyting.

The law closes in over the centuries. From 1100 a daughter under a Christian Norse lord may be sued for sorcery or for wearing women's dress; unless (in Norway) six housewives swear for her, she goes abroad for three winters or is outlawed in full, and may lead a band of her own. From 1300 a bishop may send her on the pilgrim roads instead, and where sorcery became a capital crime (Norway from 1300, Scania 1328, Sweden 1350) a woman who comes before a court again is handed to her lord's keeping, though trials stay rare. Later, a lord at war may open his ports to anyone who will harm his enemy, as the Mecklenburg lords did after 1389, and a Bædling band that takes his letters may seize an island seat.

## Yutai Dao

*Yutai Dao* (玉胎道), "the Way of the Jade Embryo", is an invented Daoist faith seated at Dunhuang, beneath the Mogao caves. In the eighth century a third of Tang Daoist abbeys were convents, and their priestesses, the *nüguan*, were a legal estate of their own; Jia Jinhua counts 37 of them at Dunhuang in 758. Tibet took the town in 786, and its Daoism went dark. When the story opens in 867, Zhang Yichao has been summoned to Chang'an, his nephew holds the city without a title, and relighting the Shenquan Abbey is the faith's first task.

Its gate is inner alchemy. Daoist masters taught that an adept carries an embryo for ten months until it is born from her as her true self; Fabrizio Pregadio traces the idea from fourth-century scriptures to Zhang Boduan's "ten months" of about 1075. Where the masters pressed on to pure yang, the Way stops at the mother, taking as its warrant Lord Lao, who in a text of about 420 took the shape of Mother Li and sheltered in his own womb. A seeker needs a guarantor, answers a priestess's "Will you ever repent?", and weathers trials: a censor threatening to strike her from the clergy roll, a letter calling her home. Then she is born from the crown and named before witnesses. There is no knife. The palace counts its eunuchs as men and would count these women with them; the Way answers with Confucius's rectification of names, and welcomes any eunuch who seeks the same birth as a sister. Its high god is the Queen Mother of the West.

It endures through women's societies modelled on real ones at Dunhuang, which paid dues at births and deaths and lent from a common "righteous store"; a bylaw of 959 makes members sisters. The faith joins them to Song law, under which a daughter, born or adopted, keeps a sonless house from the magistrate, and to the throne's name-panels that shielded houses from confiscation. Its story darkens as the law tightens under the Yuan from 1264 and the Ming from 1369. At court a daughter may take office on the eunuchs' side of the palace against the scholar-officials, at the price of her houses' protection and the danger of the purge of 903.

What sets it apart is that it treats womanhood as something carried and born, and defends it with dues and petitions, not conquest. In its own voice it claims foremothers such as Xue Xuantong, who told a divine escort "Not yet! Don't rush me!", and a bearded woman the Song court ordained a nüguan in 1124. Those claims are the faith's alone: nothing in the sources makes either woman trans.

## Ha-Nehpakhot: the women who were turned

Ha-Nehpakhot is an invented current inside Judaism, set among the real Jewish women of medieval Arles, in Provence. Its keepers are the women of the lamp: householders of the Jewish street who sit in the women's synagogue, below the men's, and keep the alms of the synagogue lamp. In 1401 the Arles council called five women to sit, "because what concerns all must be approved by all" (no women-only confraternity is attested). The sect holds no land and lives under other lords, as the Jews of Arles lived under their archbishop. In 867 its elder, Bonadonna, keeps a company of scholars and physicians under the count of Venaissin.

Its ground is the rabbis' own law, read against the grain. The Talmud counts a eunuch made by human hands a man still; no rabbinic ruling lets anyone pass from male to female. Yet it tells how Leah prayed and the child in her womb was turned into a daughter, Dinah. In the winter of 1322–23 Kalonymus ben Kalonymus, born at Arles, wrote the satire *Even Boḥan* (*The Touchstone*), in which he asks God, perhaps in jest, "would that You turn me from male to female". The sect takes all this at its word, in its own voice: only the hands of heaven turn, so it refuses the knife.

A daughter is made by the Turning, a prayer said at Purim, in Adar. An elder reads Esther and, beside "it was turned about", the other turnings, Dinah's among them. From 1323 young men put on women's dress for the joke; she keeps hers on when the joke is over. Where the men thank God each morning that He did not make them women, she asks aloud for the reverse, and an unanswered prayer is answered in its time, as Leah's was. On Shushan Purim the women teach her their blessing, "who made me a woman" (*qui fis me fenna*, written down only after 1453; that it is older is the sect's claim), and she takes a name from the scroll or the street: Esther, Dinah, Regina. Womanhood is then what she keeps: the three women's commandments Kalonymus wished for, the days of separation, the dough and the Sabbath lamp.

The street's judges must still hear her, and she answers text with text. Losing means shunning, but in Christian lands a community could ban only by its lord's licence, and a lord who protects her people lets the sentence fall. She earns that protection by service, translating from Arabic as Kalonymus did for Robert of Naples, or practising as a *fisica*, as Provençal Jewish women physicians did. Its households marry within the kahal, which keeps its children. Learned, landless and without a knife, it is a small minority arguing within its own law.

## Cūṭikkoṭutta Mālai

The Worn Garland is an invented current of Tamil devotion inside Hinduism, seated on the temple street of Kanchipuram. Its name comes from Āṇṭāḷ, the woman among the Vaiṣṇava poet-saints, who by legend wore the god's garland before it was offered and was called "the lady who gave what she had worn". From the seventh century Tamil poets, men among them, sang to the god as girls in love, and later commentators wrote that before the Lord the devotee "will wear the dress of a woman". In period this was a rite or a reading; the Garland holds that it was a life. Its other root is the temple women Leslie Orr found in Tamil inscriptions of 850–1300: unmarried donors with houses and shares on the temple street, their own wealth, and sometimes the title "daughter of God". No record puts anyone assigned male among them, so the faith is a reconstruction, and says so.

A seeker becomes a daughter through the pāvai vow, kept for a month in Mārkaḻi (December or January) with the women of her court. Before dawn she wakes her companions door to door; they sing Śiva's *Tiruvempāvai* or Āṇṭāḷ's *Tiruppāvai* while cold mornings thin the circle; she may wear the god's garland before it is offered, as Āṇṭāḷ did. At the end a staff is touched to her head and she is given a name, an old rite of Śiva's temples. What decides it is how many friends held to the end.

Her scripture is King Bhaṅgāśvana of the *Mahābhārata*, turned into a woman and offered her old body back, who answered: "I desire to remain a woman." Orthodox pandits reply with Ilā, turned back in every telling; the Garland, following Talia Bhatt, calls Ilā's half-and-half state a cage. A Hindu lord may grant a daughter a house and a share on the temple street in her own name; from 1070 she may buy one and pass it to a daughter of her choosing. Her gold becomes gifts cut in the temple wall: a lamp, a feeding house, the Lady set beside the god. Reciters bring Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār's story and Basavaṇṇa's sayings, which the faith hears as its own, and landless daughters endow lamps through market assemblies or guard caravans to the coast.

What sets the Garland apart is that it answers to both gods: Śiva's temples give it its daughters, rite and women's song, Viṣṇu's poets the bride. And where every human bride of the god in the old myths merges with the image or dies, its daughters stay in the world, own a share, and give. Āṇṭāḷ is venerated but never cast as its founder; the faith claims her vow, not her person.

## Badenya: the Mother's Children

Badenya imagines a faith of the Mande world on the upper Niger, seated at Kirina, where the Sunjata epic sets the founding of Mali. In Mande speech *badenya*, mother-childness, is the bond of children who share a mother; *fadenya*, father-childness, is the rivalry of those who share a father. The faith takes the first as its law. In Ghana, al-Bakrī wrote in 1068, the king's heir was his sister's son. At Walata in 1352 Ibn Baṭṭūṭa found the heirs were a man's sister's sons among people who prayed and studied the law; at Mali's court he saw bird-masked bards admonish the king. No source shows anyone assigned male living as a woman in West Africa before 1453: Badenya is an avowed reconstruction.

Three people make a daughter. A smith brokers her bargain with a djinn: in twentieth-century Bamana accounts the djinn asks a life from a sculptor's household, and one who gave his own manhood instead was called less than a man. Badenya reverses that value: she gives the manhood she was assigned so that no one else is given, and she is a woman. She is then "hot", the Mande idiom for being between states, until a woman claims her as her mother's child, as Tenenba, in one bard's telling recorded in 1994, claimed a hero she had not borne: "I have given birth to a child without getting pregnant." She pays bracelets for her crossing, and a bard speaks her name before the lord, her claiming mother's name first. The word cools her; no daughter names herself. The line runs by claiming, through a sister or a daughter, and the House buries its own under her own name.

Its clergy are *jelimusow*, women bards. A jelimuso sings a ruler's line, mother's name first, and is paid before everyone; paid poorly, she may mock a thin purse, never a daughter's womanhood. When a ruler dies, the heir must honour the sister's line or see the sister's child gain a claim. At a Muslim court the Maliki jurist is the faith's sharpest critic. From 1352 a ruler may name a woman associate in rule, and the associate may fall as one did in Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's account: dust or ashes from the house's women, a veiled daily ride to the audience, refuge in the preacher's house. Members and daughters with a hostile husband or lord may go to the rock of Koulikoro, below Kirina, where whoever comes is not taken. The faith calls that refuge old; it is recorded from the nineteenth century.

Badenya's gate is social, with no knife: Mali had no eunuch office. It claims no matriarchy, only the attested sister's line, and takes no living West African community as its own.

## Shared mechanics

The six faiths share a common frame. Some people are born Marked by the Mother: trans women before any rite, whom the game still counts as men. Each faith has its own gate by which a sister becomes a daughter in body. Because daughters bear no children, every faith continues by adoption, fostering and sworn sisterhood, and keeps its people together in Sisters' Houses that belong to the land and outlast the lords who tolerate them. The faiths grow slowly, by contact, as the Marked hear the call of the nearest of the six. The wider world answers them in its own idioms: Byzantine law, Islamic jurists, rabbinic courts, Brahmin pandits, Norse slander, Song magistrates. A daughter may still find a lord's protection, a refuge, or a war fought to bring her home.

**Read more:** [the shared mechanics](docs/shared_mechanics.md), feature by feature.

## Contributing

Playtest reports are the most useful contribution right now. Pull requests are welcome too:

1. Fork the project.
2. Create a branch (`git checkout -b feature/your-idea`).
3. Commit your changes. Keep script `.txt` and localisation `.yml` files in UTF-8 **with** a byte-order mark.
4. Push the branch and open a pull request.

Design notes live in [`docs/`](docs). Start with [`docs/faith_design.md`](docs/faith_design.md) and [`docs/beliefs.md`](docs/beliefs.md). [CK3 Tiger](https://github.com/amtep/tiger) validates the scripts, though it still reports some false positives against the 1.20 religion files.

Art is wanted. The faith and trait icons are placeholders, and we would like to commission proper ones.
